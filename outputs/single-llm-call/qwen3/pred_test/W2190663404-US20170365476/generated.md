# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

Silicon substrates serve as the foundational platform for a vast array of semiconductor devices, including photodetectors, solar cells, and integrated circuits, due to their well-understood material properties, abundance, and compatibility with established fabrication technologies. However, the intrinsic bandgap of silicon limits its ability to absorb photons with energies below approximately 1.1 electronvolts, restricting its utility in infrared detection and advanced photovoltaic architectures that require broadband spectral response. To overcome this limitation, researchers have pursued methods to hyperdope silicon—introducing dopant concentrations that far exceed the thermodynamic solubility limit—to induce sub-bandgap optical absorptance through the formation of intermediate electronic states. One particularly promising approach involves the creation of hyperdoped black silicon, a material that combines supersaturated dopant incorporation with a self-organized, light-trapping surface morphology characterized by conical or needle-like structures on the micrometer scale. This dual functionality enables near-unity absorptance across both above- and below-bandgap wavelengths. Despite these advantages, hyperdoped black silicon fabricated via femtosecond laser irradiation typically exhibits a polyphasic surface layer composed of amorphous silicon, pressure-induced crystalline phases, and nanoscale defects, which degrade its electrical performance and hinder device integration. Conventional thermal annealing can improve crystallinity and enhance rectifying junction quality, but it invariably causes dopant atoms to migrate to optically inactive lattice sites, thereby quenching the critical sub-bandgap absorptance. This fundamental trade-off between structural order and optical function has long impeded the practical deployment of hyperdoped black silicon in high-performance optoelectronic systems.

## SUMMARY

The invention introduces a method for processing a semiconductor substrate to simultaneously achieve high crystallinity, preserved sub-bandgap optical absorptance, and retained light-trapping surface morphology through the sequential application of thermal and nanosecond pulsed laser annealing. The method begins with the incorporation of a dopant into a surface layer of a semiconductor substrate, where the dopant concentration exceeds the solid solubility limit of the dopant in the substrate material, thereby forming a hyperdoped surface layer. This hyperdoped layer is initially generated through the application of short radiation pulses, such as femtosecond laser pulses, in the presence of a dopant-containing atmosphere or film, which simultaneously induces surface texturing and dopant incorporation. The resulting surface layer exhibits a polyphasic structure comprising amorphous silicon, pressure-induced crystalline phases, and localized crystalline domains, overlaid with light-trapping surface textures characterized by undulations on the micrometer scale. A thermal annealing step is then applied to enhance the crystallinity of the doped surface layer and to improve the formation of a rectifying homojunction with the underlying bulk substrate. However, this thermal treatment reduces the sub-bandgap optical absorptance of the material. To restore the optical functionality without sacrificing crystallinity, the thermally annealed surface layer is subjected to optical annealing using nanosecond pulsed laser radiation. The optical annealing process melts the near-surface region to a controlled depth, followed by rapid resolidification that re-incorporates dopant atoms into optically active, non-equilibrium lattice positions while preserving the underlying micrometer-scale surface textures. This process enhances the crystallinity of the polyphase surface layer, reduces lattice defects, and restores sub-bandgap optical absorptance to levels comparable to or exceeding those of the as-fabricated material. The resulting doped surface layer exhibits an absorptance greater than 50% for wavelengths below the semiconductor bandgap, maintains a dopant concentration above the solid solubility limit, and forms a functional diode junction with the substrate. The method is applicable to a range of semiconductor substrates, including silicon, germanium, silicon carbide, and gallium arsenide, and employs dopants such as sulfur, selenium, tellurium, and other chalcogens that are electron-donating or hole-donating species. The surface textures are generated using laser pulses and are preserved throughout the thermal and optical annealing steps. The optical annealing may be performed using lasers with wavelengths in the ultraviolet to near-infrared range, with pulse durations between 1 and 100 nanoseconds, and fluences sufficient to induce melting without destroying the surface morphology. The method enables the fabrication of photodetectors and photovoltaic devices with enhanced responsivity across the visible and infrared spectrum, and the process may be integrated with ion implantation for dopant incorporation, with the doped surface layer subsequently subjected to thermal and optical annealing to achieve the desired combination of structural, optical, and electrical properties.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The processing of semiconductor substrates to achieve enhanced optoelectronic performance requires precise control over dopant incorporation, crystalline structure, and surface morphology. The solid solubility limit defines the maximum concentration of a dopant that can be incorporated into a semiconductor lattice under thermodynamic equilibrium conditions; for chalcogens such as sulfur in silicon, this limit is approximately five orders of magnitude lower than the concentrations required for sub-bandgap absorptance. Hyperdoping, therefore, necessitates non-equilibrium processing techniques. The formation of a polyphasic doped surface layer occurs when a semiconductor substrate, typically silicon, is exposed to a dopant-containing environment during irradiation with short-pulse lasers. This process results in localized melting, rapid resolidification, and the trapping of dopant atoms within a thin surface layer that contains amorphous regions, high-pressure crystalline phases, and residual crystalline silicon. The surface textures that emerge from this process are characterized by undulations ranging from one to ten micrometers in height, which function as efficient light-trapping structures by reducing surface reflectance through multiple internal reflections. The polyphasic nature of this layer, while beneficial for optical absorption, introduces electrical discontinuities and poor carrier transport properties. Conventional thermal annealing can enhance crystallinity by promoting atomic rearrangement and phase transformation, but it also enables dopant diffusion to energetically favorable, optically inactive sites, thereby diminishing sub-bandgap absorptance. Optical annealing using nanosecond pulsed laser radiation provides a mechanism to reverse this degradation. The laser pulses deliver sufficient energy to melt the near-surface region to a depth of approximately 50 to 150 nanometers, initiating resolidification from the crystalline substrate interface upward. This rapid cooling rate, on the order of 10^9 to 10^10 K/s, prevents dopant segregation and traps atoms in metastable, optically active configurations. Raman spectroscopy confirms the reduction of amorphous and pressure-induced phases, with the crystalline silicon peak narrowing and shifting toward the position observed in pristine monocrystalline silicon, indicating improved lattice order. The optical absorptance, measured across ultraviolet to far-infrared wavelengths, remains above 50% for sub-bandgap photons, even after multiple annealing cycles. The diode junction formed between the doped surface layer and the underlying bulk substrate retains its rectifying characteristics, as evidenced by current-voltage measurements showing turn-on voltages consistent with homojunction behavior. The method accommodates multiple dopant incorporation strategies, including laser-assisted diffusion from gaseous or thin-film sources, as well as ion implantation followed by thermal and optical annealing. The surface textures are preserved throughout the process, as the nanosecond laser fluence is carefully selected to melt only the thin polyphasic layer without ablating or flattening the micrometer-scale features. The resulting highly crystalline doped surface layer exhibits enhanced electrical conductivity, retained sub-bandgap absorptance, and structural integrity suitable for direct integration into photodetector and photovoltaic architectures. The process may be implemented using an integrated system comprising a femtosecond laser for texturing and doping, and a nanosecond laser for optical annealing, with beam delivery controlled by scanning mirrors and translation stages to ensure uniform treatment across the substrate surface. The system may operate under controlled atmospheric conditions, with dopant gases introduced via gas lines and evacuated through pump lines to maintain process purity. The method is not limited to silicon; it is equally applicable to germanium, silicon carbide, and gallium arsenide substrates, with dopants selected to induce sub-bandgap transitions in each material system. The combination of equilibrium and non-equilibrium processing steps enables unprecedented control over the competing demands of crystallinity, optical response, and electrical functionality in hyperdoped semiconductor materials.

## EXEMPLIFICATION

In Example 1, a monocrystalline silicon wafer was irradiated with femtosecond laser pulses in the presence of sulfur hexafluoride gas to generate a hyperdoped black silicon surface layer with conical surface textures averaging ten micrometers in height. Raman spectroscopy confirmed the presence of amorphous silicon and pressure-induced crystalline phases, while optical absorptance measurements showed greater than 90% absorption across the sub-bandgap spectrum. Subsequent nanosecond laser annealing using a 355-nm Nd:YAG laser at a fluence of 2.2 J/cm² resulted in the complete elimination of amorphous and high-pressure phases, as verified by Raman spectra, with the crystalline silicon peak narrowing by approximately one reciprocal centimeter. Electrical measurements revealed no degradation in rectification, with a turn-on voltage of 0.6 volts and a rectification ratio exceeding 50 at ±10 volts. Optical absorptance remained above 85% for wavelengths between 1.2 and 4.0 micrometers, demonstrating that the sub-bandgap response was preserved. In contrast, rapid thermal annealing at 700°C for 30 minutes reduced sub-bandgap absorptance to less than 20% while enhancing crystallinity, confirming the known trade-off between structural order and optical function. Subsequent nanosecond laser annealing restored the absorptance to 75%, maintaining the high crystallinity achieved during thermal treatment. In Example 2, hyperdoped black silicon was fabricated using a lower femtosecond laser fluence to produce one-micrometer-tall conical structures. Nanosecond laser annealing at 0.6 J/cm² preserved these structures and restored sub-bandgap absorptance to 85%, while higher fluences above 2.0 J/cm² caused structural collapse and a corresponding drop in absorptance. Transmission electron microscopy confirmed the removal of amorphous skin layers and the emergence of single-crystalline domains throughout the surface layer. Current-bias measurements demonstrated consistent rectification across all processed samples, and optical absorptance was quantified using UV-VIS-NIR and FTIR spectrometers, with values calculated from reflectance and transmittance data. The results demonstrate that nanosecond laser annealing enables the reactivation of sub-bandgap optical absorptance following thermal deactivation, without compromising crystallinity or surface morphology, and that the process is tunable based on initial surface texture dimensions and laser parameters.

## CONCLUSION

The invention demonstrates that nanosecond pulsed laser annealing provides a uniquely effective means of enhancing the crystallinity of hyperdoped semiconductor surface layers while preserving their sub-bandgap optical absorptance and light-trapping morphologies. This capability resolves a longstanding challenge in the field of non-equilibrium semiconductor materials, where traditional thermal processing degrades optical functionality. The method enables the fabrication of high-performance photodetectors and photovoltaic devices with broadband spectral response, superior electrical rectification, and mechanical robustness. The process is compatible with a variety of semiconductor substrates and dopant species, and can be integrated into existing fabrication workflows involving ion implantation, chemical vapor deposition, and photolithography. While challenges remain in precisely controlling dopant concentration gradients and layer thicknesses for optimal carrier extraction, the present method provides a critical enabling step toward practical implementation of hyperdoped materials in infrared optoelectronics. Variations in laser wavelength, pulse duration, and fluence offer additional degrees of freedom for tailoring material properties, and the underlying physical principles of melting and rapid resolidification suggest broader applicability to other non-equilibrium systems beyond silicon-based hyperdoping. The combination of equilibrium and non-equilibrium processing techniques represents a paradigm shift in the design and fabrication of advanced semiconductor devices.
