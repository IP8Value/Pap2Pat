# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

- high-dose Grid radiotherapy

The present invention relates to a novel method of delivering high-dose spatially fractionated radiation therapy, specifically an advanced form of grid-based radiotherapy that employs a three-dimensional dose lattice structure to maximize therapeutic efficacy while minimizing collateral damage to surrounding healthy tissues. This method represents a significant evolution beyond the traditional two-dimensional grid techniques that have been in limited clinical use since the 1930s. Unlike conventional grid radiotherapy, which relies on static, planar shielding patterns to create alternating high- and low-dose regions primarily in the superficial layers of tissue, the invention introduces a volumetric approach wherein high-dose regions are precisely distributed throughout the three-dimensional volume of a malignant tumor. These high-dose regions, or vertices, are arranged in a spatially controlled pattern that ensures maximal concentration of radiation within the target volume while maintaining steep dose gradients between adjacent high-dose zones. The resulting dose distribution exhibits a pronounced peak-to-valley characteristic, with peak doses exceeding 12 Gy and valley doses falling below 3 cGy, thereby preserving the biological mechanisms associated with spatially fractionated radiation—such as the bystander effect and abscopal response—while eliminating the unnecessary irradiation of normal tissues external to the tumor. This innovation enables the safe delivery of ablative radiation doses directly within the tumor mass, even in anatomically complex regions such as the pancreas, lung, or mediastinum, where conventional radiotherapy is constrained by proximity to critical organs. The method is designed to be compatible with existing clinical radiotherapy platforms, including linear accelerators equipped with multileaf collimators, robotic stereotactic systems, and proton or heavy ion beam delivery systems, without requiring hardware modifications. It is particularly suited for patients with bulky, hypoxic, or radioresistant tumors that have historically responded poorly to uniform-dose radiation regimens. The invention thus redefines the clinical utility of spatially fractionated radiotherapy by transforming it from a palliative salvage technique into a potentially curative, precision-guided modality that can be integrated into multimodal treatment protocols.

## BACKGROUND

- introduce high-dose Grid radiotherapy

High-dose grid radiotherapy, historically referred to as spatially fractionated radiotherapy, was first introduced in the early 20th century as a means to deliver ablative radiation doses to large, inoperable tumors while mitigating the risk of severe skin and mucosal toxicity. Early implementations utilized orthovoltage X-ray sources and physical grids composed of metallic shields with regularly spaced apertures, typically circular or square, ranging from 0.5 to 1.5 centimeters in diameter. These grids were positioned between the radiation source and the patient to create a checkerboard-like pattern of high-dose and low-dose regions on the skin and underlying tissues. Despite its rudimentary nature, this technique demonstrated remarkable tumor regressions in cases where conventional radiotherapy had failed, particularly in advanced squamous cell carcinomas and sarcomas. The clinical rationale was grounded in the observation that surrounding undamaged tissue could facilitate rapid repair of irradiated regions, thereby allowing higher total doses to be administered without unacceptable normal tissue complications. Over time, the technique was adapted to megavoltage linear accelerators, but its application remained largely confined to superficial or accessible tumors due to the inherent limitations of two-dimensional beam geometry.

- describe early applications

In its earliest clinical applications, grid radiotherapy was employed almost exclusively for palliative purposes in patients with massive, locally advanced tumors that were deemed unresectable and unresponsive to standard radiation protocols. The treatment was typically delivered in a single session, with doses ranging from 10 to 20 Gy delivered to the high-dose regions. Clinical reports from the 1960s through the 1990s documented dramatic tumor shrinkage, sometimes exceeding 80% volume reduction, even in tumors with extensive necrosis or hypoxic cores. These outcomes were often accompanied by minimal acute toxicity, attributed to the spatial separation of high-dose zones, which preserved viable tissue for repair. However, the technique was never widely adopted due to its perceived lack of precision, inconsistent dosimetry, and the emergence of more conformal radiotherapy methods such as three-dimensional conformal radiation therapy and intensity-modulated radiation therapy.

- discuss limitations of 2D Grid therapy

The principal limitation of two-dimensional grid therapy lies in its inability to confine high-dose regions to the tumor volume. Because the grid pattern is applied in a single plane, the resulting high-dose “pipes” or columns of radiation extend through the entire depth of the tissue, often intersecting critical structures such as the spinal cord, esophagus, or bowel before reaching the tumor. Moreover, the highest radiation dose is frequently deposited in the skin and subcutaneous tissues, which are not part of the intended target, leading to unnecessary morbidity and limiting the dose that can be safely delivered. Additionally, the uniform spacing of apertures in 2D grids does not account for tumor irregularity, resulting in suboptimal coverage and potential underdosing of tumor subvolumes. The technique also lacks the ability to modulate dose intensity or adapt to respiratory motion, rendering it incompatible with modern image-guided radiotherapy standards.

- motivate 3D dose lattice radiotherapy

The advent of advanced imaging, real-time motion tracking, and highly precise beam delivery systems has rendered the limitations of 2D grid therapy obsolete. There is now a compelling clinical and biological imperative to transition from planar grid patterns to a true three-dimensional dose lattice, wherein high-dose vertices are distributed throughout the tumor volume in a manner that maximizes tumor coverage while minimizing exposure to adjacent normal tissues. Such a lattice structure preserves the essential radiobiological phenomena—bystander signaling, endothelial disruption, and abscopal immune activation—that underlie the efficacy of spatially fractionated radiation, but does so with unprecedented spatial fidelity. The invention herein proposes a systematic method for generating such a 3D dose lattice using existing clinical infrastructure, thereby transforming grid radiotherapy from an outdated palliative tool into a precision oncology modality capable of inducing durable tumor control with minimal toxicity.
