# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD AND BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

The field of this invention pertains to regenerative medicine and cellular therapy for endocrine disorders, particularly diabetes mellitus. Beta-cell replacement through transplantation of isolated islets or stem cell-derived beta cells has long been pursued as a curative strategy for insulin-dependent diabetes. Despite significant advances, the clinical translation of this approach remains severely limited by the scarcity of human donor pancreata, the inability to expand functional beta cells ex vivo, and the progressive loss of insulin-producing capacity during in vitro culture. Primary human beta cells, once isolated from pancreatic islets, rapidly dedifferentiate under standard culture conditions, losing key transcriptional regulators such as PDX1, NKX6.1, MAFA, and NEUROD1, as well as their ability to secrete insulin in a glucose-responsive manner. This dedifferentiation is accompanied by morphological changes, including increased cell size, loss of cell clustering, and acquisition of mesenchymal markers, suggesting an epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition. Prior attempts to maintain or restore beta-cell identity have relied on complex media formulations, growth factor supplementation, or co-culture systems, but none have achieved consistent, robust redifferentiation of expanded cells to a functional state. The NOTCH signaling pathway, a conserved cell-cell communication mechanism critical for cell fate decisions during embryonic development, has been implicated in the regulation of pancreatic progenitor maintenance and differentiation. In the developing pancreas, NOTCH activation suppresses endocrine commitment by repressing neurogenin 3, while its downregulation permits the emergence of insulin-producing beta cells. In adult islets, NOTCH components remain expressed, and their reactivation in cultured beta cells has been associated with dedifferentiation and proliferation. However, the precise role of NOTCH signaling in the maintenance of the dedifferentiated state of expanded human beta cells has not been fully elucidated, nor has its inhibition been systematically exploited to reverse this process. Previous studies have focused on pharmacological inhibition of gamma-secretase or genetic knockdown of NOTCH1 in rodent models, but these approaches have not been translated to human cells with sufficient specificity or efficacy to enable therapeutic application. There remains a critical unmet need for a method that enables the large-scale expansion of human beta cells followed by their reliable redifferentiation into functional, insulin-secreting cells without reliance on donor tissue.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a novel method for generating functional adult islet beta cells through the controlled inhibition of the NOTCH signaling pathway during the redifferentiation phase following in vitro expansion. The method comprises contacting expanded human beta cells with a NOTCH pathway inhibitor under serum-free conditions to induce re-expression of beta-cell-specific genes and restore glucose-responsive insulin secretion. The expanded beta cells are cultured in a defined, serum-free medium supplemented with insulin, transferrin, selenium, and albumin, and subsequently exposed to a redifferentiation medium containing elevated glucose, nicotinamide, exendin-4, and activin A to enhance cluster formation and transcriptional reprogramming. The invention further encompasses a method for treating diabetes by transplanting these redifferentiated cells into a diabetic subject, thereby restoring endogenous insulin production and glycemic control. The invention also introduces a method for purifying dedifferentiated beta cells from heterogeneous islet cell cultures by permanently tagging primary beta cells with a lineage-specific reporter construct prior to expansion. This is achieved by introducing an expression vector containing a beta-cell-specific promoter driving Cre recombinase fused to a modified estrogen receptor, in combination with a loxP-flanked stop cassette preceding a fluorescent reporter gene such as eGFP. Upon exposure to tamoxifen, only insulin-expressing beta cells activate the reporter, enabling their isolation by fluorescence-activated cell sorting. These permanently labeled dedifferentiated beta cells are then cultured under conditions that promote expansion, followed by NOTCH pathway inhibition to induce redifferentiation. The invention further provides isolated populations of redifferentiated beta cells characterized by high insulin content, expression of key beta-cell transcription factors, and absence of co-expression of other islet hormones, distinguishing them from immature or multipotent progenitors. Additionally, the invention includes a method for identifying agents that modulate beta-cell proliferation or redifferentiation by screening candidate compounds or nucleic acids in cultures of tagged dedifferentiated beta cells, followed by quantification of insulin expression, cluster formation, and cell cycle markers.

## DESCRIPTION OF THE PREFERRED EMBODIMENTS

The invention provides redifferentiated populations of expanded adult islet beta cells that exhibit restored expression of insulin, C-peptide, PDX1, NKX6.1, MAFA, and GCK, and demonstrate glucose-stimulated insulin secretion in vitro and in vivo. These cells are suitable for cell replacement therapy in insulin-dependent diabetes, offering a scalable alternative to cadaveric islet transplantation. The principles underlying this invention are based on the discovery that expanded human beta cells, despite losing their differentiated phenotype, retain an epigenetic memory that permits their reprogramming upon inhibition of the NOTCH pathway. This pathway, when activated in culture, sustains a dedifferentiated, proliferative state through upregulation of HES1, NOTCH1, and NOTCH2, while suppressing cell cycle inhibitors such as p57 and p27. The invention demonstrates that downregulation of HES1, either through RNA interference or other means, reverses this state, leading to nuclear shrinkage, cell clustering, and reactivation of the beta-cell transcriptional network. Prior art has failed to achieve consistent redifferentiation because it did not recognize HES1 as a central node in the dedifferentiation program or because it used non-specific inhibitors that induced cytotoxicity or off-target effects. The need for alternatives to human pancreas donors is driven by the global shortage of suitable organ sources, the immunological challenges of allogeneic transplantation, and the inability of current protocols to generate sufficient cell numbers for widespread clinical use. The novel conditions described herein—serum-free medium combined with NOTCH inhibition and soluble differentiation factors—create an environment that mimics key aspects of the pancreatic niche, promoting redifferentiation without inducing apoptosis or senescence. Purification of dedifferentiated beta cells from cultured islets is accomplished by transiently labeling primary beta cells with a Cre-dependent reporter system, followed by fluorescence-activated sorting to isolate a highly enriched population of lineage-traced cells. Genetic recombination approaches for cell-specific labeling involve the use of lentiviral vectors encoding the RIP-Cre/ER fusion under the control of the rat insulin promoter, coupled with a loxP-STOP-loxP-eGFP cassette. This ensures that only cells that were insulin-expressing at the time of tamoxifen exposure become permanently labeled, enabling tracking of their fate during expansion and redifferentiation. The downregulation of NOTCH pathway components is achieved through a variety of molecular agents, including short hairpin RNAs targeting HES1, NOTCH1, or NOTCH2, which are delivered via lentiviral transduction. The use of RNA silencing agents such as shRNA, siRNA, or miRNA allows for precise, post-transcriptional suppression of target genes without altering the genome. RNA interference is mediated by the Dicer enzyme and the RNA-induced silencing complex, which cleaves complementary mRNA transcripts. Long double-stranded RNAs can be employed if designed to avoid activation of interferon and PKR pathways, but preferred embodiments utilize chemically modified siRNAs with 2'-O-methyl or phosphorothioate linkages to enhance stability and reduce immune stimulation. Cell-penetrating peptides such as TAT, polyarginine, or penetratin can be conjugated to RNA silencing agents to improve intracellular delivery. Alternative agents include antisense oligonucleotides that hybridize to NOTCH mRNA, DNAzymes that cleave target sequences via the "10-23" catalytic motif, ribozymes, and triplex-forming oligonucleotides that block transcriptional elongation. Polypeptide agents such as dominant-negative NOTCH intracellular domains or engineered Notch-binding proteins may also be used. Gene delivery vehicles include lentiviral, adenoviral, and adeno-associated viral vectors, each equipped with appropriate promoters, enhancer elements, polyadenylation signals, and selectable markers. Lipid-based delivery systems such as liposomes or lipid nanoparticles can be formulated for non-viral transfection. Gamma-secretase inhibitors such as DAPT or RO4929097 are also contemplated as pharmacological inhibitors of NOTCH signaling. Insulin content is measured by ELISA, immunofluorescence, or qPCR for INS transcripts. The culturing medium for adult islet beta cells is a serum-free formulation based on CMRL 1066, supplemented with BSA, ITS, and antibiotics, and optionally enriched with nicotinamide, exendin-4, and activin A. Genetic modification of beta cells is performed using standard transduction or transfection protocols, with viral titers optimized to achieve >90% infection efficiency without inducing cellular stress. Therapeutic agents include any compound or nucleic acid capable of downregulating HES1, NOTCH1, NOTCH2, or NOTCH3. The uteroglobin gene is not directly involved in this invention but may serve as a marker for certain epithelial lineages. Methods of genetically modifying beta cells include electroporation, nucleofection, and viral transduction. Treating diabetes involves the transplantation of redifferentiated beta cells into the kidney capsule, liver portal vein, or encapsulated devices. Diabetes is defined as a metabolic disorder characterized by chronic hyperglycemia due to absolute or relative insulin deficiency. Treating refers to the administration of cells to reduce blood glucose levels, restore glucose homeostasis, or eliminate the need for exogenous insulin. Transplanting encompasses the delivery of cells into a subject via surgical, percutaneous, or device-mediated methods. The source of adult islet beta cells may be autologous or non-autologous, with allogeneic cells requiring immunosuppression or encapsulation to prevent rejection. Encapsulation techniques involve microcapsules composed of alginate, poly-L-lysine, or synthetic polymers with pore sizes permitting nutrient exchange while blocking immune cell access. Microcapsule properties include mechanical stability, biocompatibility, and resistance to fibrosis. Immunosuppressive agents such as tacrolimus, sirolimus, or anti-CD3 antibodies may be administered systemically or incorporated into the capsule matrix. In vivo treatment with NOTCH pathway downregulation agents may be achieved through systemic delivery of small molecule inhibitors or targeted RNA therapeutics. The pharmaceutical composition comprises an active ingredient, which is the population of redifferentiated beta cells, suspended in a physiologically acceptable carrier such as saline, Hank’s balanced salt solution, or a hydrogel matrix. Excipients may include albumin, dextrose, or cryoprotectants. Pharmaceutical compositions are manufactured under cGMP conditions and may be formulated for injection, transmucosal delivery, or oral administration, though parenteral delivery is preferred. Routes of administration include subcutaneous, intraperitoneal, intrahepatic, or intrarenal. Formulations for injection are sterile, pyrogen-free, and isotonic. Therapeutically effective amounts are determined by dose-response studies in animal models and are typically in the range of 1×10⁶ to 5×10⁷ cells per kilogram of body weight. Toxicity and therapeutic efficacy are evaluated by monitoring blood glucose, C-peptide levels, and graft histology. Dosage intervals are determined by the persistence of insulin secretion and may range from single administration to repeated dosing. The composition may be presented as a frozen suspension, lyophilized powder, or in a biodegradable scaffold. Purification of dedifferentiated beta cells is accomplished by lineage tracing and fluorescence-activated sorting. Permanent tagging is achieved by stable genomic integration of the Cre/loxP reporter system. Culturing of tagged cells is performed in expansion medium for 5–7 passages, followed by isolation of eGFP-positive cells. The isolated populations of dedifferentiated B cells are defined as cells that were insulin-expressing at the time of labeling, have undergone proliferation in culture, and have lost insulin expression, but retain the capacity for redifferentiation. The method of identifying agents affecting proliferation or redifferentiation involves exposing these purified populations to candidate compounds and measuring changes in insulin content, cluster formation, or transcript levels of beta-cell markers.

### Examples

The invention is further illustrated by the following examples. Islet cells from human donors were dissociated and cultured in serum-containing medium for 5–7 weeks, during which time they lost insulin expression and acquired a dedifferentiated morphology. These cells were infected with lentiviruses encoding HES1 shRNA or a non-targeting control. Following puromycin selection, cells exhibited a 40% reduction in HES1 protein and a 50% increase in p57, accompanied by a significant reduction in nuclear size and the formation of dense, islet-like clusters. qPCR analysis revealed a 6.7-fold increase in INS transcripts, a 4.5-fold increase in PDX1, and a 4.7-fold increase in NEUROD1. Lineage tracing using the RIP-Cre/ER and loxP-STOP-eGFP system confirmed that redifferentiation occurred exclusively in cells originally derived from beta cells. When combined with serum-free medium containing nicotinamide, exendin-4, and activin A, HES1 inhibition led to a 144-fold increase in insulin transcripts and a 2.6-fold increase in the proportion of C-peptide-positive cells compared to soluble factors alone. Transplantation of these cells into streptozotocin-induced diabetic mice resulted in sustained normoglycemia and detectable human C-peptide in serum, with grafts showing robust insulin and C-peptide staining. No co-expression of glucagon, somatostatin, or pancreatic polypeptide was observed in insulin-positive cells, indicating mature, monohormonal identity. These results demonstrate that inhibition of HES1 is sufficient to drive the redifferentiation of expanded human beta cells into functional, glucose-responsive insulin-secreting cells. The invention thus provides a clinically viable pathway to overcome the limitations of current beta-cell replacement therapies.
