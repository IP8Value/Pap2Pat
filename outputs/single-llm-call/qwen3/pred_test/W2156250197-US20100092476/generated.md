## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

- define field

The present invention resides in the field of molecular diagnostics and oncology, specifically in the identification and utilization of protein biomarkers for the early detection, diagnosis, and management of pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma. More particularly, the invention relates to methods for detecting altered levels of specific proteins in biological samples, including blood, serum, or plasma, that are indicative of the presence of pancreatic cancer at a preclinical or early-stage phase, prior to the manifestation of overt clinical symptoms. The invention further encompasses diagnostic kits, therapeutic strategies, and analytical platforms designed to measure these biomarkers with high sensitivity and specificity, enabling improved clinical decision-making, patient stratification, and therapeutic intervention. The field includes the application of advanced proteomic technologies such as mass spectrometry, immunoassays, chromatographic separation, and antibody-based detection systems to identify and quantify low-abundance proteins shed from developing pancreatic tumors into the systemic circulation. The invention also pertains to the integration of multi-marker panels that surpass the diagnostic performance of single biomarkers currently in clinical use, such as carbohydrate antigen 19.9, particularly in distinguishing pancreatic cancer from benign inflammatory conditions like chronic pancreatitis.

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

- motivate pancreatic cancer

Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma remains one of the most lethal malignancies affecting humans, with a five-year survival rate consistently below ten percent, largely due to the absence of early symptoms and the lack of reliable diagnostic tools capable of detecting the disease before it has metastasized. The insidious nature of this cancer, coupled with its propensity for aggressive local invasion and early systemic dissemination, results in the majority of patients being diagnosed at advanced stages when curative surgical resection is no longer feasible. Despite decades of research and substantial investment in oncology, the prognosis for pancreatic cancer has seen minimal improvement, making it the fourth leading cause of cancer-related death in the United States and a growing public health crisis worldwide. The absence of effective screening strategies for the general population, combined with the non-specific nature of late-stage symptoms such as jaundice, weight loss, and abdominal pain, underscores the urgent need for a reliable, non-invasive method capable of identifying pancreatic cancer at a stage when intervention can significantly alter the course of the disease.

- describe limitations of current diagnostic methods

Current diagnostic approaches for pancreatic cancer rely heavily on imaging modalities such as computed tomography, magnetic resonance imaging, and endoscopic ultrasound, which are expensive, operator-dependent, and often unable to detect lesions smaller than one centimeter in diameter. These methods are typically employed only after the onset of clinical suspicion, rendering them unsuitable for population-wide screening. Furthermore, the only biomarker currently approved for clinical use, carbohydrate antigen 19.9, suffers from significant limitations in both sensitivity and specificity. It is frequently elevated in benign conditions such as chronic pancreatitis, biliary obstruction, and other gastrointestinal inflammatory states, leading to a high rate of false positives. Additionally, approximately 5 to 10 percent of the population lacks the Lewis antigen necessary for CA19.9 expression, rendering the marker entirely undetectable in these individuals. These shortcomings have prevented CA19.9 from serving as a viable tool for early detection or population screening, and its utility is largely confined to monitoring disease progression in patients already diagnosed with advanced disease.

- discuss molecular pathogenesis of cancer

The molecular pathogenesis of pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma is characterized by a well-defined sequence of genetic alterations, beginning with activating mutations in the KRAS oncogene, followed by the progressive inactivation of tumor suppressor genes such as CDKN2A (p16), TP53, and SMAD4. These genetic events drive the transformation of normal pancreatic ductal epithelial cells into precursor lesions known as pancreatic intraepithelial neoplasias, which evolve through increasing grades of dysplasia before progressing to invasive carcinoma. This multistep progression occurs over a period of years, providing a critical window of opportunity for early detection. However, the molecular changes occurring during this latent phase are not routinely detectable by conventional clinical assays, and the biological signals emanating from these early lesions—such as the shedding of specific proteins into the bloodstream—have remained largely uncharacterized until now.

- summarize importance of early diagnosis

Early diagnosis of pancreatic cancer is paramount to improving patient outcomes, as surgical resection remains the only potentially curative intervention, and its success is directly correlated with tumor stage at the time of detection. Patients diagnosed with localized, resectable disease have a median survival exceeding three years, whereas those with metastatic disease rarely survive beyond one year. The ability to detect pancreatic cancer at an early, preclinical stage—before the development of symptoms or radiographic evidence of disease—could transform the natural history of this malignancy, shifting it from a uniformly fatal condition to one that is treatable and potentially curable. Such a paradigm shift requires the identification of biomarkers that are not only specific to pancreatic neoplasia but are also detectable in the circulation during the earliest phases of tumorigenesis.

- describe challenges in biomarker identification

The identification of reliable biomarkers for pancreatic cancer has been hindered by the extraordinary complexity and dynamic range of the human plasma proteome, which contains proteins spanning more than ten orders of magnitude in concentration. The overwhelming abundance of high-expression proteins such as albumin and immunoglobulins masks the presence of low-abundance tumor-derived proteins, many of which may be secreted in minute quantities by early-stage lesions. Furthermore, systemic inflammatory responses, comorbid conditions, and inter-individual variability in protein expression complicate the distinction between cancer-specific signals and non-specific physiological changes. Traditional proteomic approaches have been insufficiently sensitive or specific to overcome these obstacles, resulting in a paucity of validated biomarkers despite extensive research efforts.

- discuss genetically engineered mouse models

Genetically engineered mouse models that recapitulate the key genetic drivers of human pancreatic cancer—particularly those harboring activated KRAS and inactivated CDKN2A or TP53—have emerged as powerful tools for studying tumor biology and identifying disease-associated molecular changes. These models exhibit histopathological and molecular features highly analogous to human pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma, including the stepwise progression from pancreatic intraepithelial neoplasia to invasive carcinoma, the development of a dense desmoplastic stroma, and the acquisition of metastatic potential. Importantly, these models allow for the longitudinal collection of biological samples from animals at precisely defined stages of disease, enabling the temporal resolution of protein changes associated with tumor initiation and progression under controlled experimental conditions.

- motivate blood-based biomarkers

The pursuit of blood-based biomarkers for pancreatic cancer is driven by the need for a minimally invasive, cost-effective, and scalable screening tool that can be integrated into routine clinical care. Blood, as a readily accessible biofluid, provides a systemic reflection of tumor biology through the release of proteins, nucleic acids, and other molecular signatures shed by neoplastic cells. The identification of proteins that are specifically elevated in the plasma of individuals with early-stage pancreatic cancer, even before radiographic detection, represents a transformative opportunity to detect the disease at a curable stage. The development of such biomarkers would enable targeted surveillance of high-risk populations, including those with hereditary syndromes, chronic pancreatitis, or new-onset diabetes, thereby improving early detection rates and ultimately reducing mortality.
