## TECHNICAL FIELD

The present invention relates to advanced tactile sensing systems for robotic and prosthetic limbs, specifically to multi-modal fingertip sensors capable of simultaneously detecting proximity, contact, force magnitude, spatial location of impact, and angular orientation of applied loads. The invention integrates miniature electromechanical components into a conformable elastomeric structure to enable high-fidelity sensory feedback in artificial hands, thereby addressing critical limitations in current prosthetic and robotic systems that rely on single-modality force sensors. The sensor system is designed for integration into upper limb prostheses and dexterous robotic manipulators, where precise environmental interaction is essential for functional autonomy and user embodiment. The invention further encompasses the associated signal processing, calibration, and machine learning methodologies required to convert raw sensor outputs into actionable neuromorphic feedback signals, enabling closed-loop control architectures that mimic biological somatosensory function.

## BACKGROUND

The field of robotic and prosthetic limb design has advanced significantly in recent decades, with modern devices capable of complex multi-degree-of-freedom actuation, high-force output, and rapid response times that enable a wide range of daily activities. However, despite these mechanical advancements, the absence of reliable, high-resolution tactile feedback remains a fundamental barrier to achieving natural, intuitive control and true functional restoration for users. Traditional tactile sensors employed in prosthetic hands, such as force-sensitive resistors and piezoresistive arrays, provide only scalar measurements of normal force at a single point, offering no information regarding the spatial distribution of contact, the direction of applied load, or the presence of nearby objects prior to physical contact. This limitation severely restricts the user’s ability to perform delicate manipulation tasks, estimate object compliance, or anticipate slippage, all of which are naturally mediated by the human somatosensory system. The inability to detect off-center or angled forces further diminishes the utility of existing systems, as real-world grasping invariably involves non-uniform contact geometries that vary in both location and orientation across the fingertip surface. Moreover, conventional sensors are often incompatible with compact, self-contained prosthetic architectures due to cumbersome wiring, lack of standardized digital interfaces, and insufficient signal fidelity under dynamic loading conditions. The absence of a robust, multi-dimensional tactile sensing modality has thus hindered the development of truly dexterous, user-centric prosthetic systems that can replicate the nuanced sensory capabilities of the human hand.

## SUMMARY

The invention introduces a multi-modal fingertip sensor that integrates an infrared proximity detector and a barometric pressure sensor into a unified, miniaturized assembly embedded within a compliant elastomeric fingertip structure. The infrared emitter-detector pair generates a proximity signal sensitive to the distance of approaching objects, enabling pre-contact detection and environmental mapping, while the barometric pressure sensor measures the localized compression of the elastomer to produce a calibrated force signal proportional to applied load. These two distinct sensory modalities are co-located on a printed circuit board positioned beneath the elastomeric surface and communicate via standardized I²C protocol to a central microcontroller. The sensor assembly includes analog-to-digital converters for each modality, a multiplexing logic circuit for managing multiple sensor channels, and firmware for real-time signal calibration, temperature compensation, and contact event detection. The resulting system generates three primary output signals: a proximity signal indicating the presence of nearby objects, a contact signal derived from the rate of change of the proximity signal, and a calibrated force signal synthesized from the combined barometric and infrared data. The invention further encompasses computer-readable storage media containing executable instructions that implement machine learning algorithms for classifying the spatial location and angular orientation of applied forces, enabling the sensor to distinguish between five distinct contact points and three angular incidence angles with high accuracy. The system is designed for seamless integration into prosthetic and robotic hands, providing rich, multi-dimensional sensory feedback that surpasses the capabilities of any single-modality tactile sensor currently in use.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The invention is directed to a prosthetic and robotic fingertip sensor system that overcomes the limitations of conventional tactile sensors by combining proximity detection, contact sensing, and calibrated force measurement within a single, compact, and durable module. In traditional prosthetic hands, tactile feedback is typically limited to force-sensitive resistors that measure only normal pressure at a central point, failing to capture the spatial and angular complexity of real-world interactions. This deficiency results in poor object manipulation, increased cognitive load for the user, and reduced embodiment of the prosthesis. To address these shortcomings, the present invention integrates an infrared emitter-detector and a microelectromechanical barometric pressure sensor into a custom-designed substrate that is overmolded with a medical-grade elastomer to form a resilient, bio-inspired fingertip surface. The infrared sensor detects changes in reflected light intensity to determine proximity to objects, while the barometer measures the subtle pressure changes induced by elastomer deformation under load. These sensors are mounted on a printed circuit board aligned along the midline of the fingertip, with each sensor connected to an integrated analog-to-digital converter and I²C communication interface. A multiplexing circuit, controlled by a microcontroller, enables simultaneous data acquisition from multiple sensor modules across all fingers of the prosthetic hand, eliminating the need for individual wiring harnesses and reducing system complexity. The microcontroller executes firmware that performs real-time calibration of the barometric signal using temperature compensation algorithms and computes a contact event trigger by analyzing the first derivative of the infrared signal, detecting when its rate of change exceeds a predefined threshold. The system further includes a custom LabVIEW-based software interface for visualization, data logging, and offline analysis. The elastomeric overmolding is formed using a vacuum-assisted casting process to ensure complete encapsulation of the sensor assembly without air pockets, enhancing mechanical durability and signal consistency. The sensor’s physical structure includes a precisely machined cavity within the 3D-printed finger body to accommodate the PCB and ensure optimal mechanical coupling between the elastomer and the pressure sensor. The entire assembly is designed for scalability and modularity, allowing deployment across multiple fingers without redesign. The multi-modal data streams from the infrared and barometric sensors are fused using Gaussian process regression to generate a calibrated force signal that remains accurate regardless of contact location or angle. This fusion approach accounts for the non-linear and position-dependent responses of each sensor, producing a robust estimate of applied force with an R² value exceeding 0.99. For classification tasks, supervised machine learning models—including support vector machines and convolutional neural networks—are trained on labeled datasets of sensor responses to determine the spatial location of impact and the angular orientation of the applied force. The convolutional neural network processes raw time-series data directly, eliminating the need for manual feature extraction, while the support vector machine leverages engineered features such as peak force ratios and maximum-minimum signal differentials. Both models achieve classification accuracies above 90% for spatial localization and above 85% for angular orientation under controlled experimental conditions. The system’s technical advantages include its compact form factor, compatibility with standard digital communication protocols, resistance to environmental variability, and ability to detect both pre-contact and contact events with high temporal resolution. The term “in some embodiments” is used throughout to denote optional configurations, including variations in sensor type, elastomer composition, communication protocol, or computational architecture, without limiting the scope of the invention. Experimental characterization using an Instron material testing machine confirmed consistent performance across loads ranging from 1 to 50 N, with repeatable responses across multiple spatial and angular conditions. The sensor’s ability to classify contact location and orientation enables advanced control paradigms such as shared autonomy, where the prosthesis adapts its grasp strategy based on real-time sensory input, and neuroprosthetic feedback systems that map sensor outputs to neural stimulation patterns for sensory restoration in amputees.

## CONCLUSION

For the purposes of this patent application, the term “fingertip sensor” refers to a self-contained module comprising at least one proximity sensor and one pressure sensor embedded within a deformable elastomeric structure, configured to generate multiple output signals corresponding to proximity, contact, and calibrated force. The phrase “multi-modal” denotes the simultaneous acquisition and fusion of two or more distinct physical measurements from different sensor types. The term “calibrated force signal” refers to a quantitative estimate of applied load derived from the combined outputs of the proximity and pressure sensors, adjusted for spatial and angular variations through computational modeling. The term “machine-readable medium” includes any non-transitory storage device capable of holding executable instructions, including solid-state memory, magnetic storage, and optical media. The term “Gaussian process” refers to a non-parametric probabilistic model used to infer a continuous function from discrete observations, particularly suited for small datasets with non-linear relationships. The term “supervised learning” encompasses any algorithm trained on labeled input-output pairs to predict outcomes, including but not limited to support vector machines, convolutional neural networks, and k-nearest neighbor classifiers.
