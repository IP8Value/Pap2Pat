## BACKGROUND

Advanced manufacturing technologies have undergone transformative evolution over the past two decades, particularly in the domain of additive manufacturing, which enables the direct fabrication of complex three-dimensional structures from digital models without the need for molds, dies, or subtractive machining. These technologies have revolutionized industries ranging from aerospace and biomedical devices to consumer electronics, offering unprecedented design freedom, material efficiency, and customization capabilities. However, the application of additive manufacturing to advanced ceramic materials—particularly those used in high-temperature electrochemical devices—has been significantly hindered by inherent material challenges. Ceramics, while possessing exceptional thermal stability, chemical inertness, and ionic conductivity, are inherently brittle, prone to cracking during thermal cycling, and exhibit substantial volumetric shrinkage during conventional sintering. Traditional fabrication methods such as dry pressing, tape casting, and screen printing are limited to simple geometries, suffer from poor dimensional accuracy, and are ill-suited for producing multi-layered, functionally graded, or internally structured components. Even when combined with post-processing techniques like machining or glazing, these methods fail to achieve the geometric complexity required for next-generation protonic ceramic energy devices, which demand intricate internal channels, curved surfaces, sealed end caps, and lobed architectures to maximize surface-area-to-volume ratios. Furthermore, the integration of multiple functional layers—such as porous anodes, dense electrolytes, and catalytic cathodes—into a single monolithic structure remains a formidable challenge due to mismatched thermal expansion coefficients, interfacial delamination, and prolonged sintering cycles that degrade performance. Conventional furnace-based sintering, while effective for small laboratory-scale samples, is energy-intensive, time-consuming, and incapable of localized heating necessary for preserving microstructural gradients or embedding sensitive components such as optical sensors. As a result, the scalability, reproducibility, and economic viability of protonic ceramic energy devices have been severely constrained, limiting their transition from laboratory curiosities to commercially deployable technologies.

## SUMMARY

A novel method for fabricating protonic ceramic energy devices is disclosed, comprising the integration of microextrusion-based three-dimensional printing with in-situ laser processing to achieve rapid, precise, and crack-free manufacturing of complex ceramic components. The method involves the preparation of printable ceramic pastes composed of proton-conducting oxide precursors, binders, and dispersants, which are deposited layer-by-layer using a microextruder with a nozzle diameter of less than one millimeter. Each deposited layer is immediately subjected to rapid drying using a CO₂ laser operating at a wavelength of 10.64 micrometers, with laser power density optimized to evaporate solvents without inducing thermal stress or deformation. Following drying, the green body is consolidated through rapid laser reactive sintering using the same CO₂ laser system, which is configured with a cylindrical lens to generate a linear beam spot of approximately eight millimeters in width, enabling uniform energy distribution across the entire layer. The laser scanning speed is maintained between 0.05 and 0.5 millimeters per second, with laser power ranging from 15 to 90 watts, depending on the composition and thickness of the ceramic layer. Multiple scanning paths are employed to ensure complete densification, with overlapping trajectories that eliminate voids and promote grain growth. The ceramic paste materials include barium cerate-zirconate-yttrate-ytterbiate, barium zirconate-yttrate, and barium cerate-ferrite-zirconate-yttrate, each formulated with nickel oxide as a reducible anode component. The method further enables the direct three-dimensional printing of fused silica glass structures through controlled laser melting, wherein the laser power and scanning speed are adjusted to achieve full densification without devitrification or bubble formation. A picosecond laser microfabrication system is integrated into the platform to perform high-resolution machining of microchannels, surface polishing, and edge trimming, with parameters set to a repetition rate of 10 kilohertz, pulse energy of 150 microjoules, and scan velocity of five millimeters per second. The fabrication of optical fiber sensors is accomplished by embedding all-glass fiber-optic elements within ceramic substrates through a two-step microchannel design, wherein the channel is first machined using the picosecond laser and subsequently filled with ceramic slurry that is sintered in-situ using the CO₂ laser, thereby achieving hermetic sealing without damaging the embedded sensor. The housing of the sensor is fabricated through extrusion of fused silica paste followed by laser melting, with the extrusion path precisely synchronized to the laser irradiation zone to ensure dimensional fidelity. The method further enables the fabrication of protonic ceramic fuel cells through rapid laser reactive sintering of multilayered green bodies, wherein anode, electrolyte, and cathode layers are deposited sequentially and consolidated in a single thermal cycle, eliminating the need for separate sintering steps and reducing processing time from hours to minutes. The resulting components exhibit high relative density, phase purity, and proton conductivity, with microstructures engineered through controlled laser parameters to optimize ionic transport and mechanical integrity.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

An integrated laser additive manufacturing and processing system is disclosed for the fabrication of protonic ceramic energy devices, comprising a multi-axis motion platform, a microextrusion system, a CO₂ laser heating system, a picosecond laser microfabrication system, an ultrasonic spray coating system, and a centralized control unit. The motion platform includes high-precision X-Y-Z stages capable of sub-micron positioning accuracy and velocities up to five meters per second, enabling synchronized movement between the extrusion nozzle and laser focal points. The microextrusion system utilizes a pressurized syringe feed mechanism with a needle-type nozzle of 0.3 to 0.8 millimeters in diameter, delivering ceramic pastes with viscosities between 100 and 2000 Pascal-seconds, formulated from ball-milled oxide precursors, water, dispersants, and hydroxypropyl methylcellulose binder. The CO₂ laser heating system operates at a wavelength of 10.64 micrometers and is equipped with a Galvano scanner for high-speed beam deflection, allowing for rapid drying, sintering, and melting operations with adjustable spot sizes ranging from 0.5 to 15 millimeters. The picosecond laser microfabrication system employs a 1064-nanometer YAG laser with pulse durations of less than 10 picoseconds, focused through a 5× objective lens to achieve a spot size of 18 micrometers, enabling precision cutting, polishing, and microchannel machining without thermal damage to adjacent regions. The ultrasonic spray coating system is utilized for the deposition of thin electrolyte precursor films onto porous anode substrates, with droplet size and frequency controlled to achieve uniform layer thicknesses below 20 micrometers. The additive manufacturing capability of the system is characterized by in-situ material processing, wherein each deposited layer is immediately dried, sintered, or machined before the next layer is applied, thereby minimizing warping, delamination, and residual stress. The CO₂ laser processing parameters are optimized for each ceramic composition, with laser power density calibrated to induce solid-state reactive sintering without melting, and scanning speed adjusted to balance densification kinetics with thermal gradient control. The Galvano scanner parameters are configured to achieve scanning frequencies up to 100 hertz, with acceleration rates exceeding 1000 meters per second squared, enabling complex toolpaths for lobed tubes, curved membranes, and internal channels. The picosecond laser parameters are selected to achieve ablation thresholds below the thermal damage threshold of the ceramic matrix, with pulse overlap ratios maintained at 80% to ensure continuous surface modification. Optimal printing parameters include a layer thickness of 300 to 500 micrometers, extrusion flow rate of 0.2 to 0.5 milliliters per minute, and stage speed of 10 to 20 millimeters per second, which collectively ensure filament fusion and dimensional stability. The system is applicable to ceramic, glass, and carbide materials, including barium cerate-zirconate-yttrate-ytterbiate, fused silica, and silicon carbide, each processed under tailored thermal profiles. The CO₂ laser heating system is employed for both drying and sintering, with defocus distances adjusted between 10 and 30 millimeters to control energy density and thermal penetration depth. The picosecond laser microfabrication system is used for creating microchannels in green bodies prior to sintering, with channel dimensions ranging from 100 to 500 micrometers in width and depth. Free-forming extrusion enables the fabrication of tubular, conical, and lobed geometries with closed ends, while ultrasonic spray coating facilitates the deposition of nanoscale electrolyte films. The additive manufacturing capability is demonstrated through the direct fabrication of protonic ceramic fuel cell half-cells, wherein anode and electrolyte layers are printed and sintered in sequence without intermediate handling. In-situ material processing is achieved by synchronizing the extrusion, drying, and sintering operations within a single continuous workflow, eliminating the need for post-processing furnaces. The CO₂ laser processing parameters are further optimized for glass fabrication, with laser power set between 40 and 80 watts and scanning speed between 0.1 and 0.3 millimeters per second to achieve optical transparency and low scattering loss. Silicon carbide applications include the fabrication of heterogeneous composites with boron nitride interfacial layers, wherein laser tracking is employed to accelerate solvent evaporation during precursor deposition. Protonic ceramic fuel cell applications are enabled through rapid laser reactive sintering of multilayered green bodies, achieving full densification in under five minutes. Glass object fabrication is accomplished through direct laser melting of fused silica paste, resulting in components with transmission spectra comparable to commercial optical substrates. Optimal laser processing parameters for glass include a defocus distance of 25 millimeters, laser power of 60 watts, and scan speed of 0.2 millimeters per second, yielding crack-free, fully dense structures with surface roughness below 50 nanometers.

## EXAMPLES

### Example 1—Integrated Multi-Laser 3D Manufacturing System

The integrated multi-laser 3D manufacturing system comprises a rigid aluminum frame housing a high-precision X-Y-Z translation stage, a dual-nozzle microextruder system, a 100-watt CO₂ laser source, a 10-watt picosecond YAG laser, and a Galvano scanner with a 14-millimeter scan field. The CO₂ laser is mounted on a Z-axis carriage and focused through a cylindrical lens to produce a linear beam spot of eight millimeters in length, enabling uniform heating across the entire printed layer. The picosecond laser is coupled to a 5× objective lens with a numerical aperture of 0.13, producing a focused spot of 18 micrometers for microfabrication tasks. The Galvano scanner directs both laser beams with a positioning accuracy of 1 micrometer and a maximum scan velocity of five meters per second. The microextruder system is driven by compressed air at 3 bar and delivers ceramic paste through a 0.5-millimeter nozzle at a flow rate of 0.3 milliliters per minute. The system is controlled by a real-time computer interface that synchronizes extrusion, laser drying, laser sintering, and laser cutting operations based on a pre-programmed toolpath derived from a CAD model. The laser beam focusing is achieved through adjustable collimators and lenses, with beam alignment verified using infrared imaging. The picosecond laser beam is monitored in real time using a photodiode array to ensure consistent pulse energy and spot stability. The free-forming extrusion system is calibrated for each ceramic paste formulation, with nozzle-to-substrate distance maintained at 450 micrometers to ensure layer uniformity. The control center integrates motion control, laser power modulation, and temperature feedback from an infrared pyrometer mounted adjacent to the laser focal point. Toolpath generation is performed using proprietary software that converts 3D models into layer-by-layer extrusion sequences, with laser processing commands embedded at each layer transition. The system arrangement includes a sealed environmental chamber with controlled humidity and inert gas purging to prevent carbonate contamination during processing. All components are mounted on vibration-damped optical tables to ensure dimensional stability during high-speed laser scanning and extrusion.

### Example 2—CO2 Laser Sintering of Sol-Gel Deposition

A sol-gel-derived silica film was prepared by hydrolysis of tetraethyl orthosilicate in an ethanol-water mixture catalyzed by hydrochloric acid, followed by aging for 24 hours to form a viscous precursor solution. Conventional sol-gel processing requires prolonged thermal treatment at elevated temperatures to achieve densification, often resulting in cracking due to capillary stress during solvent evaporation. The present method overcomes this limitation by employing a CO₂ laser to sinter the deposited sol-gel film in situ immediately after dip-coating onto a fused silica substrate. The dip-coating process was performed at a withdrawal speed of 5 millimeters per second, yielding a wet film thickness of approximately 15 micrometers. The laser sintering was conducted using a 10.64-micrometer CO₂ laser with a defocus distance of 15 millimeters, producing a spot diameter of 1 millimeter and a power density of 12 watts per square centimeter. The laser scanned the film at a speed of 15 millimeters per second, with overlapping passes spaced 0.8 millimeters apart to ensure complete coverage. The resulting film exhibited a shrinkage ratio of 22% and a relative density of 94%, compared to 18% and 88% for furnace-sintered controls. Scanning electron microscopy revealed a dense, pore-free microstructure with grain sizes below 500 nanometers. Atomic force microscopy confirmed a surface roughness of 8 nanometers, significantly lower than the 25-nanometer roughness of conventionally sintered films. The laser-sintered films demonstrated superior mechanical integrity, with no observable cracking even after thermal cycling between 25 and 800 degrees Celsius. Finite element modeling of the thermal gradient predicted a maximum temperature differential of 120 degrees Celsius across the film-substrate interface, well below the threshold for fracture. The repeatability of the process was confirmed over 50 consecutive runs, with thickness variation less than 3%. Optical images of thick films showed uniform coloration and no visible defects, confirming the scalability of the technique. The ability to control shrinkage and porosity through laser parameters enables the fabrication of defect-free silica films for optical and sensor applications.

### Example 3—CO2 Laser Melting of Ceramic Paste

A ceramic paste composed of 70 weight percent alumina and 30 weight percent fused silica was prepared by ball-milling commercial powders with 1.5 weight percent hydroxypropyl methylcellulose binder and 10 weight percent water. The paste was extruded onto a ceramic substrate at a layer thickness of 400 micrometers and immediately subjected to CO₂ laser melting using a 20-watt laser with a 10-millimeter defocus distance and a scan speed of 0.1 millimeters per second. The laser beam produced a molten pool with a width of 1.2 millimeters and a depth of 350 micrometers, fully penetrating the green layer. Scanning electron microscopy of the laser-melted tracks revealed a dense, homogeneous microstructure with minimal porosity. The scanning speed was found to critically influence the melt pool morphology: at speeds below 0.05 millimeters per second, excessive melting led to balling and surface irregularities, while speeds above 0.2 millimeters per second resulted in incomplete densification. The optimal speed of 0.1 millimeters per second produced a smooth, continuous track with a surface roughness of 12 micrometers. Cross-sectional analysis showed a dual-layered structure consisting of a fully dense upper region and a partially sintered lower region, attributed to thermal gradient-induced density stratification. The permeation of the melted alumina into the underlying porous compact was modeled using the Lucas-Washburn equation, with capillary pressure estimated from surface tension and contact angle measurements. Line spacing was optimized at 0.8 millimeters to ensure complete coverage without overlap-induced cracking. The process was extended to fused silica paste, where the lower thermal conductivity of silica reduced the risk of thermal shock, enabling the fabrication of crack-free layers with surface smoothness comparable to polished glass. The controllability of the fused silica paste was demonstrated through the fabrication of curved and overhanging structures, which retained their shape after cooling. The thermal properties of alumina and fused silica were compared, revealing that the higher specific heat and lower thermal conductivity of silica promoted more uniform heat distribution, reducing internal stresses. The direct laser melting approach eliminated the need for post-sintering furnaces, reducing processing time from hours to minutes and enabling the fabrication of complex ceramic geometries with high dimensional accuracy.

### Example 4—Thick Er-Doped Silica Films Sintered by CO2 Laser for Scintillation Applications

Thick films of erbium-doped silica were fabricated by preparing a sol-gel precursor solution containing tetraethyl orthosilicate, erbium nitrate, and poloxamer 407 as a viscosity modifier. The solution was dip-coated onto fused silica substrates to achieve thicknesses ranging from 50 to 300 micrometers. The coated films were immediately sintered using a CO₂ laser operating at 25 watts with a 20-millimeter defocus distance and a scan speed of 0.1 millimeters per second. The resulting films exhibited a shrinkage ratio of 28%, significantly higher than the 19% observed in furnace-sintered controls, yet remained crack-free due to the controlled thermal gradient induced by the laser. Photoluminescence spectra under 980-nanometer excitation revealed strong emission peaks at 1530 nanometers, characteristic of erbium transitions, with intensity 3.5 times greater than that of furnace-sintered films. Radioluminescence measurements under X-ray excitation confirmed enhanced scintillation efficiency, attributed to improved crystallinity and reduced defect density. Scanning electron microscopy showed a uniform microstructure with grain sizes of 1 to 3 micrometers, while atomic force microscopy indicated a surface roughness of 15 nanometers. Finite element modeling of thermal stress distribution predicted peak tensile stresses of 85 megapascals, well below the fracture strength of silica, with stress relaxation occurring through viscous flow during laser heating. The spacing between microcracks, when present in control samples, was measured at 150 micrometers, whereas laser-sintered films showed no detectable cracking. The optical microscopy images confirmed the absence of delamination at the substrate interface. The laser-sintered films demonstrated superior stability under thermal cycling, with no degradation in luminescence intensity after 100 cycles between 25 and 700 degrees Celsius. The technique enabled the fabrication of large-area scintillation detectors with thicknesses previously unattainable by conventional methods, opening new possibilities for radiation sensing in high-temperature environments.

### Example 5—High-Resolution Laser 3D Printing of Transparent Fused Silica Glass

Fused silica glass was fabricated into complex three-dimensional structures through direct laser melting of a paste composed of 90 weight percent fused silica powder, 5 weight percent polyethylene glycol, and 5 weight percent ethanol. The paste was extruded layer-by-layer using a 0.5-millimeter nozzle, with each layer immediately melted by a CO₂ laser operating at 60 watts and a scan speed of 0.2 millimeters per second. The laser beam was defocused to 25 millimeters, producing a spot diameter of 12 millimeters to ensure uniform energy distribution. The resulting structures included curved thin-walled tubes, lattice frameworks, and hemispherical domes with wall thicknesses as low as 80 micrometers. Scanning electron microscopy confirmed full densification with no visible pores or voids. Transmission spectra measured across the visible and near-infrared range showed optical transmittance exceeding 90% at 550 nanometers, comparable to commercial fused silica substrates. The refractive index of the laser-melted glass was measured at 1.458, matching that of commercial fused silica. The effect of laser output power was investigated, with powers below 50 watts resulting in incomplete melting and powers above 70 watts causing surface boiling and bubble formation. The optimal power of 60 watts yielded the highest optical quality. The layer thickness was controlled by adjusting the nozzle-to-substrate distance, with a 400-micrometer gap producing layers of 350 micrometers after melting. The melting depth was precisely controlled by varying the laser dwell time and scan speed, enabling the fabrication of multi-layered structures with interlayer bonding strength exceeding 95% of the bulk material. The process demonstrated the ability to fabricate optical components with complex geometries previously impossible to produce using conventional glass molding or machining techniques.

### Example 6—Laser-Assisted Embedding of all-Glass Optical Fiber Sensors into Bulk Ceramics for High Temperature Applications

All-glass optical fiber sensors based on Fabry-Perot interferometry were embedded into alumina ceramic substrates using a two-step laser-assisted process. A microchannel with a depth of 150 micrometers and width of 120 micrometers was first machined into a green alumina substrate using a picosecond laser operating at 10 kilohertz repetition rate and 150 microjoules pulse energy. The fiber was then inserted into the channel and surrounded by a slurry composed of alumina particles, terpineol, and polyethylene glycol. The slurry was deposited using a microextruder and immediately sintered in-situ using a CO₂ laser operating at 40 watts with a 15-millimeter defocus distance and a scan speed of 0.1 millimeters per second. The laser sealing process was monitored in real time by measuring the reflection spectrum of the embedded fiber during sintering, ensuring that the sensor remained intact and functional. The resulting embedded sensor exhibited no spectral shift or signal degradation after sintering, confirming the absence of thermal damage. The transmission spectrum of the embedded fiber remained unchanged, with attenuation below 0.2 decibels per centimeter. The sensor was subjected to thermal cycling from 25 to 800 degrees Celsius, demonstrating a linear spectral shift of 0.08 nanometers per degree Celsius with a standard deviation of 0.005 nanometers. A dummy fiber was co-embedded to compensate for thermal strain, reducing the deviation in sensor response to less than 1%. Scanning electron microscopy confirmed complete sealing of the fiber within the ceramic matrix, with no gaps or delamination. The high-temperature stability of the embedded sensor was maintained for over 100 hours at 750 degrees Celsius, demonstrating the suitability of the method for structural health monitoring in extreme environments.

### Example 7—SiC Applications

Silicon carbide-based composite components were fabricated using an integrated additive manufacturing and laser processing platform. A paste composed of silicon carbide powder, boron nitride precursor, and zirconium diboride additive was extruded to form a lattice-like microstructure with alternating SiC and BN domains. The BN precursor was deposited using inkjet printing, with solvent evaporation accelerated by a CO₂ laser to prevent droplet spreading. The green body was then subjected to pyrolysis and consolidation under controlled atmosphere, with laser tracking ensuring uniform heating. The resulting composite exhibited a microstructure with 15-micrometer BN interfacial layers, enhancing fracture toughness by promoting crack deflection. The ZrB₂ additive improved thermal conductivity by 40% compared to pure SiC. The system enabled the fabrication of heterogeneous micro-domains with precise geometry, overcoming the limitations of traditional powder pressing and sintering methods. The integration of picosecond laser micromachining allowed for the creation of internal cooling channels and surface textures, enhancing performance in high-temperature applications such as turbine blades and heat exchangers.

### Example 8—3D Printing of all-Glass Fiber-Optic Pressure Sensor for High-Temperature Applications

A fiber-optic pressure sensor based on an extruded all-glass Fabry-Perot interferometer was fabricated using a CO₂ laser melting system. A cone-shaped housing and a cylindrical tube were extruded from fused silica paste and immediately melted by a 60-watt laser to form a monolithic structure. A gold coating was sputtered onto the internal surface of the cavity to enhance optical reflectivity. An optical fiber was inserted into the tube and fixed using localized laser irradiation, creating a sealed air gap of 15 micrometers. Finite element analysis predicted a pressure sensitivity of 0.12 nanometers per kilopascal, which was experimentally validated with a measured sensitivity of 0.11 nanometers per kilopascal. The sensor exhibited a linear response to pressures ranging from 0 to 100 kilopascals, with a resolution of 0.5 kilopascals. Temperature dependence was characterized from 25 to 800 degrees Celsius, with a thermal drift of 0.008 nanometers per degree Celsius, corrected using a dummy fiber reference. The sensor demonstrated long-term stability over 500 thermal cycles, with no degradation in signal quality. The fabrication process eliminated the need for adhesive bonding, enabling operation in aggressive chemical environments.

### Example 9—Laser-Assisted Embedding of all-Glass Optical Fiber Sensors into Bulk Ceramics for High-Temperature Applications

A laser-assisted embedding process was developed for the integration of interferometric fiber-optic sensors into alumina ceramic substrates. A two-step microchannel was fabricated using a picosecond laser, followed by slurry filling and CO₂ laser sintering. The sensor spectrum was monitored in real time during sintering to ensure structural integrity. The embedded sensor exhibited a thermal sensitivity of 0.08 nanometers per degree Celsius, with a standard deviation of 0.004 nanometers. The use of a dummy fiber eliminated thermal strain effects, improving measurement accuracy. The sensor remained stable after 1000 hours at 800 degrees Celsius, demonstrating the feasibility of long-term structural monitoring in extreme environments.

### Example 10—Rapid Laser Reactive Sintering

Rapid laser reactive sintering was employed to fabricate protonic ceramic electrolyte films of barium cerate-zirconate-yttrate-ytterbiate on fused silica substrates. The green film, 40 micrometers thick, was sintered using a 20-watt CO₂ laser with a scan speed of 0.1 millimeters per second and a defocus distance of 20 millimeters. The resulting film exhibited a relative density of 96%, a grain size of 1.8 micrometers, and a proton conductivity of 6.95 × 10⁻³ siemens per centimeter at 600 degrees Celsius under wet hydrogen. X-ray diffraction confirmed a pure perovskite phase, with no secondary phases detected. The process reduced sintering time from 12 hours to 8 minutes, with energy consumption reduced by 95% compared to conventional furnace sintering. The method was extended to multiple protonic ceramic compositions, demonstrating universal applicability.

### Example 11—Rapid Laser Reactive Sintering of Protonic Ceramic Components

Protonic ceramic components including electrolytes, anodes, and interconnects were fabricated using rapid laser reactive sintering. Green pastes of barium cerate-zirconate-yttrate-ytterbiate, lanthanum strontium chromite, and barium cerate-zirconate-yttrate were printed and sintered under optimized laser parameters. The method enabled the fabrication of multilayered stacks without interfacial delamination, demonstrating the potential for direct digital manufacturing of complete protonic ceramic energy devices.

### Example 12—RLRS of La0.8Sr0.2CrO3-δ Interconnects

### Example 13—Direct Inkjet Printing of Mullite Nano-Ribbons

Mullite nano-ribbons were fabricated by inkjet printing a sol-gel precursor ink containing aluminum and silicon alkoxides, with polyvinylpyrrolidone added as a stress relaxer. The ribbons exhibited a Young’s modulus of 220 gigapascals and a fracture toughness of 2.8 megapascals per square root meter, demonstrating the potential for bio-inspired ceramic architectures.

### Example 15—Novel RLRS Method for One-Step Co-Sintering
