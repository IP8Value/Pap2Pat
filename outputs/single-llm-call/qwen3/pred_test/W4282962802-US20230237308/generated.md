## BACKGROUND

The exponential growth in computational demands of deep neural networks has necessitated the development of efficient implementation strategies that reduce resource consumption without sacrificing predictive accuracy. Modern architectures, particularly those deployed in vision and language tasks, rely on billions of parameters and trillions of floating-point operations per inference, rendering them impractical for deployment on edge devices or in latency-sensitive environments. While model compression techniques such as pruning and knowledge distillation have demonstrated utility, quantization remains one of the most effective and hardware-compatible approaches for reducing both memory footprint and computational overhead. Quantization enables the representation of weights and activations using low-bit integer formats, thereby facilitating the use of specialized integer arithmetic units, reducing memory bandwidth requirements, and lowering power consumption. However, the transition from full-precision floating-point to low-bit integer representations introduces quantization noise, which can severely degrade model performance if not carefully managed. Conventional approaches to quantization often rely on heuristic methods for determining the scaling factors that map continuous values to discrete levels, such as using the maximum absolute value in a tensor or applying fixed percentiles derived from calibration datasets. These methods, while simple to implement, fail to account for the non-uniform statistical properties of neural network activations and weights across layers and training iterations. As a result, they frequently produce suboptimal quantization intervals that either over-allocate precision to rare outliers or under-allocate it to the bulk of the data distribution, leading to excessive clipping noise or insufficient resolution in the central region. The need for a principled, data-driven method to determine optimal clipping scalars that minimize quantization error in real time has become increasingly urgent, particularly in the context of quantization-aware training, where the dynamic evolution of tensor statistics during optimization demands adaptive and precise control over the quantization process. Without such a method, the benefits of reduced precision are often offset by the need for extensive hyperparameter tuning, calibration overhead, or architectural modifications that compromise reproducibility and generalizability.

## SUMMARY

This invention introduces a novel system and method for quantizing tensors and vectors in deep neural networks using optimally clipped quantization scalars determined through a recursive, analytically derived algorithm that minimizes mean squared error in real time. The system operates by dynamically computing, at each training iteration, a clipping scalar for each tensor that balances the competing sources of quantization noise—clipping noise from data exceeding the quantization range and discretization noise from finite resolution within the range—thereby achieving minimal quantization error without requiring prior calibration or learned parameters. The method begins by receiving an input tensor representing weights or activations from a neural network layer, then computes an initial estimate of the clipping scalar based on the tensor’s statistical properties, such as its maximum absolute value or standard deviation. A recursive update rule, derived from the Newton-Raphson optimization method, is then applied iteratively to refine this estimate by minimizing an analytical expression of the mean squared error between the original tensor and its quantized representation under uniform quantization with clipping. This update rule incorporates the empirical distribution of the tensor’s absolute values and explicitly accounts for the trade-off between discretization and clipping noise, ensuring convergence to the global optimum of the convex mean squared error function. The resulting clipping scalar is then used to scale the tensor prior to quantization, mapping its values into a fixed number of discrete levels defined by the target bit-width. The quantized tensor is subsequently used in forward and backward propagation, enabling seamless integration into standard training pipelines without modification to the underlying optimizer, loss function, or network architecture. The system is designed to be compatible with both dynamic and static quantization schemes, and may be extended to per-channel or per-feature scaling for convolutional and fully connected layers. By eliminating the need for offline calibration, heuristic scaling, or additional learnable parameters, this method enables quantization-aware training with minimal quantization noise at every iteration, significantly improving accuracy while preserving the simplicity and reproducibility of the baseline training recipe.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The invention encompasses a system for quantizing tensors and vectors in neural networks using optimally clipped quantization scalars computed via a recursive algorithm that minimizes mean squared error. An optimally clipped tensor is defined as a tensor whose values are mapped to a discrete set of levels within a symmetric interval centered at zero, where the bounds of the interval are determined not by the maximum value in the tensor but by a scalar that minimizes the expected squared difference between the original and quantized values. This scalar, referred to as the clipping scalar, is selected to balance the trade-off between discretization noise, which increases with larger quantization step sizes, and clipping noise, which arises when values exceed the interval bounds and are forced to the nearest endpoint. The quantization-aware training process integrates this clipping scalar computation directly into the training loop, allowing the quantization parameters to adapt dynamically to the evolving statistics of weights and activations as the network learns. The system defines quantization-aware training as a procedure in which both forward and backward passes operate on quantized representations of weights and activations, with gradients estimated using a magnitude-aware differentiation technique that preserves the flow of information through clipped regions. Dynamic quantization is implemented by computing the clipping scalar for each tensor independently at every training iteration, ensuring that the quantization range adapts to the current distribution of values, which is especially critical during training-from-scratch and fine-tuning phases where tensor statistics change rapidly. In contrast, static quantization is supported by precomputing the clipping scalars using the same algorithm on a calibration dataset, thereby enabling deployment scenarios where dynamic computation is infeasible. The use of max-scaling, which sets the clipping scalar equal to the maximum absolute value in the tensor, is recognized as a baseline approach that is simple to implement but suffers from high quantization noise due to its failure to account for the concentration of data near zero. The advantages of max-scaling lie in its determinism and lack of hyperparameters, but its limitations are mitigated by the invention’s data-driven clipping scalar optimization, which reduces quantization noise by up to an order of magnitude compared to max-scaling while maintaining computational efficiency. The limitation of max-scaling is that it allocates equal precision to outliers and central values, resulting in unnecessarily large step sizes that degrade resolution where it matters most. Data clipping is introduced as a mechanism to narrow the quantization range to a more representative subset of the data, thereby reducing the step size and improving resolution for the majority of values. ### QAT Gradient Estimation

The quantization-aware training process relies on the estimation of gradients through discontinuous quantization operators, which are inherently non-differentiable. The invention introduces a magnitude-aware derivative that overcomes the limitations of conventional gradient estimators such as the straight-through estimator and piece-wise linear approximations. The straight-through estimator, which assigns a gradient of one to all values regardless of clipping, leads to an exponential explosion of back-propagated gradient variance, causing instability in training. The piece-wise linear estimator, which assigns zero gradient to clipped values, results in premature convergence by halting the learning of weights that exceed the clipping threshold. The invention formulates the clipping operation as a magnitude attenuation, where the output is scaled by a factor that depends on the relative magnitude of the input and the clipping threshold. This formulation leads to the magnitude-aware derivative, which assigns a continuous, non-zero gradient to all values, ensuring that all parameters continue to be updated throughout training. The magnitude-aware derivative is derived by treating the attenuation factor as constant during back-propagation, yielding a gradient that is proportional to the inverse of the input magnitude in the clipping region. This approach avoids the gradient explosion of the straight-through estimator and the convergence stoppage of the piece-wise linear estimator, resulting in more stable and accurate training. The magnitude-aware derivative is applied to weight gradients, while the piece-wise linear estimator is retained for activation gradients to preserve a regularization effect analogous to dropout. The combination of these two strategies, termed the magnitude-aware derivative hybrid, leads to improved accuracy in quantization-aware training. The benefits of OCTAV-enabled quantization-aware training include improved convergence, higher accuracy, and compatibility with standard training recipes, enabling the deployment of low-precision models without the need for specialized hyperparameter tuning or architectural modifications.
