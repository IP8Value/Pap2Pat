# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

Intracellular pathogens, such as Mycobacterium avium subspecies paratuberculosis, pose a significant challenge to clinical diagnostics due to their ability to reside and replicate within host immune cells, particularly macrophages and dendritic cells. This intracellular sequestration shields the pathogen from direct detection by conventional serological methods, which rely on the presence of free-floating antigens or antibodies in biological fluids. As a result, the identification of such pathogens typically necessitates the isolation of infected cells, followed by the extraction of their nucleic acid content. This process is inherently labor-intensive and prone to loss of target material due to the low abundance of pathogens within clinical specimens. The subsequent detection of pathogen-specific genomic sequences is commonly performed using polymerase chain reaction (PCR) methodologies, which, despite their high specificity, require purified DNA templates and multiple amplification cycles that extend the diagnostic timeline to several hours or even days. Furthermore, the presence of inhibitors in complex biological matrices—such as proteins, lipids, and cellular debris—can severely compromise PCR efficiency, leading to false-negative outcomes. An alternative approach involves the cultivation of viable pathogens from clinical samples prior to molecular detection, but this method is constrained by the slow growth kinetics of intracellular organisms like Mycobacterium avium subspecies paratuberculosis, which may require up to twelve weeks for visible colony formation. Even then, many clinical isolates fail to grow in culture due to suboptimal conditions or the presence of inhibitory substances, rendering this approach unreliable for routine diagnostics. These limitations underscore the urgent need for a novel diagnostic technology capable of rapid, sensitive, and specific detection of intracellular pathogens directly in minimally processed clinical samples without reliance on amplification or prolonged incubation. Nanotechnology offers a promising avenue for addressing these challenges through the development of engineered nanoscale systems that exploit unique physical properties for molecular sensing. Among these, magnetic relaxation nanosensors have emerged as a powerful platform due to their ability to detect molecular binding events through changes in the magnetic relaxation behavior of surrounding water protons. Unlike traditional magnetic labeling techniques that depend on nanoparticle aggregation or external field measurements, magnetic relaxation nanosensors operate by modulating the transverse relaxation time of water molecules in proximity to the nanoparticle surface upon target binding. This mechanism provides intrinsic signal amplification, enabling detection at extremely low analyte concentrations without requiring enzymatic amplification or complex sample preparation. The unique interaction between the nanoparticle and its target, which does not necessitate clustering, allows for faster binding kinetics and improved sensitivity, particularly when the density of binding ligands on the nanoparticle surface is carefully controlled. This property has been leveraged to develop a novel diagnostic system capable of detecting genomic biomarkers of intracellular pathogens in complex biological fluids with unprecedented speed and accuracy.

## SUMMARY

The present invention discloses a hybridizing magnetic relaxation nanosensor, or hMRS, specifically designed for the detection of Mycobacterium avium subspecies paratuberculosis through its unique IS900 genomic insertion sequence. The hMRS consists of a polyacrylic acid-coated iron oxide nanoparticle functionalized with a single-stranded oligonucleotide probe complementary to a conserved 15-base-pair region within the IS900 element. Upon exposure to a biological sample containing trace amounts of Mycobacterium avium subspecies paratuberculosis DNA, the hMRS binds specifically to its target sequence, inducing a measurable increase in the transverse relaxation time of water protons surrounding the nanoparticle. This change in magnetic relaxation signal is directly proportional to the concentration of the target DNA and is detectable within thirty minutes using a compact nuclear magnetic resonance instrument. The hMRS demonstrates exceptional specificity, as no significant signal alteration is observed when exposed to DNA from other mycobacterial species or common bacterial pathogens. The sensor is capable of detecting a single genome equivalent of Mycobacterium avium subspecies paratuberculosis even in crude DNA preparations derived from unprocessed blood or tissue homogenates, eliminating the need for time-consuming DNA purification steps. Performance evaluations conducted on clinical samples from patients with Crohn’s disease and cattle with Johne’s disease confirm that the hMRS outperforms conventional nested PCR methods in both sensitivity and diagnostic accuracy, particularly in cases where PCR yields ambiguous or false-negative results. The hMRS enables rapid, point-of-care-compatible detection of the pathogen in peripheral blood, tissue biopsies, and cultured isolates, providing clinicians with a reliable tool for early diagnosis and disease monitoring.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The scope of this disclosure encompasses a novel diagnostic platform based on hybridizing magnetic relaxation nanosensors for the detection of Mycobacterium avium subspecies paratuberculosis and related mycobacterial infections in clinical and environmental samples. The term “hMRS” refers specifically to a magnetic nanoparticle conjugated with a nucleic acid probe that hybridizes to a defined genomic sequence within the IS900 element of Mycobacterium avium subspecies paratuberculosis. The term “nucleic acid analyte” refers to any DNA molecule containing the IS900 sequence, whether derived from viable, heat-inactivated, or lysed bacterial cells. A “sample” includes any biological fluid or tissue homogenate, including but not limited to peripheral blood, lymphatic fluid, biopsy specimens, fecal matter, and cultured bacterial isolates. The term “test sample” denotes a sample that has undergone minimal processing, such as heating and centrifugation, without purification or amplification. The singular and plural forms of all terms are used interchangeably unless context dictates otherwise. All publications referenced herein are incorporated by reference in their entirety for the purpose of describing the state of the art and supporting the enabling disclosure. The detection method relies on the principle that binding of the hMRS to its target induces a measurable increase in the transverse relaxation time of water protons, a phenomenon that occurs independently of nanoparticle clustering. The hMRS configuration comprises an iron oxide core coated with polyacrylic acid, to which an oligonucleotide probe is covalently attached via “click” chemistry. The probe sequence is 5′-ATGTGGTTGCTGTGT-3′, which is fully complementary to a 15-base segment of the IS900 insertion sequence. Hybridization occurs under conditions of controlled heating and cooling, which reduce DNA torsional strain and facilitate stable duplex formation. Stringent hybridization conditions ensure specificity, with complementarity required to be at least 95% over the full length of the probe. The nanoparticle may be further modified with functional groups to enhance stability, reduce non-specific binding, or enable conjugation with additional ligands. Nucleic acid capture probes may be composed of natural DNA, RNA, or synthetic analogs including locked nucleic acids, peptide nucleic acids, morpholino oligomers, and cyclohexene nucleic acids, provided they maintain sufficient binding affinity and specificity for the IS900 sequence. The magnetic nanoparticles may be composed of iron oxide, iron, cobalt, nickel, or their oxides, and may be coated with dextran, polyacrylic acid, or other biocompatible polymers. The binding moiety may be linked to the nanoparticle through covalent attachment, avidin-biotin interactions, or electrostatic forces. The method of detecting the nucleic acid analyte involves contacting the sample with the hMRS, applying a thermal cycling protocol of two brief heating and cooling steps, and measuring the resulting change in transverse relaxation time using a compact nuclear magnetic resonance instrument. The presence or absence of the target is determined by comparing the measured ΔΤ2 + value to a predefined threshold, which is established using control samples. The concentration of the analyte may be quantified by interpolation against a standard curve generated from known concentrations of purified IS900 DNA. The method may be applied to homogenized tissues, blood, or other biological matrices without prior DNA extraction. The hMRS may be stored at 4°C for extended periods without loss of activity. The detection of Mycobacterium avium subspecies paratuberculosis is particularly useful for diagnosing Crohn’s disease in humans and Johne’s disease in ruminants, given the established association between the pathogen and these conditions. The hMRS enables differentiation between infected and non-infected individuals with greater accuracy than culture-based or direct nested PCR methods, especially in cases of low bacterial load. The method may be adapted for the detection of other intracellular pathogens by replacing the oligonucleotide probe with one complementary to a unique genomic sequence of the target organism. The hMRS may be integrated into portable diagnostic devices for point-of-care use in clinical, veterinary, or field settings. The term “microorganism” encompasses any bacterium, virus, fungus, or parasite capable of causing disease, and the term “antibody-specific nanosensor” refers to a variant of the hMRS in which the capture probe is replaced with a protein-binding ligand such as an antibody or aptamer. The method may also be employed to detect the presence of antibodies against Mycobacterium avium subspecies paratuberculosis by conjugating the nanoparticle with a pathogen-derived epitope and measuring the resulting magnetic relaxation change upon antibody binding. The hMRS system provides a robust, scalable, and cost-effective alternative to conventional diagnostic methods for intracellular pathogens.

### Design and Synthesis of hMRS

The hMRS were synthesized by first preparing polyacrylic acid-coated iron oxide nanoparticles through co-precipitation and surface functionalization. Propargyl groups were introduced onto the nanoparticle surface via carbodiimide-mediated coupling, enabling subsequent conjugation with azide-modified oligonucleotides through copper-catalyzed azide-alkyne cycloaddition. The oligonucleotide sequence was designed to be fully complementary to a 15-nucleotide segment within the IS900 insertion sequence of Mycobacterium avium subspecies paratuberculosis. The resulting conjugate exhibited a hydrodynamic diameter of approximately 78 nanometers and a zeta potential of -35 mV, indicating colloidal stability in physiological buffers. The average number of oligonucleotides per nanoparticle was determined to be 55, a valency level previously associated with low-affinity, high-sensitivity binding behavior. The nanoparticles were characterized for their magnetic relaxivities, yielding R1 and R2 values of 45 and 60 mM⁻¹s⁻¹, respectively, at 0.47 Tesla. The binding mechanism involves the hybridization of the hMRS probe to the IS900 sequence following a brief thermal denaturation and controlled cooling of the sample, which facilitates the release of DNA from cellular structures and promotes stable duplex formation. This binding event induces a measurable increase in the transverse relaxation time of surrounding water molecules, detectable within thirty minutes using a compact nuclear magnetic resonance instrument. The absence of nanoparticle aggregation was confirmed by dynamic light scattering, indicating that detection is driven by single binding events rather than clustering. The heating and cooling protocol reduces torsional strain in the circular plasmid DNA of Mycobacterium avium subspecies paratuberculosis, enhancing hybridization efficiency. The magnetic signal increase is directly proportional to the concentration of target DNA and is not influenced by the presence of non-target nucleic acids or biological matrix components. This design enables rapid, specific, and sensitive detection without the need for enzymatic amplification or extensive sample preparation.

### Discussion

The development of the hMRS represents a paradigm shift in the detection of intracellular pathogens by eliminating the reliance on time-consuming culture methods and error-prone PCR amplification. The hMRS achieves single-genome detection in minimally processed clinical samples, providing diagnostic results in under one hour compared to the weeks required for culture-based methods or the hours required for nested PCR. The system’s robustness in complex media, including blood and tissue homogenates, demonstrates its suitability for real-world clinical applications where sample purity cannot be guaranteed. Its ability to resolve ambiguous PCR results, particularly in samples with low bacterial loads, enhances diagnostic accuracy and reduces false-negative rates. The hMRS is inherently scalable, as the nanoparticles can be mass-produced using standardized chemical synthesis, and the detection instrument is compact and portable, enabling deployment in resource-limited settings. The technology’s potential extends beyond Mycobacterium avium subspecies paratuberculosis to other slow-growing or intracellular pathogens, such as Mycobacterium tuberculosis, Chlamydia trachomatis, and Treponema pallidum, by simply redesigning the oligonucleotide probe. The hMRS also provides a quantitative measure of pathogen load, offering clinicians a tool not only for diagnosis but also for monitoring disease progression and therapeutic response. The correlation between hMRS signal intensity and clinical symptomology in Crohn’s disease patients suggests that this platform may serve as a biomarker for disease activity, potentially guiding personalized treatment strategies. Furthermore, the ability to detect the pathogen in peripheral blood opens new avenues for non-invasive screening and early intervention. The hMRS thus represents a transformative diagnostic technology with broad implications for infectious disease management, public health surveillance, and the study of pathogen-host interactions.

### Methods

The preparation of IS900-specific hMRS involved the synthesis of polyacrylic acid-coated iron oxide nanoparticles, followed by the incorporation of propargyl groups via carbodiimide chemistry. Azide-modified oligonucleotides complementary to the IS900 sequence were then conjugated to the nanoparticles using copper-catalyzed azide-alkyne cycloaddition. The resulting conjugates were purified by dialysis and magnetic separation. Epitope-sensing MRS were prepared by conjugating polyacrylic acid-coated nanoparticles to Protein G via EDC/NHS chemistry, followed by immobilization of a monoclonal antibody specific to a conserved Mycobacterium avium subspecies paratuberculosis surface antigen. Nanoparticle size and zeta potential were determined by dynamic light scattering and electrophoretic mobility, respectively. Relaxivities were measured using a 0.47 T Bruker Minispec instrument. Oligonucleotide density was quantified via UV absorbance at 260 nm. Mycobacterium avium subspecies paratuberculosis strains and clinical isolates were cultured in BACTEC 12B bottles and heat-inactivated prior to DNA extraction. Pure DNA was isolated using phenol-chloroform extraction, while crude DNA was obtained by heating bacterial suspensions at 100°C for 30 minutes. For hMRS testing, 200 µL of nanoparticle suspension was mixed with 1 µL of DNA sample, subjected to two 3-minute heating cycles at 95°C, and cooled to room temperature before T2 measurement. Nested PCR was performed using established primers and protocols. Blood samples from Crohn’s disease patients and healthy controls were collected under institutional review board approval. White blood cells were isolated by centrifugation, and DNA was extracted either in pure or crude form for parallel testing. Culture-based nPCR involved inoculating buffy coat samples into MGIT bottles and incubating for twelve weeks before DNA extraction and PCR. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software, with receiver operating characteristic curves generated to evaluate diagnostic performance. All experiments were conducted in triplicate, and data are reported as mean ± standard error.
