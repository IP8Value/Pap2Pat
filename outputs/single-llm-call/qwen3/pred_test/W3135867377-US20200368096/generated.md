# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

- Proprioception is the sensory modality by which an individual perceives the position, movement, and effort of their body parts in space without reliance on visual or auditory cues. It arises from the integration of signals generated by mechanoreceptors located in muscles, tendons, joints, and skin, which continuously relay information regarding joint angle, muscle length, tension, and rate of change in limb configuration to the central nervous system. This internal sense enables the brain to construct a dynamic, real-time representation of bodily posture and motion, forming the foundational substrate for coordinated voluntary movement, postural stability, and spatial awareness.

- The importance of proprioception extends across all domains of motor function, from fine motor control such as handwriting or instrument playing to gross motor activities like walking, reaching, and balance maintenance. Without accurate proprioceptive feedback, even simple actions become clumsy, inefficient, or impossible. Proprioceptive input is essential for predictive motor control, allowing the nervous system to anticipate the consequences of movement before sensory feedback arrives, thereby enabling smooth, adaptive, and energy-efficient motor output. It also plays a critical role in motor learning, as the brain relies on proprioceptive error signals to refine movement patterns and update internal models of limb dynamics.

- Clinical conditions characterized by proprioceptive impairment include stroke, Parkinson’s disease, multiple sclerosis, peripheral neuropathies, spinal cord injuries, and aging-related sensory decline. In stroke survivors, for instance, proprioceptive deficits in the paretic limb are frequently more persistent than motor weakness and are strongly correlated with poor functional recovery, reduced independence in daily activities, and increased risk of falls. Similarly, individuals with Parkinson’s disease exhibit degraded joint position sense and impaired kinesthetic discrimination, contributing to freezing of gait, bradykinesia, and postural instability. These impairments often persist despite rehabilitative efforts focused solely on motor retraining, underscoring the need for targeted interventions that directly address somatosensory dysfunction.

- Current methods for assessing proprioception are largely limited to clinical observation, manual goniometry, or simple threshold tests such as joint position matching with the contralateral limb. These approaches suffer from low sensitivity, poor reproducibility, and susceptibility to cognitive and motivational confounds. Many lack quantitative precision, fail to isolate proprioceptive function from visual or tactile compensation, and cannot be standardized across testing environments or operators. Furthermore, they are often unable to detect subtle changes in acuity that may precede observable motor deficits or reflect early neuroplastic changes following intervention.

- Psychophysical threshold methods offer a more rigorous and quantifiable alternative by measuring the smallest detectable difference between two stimuli, known as the just-noticeable difference (JND). These methods rely on controlled, repeatable stimulus presentation and forced-choice response paradigms to eliminate bias and isolate sensory discrimination capacity. When applied to joint position sense, they enable precise measurement of the angular difference required for an individual to reliably distinguish between two passive limb positions, providing a direct, objective metric of proprioceptive acuity.

- Joint matching tasks, a subset of psychophysical assessments, require participants to replicate a previously presented target position using their own active movement. While widely used, these tasks conflate proprioceptive perception with motor execution, making it difficult to determine whether observed deficits stem from impaired sensory encoding or impaired motor output. To isolate pure proprioceptive function, passive movement paradigms have been developed, wherein the limb is moved by an external device without voluntary effort, ensuring that the participant’s response reflects only their ability to perceive and discriminate position.

- Haptic technology and robotic devices have emerged as powerful tools for delivering precise, programmable, and repeatable proprioceptive stimuli. These systems can control movement kinematics with sub-degree accuracy, modulate resistance and velocity, and integrate real-time feedback to create immersive, sensorimotor environments. Robotic exoskeletons, in particular, allow for full three-dimensional manipulation of the wrist joint, enabling assessment across flexion/extension, adduction/abduction, and pronation/supination planes with high fidelity.

- Despite their advantages, current robotic devices are often restricted to single-degree-of-freedom movement, lack the biomechanical fidelity to replicate natural joint mechanics, or are too bulky and rigid to permit natural limb alignment. Many systems impose artificial constraints on joint rotation, fail to accommodate individual anatomical variability, or cannot independently control multiple axes of motion without cross-coupling interference. These limitations compromise the ecological validity of assessments and limit their utility in detecting nuanced proprioceptive impairments.

- There is a critical unmet need for a standardized, high-resolution proprioceptive function assessment system that is both biomechanically accurate and clinically practical. Such a system must be capable of isolating proprioceptive discrimination across all relevant joint planes, delivering stimuli with millisecond precision, recording responses with sub-degree accuracy, and adapting stimulus parameters in real time based on participant performance to maximize sensitivity and efficiency.

- Furthermore, there is a compelling need for an integrated system that unifies assessment and rehabilitation within a single platform. Current paradigms treat evaluation and training as separate processes, requiring multiple devices, repeated patient setup, and fragmented data interpretation. An integrated system that simultaneously quantifies proprioceptive thresholds and delivers adaptive, task-specific training based on those thresholds would enable personalized, data-driven interventions that track neuroplastic changes over time and optimize therapeutic outcomes.

## SUMMARY

- A novel wrist joint proprioception system is introduced that enables precise, multi-axis assessment and training of proprioceptive function through a fully integrated robotic exoskeleton and closed-loop control architecture. This system is designed to isolate and quantify position sense and motion sense discrimination in the human wrist while simultaneously delivering targeted sensorimotor training protocols that promote neuroplastic adaptation.

- The manipulandum unit comprises a three-degree-of-freedom exoskeletal structure that conforms to the anatomical range of motion of the human wrist, allowing independent, non-coupled actuation of flexion/extension, adduction/abduction, and forearm rotation. This unit is engineered to deliver passive joint displacements with sub-degree precision while maintaining natural biomechanical alignment, ensuring that proprioceptive stimuli are delivered in a physiologically valid manner.

- The controller is a real-time embedded system that coordinates the manipulandum’s motors, processes feedback from high-resolution rotary encoders, executes adaptive psychophysical algorithms, and manages the delivery of training tasks. It operates with a sampling rate of 200 Hz, enabling continuous monitoring of joint position, velocity, and torque, and dynamically adjusts stimulus parameters based on participant responses to optimize threshold estimation and training progression.

- The proprioception assessment operation employs a two-alternative forced-choice paradigm in which the wrist is passively moved to two distinct positions in succession, and the participant indicates which position was farther from a neutral reference. Stimulus differences are adaptively modulated using a psi-marginal algorithm to converge rapidly on the participant’s just-noticeable difference threshold for each joint plane.

- The position sense routine is a core component of the assessment protocol, wherein the system presents a standard stimulus at a fixed reference angle (e.g., 15° flexion) followed by a comparison stimulus of varying angular offset. The participant’s verbal response is recorded, and the system iteratively refines the difference between stimuli to determine the smallest detectable angular displacement, yielding a quantitative measure of position sense acuity.

- Additional embodiments include motion sense discrimination tasks that assess the ability to detect the direction and magnitude of passive wrist movement, as well as integrated rehabilitation modules that deliver visuomotor training tasks requiring precise, small-amplitude corrections to maintain a virtual object in equilibrium, thereby challenging and enhancing proprioceptive feedback loops.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

- The manipulandum unit 22 is a wearable, three-degree-of-freedom robotic exoskeleton designed to interface with the human wrist and forearm, enabling precise, passive movement across the flexion/extension, adduction/abduction, and pronation/supination planes. It is constructed to replicate the natural kinematics of the wrist joint while eliminating confounding influences from muscle activation, skin stretch, or external torque.

- The manipulandum unit components include a rigid base, a handle for hand engagement, a linkage assembly comprising two articulated arms, a track system for adduction/abduction motion, a guide track for flexion/extension motion, and four brushless servo motors with integrated rotary encoders for closed-loop control.

- The manipulandum unit 22 operates by receiving commands from the controller to move the wrist through predefined trajectories, applying torque to rotate the joint at controlled velocities, and recording the exact angular position at every millisecond via high-resolution encoders. The system is fully backdrivable, allowing for passive movement without resistance, and is calibrated to ensure zero backlash and minimal friction.

- The base 42 is a rigid, anatomically contoured structure that supports the forearm in a neutral, supinated position, ensuring consistent limb alignment across testing sessions. It is constructed from lightweight aerospace-grade composite materials and includes adjustable straps to secure the forearm without restricting circulation or inducing discomfort.

- The base 42 features a modular mounting interface that allows for rapid attachment and detachment from a fixed workstation, enabling use in clinical, laboratory, or home-based settings. It incorporates embedded force sensors to detect unintended limb movement and automatically halt motion if excessive torque is detected, ensuring participant safety.

- The handle 44 is ergonomically shaped to accommodate a wide range of hand sizes and is covered in a soft, non-slip material to enhance grip stability without introducing tactile cues. It is rigidly coupled to the distal end of the linkage assembly, ensuring that all wrist movements are transmitted directly to the manipulandum without slippage or misalignment.

- The handle 44 includes a thumb rest and finger grooves that promote natural hand posture and minimize compensatory movements. It is designed to be worn without gloves, allowing for direct skin-to-handle contact that preserves proprioceptive feedback from the hand while preventing slippage during rapid movements.

- The linkage assembly 46 consists of two parallel, rigid arms connected by pivot joints that allow independent rotation about the flexion/extension and adduction/abduction axes. These arms are constructed from carbon fiber-reinforced polymer to minimize mass and maximize torsional stiffness.

- The PS transmission sub-assembly 70 is responsible for adduction/abduction motion and comprises a lead screw mechanism driven by a first motor, which translates linear motion into rotational displacement of the handle via a precision gear train. This sub-assembly ensures smooth, frictionless motion with a resolution of 0.0075 degrees.

- The track 80 is a linear rail system mounted to the base 42 that guides the proximal end of the linkage assembly during adduction/abduction movements. It is constructed from hardened steel with a low-friction polymer coating to ensure consistent motion and long-term durability.

- The track 80 features precision-machined grooves that engage with rollers on the linkage assembly, eliminating lateral play and ensuring that motion occurs exclusively along the intended axis. It includes embedded optical sensors that detect the position of the carriage and provide real-time feedback to the controller.

- The arms 82, 84 are symmetrical, rigid links that connect the handle 44 to the carriage 122 and the track 80, forming a parallelogram mechanism that preserves the orientation of the wrist throughout motion. Each arm is hollow to reduce weight and contains internal wiring for motor power and encoder signal transmission.

- The arms 82, 84 are designed with a slight pre-tension to eliminate slack and ensure immediate torque transmission. Their length and pivot locations are calibrated to match the average anatomical dimensions of the human wrist, minimizing joint misalignment and maximizing biomechanical fidelity.

- The FE transmission sub-assembly 72 governs flexion/extension motion and consists of a second motor, a timing belt, and a pulley system that rotates the handle relative to the linkage arms. This sub-assembly is mounted orthogonally to the PS transmission to ensure independent control of each axis.

- The guide track 120 is a curved, precision-machined rail that constrains the motion of the carriage 122 during flexion/extension movements, ensuring that the wrist rotates around a fixed anatomical axis. It is shaped to replicate the natural arc of wrist flexion and extension as defined by cadaveric kinematic studies.

- The carriage 122 is a sliding component that moves along the guide track 120 and connects to the proximal ends of the arms 82, 84. It is constructed from anodized aluminum and includes four ball bearings that roll along the track, minimizing friction and wear.

- The carriage 122 features a central bore that accommodates the output shaft of the second motor, and it includes a magnetic encoder ring that provides continuous angular feedback to the controller. It is designed to withstand over 100,000 cycles of motion without degradation in performance.

- The legs 124, 126 are rigid, vertical supports that connect the carriage 122 to the base 42 and maintain the structural integrity of the manipulandum during high-torque movements. They are aligned to ensure that the center of rotation of the wrist remains fixed throughout the range of motion.

- The legs 124, 126 are adjustable in height to accommodate individuals of varying forearm lengths, and they include locking mechanisms that secure the position after calibration. Each leg is equipped with a torque sensor to monitor for unintended limb movement and trigger safety protocols if necessary.

- The AA axis is established by the rotational center of the pronation/supination motor, which is aligned with the anatomical axis of the radioulnar joint. This axis is independently controlled by a third motor and is isolated from flexion/extension and adduction/abduction motions to prevent cross-coupling.

- The FE axis is established by the pivot point of the guide track 120, which is positioned to coincide with the center of rotation of the wrist joint during flexion and extension. This alignment is verified through cadaveric kinematic mapping and confirmed during each system calibration.

- The PS axis is established by the centerline of the lead screw mechanism in the PS transmission sub-assembly 70, which is oriented perpendicular to the FE axis and passes through the anatomical center of the wrist. This configuration ensures that adduction and abduction occur in the plane of the forearm, consistent with natural joint mechanics.

- The motor M2 drives the FE transmission sub-assembly 72 and is selected for its high torque-to-weight ratio and low inertia, enabling rapid acceleration and deceleration of the wrist during passive movement. It is controlled via a closed-loop PID algorithm that maintains velocity accuracy within ±0.1°/s.

- The motors M3 and M4 are responsible for adduction/abduction and pronation/supination, respectively. Each is a brushless DC motor with integrated absolute encoders and thermal sensors to prevent overheating during prolonged use. They are mounted externally to the manipulandum to reduce mass on the moving limb.

- The motor M1 controls the linear motion of the carriage 122 along the guide track 120 and is coupled to the PS transmission sub-assembly 70 via a precision ball screw. It operates with a resolution of 0.0075 degrees and is capable of generating sufficient torque to overcome inertial loads during rapid motion.

- The motor M1 and track 80 interface is designed to eliminate backlash through the use of dual-nut preload mechanisms and magnetic linear encoders that provide continuous position feedback. This ensures that every millimeter of linear motion corresponds precisely to a known angular displacement of the wrist.

- The motor M2 and guide track 120 interface is achieved through a timing belt and pulley system that transmits rotational motion from the motor to the carriage 122. The belt is made of reinforced polyurethane with fiberglass tensile cords to ensure minimal stretch and long-term durability.

- The motor M3 and M4 arm interface is established through direct coupling to the proximal ends of the linkage arms, with each motor’s output shaft aligned to the rotational axis of the corresponding joint. This eliminates the need for intermediate gears or linkages, reducing mechanical complexity and increasing system reliability.

- Motor operation in the PS plane is characterized by linear translation of the carriage 122 along the track 80, which induces a rotational displacement of the wrist in the adduction/abduction plane. The relationship between linear displacement and angular rotation is calibrated using a laser interferometer to ensure sub-degree accuracy.

- Motor operation in the FE plane involves rotation of the handle 44 around the FE axis, driven by the second motor M2 through the timing belt and pulley system. The motion is constrained by the curved guide track 120 to follow the natural arc of wrist flexion and extension.

- Motor operation in the AA plane is achieved by independent rotation of the handle 44 around the AA axis, driven by the third motor M3. This motion is decoupled from the other axes through mechanical isolation and software compensation, ensuring that rotation in one plane does not induce unintended motion in another.

- The three degrees of freedom of the manipulandum unit 22—flexion/extension, adduction/abduction, and pronation/supination—allow for complete, unconstrained manipulation of the wrist joint in all anatomical planes. This enables the system to assess proprioceptive acuity across the full functional range of the wrist, unlike single-axis devices that fail to capture multidimensional deficits.

- AA axis movement is characterized by rotation of the forearm around its long axis, with the handle rotating in a plane perpendicular to the forearm. This motion is critical for tasks requiring hand orientation, such as turning a doorknob or using a screwdriver, and is assessed independently to detect isolated deficits in supination/pronation discrimination.

- FE axis movement involves flexion from 0° to 45° and extension from 0° to 30°, covering the full physiological range of the wrist. The system is calibrated to ensure that the neutral position corresponds to the anatomical midline, and motion is restricted to prevent hyperextension or hyperflexion beyond safe limits.

- PS axis movement spans 20° of adduction and 25° of abduction, encompassing the natural range of wrist deviation. The system ensures that motion occurs in a plane parallel to the forearm, avoiding any unintended rotation or tilting of the hand.

- The range of motion of the manipulandum unit 22 is designed to match the average human wrist joint, with flexion/extension ranging from −30° to +45°, adduction/abduction from −25° to +20°, and pronation/supination from −85° to +85°. This ensures that the system can accommodate individuals across a broad demographic range without requiring adjustments.

- The ROM of the manipulandum unit 22 is comparable to that of the human wrist, as verified through comparative kinematic analysis of cadaveric specimens and in vivo motion capture studies. This biomechanical fidelity is essential for ensuring that proprioceptive assessments reflect true physiological function rather than artificial constraints imposed by the device.

- The manipulandum unit 22 operates by receiving a trajectory command from the controller, executing the motion through coordinated motor actuation, and recording the exact angular position at every 5 milliseconds. Feedback from the encoders is used to correct for drift, compensate for mechanical compliance, and ensure that the intended stimulus is delivered with maximal precision.

- The second motor M2 is a high-torque, low-inertia brushless DC motor that drives the flexion/extension axis through a timing belt and pulley system. It is selected for its ability to accelerate the wrist rapidly while maintaining precise velocity control, enabling the delivery of stimuli at velocities up to 12°/s.

- The operation of the second motor M2 is governed by a real-time PID control loop that adjusts current output based on encoder feedback to maintain a constant velocity profile. The system compensates for gravitational and inertial loads to ensure that the wrist experiences identical motion regardless of starting position.

- The third motor M3 is responsible for adduction/abduction motion and is coupled to the PS transmission sub-assembly 70. It operates with a resolution of 0.0075 degrees and is capable of generating up to 0.8 Nm of torque, sufficient to overcome the passive resistance of the wrist joint during rapid movements.

- The operation of the third motor M3 is synchronized with the motion of the carriage 122 along the track 80, ensuring that the angular displacement of the wrist is directly proportional to the linear displacement of the carriage. Calibration is performed at the start of each session using a laser displacement sensor.

- The fourth motor M4 controls pronation and supination of the forearm and is mounted externally to the base 42. It is connected to the handle 44 via a direct drive shaft, eliminating the need for belts or gears and minimizing mechanical hysteresis.

- The operation of the fourth motor M4 is isolated from the other axes through software-based decoupling algorithms that prevent cross-coupling during multi-axis movements. This ensures that when the wrist is moved in the FE plane, no unintended rotation occurs in the AA plane.

- Motor selection for each axis was based on criteria of torque output, resolution, speed, durability, and backdrivability. Brushless DC motors were chosen for their efficiency, low noise, and precise control characteristics, while encoders were selected for their sub-degree resolution and immunity to electromagnetic interference.

- The motor configuration is arranged in a serial kinematic chain, with each axis operating independently but coordinated through a central controller. This architecture allows for complex, multi-axis trajectories to be generated while maintaining full control over each degree of freedom.

- The controller 24 is a real-time embedded system based on a 32-bit ARM Cortex-M7 microprocessor, running a deterministic real-time operating system with a 5-millisecond control cycle. It manages motor control, data acquisition, stimulus delivery, and participant response logging.

- The memory used by the controller 24 includes 1 GB of non-volatile flash storage for storing assessment protocols, training modules, participant data, and calibration files. It also includes 512 MB of dynamic RAM for real-time processing of sensor data and algorithm execution.

- The computer storage media includes a removable SD card slot that allows for secure export of data to external systems, and it supports encrypted file formats to ensure patient privacy and compliance with HIPAA regulations.

- The operation of the controller 24 involves continuous sampling of encoder data, execution of adaptive psychophysical algorithms, generation of motor control signals, and logging of participant responses. It operates autonomously once initialized, requiring minimal operator intervention.

- Motor control is achieved through closed-loop PID algorithms that regulate current output based on position and velocity feedback from the encoders. Each motor is independently tuned to account for its unique mechanical characteristics, ensuring consistent performance across all axes.

- Rotary encoders are high-resolution optical sensors attached to each motor shaft, providing absolute position feedback with a resolution of 0.0075 degrees. They are calibrated at the start of each session using a reference goniometer to ensure accuracy.

- Input/output modules include a touch-screen interface for operator control, a voice-recognition module for participant responses, and a USB port for data export. The system also includes LED indicators for status monitoring and an emergency stop button for safety.

- Interface formats include Bluetooth 5.0 for wireless communication with external devices, Ethernet for network connectivity, and a proprietary serial protocol for communication with the manipulandum unit. All interfaces are compliant with industry standards for medical device interoperability.

- The control architecture is hierarchical, with a top-level task scheduler coordinating lower-level modules for motor control, stimulus delivery, data logging, and user interface. Each module operates independently but communicates through a shared memory buffer to ensure synchronization.

- Proprioception-related operations include the execution of position sense and motion sense discrimination tasks, the application of adaptive psychophysical algorithms, and the calculation of just-noticeable difference thresholds for each joint plane.

- The assessment program 300 is a modular software suite that runs on the controller 24 and orchestrates all proprioceptive assessments. It includes subroutines for stimulus generation, response collection, threshold estimation, and data analysis.

- The Position Sense category 302 encompasses all tasks designed to measure the ability to discriminate between two static joint positions. It includes the Position Sense Detection Module S.1 and the Position Sense Discrimination Module S.2.

- The Position Sense Detection Module S.1 presents a standard stimulus at a reference angle (e.g., 15° flexion) followed by a comparison stimulus of variable angular offset. The participant indicates which position was farther from neutral, and the system adjusts the difference between stimuli using a psi-marginal algorithm to converge on the JND threshold.

- The operation of the Position Sense Detection Module S.1 involves passive movement of the wrist at a constant velocity of 6°/s, followed by a 2-second hold at each position. The participant responds verbally, and the system records the response and the angular difference between stimuli.

- The Position Sense Discrimination Module S.2 applies a two-alternative forced-choice paradigm with randomized stimulus order and adaptive threshold estimation. It runs for 30 trials per plane and calculates the JND by fitting the response data to a Weibull psychometric function.

- The operation of the Position Sense Discrimination Module S.2 includes automatic outlier detection, lapse rate correction, and statistical confidence interval estimation. The final JND is reported as the 75% correct threshold with a 95% confidence interval.

- The Motion Sense category 304 includes tasks that assess the ability to detect the direction and magnitude of passive wrist movement. It includes the Motion Sense Detection Module S.3 and the Motion Sense Discrimination Module S.4.

- The Motion Sense Detection Module S.3 presents a passive movement of variable amplitude and direction, and the participant indicates whether the motion was flexion or extension, adduction or abduction, or pronation or supination.

- The operation of the Motion Sense Detection Module S.3 involves movement at a constant velocity of 8°/s, with amplitudes ranging from 1° to 10°. The system uses an adaptive staircase algorithm to determine the minimum detectable displacement.

- The Motion Sense Discrimination Module S.4 evaluates the ability to discriminate between two different movement velocities or directions. It presents two sequential movements and asks the participant to identify which was faster or which direction was different.

- The operation of the Motion Sense Discrimination Module S.4 employs a method of constant stimuli with 10 levels of velocity difference, and the participant’s responses are fitted to a cumulative Gaussian function to determine the discrimination threshold.

- Other sensory assessment modules include tactile sensitivity testing, vibration detection, and force discrimination, which can be optionally integrated to provide a comprehensive somatosensory profile.

- The rehabilitation training program 400 is a suite of visuomotor tasks designed to enhance proprioceptive acuity through sensorimotor learning. It includes discrete and continuous training modules that challenge the participant to make precise, small-amplitude corrections to maintain a virtual object in equilibrium.

- The Discrete category 402 includes tasks that require the participant to move the wrist to a target position and hold it for a specified duration. It includes the Center-Out Task module T.1 and the Follow the Target module T.2.

- The Center-Out Task module T.1 presents a target at a random location within the wrist’s range of motion, and the participant must actively move the wrist to align the virtual ball with the target. No visual feedback is provided during movement, forcing reliance on proprioception.

- The operation of the Center-Out Task module T.1 involves presenting a target, initiating movement, and recording the angular error at the end of the movement. The difficulty is adaptively increased by reducing the target size or increasing the required precision.

- The Follow the Target module T.2 requires the participant to track a moving target with their wrist, maintaining alignment as the target moves along a predefined trajectory. The trajectory includes sinusoidal, circular, and figure-eight patterns.

- The operation of the Follow the Target module T.2 involves real-time tracking of wrist position and calculation of root mean square error between the participant’s movement and the target path. Performance is scored based on accuracy and smoothness of motion.

- The Continuous Sensorimotor Training category 404 includes tasks that require ongoing, dynamic control of the wrist to maintain balance of a virtual object. It includes the Virtual Object Balancing module T.3 and the Figure Eight Tracking module T.4.

- The Virtual Object Balancing module T.3 simulates a ball on a tiltable surface, where wrist movement tilts the surface and the ball rolls. The participant must make continuous, small corrections to keep the ball within a target zone, challenging proprioceptive feedback loops.

- The operation of the Virtual Object Balancing module T.3 involves real-time simulation of physics-based dynamics, including mass, friction, and inertia. The difficulty is increased by reducing friction, increasing mass, or decreasing the target zone size.

- The Figure Eight Tracking module T.4 requires the participant to trace a figure-eight pattern with their wrist, following a visual path displayed on a screen. The path is presented in real time, and the participant must match the trajectory as closely as possible.

- The operation of the Figure Eight Tracking module T.4 involves recording the angular deviation from the target path at 200 Hz and calculating a path error score based on the area between the actual and desired trajectories.

- Other sensory rehabilitation training modules include mirror therapy, dual-limb coordination tasks, and proprioceptive memory recall exercises, all designed to promote interlimb transfer and cortical reorganization.

- The integration of assessment and training is achieved through a unified software platform that uses the results of the proprioceptive assessment to automatically tailor the difficulty, duration, and type of training task for each participant. This ensures that training is always aligned with the individual’s current sensory capacity.

- The benefits of the system include objective, quantitative measurement of proprioceptive acuity; adaptive, personalized training protocols; real-time feedback and progress tracking; seamless integration of assessment and therapy; and the potential for interlimb transfer of learning, enabling rehabilitation of the affected limb through training of the unaffected limb. The system is suitable for use in clinical, rehabilitation, and home-based settings, and it provides a standardized platform for longitudinal monitoring of sensorimotor recovery.
