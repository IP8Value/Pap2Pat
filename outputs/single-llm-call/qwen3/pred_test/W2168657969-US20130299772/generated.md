# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates to doped semiconductor nanoparticles, specifically colloidal quantum dots composed of a semiconductor matrix into which one or more dopant atoms are incorporated at high concentrations, thereby enabling precise and tunable control over the electronic and optical properties of the nanoscale material. These doped nanoparticles exhibit modified density of states, altered Fermi energy levels, and emergent impurity band formation that are not achievable through conventional doping methods in bulk semiconductors or in lightly doped nanocrystals. The invention encompasses nanoparticles in which dopant atoms are dispersed within the crystal lattice of the semiconductor material, either substitutionally or interstitially, resulting in the generation of free charge carriers and the modification of the band structure through quantum confinement effects. The doped nanoparticles are particularly suited for applications in optoelectronic devices, photovoltaics, infrared sensors, and advanced electronic circuits where controlled carrier concentration, narrow emission linewidths, and tunable absorption edges are critical. The invention further extends to hybrid nanostructures in which the doped semiconductor core is surrounded by surface ligands or coated with metallic or alloyed regions, enabling multifunctional behavior and enhanced interfacial charge transfer.

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

Doping of bulk semiconductors has long been a cornerstone of solid-state electronics, enabling the creation of p-n junctions, transistors, and other essential components by introducing controlled concentrations of impurity atoms that donate or accept electrons. In bulk materials, dopant atoms are typically introduced during crystal growth or via diffusion processes, and their effectiveness is governed by thermodynamic equilibrium, solubility limits, and lattice compatibility. However, when scaling down to the nanoscale, the traditional paradigms of doping break down due to the dominance of quantum confinement, surface-to-volume ratios, and the inability to achieve sufficient dopant incorporation without inducing phase separation or structural collapse. Colloidal nanocrystals, in particular, have proven resistant to conventional doping techniques because their synthesis occurs in solution under non-equilibrium conditions, where dopant precursors often remain segregated on the surface or form secondary phases rather than integrating into the lattice. Previous attempts to dope quantum dots have yielded only marginal carrier concentrations, inconsistent results, or the formation of surface states that quench photoluminescence rather than enhance conductivity. Strategies such as post-synthetic ion exchange, in situ doping during nucleation, or ligand-assisted incorporation have been explored, but none have succeeded in achieving the high, uniform, and stable doping levels required for practical device applications. Recent progress in the field has focused on using isovalent dopants or surface passivation to mitigate non-radiative recombination, but these approaches fail to introduce the charge carriers necessary for electronic modulation. Consequently, there remains a critical unmet need for a method to produce colloidal semiconductor nanocrystals that are not only doped at high concentrations but also exhibit predictable, reproducible, and tunable electronic properties consistent with theoretical predictions of impurity band formation and band-tailing under strong confinement.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides heavily doped colloidal semiconductor nanocrystals in which dopant atoms are incorporated into the crystal lattice at concentrations sufficient to induce measurable changes in the density of states, Fermi energy position, and optical absorption and emission characteristics. The nanoparticles consist of a semiconductor material doped with at least one dopant material, wherein the dopant atoms are dispersed throughout the interior of the nanocrystal rather than confined to the surface or forming separate phases. The semiconductor material may be selected from Group III-V, II-VI, or IV-VI compounds, including but not limited to InAs, InP, CdSe, PbS, and their respective hydrates or alloyed variants. The dopant material is heterovalent relative to the host semiconductor atoms and is selected from transition metals such as copper, silver, gold, or their respective salts, which are introduced post-synthesis via a controlled solution-phase treatment. The doping process results in the generation of charge carriers—electrons or holes—depending on the valence state of the dopant, thereby enabling n-type or p-type behavior in the nanocrystals. The invention further encompasses nanoparticles containing at least two atoms of dopant material per nanocrystal, with the dopant concentration ranging from 0.5 to 15 atoms per nanocrystal depending on size and synthesis parameters. The presence of multiple dopant atoms within a single nanocrystal leads to delocalization and interaction of impurity-derived charge carriers, resulting in the emergence of a confined impurity band that manifests as band-tailing in optical spectra and shifts in the Fermi level as measured by scanning tunneling spectroscopy. The invention enables control over the band gap, Fermi energy, and carrier density through precise tuning of dopant identity, concentration, and nanocrystal size. Optical measurements reveal systematic red or blue shifts in absorption and photoluminescence spectra depending on the nature of the dopant, while scanning tunneling spectroscopy confirms shifts in the Fermi level and the appearance of localized in-gap states consistent with theoretical models of strongly interacting impurities. The invention further includes hybrid nanoparticles comprising a doped semiconductor core and a surface layer of metal or metal alloy, enabling the formation of Schottky junctions or plasmonic coupling. The nanoparticles exhibit dimensions between 1.5 and 6.0 nanometers in diameter, with narrow size distributions below 12% standard deviation. The method for preparing the doped nanoparticles involves contacting pre-synthesized undoped semiconductor nanocrystals with a solution containing a dopant precursor, wherein the dopant precursor is dissolved in an organic solvent in the presence of surface ligands that stabilize the nanocrystals and facilitate dopant diffusion. The ligands may include dodecylamine, didodecyldimethylammonium bromide, or other long-chain amines and surfactants that prevent aggregation and promote uniform dopant incorporation. The resulting doped nanoparticles are isolated via precipitation, washed, and re-dispersed in non-polar solvents for device integration. The invention further includes electronic devices comprising layers or regions of differently doped nanoparticles, such as bipolar transistors, diodes, and large-area display arrays, wherein the doped nanoparticles serve as active elements with tunable conductivity and optical response. Specific embodiments include Ag-doped InAs nanoparticles exhibiting p-type behavior, Cu-doped InAs nanoparticles exhibiting n-type behavior, and Au-doped InAs nanoparticles exhibiting minimal electronic perturbation due to isovalent substitution. The invention enables the fabrication of semiconductor nanocrystals with electronic properties that surpass those of bulk counterparts in terms of tunability, stability, and compatibility with solution-processable device architectures.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF EMBODIMENTS

The invention provides a method for the controlled doping of colloidal semiconductor nanocrystals that results in the formation of heavily doped quantum dots with tunable electronic properties. The process begins with the synthesis of undoped semiconductor nanocrystals, such as InAs, via a high-temperature injection method using indium and arsenic precursors in a coordinating solvent such as trioctylphosphine. The resulting nanocrystals are purified by size-selective precipitation using toluene and methanol to achieve a narrow size distribution. Subsequently, the nanocrystals are exposed to a solution containing a dopant precursor, such as CuCl₂, AgNO₃, or AuCl₃, in the presence of surface ligands including dodecylamine and didodecyldimethylammonium bromide. The dopant solution is added dropwise under inert atmosphere, and the mixture is stirred for a defined period to allow diffusion of the dopant atoms into the nanocrystal lattice. The dopant concentration is calibrated by varying the molar ratio of dopant precursor to nanocrystals, and the resulting samples are precipitated and washed to remove excess precursor. The incorporation of dopant atoms into the lattice is confirmed through a combination of inductively coupled plasma atomic emission spectroscopy, X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy, and X-ray diffraction, which demonstrate the presence of dopant elements within the nanocrystal core and the preservation of the original crystal structure. Scanning tunneling spectroscopy reveals a shift in the Fermi level toward the conduction band for Cu-doped InAs nanocrystals, indicative of n-type doping, and toward the valence band for Ag-doped nanocrystals, indicative of p-type doping, while Au-doped nanocrystals show minimal shift due to isovalent substitution. The optical absorption spectra of the doped nanocrystals exhibit distinct shifts: Cu doping leads to a blue shift in the first exciton peak, while Ag doping induces a red shift, consistent with the introduction of acceptor or donor states within the band gap. Photoluminescence spectra show quenching upon doping, consistent with the formation of non-radiative recombination pathways associated with deep impurity levels. Theoretical modeling based on a pseudo-crystal model incorporating randomly distributed impurity states and strong electron localization reproduces the observed spectral shifts and confirms that the dopant atoms form deep, localized states that interact to form a confined impurity band. The degree of band-tailing observed in the absorption edge is directly correlated with the number of dopant atoms per nanocrystal and the spatial distribution of dopants, with higher concentrations leading to broader tails and greater Fermi level shifts. The lattice parameter remains largely unchanged, as confirmed by XRD peak broadening analysis, indicating that dopant incorporation occurs without significant strain-induced phase separation. The diffusion of dopant atoms into the nanocrystal core is facilitated by the relatively large root-mean-square displacement of the dopant ions at room temperature, which exceeds the nanocrystal diameter, enabling rapid penetration within minutes. The resulting doped nanoparticles exhibit enhanced electrical conductivity, tunable carrier densities, and size-dependent optical responses that are not achievable through conventional doping methods. The invention further encompasses the use of these nanoparticles in electronic devices, including diodes with rectifying behavior, bipolar transistors with gate-tunable current flow, and large-area display arrays with color-tunable emission, wherein the doped nanoparticles serve as the active semiconducting component. The method is scalable, reproducible, and compatible with solution-based fabrication techniques, enabling the integration of doped quantum dots into next-generation optoelectronic systems.

### EXPERIMENTAL

The materials used in the synthesis include indium(III) chloride, tris(trimethylsilyl)arsine, trioctylphosphine, dodecylamine, didodecyldimethylammonium bromide, toluene, methanol, and metal precursors including copper(II) chloride, silver nitrate, silver chloride, and gold(III) chloride. InAs nanocrystals were synthesized by injecting a solution of tris(trimethylsilyl)arsine into a heated trioctylphosphine solution of indium chloride under inert atmosphere, followed by controlled growth at 260°C. The nanocrystals were purified by size-selective precipitation using toluene and methanol. For doping, metal precursors were dissolved in toluene with dodecylamine and didodecyldimethylammonium bromide to form a homogeneous solution, which was then added dropwise to a toluene dispersion of InAs nanocrystals. After 15 minutes of stirring, the samples were precipitated using methanol or acetone depending on the dopant. UV-Vis-NIR absorption spectra were recorded using a JASCO V-570 spectrometer, photoluminescence spectra using an Ocean Optics NIR-512 spectrophotometer with 633 nm excitation, and transmission electron microscopy using a Tecnai T12 G2 Spirit operating at 120 kV. Powder X-ray diffraction was performed using a Phillips PW1830/40 diffractometer with Cu Kα radiation, and X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy was carried out using a Kratos Analytical Axis Ultra instrument with Al Kα radiation. Inductively coupled plasma atomic emission spectroscopy was performed using a Perkin-Elmer Optima 3000 to quantify the number of dopant atoms per nanocrystal. Scanning tunneling microscopy and spectroscopy were conducted on Au(111)-supported nanocrystals using a cryogenic STM at 4.2 K. All procedures were performed under inert atmosphere to prevent oxidation. The data obtained confirmed the successful incorporation of dopant atoms into the nanocrystal lattice, the emergence of impurity states, and the tunable shifts in electronic and optical properties as a function of dopant identity and concentration.
