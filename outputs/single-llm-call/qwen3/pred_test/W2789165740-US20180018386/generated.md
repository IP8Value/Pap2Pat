## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The invention pertains to the field of data analysis and visualization, particularly as applied to high-dimensional biological data generated by flow cytometry and mass cytometry platforms. It further relates to computational methods for the automated comparison, alignment, and interpretation of complex cellular populations across multiple biological samples. The invention encompasses software-implemented systems and algorithms designed to process multidimensional measurement data, identify phenotypically distinct cell subsets, and quantitatively assess their similarities and differences through adaptive binning and multivariate distance metrics. The invention also extends to graphical user interfaces that facilitate interactive exploration, manual refinement, and real-time feedback during the analysis of high-dimensional cytometric datasets. Applications include clinical diagnostics, immunological research, oncology, infectious disease monitoring, and precision medicine workflows where reproducible, objective, and scalable comparison of cell populations is essential.

## BACKGROUND

The accurate comparison of cellular populations across biological samples is a fundamental requirement in biomedical research and clinical diagnostics, particularly in fields such as immunology, oncology, and translational medicine. Researchers routinely analyze control and experimental samples to detect changes induced by disease progression, therapeutic intervention, genetic manipulation, or environmental exposure. These comparisons depend on the reliable identification and alignment of corresponding cell subsets between samples. Traditionally, such comparisons have relied on manual gating, a labor-intensive process in which analysts visually define regions of interest on two-dimensional scatter plots based on prior knowledge and subjective judgment. This approach is inherently inconsistent, time-consuming, and incapable of scaling to the high-dimensional datasets now routinely generated by modern cytometers. Manual gating also introduces significant inter-operator variability, undermines reproducibility, and fails to capture subtle but biologically meaningful shifts in cell phenotype that occur across dimensions.

Automated clustering methods have been developed to overcome the limitations of manual gating, enabling the unbiased identification of cell populations in high-dimensional space. However, these methods face the well-documented “curse of dimensionality,” wherein the sparsity of data in high-dimensional spaces renders traditional statistical techniques ineffective and computationally prohibitive. Existing approaches to cluster matching fall into two broad categories. The first involves clustering each sample independently and then attempting to match clusters post hoc, as implemented in tools such as FLAME and flowMatch. While computationally efficient in low dimensions, these methods are highly sensitive to variations in population location, frequency, or morphology between samples. When populations shift significantly due to biological or technical variation—such as instrument drift, staining inconsistencies, or disease-induced phenotypic changes—these methods frequently fail to identify correct correspondences, leading to misalignment and erroneous quantification.

The second category employs joint clustering and matching strategies, such as ASPIRE and Joint Clustering and Matching, which pool data from multiple samples to construct a unified template of meta-clusters. Although these methods improve robustness by modeling biological variation across samples simultaneously, they are computationally intensive and rely on parametric assumptions about the underlying distribution of cell populations. These assumptions are often violated in real-world cytometric data, where cell subsets exhibit complex, non-Gaussian, and overlapping distributions. Furthermore, fitting mixture models or graph-based clustering algorithms in more than four dimensions becomes intractable due to the exponential growth in parameter space, rendering these methods impractical for routine use in high-dimensional settings. Neither approach adequately addresses the need for a method that is both computationally efficient and capable of detecting subtle, multidimensional differences—including the complete absence or splitting of cell populations—without requiring manual intervention or extensive parameter tuning.

There remains a critical unmet need for a method that can robustly match cell populations across diverse samples regardless of differences in marker expression levels, population frequencies, or spatial distribution, while simultaneously providing intuitive visualization tools that guide users through sequential gating decisions. Such a method must be non-parametric, insensitive to sample size, computationally scalable, and capable of distinguishing biologically relevant variation from technical noise. It must also support interactive user guidance to enable hypothesis-driven refinement of automated results, thereby bridging the gap between algorithmic objectivity and biological insight.

## BRIEF SUMMARY

The invention introduces a novel method for multidimensional cluster matching and visualization of high-dimensional biological data, enabling automated, objective, and biologically meaningful comparison of cell populations across multiple samples. The method employs a multivariate extension of the quadratic form distance to quantify dissimilarity between cell subsets, overcoming the limitations of prior approaches by incorporating spatial relationships between data bins and maintaining computational efficiency even in high-dimensional spaces. The invention further provides a system and method for generating interactive graphical displays that guide users through sequential gating procedures, enhancing reproducibility and reducing analytical bias.

The method begins by obtaining first sample data and second sample data, each comprising multidimensional measurements of cellular markers from distinct biological specimens. Clusters are identified within each sample using an automated clustering algorithm, such as density-based merging, to define phenotypically distinct cell populations. A combined binning pattern is determined through multivariate adaptive binning applied to the merged dataset of both samples, ensuring that bins adapt to the underlying data structure by recursively partitioning along the dimension of highest variance until a predefined bin size threshold is met. This combined binning pattern is then applied separately to each original sample, preserving the same bin boundaries across both datasets.

Histograms are generated for each identified cluster in each sample, representing the relative frequency of events within each bin. Dissimilarity scores are computed for every possible pair of clusters—one from each sample—using a quadratic form distance metric that incorporates a spatial dissimilarity matrix derived from the Euclidean distances between bin centers. The dissimilarity score is calculated using a mathematical formulation that ensures non-negativity and monotonic sensitivity to both frequency and spatial displacement of cell populations. Matched clusters are identified as those pairs exhibiting the lowest dissimilarity scores, while remaining unmatched clusters are designated as merging candidates.

Each merging candidate is combined with its nearest neighboring cluster within the same sample, and the dissimilarity score is recalculated. A decrease in the dissimilarity score following merging indicates that the original cluster was split across multiple subpopulations, whereas an increase indicates that a cluster is missing in one sample relative to the other. The method further calculates the distance between geometric means of matched clusters in each dimension, expressed in standard deviation units, and records relative frequencies to provide comprehensive quantitative context for each alignment.

The invention further includes a system for rendering a graphical user interface comprising two interactive displays: a first display showing a two-dimensional projection of the clustered data, and a second display presenting single-parameter histograms or bar charts for each marker. The system receives user input to select a guidance feature, such as a specific cell population or marker combination, and dynamically modifies the second display to highlight relevant parameters, indicate prior selections, and overlay threshold lines based on fluorescence-minus-one controls. User selection of a region within the two-dimensional plot triggers synchronized updates to the single-parameter charts, allowing real-time visualization of how gating decisions affect population distribution across dimensions. The interface further includes features for color-coded density distributions, threshold adjustment, median value display, and the ability to hide or reveal specific markers, enabling intuitive navigation of complex gating sequences.

### Example 1—Workflow for Automated Clustering and Alignment of Cell Populations in Flow Cytometry Data

Example 1 demonstrates the application of the invention to a dataset of human peripheral blood mononuclear cells stained for a 12-parameter flow cytometry panel. Data were preprocessed using Logicle transformation and clustered using a two-dimensional density-based merging algorithm. The QFMatch algorithm was applied to align clusters between a healthy control sample and a sample from a patient with chronic inflammation. Adaptive binning was performed on the combined dataset using 256 bins, resulting in a binning pattern that captured the heterogeneity of both samples. Histograms were generated for each cluster, and pairwise QF dissimilarity scores were computed. Matched clusters were identified based on minimal dissimilarity, and merging candidates were detected for populations that did not correspond directly. The algorithm correctly identified a split in the CD4+ T cell population, where one subset exhibited elevated CD25 expression in the patient sample, and a missing population of regulatory T cells in the control sample. The dissimilarity score increased upon merging, confirming absence. The geometric mean displacement between matched clusters was less than 0.5 standard deviations in all dimensions, indicating high alignment fidelity. The entire process completed in under 15 seconds on a standard desktop computer, demonstrating practical utility for clinical workflows.

### Example 2—Matching of Basophil Populations Between Patient Samples, Even when Marker Expression Levels Vary Between Patients

Example 2 applied the invention to fluorescence flow cytometry data from three patients with suspected allergic disorders, where basophil populations were identified using CD123 and FcεRI expression. Marker intensity (MFI) varied from 1033 to 6672 across samples, and relative frequencies ranged from 0.1% to 0.8%. Conventional clustering methods failed to align these populations due to the large spatial separation and intensity differences. The QFMatch algorithm successfully matched the basophil clusters across all three samples by recognizing that the spatial distribution of events, rather than absolute intensity, defined biological homology. The dissimilarity score between the most divergent basophil populations was 0.018, significantly lower than scores between non-homologous populations, which exceeded 0.15. The algorithm correctly identified the basophil cluster in each sample as a matched pair, despite a 5.1 standard deviation shift in one dimension. The relative frequency of basophils was accurately quantified in each sample, enabling comparison of activation states. The guidance window highlighted CD123 and FcεRI as the most informative parameters, and users were able to verify the match by toggling between two-dimensional projections and single-parameter histograms.

### Example 3—Detection of Missing Lymphocyte Populations in the Peritoneal Cavity of RAG Knockout (RAG−/−) Mice

Example 3 utilized the invention to compare peritoneal cavity cells from wild-type BALB/c mice and RAG−/− knockout mice, which lack mature T and B lymphocytes. Clusters were identified using CD5 and CD19 expression. In the wild-type sample, two distinct populations were observed: CD5hiCD19− (T cells) and CD19hiCD5lo/− (B cells). In the RAG−/− sample, these populations were absent. The QFMatch algorithm identified all other populations as matched, including macrophages, dendritic cells, and granulocytes. The unmatched clusters corresponding to T and B cells were designated as merging candidates, but merging them with neighboring populations resulted in a significant increase in dissimilarity score, confirming their absence. The algorithm generated a clear output indicating that the lymphoid compartment was missing, with no false positives. This result was validated by manual gating and flow cytometry histograms, demonstrating the method’s ability to objectively detect complete population loss without prior knowledge of expected phenotypes.

### Example 4—Cluster Matching of the Murine Lymphoid, Myeloid and Granuloid Lineages Between PerC and Spleen

Example 4 applied the invention to compare immune cell populations in the peritoneal cavity and spleen of wild-type mice. Despite significant differences in the composition of lymphoid, myeloid, and granuloid lineages between these tissues, the algorithm successfully matched shared populations such as dendritic cells, natural killer cells, and eosinophils. Unmatched clusters were automatically identified as tissue-specific populations: mast cells and large peritoneal macrophages were present only in the peritoneal cavity, while monocytes and neutrophils were enriched in the spleen. The QF dissimilarity scores for matched populations were consistently low, while scores for unmatched populations were high, enabling clear distinction. The method further revealed that splenic eosinophils expressed higher levels of Gr-1 than their peritoneal counterparts, a finding confirmed by sequential matching along the most informative dimension pairs. The guidance window enabled users to visualize this difference in real time, highlighting Gr-1 as the key differentiating marker. The algorithm completed alignment in under 20 seconds, demonstrating robustness across complex, tissue-specific datasets.

### Example 5—Guided Gating of SSc Eosinophil Data

Example 5 demonstrated the utility of the graphical user interface in guiding the gating of eosinophil populations in systemic sclerosis patient samples. The system rendered a two-dimensional plot of CD123 versus Siglec-8, with synchronized single-parameter histograms for all 12 markers. A user selected a region in the two-dimensional plot corresponding to a putative eosinophil population. The guidance window automatically updated to highlight CD123, Siglec-8, CCR3, and CD49b as the most informative markers, and overlaid fluorescence-minus-one control thresholds. The user adjusted the threshold line to exclude background events, and the system recalculated the population size and dissimilarity score in real time. The color-coded density distribution revealed a bimodal structure in Siglec-8 expression, prompting the user to perform a secondary gate. The system preserved the gating sequence and saved it as an analysis template. The final alignment matched eosinophils across 15 patient samples with a mean dissimilarity score of 0.021, demonstrating high reproducibility. The interface allowed rapid navigation of the gating sequence, reducing analysis time from hours to minutes while improving accuracy.
