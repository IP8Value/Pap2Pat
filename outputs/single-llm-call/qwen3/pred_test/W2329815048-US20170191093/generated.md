# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

Lipids are essential biomolecules that serve as energy storage compounds, structural components of cellular membranes, and precursors for signaling molecules in living organisms. Their hydrophobic nature allows for efficient energy density storage, making them critical for survival under nutrient-limiting conditions. In microbial systems, particularly oleaginous yeasts, lipids accumulate primarily in the form of triacylglycerols, which are sequestered within specialized intracellular organelles known as lipid droplets. The biosynthesis of these lipids involves a tightly regulated metabolic network that converts carbon sources such as glucose into fatty acids and subsequently esterifies them onto a glycerol backbone. The efficiency of this process is governed by the coordinated activity of enzymes involved in acetyl-CoA generation, fatty acid elongation, and final esterification steps. Among these, the terminal enzymatic reactions that catalyze the incorporation of the third acyl chain onto diacylglycerol to form triacylglycerol represent a key regulatory bottleneck. These reactions are mediated by a family of enzymes known as diacylglycerol acyltransferases, which exhibit distinct isoforms with differing subcellular localization, substrate specificity, and kinetic properties. While some organisms naturally accumulate high levels of lipids, their productivity is often limited by the capacity of these enzymes to drive flux through the pathway. Genetic manipulation of these enzymes has emerged as a central strategy for enhancing lipid yields in industrial biotechnology applications. In addition to the enzymes responsible for lipid synthesis, the degradation of stored triacylglycerols by lipases represents a competing pathway that can significantly reduce net accumulation, particularly during the stationary phase of fermentation when nutrient depletion triggers catabolic responses. The interplay between biosynthetic and catabolic enzymes therefore determines the net lipid content of the cell and presents a dual target for metabolic engineering.

## SUMMARY

A transformed microbial cell is disclosed that exhibits significantly elevated triacylglycerol content as a result of specific genetic modifications designed to enhance lipid biosynthesis and suppress lipid degradation. The first genetic modification involves the introduction of an exogenous nucleic acid sequence encoding a diacylglycerol acyltransferase type 1 protein, which is functionally expressed in the host cell to catalyze the esterification of diacylglycerol with acyl-CoA, thereby promoting the formation of triacylglycerol. The second genetic modification comprises the introduction of an exogenous nucleic acid sequence encoding a diacylglycerol acyltransferase type 2 protein, which operates in parallel with the type 1 enzyme to further drive triacylglycerol synthesis through a distinct biochemical mechanism. Optionally, a third genetic modification may be introduced to reduce the activity of a triacylglycerol lipase gene, thereby inhibiting the hydrolytic breakdown of stored triacylglycerols during late-phase fermentation. The method of increasing lipid content in a microbial cell involves the stable integration of the first and second nucleotide sequences into the host genome under the control of constitutive or inducible promoters, ensuring sustained expression of the encoded diacylglycerol acyltransferase proteins. When the optional third nucleotide sequence is employed, it is configured to disrupt, delete, or downregulate the expression of a native triacylglycerol lipase gene, thereby preventing the mobilization of lipid reserves. In an alternative embodiment, a single nucleotide sequence may be introduced to simultaneously express both a type 1 and a type 2 diacylglycerol acyltransferase from a single transcriptional unit. The method for increasing triacylglycerol content further includes the cultivation of the transformed cell under nutrient-limited conditions, particularly nitrogen limitation, to trigger lipid accumulation. Upon completion of fermentation, the triacylglycerol may be recovered from the cell biomass through extraction and purification processes, yielding a lipid product suitable for use in biofuels, oleochemicals, or nutritional supplements.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

### Overview

The triacylglycerol content of a microbial cell may be substantially increased through the coordinated modulation of three key enzymatic activities within the lipid biosynthesis and degradation pathways. The overexpression of diacylglycerol acyltransferase type 1 enhances the rate of triacylglycerol synthesis by catalyzing the final acylation step using a distinct substrate preference and subcellular localization compared to other isoforms. Similarly, the overexpression of diacylglycerol acyltransferase type 2 complements this activity by providing an additional catalytic route for triacylglycerol formation, thereby alleviating potential metabolic bottlenecks. The down-regulation of triacylglycerol lipase activity, particularly during the stationary phase of growth, prevents the re-mobilization of stored lipids, thereby preserving the net accumulation achieved through biosynthetic enhancement. The combination of these three modifications—overexpression of type 1 and type 2 diacylglycerol acyltransferases and suppression of triacylglycerol lipase—results in a synergistic increase in triacylglycerol content that exceeds the sum of the effects observed with individual modifications. This multi-target approach ensures that lipid production is maximized while degradation is minimized, leading to a highly efficient biocatalyst for industrial lipid production.

### Definitions

The articles “a” and “an” are used herein to refer to one or more than one of the element to which the article applies, unless otherwise indicated. “Activity” refers to the catalytic function or biochemical capacity of a protein to perform a specific reaction, including but not limited to the transfer of an acyl group from acyl-CoA to diacylglycerol. Genetic modifications may enhance, diminish, or abolish such activity depending on the nature of the alteration. A “biologically-active portion” of a protein is a fragment or variant that retains at least 50% of the full-length protein’s catalytic activity under standard assay conditions. Biologically-active portions of the DGA1 protein include conserved domains involved in acyl-CoA binding and membrane association, while those of the DGA2 protein encompass regions critical for diacylglycerol recognition and catalytic triad formation. DGAT1 refers to diacylglycerol acyltransferase type 1, DGAT2 to type 2, and DGAT3 to type 3, each representing distinct gene families with non-redundant roles in triacylglycerol synthesis. “Diacylglyceride,” “diacylglycerol,” and “diglyceride” are used interchangeably to denote a glycerol molecule esterified with two fatty acid chains. A “diacylglycerol acyltransferase” is an enzyme that catalyzes the formation of triacylglycerol from diacylglycerol and acyl-CoA. “Diacylglycerol acyltransferase, type 1” refers to enzymes encoded by the DGA2 gene family, while “type 2” refers to those encoded by the DGA1 gene family. A “domain” is a structurally and functionally discrete unit within a protein that contributes to its overall activity. A “drug” refers to any chemical agent used to select for or against a genetic modification, including antibiotics such as hygromycin, zeocin, or nourseothricin. “Dry weight” and “dry cell weight” refer to the mass of a microbial cell population after removal of all water content, typically determined by lyophilization. “Encode” means to specify the amino acid sequence of a protein through a nucleic acid sequence. “Exogenous” denotes a nucleic acid or gene introduced from an external source, not native to the host organism. An “exogenous nucleic acid” or “exogenous gene” is a DNA sequence that has been transferred into a cell and is not naturally present in its genome. “Expression” refers to the process by which a gene is transcribed into mRNA and translated into a functional protein. Increased expression of a gene or amino acid sequence results in higher intracellular concentrations of the encoded product. A “gene” is a sequence of nucleotides that encodes a functional product, including proteins or RNA molecules. “Genetic modification” encompasses any intentional alteration of a cell’s genome, including insertion, deletion, substitution, or regulation of gene expression. A “homolog” is a gene or protein sharing evolutionary ancestry and structural similarity with another. An “inducible promoter” is a regulatory sequence that activates transcription only in the presence of a specific inducer molecule. “Integrated” means that a nucleic acid sequence has been stably incorporated into the host genome. “In operable linkage” describes the physical and functional connection between a promoter and a coding sequence such that transcription is initiated and proceeds through the gene. A “knockout mutation” is a genetic alteration that completely abolishes the function of a gene. “Native” refers to a gene or protein naturally occurring in a given organism. A “nucleic acid” is a polymer of nucleotides, including DNA or RNA. A “parent cell” is the unmodified host strain from which a transformed cell is derived. A “plasmid” is a circular, extrachromosomal DNA molecule capable of autonomous replication. A “portion” is a segment of a larger molecule. A “promoter” is a DNA sequence that directs the initiation of transcription. “Recombinant” refers to a nucleic acid molecule artificially constructed from different sources. A “regulatory region” includes promoter, enhancer, terminator, and other sequences that control gene expression. “Transformation” is the process by which a cell takes up and incorporates exogenous nucleic acid. A “transformed cell” is a cell that has been genetically modified to contain one or more exogenous nucleic acids. “Triacylglyceride” and “triacylglycerol” are synonymous terms referring to a glycerol molecule esterified with three fatty acid chains. A “triacylglycerol lipase” is an enzyme that hydrolyzes triacylglycerol into diacylglycerol and free fatty acids. A “vector” is a nucleic acid construct designed to deliver and express an exogenous gene in a host cell.

### Microbe Engineering

Genetic modification of microorganisms for enhanced lipid production involves the introduction of exogenous nucleic acid sequences into suitable host cells, which may be prokaryotic or eukaryotic, including bacteria, fungi, yeast, and algae. Suitable host cells include species of the genera Yarrowia, Arxula, Rhodosporidium, Lipomyces, and Saccharomyces. Microbial expression systems are selected based on their capacity for high-density fermentation, genetic tractability, and native lipid biosynthetic capacity. Chimeric genes may be constructed by fusing heterologous coding sequences with native or synthetic promoters and terminators to optimize expression. Transformation techniques include electroporation, biolistics, and chemical-mediated uptake, each adapted to the cell wall composition of the host. Plasmid construction employs standard molecular cloning methods using restriction enzymes, ligases, and PCR amplification. Vector design incorporates selectable markers such as hygromycin resistance, zeocin resistance, or nourseothricin resistance to facilitate the selection of transformants. Homologous recombination is employed to integrate exogenous sequences into specific genomic loci, ensuring stable inheritance and minimizing positional effects. Co-opting host regulatory elements, such as native promoters or terminators, enhances compatibility and expression efficiency. Modifying regulatory sequences may involve replacing native promoters with stronger constitutive or inducible variants, while modifying protein-coding regions may include codon optimization to match host tRNA abundance. Targeting constructs are designed with homologous flanking regions to direct integration at desired genomic sites. Transformation of these constructs is optimized by adjusting DNA concentration, cell preparation, and recovery conditions. Increasing recombination efficiency may involve the use of hydroxyurea to arrest cells in S-phase or the deletion of non-homologous end joining genes. Vector components include promoters, terminators, selectable markers, and origin of replication elements. Control sequences regulate transcriptional and translational efficiency. Promoters such as GPD1, TEF1, and EXP1 are commonly used for constitutive expression in yeast. Terminators such as ADH1 or CYC1 ensure proper mRNA processing. Gene and codon optimization are performed using computational algorithms to maximize translation efficiency. Expression cassettes combine promoter, coding sequence, and terminator into a single transcriptional unit. Selectable markers enable isolation of transformed cells. Codon usage optimization aligns codon frequency with host tRNA pools to enhance protein yield. Transformation techniques such as biolistics involve particle-mediated delivery, while electroporation uses electrical pulses to transiently permeabilize membranes. Examples of transgene expression include the production of DGA1 and DGA2 proteins in Yarrowia lipolytica at levels exceeding endogenous expression by more than tenfold.

## EXEMPLIFICATION

### Example 1: Method to Increase the Activity of a DGA1 Protein (DGAT2 Gene)

A nucleic acid construct for expressing the DGA1 gene from Rhodosporidium toruloides was assembled by cloning the synthetic cDNA sequence designated NG66 into a linear expression vector under the control of the Yarrowia lipolytica GPD1 promoter and flanked by homologous sequences for chromosomal integration. The construct also contained the nat1 gene as a selectable marker for nourseothricin resistance. The linear DNA was transformed into Yarrowia lipolytica strain NS18 using electroporation. Transformants were selected on YPD agar supplemented with nourseothricin. Lipid content was assessed by Bodipy fluorescence and gas chromatography. Strains expressing NG66 exhibited a threefold increase in lipid content compared to the parental strain, confirming enhanced diacylglycerol acyltransferase activity.

### Example 2: Lipid Assay

Yarrowia lipolytica strains were inoculated into nitrogen-limited media containing 100 g/L glucose and incubated at 30°C for 96 hours. Cells were harvested, washed, and mixed with ethanol and Bodipy 493/503 dye. Fluorescence was measured at excitation 484 nm and emission 510 nm using a microplate spectrophotometer. Optical density at 600 nm was recorded for normalization. The fluorescence signal correlated linearly with triacylglycerol content as confirmed by gas chromatography analysis of fatty acid methyl esters.

### Example 3: Analysis and Screening of Y. lipolytica Strains that Express DGA1

Nine DGA1 genes from diverse oleaginous organisms were expressed in Yarrowia lipolytica NS18 under the GPD1 promoter. Transformants were screened in 96-well plates using fluorescence-based lipid assays. The top-performing isolates were advanced to 50-mL shake flasks and then to 1-L bioreactors. Strains expressing R. toruloides DGA1 (NG66 and NG67) showed the highest lipid accumulation, with NG67 achieving 72% lipid content by dry weight. Glucose consumption was monitored by HPLC, confirming efficient substrate utilization. Lipid content in NS281, expressing NG66, was 68% compared to 32% in wild-type NS18.

### Example 4: Method to Knockout Triacylglycerol Lipase Knockout Gene in Y. lipolytica

A deletion cassette for the TGL3 gene was constructed by flanking the hygromycin resistance gene with 50-bp homologous regions upstream and downstream of the TGL3 coding sequence. The cassette was co-transformed with a second marker into Yarrowia lipolytica NS18 and NS281. Transformants were selected on hygromycin-containing media. PCR analysis confirmed deletion of the TGL3 gene and absence of wild-type alleles. Strains with TGL3 deletion showed no growth defect but exhibited 22% higher lipid content at 140 hours compared to controls, indicating suppressed lipid degradation.

### Example 5: Cells that Overexpress Both DGA1 and DGA2 and That Contain a TGl3 Deletion Accumulate More TAGs Than Cells That Do Not Overexpress DGA2

Strain NS377, expressing R. toruloides DGA1 and lacking TGL3, was transformed with a plasmid expressing C. purpurea DGA2 under the TEF1 promoter. The resulting strain NS432 was cultivated in 1-L bioreactors under fed-batch conditions. Lipid content was measured by GC and normalized to dry cell weight. NS432 accumulated 73% lipid by dry weight, whereas strain NS450, lacking TGL3 deletion but expressing DGA1 and DGA2, accumulated only 65%. The addition of TGL3 deletion increased lipid retention by 12% during late fermentation.

### Example 6: Increasing the Activity of DGA1, DGA2, or DGA3 in Arxula adeninivorans

Twenty-nine DGA genes from diverse donors were synthesized and cloned into expression vectors under the control of the A. adeninivorans TEF1 promoter. Transformants were generated via electroporation and selected with zeocin. Lipid content was assessed by Bodipy staining after 96 hours of fermentation. DGA2 genes from Yarrowia lipolytica and Chaetomium globosum yielded the highest lipid accumulation, with increases of 2.8-fold and 3.1-fold over the control, respectively. DGA1 and DGA3 genes showed moderate but significant improvements.

### Example 7: Increasing the Activity of DGA1, DGA2, or DGA3 in Yarrowia lipolytica

Eighteen DGA genes were expressed in Yarrowia lipolytica strain NS598 using the GPD1 promoter. Transformants were screened by fluorescence assay. DGA2 from Claviceps purpurea produced the highest lipid content, followed by DGA1 from Rhodosporidium toruloides. DGA3 from Yarrowia lipolytica showed a 1.7-fold increase. All constructs were confirmed by qPCR and Western blot.
