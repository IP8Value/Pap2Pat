# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

- Hematologic diseases represent a broad and clinically significant category of medical conditions that affect the production, function, or destruction of blood cells and their components. These disorders encompass a wide spectrum of pathologies, ranging from mild, chronic anemias to life-threatening malignancies such as leukemia and lymphoma. The global burden of hematologic disease is substantial, with millions of individuals affected annually, particularly in low- and middle-income regions where access to diagnostic infrastructure is limited. Among the most prevalent of these conditions is anemia, a syndrome characterized by a reduction in the concentration of hemoglobin in circulating red blood cells below established physiological thresholds. Anemia impairs oxygen delivery to vital organs, leading to fatigue, cognitive dysfunction, cardiac strain, and in severe cases, organ failure. Its etiology is multifactorial, including nutritional deficiencies such as iron, vitamin B12, and folate insufficiency; chronic inflammation; blood loss; and inherited hemoglobinopathies. The clinical consequences of untreated anemia are profound, contributing to increased maternal and perinatal mortality, reduced work productivity, and diminished quality of life across all age groups.

- Sickle cell disease (SCD) is a hereditary hemoglobinopathy that exemplifies the complexity and severity of hematologic disorders. It arises from a single nucleotide substitution in the β-globin gene, resulting in the production of abnormal hemoglobin S, which polymerizes under low-oxygen conditions, causing red blood cells to assume a rigid, sickle-like morphology. These deformed cells obstruct microvascular flow, induce chronic hemolysis, and trigger recurrent vaso-occlusive crises that lead to tissue ischemia, acute pain episodes, and progressive end-organ damage. SCD is particularly prevalent in sub-Saharan Africa, where it affects approximately 250,000 newborns annually, and remains a leading cause of childhood mortality in regions with limited healthcare resources. The disease imposes a heavy economic burden, with annual healthcare expenditures in the United States alone exceeding $1.5 billion, driven by frequent hospitalizations, transfusion therapies, and long-term complications such as stroke, renal failure, and pulmonary hypertension. Despite its well-documented clinical impact, the diagnosis and ongoing management of SCD remain hindered by the lack of accessible, reliable, and affordable tools for routine hemoglobin monitoring.

- Current methods for measuring hemoglobin levels rely predominantly on invasive laboratory techniques, such as the cyanmethemoglobin assay or automated complete blood count (CBC) systems, which require venipuncture, sample transport to centralized laboratories, and extended processing times. These procedures are not only physically intrusive and psychologically distressing for patients—particularly children, individuals with needle phobia, or those with compromised venous access—but also logistically impractical in resource-limited settings. The cost of each laboratory test, though modest in isolation, accumulates to millions of dollars annually at the national level when scaled across outpatient and inpatient populations. Furthermore, the delay between sample collection and result delivery compromises timely clinical decision-making, especially in emergency or acute care contexts where rapid assessment of hemoglobin status is critical. These limitations underscore a critical gap in global health infrastructure: the absence of a rapid, non-invasive, and widely deployable method for hemoglobin quantification that can be integrated into primary care, community health programs, and mobile medical units.

- While portable point-of-care devices have been developed to address some of these challenges, many remain constrained by their reliance on disposable reagents, complex calibration protocols, or proprietary hardware that limits scalability and affordability. Existing commercial solutions often require specialized training for operation, are sensitive to environmental variables such as ambient light or temperature, and exhibit inconsistent performance across diverse skin pigmentation types or physiological conditions. Moreover, the majority of these tools are not designed for integration with ubiquitous consumer electronics, thereby restricting their potential for widespread adoption in low-resource environments where smartphones are increasingly accessible. The persistent reliance on invasive testing not only perpetuates disparities in healthcare delivery but also discourages routine screening, leading to delayed diagnosis and preventable complications.

- Non-invasive point-of-care tools represent a transformative paradigm in medical diagnostics, offering the potential to deliver accurate, real-time hemoglobin measurements without the need for blood extraction. Recent advances in optical sensing, digital imaging, and computational modeling have enabled the development of systems that leverage the intrinsic optical properties of biological tissues to infer hemoglobin concentration. These technologies exploit the differential absorption of light at specific wavelengths by oxygenated and deoxygenated hemoglobin, as well as the pulsatile nature of arterial blood flow, to extract physiologically relevant signals from superficial tissues. When combined with the computational power and imaging capabilities of modern smartphones, such approaches can be transformed into accessible, low-cost diagnostic platforms that require no additional consumables or laboratory infrastructure.

- The urgent need for non-invasive hemoglobin measurement stems from the convergence of several global health imperatives: the high prevalence of anemia and hemoglobinopathies, the logistical and economic barriers to conventional testing, and the increasing penetration of mobile technology in underserved communities. A reliable, non-invasive method that can be deployed via a smartphone would enable widespread screening in schools, clinics, pharmacies, and even homes, facilitating early detection, continuous monitoring, and timely intervention. Such a tool would empower community health workers, reduce dependency on centralized laboratories, and ultimately save lives by making hemoglobin assessment as routine and accessible as taking a photograph.

## SUMMARY

- A novel method for non-invasive hemoglobin measurement has been developed that utilizes time-based series of images captured by a standard smartphone camera under controlled near-infrared illumination to quantify hemoglobin concentration without requiring blood extraction. The method operates by illuminating a subject’s fingertip with specific wavelengths of near-infrared light and recording the transmitted light through the tissue using the device’s built-in camera sensor. The resulting video data contains subtle, pulsatile variations in light intensity that correspond to cardiac-driven changes in blood volume, forming the basis for deriving a photoplethysmographic signal. These variations are analyzed through a series of computational steps that extract physiologically meaningful features related to hemoglobin content, enabling accurate estimation of hemoglobin levels from non-invasive optical measurements.

- The process begins with the acquisition of a time-based series of images from the ventral pad of the finger, where near-infrared light emitted from strategically positioned light-emitting diodes penetrates the tissue and is detected by the camera sensor on the opposite side. The captured video frames are processed to isolate the dynamic optical response associated with arterial pulsation, while suppressing static background signals and motion artifacts. Each frame is subdivided into a grid of spatial blocks, and a time-series signal is generated for each block by analyzing the intensity variations of the pixels over time. These individual signals are then aggregated and filtered to produce a clean, high-fidelity photoplethysmographic waveform that reflects the underlying hemodynamic changes in the microvasculature.

- The photoplethysmographic signal is further processed to identify individual cardiac cycles, allowing for the extraction of key temporal and amplitude-based features such as peak systolic height, diastolic trough, pulse rise time, and pulse transit time. These features are normalized and combined with a ratio-based metric derived from the relative absorption of light at two distinct near-infrared wavelengths—850 nanometers and 1070 nanometers—to form a comprehensive input vector for predictive modeling. The ratio of the alternating current (AC) component to the direct current (DC) component at each wavelength is calculated to account for variations in tissue thickness, skin pigmentation, and ambient light interference, thereby enhancing signal consistency across diverse physiological conditions.

- A predictive model is constructed using Support Vector Machine Regression, trained on a dataset of paired measurements from the non-invasive system and laboratory-grade hemoglobin values obtained from venous blood samples. The model learns the nonlinear relationship between the extracted optical features and the reference hemoglobin concentrations, enabling accurate estimation of hemoglobin levels from new, unseen data. The performance of the model is rigorously evaluated using statistical metrics including the mean absolute percentage error, correlation coefficient, and Bland-Altman analysis to quantify agreement between the predicted and reference values. The system is designed to operate seamlessly on a smartphone platform, with all image acquisition, signal processing, and prediction execution performed locally or via secure wireless transmission to a remote computational server.

- The system comprises a compact, low-cost illumination module containing near-infrared light-emitting diodes operating at 850 nm and 1070 nm, integrated with a smartphone camera and enclosed within a light-restrictive housing to eliminate ambient interference. The device is designed for easy coupling with the fingertip, ensuring consistent positioning and reproducible signal acquisition. A mobile application guides the user through the measurement process, captures the video data, processes the signal in real time, and displays the estimated hemoglobin level with confidence indicators. The predictive model is continuously refined through cloud-based data aggregation, allowing for adaptive calibration across diverse populations and demographic groups.

- The method eliminates the need for blood collection, reagents, or laboratory equipment, enabling deployment in settings ranging from rural clinics to urban emergency departments. By leveraging the ubiquity of smartphones and the robustness of optical signal processing, this approach provides a scalable, cost-effective, and patient-friendly solution for hemoglobin assessment that can be integrated into routine clinical workflows, public health screening programs, and home-based monitoring systems.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

- The photoplethysmogram (PPG) technique is a well-established optical method for non-invasively detecting changes in blood volume within peripheral tissues through the measurement of light absorption over time. It operates on the principle that arterial blood volume fluctuates with each cardiac cycle, inducing corresponding variations in the intensity of transmitted or reflected light. These fluctuations, though subtle, can be captured using photodetectors and converted into an electrical signal that reflects the pulsatile nature of arterial flow. In this invention, the PPG technique is adapted for hemoglobin quantification by utilizing near-infrared wavelengths that penetrate deeply into soft tissue and are differentially absorbed by hemoglobin species, enabling the extraction of concentration-dependent optical signatures without requiring blood sampling.

- The PPG system employed in this invention consists of three primary components: a controlled illumination source, a high-resolution video camera, and a signal processing unit. The illumination source comprises two near-infrared light-emitting diodes (LEDs) emitting at 850 nm and 1070 nm, selected based on their distinct absorption characteristics by oxygenated and deoxygenated hemoglobin, as well as their optimal transmission through human tissue. These LEDs are arranged in a fixed geometric configuration on one side of a finger-clamping housing, while the smartphone camera is positioned directly opposite to capture transmitted light. The entire assembly is enclosed within a light-tight structure to prevent ambient light contamination, ensuring signal fidelity and measurement reproducibility.

- The PPG signal generated by this system comprises two distinct components: a direct current (DC) component representing the baseline absorption due to static tissue elements such as skin, bone, and venous blood, and an alternating current (AC) component reflecting the pulsatile changes in arterial blood volume synchronized with the cardiac cycle. The AC component is modulated by the concentration of hemoglobin, its oxygenation state, and the physical properties of the surrounding tissue. By isolating and analyzing the AC-to-DC ratio at each illumination wavelength, the system effectively normalizes for inter-individual variability in tissue thickness, skin pigmentation, and ambient conditions, thereby enhancing the robustness of the hemoglobin estimation.

- Near-infrared light in the range of 850 nm to 1070 nm is employed because it resides within the so-called “optical window” of biological tissue, where absorption by water, melanin, and other chromophores is minimized, allowing photons to penetrate several millimeters into the dermis and subcutaneous layers. This depth of penetration enables the detection of arterial blood flow in the finger pulp, where the microvascular network is dense and accessible. At 850 nm, hemoglobin exhibits moderate absorption, while at 1070 nm, absorption is dominated by the oxygen saturation state of hemoglobin and the presence of plasma components, creating a complementary spectral signature that enhances the specificity of hemoglobin quantification.

- The hemoglobin response to near-infrared illumination is characterized by its concentration-dependent absorption profile, which varies predictably with the molar extinction coefficients of oxyhemoglobin and deoxyhemoglobin at the selected wavelengths. Simultaneously, the blood plasma component contributes to the baseline optical density, particularly at longer wavelengths where water absorption becomes more pronounced. By modeling the relative contributions of hemoglobin and plasma to the total optical attenuation, the system derives a ratio-dependent metric that correlates strongly with laboratory-measured hemoglobin concentrations, independent of individual physiological variations.

- A video camera integrated into a standard smartphone captures the transmitted light exiting the ventral surface of the finger during illumination. The camera records a continuous stream of frames at a rate sufficient to resolve the cardiac pulse (typically 30 to 60 frames per second), generating a spatiotemporal dataset that encodes the pulsatile optical response. Each frame contains thousands of pixels, each representing a localized measurement of light intensity. The temporal variation of these pixel intensities across successive frames forms the raw signal from which the PPG waveform is extracted.

- The pulsation response is isolated from the raw video data by applying a series of signal processing techniques designed to remove noise, motion artifacts, and non-pulsatile background signals. The video is first converted into a grayscale representation to simplify computational complexity, and each frame is divided into a uniform grid of spatial blocks. For each block, a time-series signal is generated by averaging the pixel intensities across all pixels within the block over time. This block-based approach enhances signal stability and reduces sensitivity to localized motion or sensor misalignment.

- The resulting time-series signals are subjected to a bandpass filter to retain only frequencies corresponding to the physiological heart rate range (typically 0.5 Hz to 4 Hz), effectively eliminating low-frequency drifts caused by respiration or thermal drift and high-frequency noise from camera sensor artifacts. The filtered signals are then sampled at the Nyquist frequency to preserve signal integrity, and segments exhibiting excessive motion or signal instability are automatically excluded. The remaining data are cropped and normalized to produce a clean, artifact-free PPG signal suitable for feature extraction.

- The PPG signal is further processed by computing the AC and DC components for each wavelength. The AC component is derived from the peak-to-peak amplitude of the pulsatile waveform, while the DC component is calculated as the mean intensity over the entire signal duration. The ratio of AC to DC at 850 nm is designated as R850, and the corresponding ratio at 1070 nm is designated as R1070. The ratio of these two metrics, R850/R1070, is computed as a primary feature for hemoglobin estimation, as it encapsulates the differential absorption behavior of hemoglobin at the two wavelengths.

- Additional features are extracted from the PPG waveform, including the time to peak systole, pulse width, pulse rise time, and the slope of the dicrotic notch. These features are normalized using z-score standardization to ensure compatibility across diverse subjects and device configurations. The normalized features, along with the R850/R1070 ratio, form a multidimensional input vector that is fed into a predictive model.

- The predictive model is implemented using Support Vector Machine Regression (SVR), a machine learning algorithm capable of modeling complex, nonlinear relationships between input features and continuous output variables. The SVR is trained on a dataset of paired measurements comprising the extracted optical features and reference hemoglobin values obtained from venous blood samples analyzed via standardized laboratory methods. The model employs a radial basis function kernel to capture the nonlinear dependencies between the optical signatures and hemoglobin concentration, optimizing the margin of error between predicted and actual values.

- The performance of the model is evaluated using the mean absolute percentage error (MAPE), defined as the average of the absolute differences between predicted and reference values, expressed as a percentage of the reference values. The correlation coefficient R is computed to quantify the linear association between the predicted and measured hemoglobin levels, with values approaching 1.0 indicating strong agreement. The Bland-Altman plot is used to visualize the bias and limits of agreement between the non-invasive method and the gold-standard laboratory test, providing a clinically meaningful assessment of measurement reliability.

- The system is implemented as a mobile application that guides the user through the measurement procedure, captures video data, processes the signal locally or via secure wireless transmission to a remote server, and displays the estimated hemoglobin level with an associated confidence interval. The device is designed to be lightweight, portable, and compatible with a wide range of smartphone models, enabling deployment in diverse clinical and community settings. The illumination module is constructed with a light-restrictive enclosure to ensure consistent optical coupling, and the entire system is calibrated using a population-based training dataset to account for variations in skin tone, age, sex, and physiological conditions.

- The predictive model is continuously updated through cloud-based data aggregation, allowing for adaptive recalibration as new data from diverse populations are collected. This iterative refinement ensures long-term accuracy and generalizability across geographic, ethnic, and socioeconomic groups. The system requires no consumables, no venipuncture, and no laboratory infrastructure, making it uniquely suited for use in low-resource environments, home monitoring, and large-scale public health screening programs.
