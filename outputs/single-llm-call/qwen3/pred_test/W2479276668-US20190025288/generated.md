# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD OF INVENTION

- relate to predicting in vivo toxicity of oligonucleotides

The present invention relates to methods for predicting the in vivo hepatotoxic potential of oligonucleotides prior to administration in animal models or humans. Specifically, the invention provides a predictive in vitro assay system that utilizes primary mammalian hepatocytes to assess the likelihood of liver injury induced by oligonucleotide therapeutics. This approach enables the early identification of compounds with unacceptable toxicity profiles, thereby facilitating the selection of safer candidates for further development. The method is particularly applicable to antisense oligonucleotides, including those with modified backbones and nucleoside analogs, which are known to accumulate in hepatic tissue and may elicit dose-dependent cytotoxic responses. By recapitulating key features of in vivo hepatotoxicity through physiologically relevant cellular models and biomarker readouts, the invention overcomes longstanding limitations in preclinical safety screening and provides a robust platform for de-risking oligonucleotide drug candidates before initiation of costly and ethically demanding in vivo studies.

## BACKGROUND

- recognize promise of antisense oligonucleotides
- describe complexity of oligonucleotide development
- highlight unpredictability of toxicity
- discuss limitations of in vitro toxicity assays
- introduce bioinformatics approaches to predict toxicity
- mention need for predictive assays

Antisense oligonucleotides represent a promising class of therapeutic agents capable of modulating gene expression through sequence-specific hybridization to target RNA transcripts, leading to degradation or functional inhibition. Despite their potential for precise molecular intervention in genetic and acquired diseases, the clinical translation of antisense oligonucleotides has been hindered by an unpredictable and often severe hepatotoxicity profile. While some compounds accumulate extensively in the liver without eliciting cellular damage, others induce significant hepatocellular injury even at lower tissue concentrations, suggesting that toxicity is not solely determined by pharmacokinetics but is instead influenced by complex molecular interactions that remain poorly understood. Conventional in vitro toxicity assays using immortalized cell lines such as HepG2 have proven inadequate for predicting in vivo outcomes, primarily due to their inability to support the natural uptake mechanisms and intracellular trafficking pathways required for oligonucleotide activity. Bioinformatics approaches that attempt to correlate sequence motifs with toxicity risk have provided limited utility, as they fail to account for structural, stereochemical, and conformational variables that critically influence biological behavior. Consequently, the field has lacked a reliable, high-throughput, and physiologically relevant in vitro system capable of accurately forecasting hepatotoxic liability prior to animal testing. There remains a critical unmet need for an assay that integrates cellular physiology, biomarker sensitivity, and species-relevant biology to enable early and accurate prioritization of oligonucleotide candidates with favorable safety profiles.

The invention provides a novel in vitro method for predicting in vivo hepatotoxicity of oligonucleotides by exposing primary mammalian hepatocytes to test compounds under conditions that mimic physiological uptake without the use of transfection agents or delivery vehicles. The method involves the administration of oligonucleotides to primary hepatocytes via gymnotic delivery, followed by culture under standardized conditions for a period sufficient to elicit cytotoxic responses, typically between 24 and 72 hours. During this time, multiple molecular biomarkers of hepatocellular stress and damage are measured in both the culture supernatant and intracellular compartments. The measured biomarker profiles are compared to reference data derived from control oligonucleotides with known in vivo toxicity profiles, enabling the classification of test compounds as either hepatotoxic or non-hepatotoxic. This correlation allows for the reliable selection of oligonucleotides with low predicted in vivo toxicity for subsequent administration in animal models or clinical settings. The invention further encompasses the use of co-cultures of hepatocytes and non-parenchymal liver cells to enhance the sensitivity and physiological relevance of the assay. The method is applicable to diverse oligonucleotide chemistries, including gapmers, LNA-modified sequences, and stereodefined phosphorothioate backbones, and can be scaled for high-throughput screening of large libraries of variants. The resulting predictive capability significantly reduces the attrition rate in oligonucleotide development by eliminating high-risk candidates early in the pipeline.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

### Prediction of In Vivo Toxicity

- predict in vivo toxicity of oligonucleotide
- assess comparative toxicity profile
- identify non-toxic oligonucleotides
- select oligonucleotides with wide safety margin
- calculate therapeutic index

The invention enables the prediction of in vivo hepatotoxicity by establishing a quantitative relationship between in vitro biomarker responses and documented in vivo liver injury. By exposing primary hepatocytes to a panel of oligonucleotides with known clinical or preclinical toxicity profiles, a reference signature of hepatotoxicity is generated based on the magnitude and kinetics of biomarker alterations. This signature is then applied to novel compounds to determine whether their in vitro behavior aligns with that of toxic or non-toxic references. Compounds exhibiting biomarker profiles similar to those of known hepatotoxic agents are classified as high risk, while those whose profiles resemble non-toxic controls are deemed suitable for progression. This comparative assessment allows for the identification of oligonucleotides with a wide safety margin, defined as a substantial separation between the concentration required for target engagement and the concentration at which cytotoxic biomarkers are activated. The therapeutic index can be calculated by comparing the EC50 for target knockdown to the IC50 for biomarker perturbation, providing a quantitative metric for prioritizing candidates with optimal efficacy-to-toxicity ratios. This predictive framework transforms the traditional trial-and-error approach to oligonucleotide development into a data-driven selection process grounded in mechanistic biology.

The predictive power of the invention is derived from the use of primary mammalian hepatocytes, which retain the metabolic, transport, and signaling functions essential for authentic oligonucleotide uptake and response. These cells are isolated from rodents or humans via retrograde collagenase perfusion of the liver, yielding viable, polarized parenchymal cells capable of gymnotic internalization. Cryopreserved primary hepatocytes from human donors are equally suitable, allowing for cross-species comparison and enhanced clinical translatability. Additionally, hepatocyte-like cells derived from induced pluripotent stem cells may be employed when primary tissue is unavailable, provided they demonstrate comparable uptake kinetics and biomarker responsiveness. The assay may be conducted in monoculture systems of hepatocytes alone or in co-culture with non-parenchymal liver cells, including Kupffer cells, stellate cells, and endothelial cells, to model the cellular crosstalk that contributes to in vivo toxicity. In co-cultures, the secretion of cytokines such as IL-1α and MIP-1α becomes a particularly sensitive indicator of immune-mediated hepatocellular stress. Intracellular biomarkers such as ATP and glutathione reflect metabolic disruption and oxidative stress, while extracellular microRNA-122 serves as a hepatocyte-specific marker of membrane integrity loss. Together, these biomarkers form a multi-dimensional fingerprint that accurately distinguishes toxic from non-toxic oligonucleotides.

Oligonucleotides are administered to primary hepatocytes via gymnotic delivery, a process wherein compounds are introduced into the culture medium without the aid of transfection reagents, lipids, or conjugates, thereby mimicking the natural uptake mechanisms observed in vivo. This method ensures that toxicity is driven by intrinsic molecular properties rather than artificial delivery artifacts. The oligonucleotides are typically added at concentrations ranging from 1 to 30 micromolar, with exposure durations of 24 to 72 hours, allowing sufficient time for cellular internalization, target engagement, and downstream cytotoxic signaling. Culture conditions are maintained at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO₂, using serum-free William’s Medium E supplemented with insulin, transferrin, and selenium to preserve hepatocyte function. During this period, the release of lactate dehydrogenase and the depletion of intracellular ATP are measured as primary indicators of cytotoxicity, and compared to reference values established from control compounds. The activation of caspase-3/7 is observed as an early apoptotic signal preceding overt membrane damage, providing a mechanistic link between oligonucleotide exposure and cell death. The advantages of gymnotic delivery include the preservation of native endocytic pathways, the avoidance of non-specific cellular stress induced by transfection agents, and the ability to detect both hybridization-dependent and hybridization-independent toxicities, making the assay uniquely suited for comprehensive safety profiling.

### Control Data/Reference Values

Control data are generated using a panel of well-characterized oligonucleotides with established in vivo hepatotoxicity profiles, including both non-toxic and hepatotoxic references. Negative controls consist of scrambled sequences or non-binding oligonucleotides that do not induce cytotoxicity in vivo, while positive controls include compounds known to cause elevated plasma transaminases, histopathological necrosis, or cytokine release in animal models. The biomarker responses elicited by these controls are quantified across multiple experiments to establish baseline ranges and statistical thresholds for toxicity classification. A consistent decrease in cellular ATP levels below 70% of vehicle-treated controls, coupled with an elevation of extracellular LDH exceeding 1.5-fold above baseline, is indicative of hepatotoxicity. Similarly, increases in microRNA-122 greater than threefold and elevations in IL-1α or MIP-1α beyond two standard deviations from negative controls are considered predictive of in vivo liver injury. These reference values are compiled into a database derived from a library of oligonucleotides spanning diverse chemistries, sequences, and stereochemistries, enabling the calibration of the assay for new compound classes and ensuring reproducibility across laboratories and cell sources.

Hepatotoxicity, as defined herein, refers to the induction of structural or functional damage to hepatocytes resulting in the release of intracellular components, metabolic dysfunction, or activation of inflammatory pathways following exposure to an oligonucleotide. The invention employs a suite of validated biomarkers to detect this damage in vitro, with high concordance to in vivo outcomes. A library of oligonucleotide variants is generated by systematically modifying the nucleobase sequence, sugar moiety, or internucleoside linkage of a parent antisense oligonucleotide, particularly those incorporating locked nucleic acid residues or phosphorothioate backbones. ### Parent Oligonucleotide

- describe non-stereodefined phosphorothioate oligonucleotide

The parent oligonucleotide is typically a non-stereodefined phosphorothioate antisense molecule, in which the phosphorothioate backbone contains a racemic mixture of Rp and Sp diastereomers at each linkage. This configuration is commonly used in early-stage drug candidates due to its synthetic accessibility and nuclease resistance. However, the invention demonstrates that such mixtures often exhibit higher hepatotoxic potential compared to their stereodefined counterparts. The invention further encompasses the use of a parent oligonucleotide in which one or more phosphorothioate internucleoside linkages are replaced with stereodefined configurations, either as a single diastereomer or as a controlled subset of defined stereoisomers. These modified parent structures serve as the foundation for generating libraries of child oligonucleotides, each differing in the position and stereochemistry of phosphorothioate linkages, thereby enabling the systematic evaluation of how stereochemical variation influences toxicity. The parent oligonucleotide with stereodefined linkages is not merely a derivative but a structural innovation that forms the basis for a new class of safer antisense therapeutics.

A library of oligonucleotide variants is created by synthesizing a series of child molecules derived from a single parent sequence, wherein each variant contains a distinct pattern of stereodefined phosphorothioate internucleoside linkages. Each member of the library retains the original nucleobase sequence and the overall gapmer architecture, including flanking wing regions composed of high-affinity nucleosides such as LNA and a central DNA gap region capable of recruiting RNase H. The library may include compounds with two to twenty independently stereodefined phosphorothioate linkages, with some variants containing all phosphorothioate linkages in a defined stereochemical state. The position and stereochemistry of each linkage are systematically varied to explore the impact of local backbone geometry on cellular uptake, protein binding, and downstream toxicity. The resulting library is screened using the hepatocyte assay to identify variants that exhibit reduced biomarker perturbation while preserving target knockdown efficacy. The retained pattern of modified and unmodified nucleosides ensures that the pharmacological activity of the parent molecule is preserved, while the stereochemical refinement selectively mitigates off-target interactions responsible for hepatotoxicity.

### Primary Hepatocytes Assay Example:

- describe cell culture conditions
- specify cell seeding and treatment protocol
- detail cell culture medium composition
- provide example cell culture parameters

Primary mouse hepatocytes are isolated via retrograde collagenase perfusion of the liver and seeded at a density of 25,000 cells per well in collagen-coated 96-well plates. Human cryopreserved hepatocytes are thawed and plated at a density of 40,000 cells per well under identical conditions. Cells are allowed to adhere for 3 to 4 hours in William’s Medium E supplemented with 10% fetal calf serum, penicillin, and streptomycin. The medium is then replaced with serum-free William’s Medium E containing insulin, transferrin, and selenium, and oligonucleotides are added at concentrations ranging from 1 to 30 micromolar in phosphate-buffered saline. Cultures are maintained at 37°C in a 5% CO₂ humidified incubator for 72 hours. Supernatants are collected at 24, 48, and 72 hours for biomarker analysis, while cells are lysed for intracellular ATP and glutathione measurements. All assays are performed in triplicate, with vehicle-treated wells serving as negative controls. The use of serum-free conditions during treatment ensures that exogenous factors do not interfere with oligonucleotide uptake or biomarker expression. This protocol has been validated across multiple donor sources and species, demonstrating robust reproducibility and high predictive accuracy for in vivo hepatotoxicity.

### Oligonucleotide

An oligonucleotide is a synthetic polymer composed of a sequence of nucleosides linked by internucleoside bonds, typically ranging from 12 to 30 nucleotides in length. These molecules are designed to hybridize with complementary RNA sequences to modulate gene expression through mechanisms such as RNase H-mediated degradation or steric blockade. Oligonucleotides may contain a variety of chemical modifications, including altered sugar moieties, non-natural nucleobases, and modified internucleoside linkages such as phosphorothioate, phosphorodiamidate, or methylphosphonate. Synthesis is performed using solid-phase phosphoramidite chemistry, followed by purification by high-performance liquid chromatography or polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis. Applications include gene silencing, exon skipping, and splice modulation, with common types including antisense oligonucleotides, small interfering RNAs, and aptamers. The invention is particularly directed toward antisense oligonucleotides with gapmer architectures and phosphorothioate backbones.

### Antisense Oligonucleotides (ASOs)

- define antisense oligonucleotides
- describe function
- specify types of ASOs
- describe applications

Antisense oligonucleotides are single-stranded nucleic acid molecules designed to bind to a complementary mRNA sequence through Watson-Crick base pairing, thereby inducing its degradation or preventing its translation. This binding can recruit endogenous enzymes such as RNase H to cleave the target RNA or sterically block ribosomal access. Antisense oligonucleotides include gapmers, mixmers, and fully modified sequences, each differing in the pattern of modified and unmodified nucleosides. Gapmers, which feature a central DNA region flanked by modified nucleosides such as LNA, are particularly effective at recruiting RNase H. Applications span the treatment of genetic disorders, cancers, viral infections, and metabolic diseases, where precise modulation of gene expression is required. The invention provides a method to predict and mitigate the hepatotoxicity associated with these agents, enabling safer clinical deployment.

### Modified Internucleoside Linkages

Modified internucleoside linkages are chemical alterations to the natural phosphodiester backbone of oligonucleotides that enhance stability, cellular uptake, or binding affinity. Phosphorothioate linkages, in which a sulfur atom replaces one of the non-bridging oxygen atoms, are the most widely used modification due to their resistance to nucleases and improved pharmacokinetics. Other modifications include phosphorodiamidate morpholino, methylphosphonate, and boranophosphate linkages, each conferring distinct biophysical properties. Neutral linkages such as alkylphosphonates or amide backbones may be incorporated to reduce protein binding and associated toxicity. Nuclease-resistant linkages are essential for maintaining oligonucleotide integrity in vivo. The invention encompasses the use of phosphorothioate linkages in both stereorandom and stereodefined configurations, with the latter providing superior toxicity profiles.

Modulation of expression refers to the alteration of gene transcription or translation through targeted interaction with RNA molecules. Inhibition of expression is achieved by antisense oligonucleotides that promote RNA degradation or block ribosomal scanning. Restoration of expression may occur through splice-switching oligonucleotides that correct aberrant splicing events. Target modulation is determined by measuring changes in mRNA or protein levels using quantitative PCR, RNA sequencing, or immunoassays. The invention does not require target modulation for toxicity prediction, as hepatotoxicity can occur independently of target engagement, but modulation is used to confirm functional activity of selected candidates.

Modified oligonucleotides encompass a broad class of synthetic nucleic acid analogs engineered to enhance stability, binding affinity, and pharmacokinetic properties. Phosphorothioate internucleoside linkages are among the most prevalent modifications, conferring resistance to nucleases and improving tissue distribution. High-affinity modified nucleosides, such as locked nucleic acid (LNA), 2'-O-methoxyethyl, and 2'-fluoro nucleosides, increase hybridization strength to complementary RNA. LNA nucleosides are characterized by a methylene bridge connecting the 2'-oxygen and 4'-carbon, forming a rigid bicyclic structure that locks the sugar in the C3'-endo conformation. Structural variants include thio-LNA, amino-LNA, and ENA, each with distinct stereochemical and electronic properties. Gapmer designs consist of a central DNA region flanked by modified wings, enabling RNase H recruitment while maintaining high target affinity. The invention applies to all such designs, including mixed-wing gapmers and gapbreaker oligonucleotides, wherein stereodefined phosphorothioate linkages are strategically incorporated to reduce toxicity without compromising efficacy.

### RNase H Activity and Recruitment

- define RNase H activity

RNase H activity refers to the endogenous enzymatic cleavage of RNA in RNA-DNA heteroduplexes, a mechanism exploited by antisense oligonucleotides to induce target degradation. The central DNA region of gapmer oligonucleotides is essential for recruiting RNase H, while the flanking modified nucleosides enhance binding affinity. The invention does not require RNase H activity for toxicity prediction, as hepatotoxicity can occur independently of target engagement, but RNase H recruitment is used to confirm functional activity of selected candidates.

### Efficacy

- motivate efficacy testing
- describe efficacy determination methods
- specify efficacy criteria
- discuss therapeutic index calculation
- provide potency retention examples

Efficacy is determined by measuring the reduction in target mRNA or protein levels following oligonucleotide treatment, using quantitative PCR, Northern blotting, or immunoblotting. Efficacy criteria include a minimum of 50% target knockdown at non-toxic concentrations. The therapeutic index is calculated as the ratio of the concentration causing 50% biomarker perturbation (IC50) to the concentration achieving 50% target knockdown (EC50). Compounds with a therapeutic index greater than 10 are prioritized for in vivo testing. Examples demonstrate that stereodefined phosphorothioate variants retain full potency while exhibiting significantly reduced cytotoxicity.

### Stereodefined Oligonucleotides

- define stereodefined oligonucleotides
- describe stereodefined phosphorothioate linkages

Stereodefined oligonucleotides are molecules in which one or more phosphorothioate internucleoside linkages are synthesized as a single diastereomer, either Rp or Sp, rather than as a racemic mixture. This stereochemical precision alters the three-dimensional conformation of the backbone, reducing non-specific protein binding and mitigating hepatotoxicity. Stereoselectivity refers to the preferential formation of one diastereomer, while stereospecificity denotes the exclusive presence of a single configuration. The invention demonstrates that stereodefined oligonucleotides exhibit improved safety profiles without loss of potency.

A panel of seven LNA gapmer oligonucleotides with known in vivo hepatotoxicity profiles was tested in primary mouse hepatocytes. Toxic oligonucleotides consistently elevated LDH and decreased ATP, while non-toxic compounds did not. miR-122 and cytokine release were elevated only in toxic conditions. The assay correctly classified all seven compounds, demonstrating 100% concordance with in vivo data.

IL-1α and MIP-1α were elevated in a time-dependent manner in co-cultures treated with toxic oligonucleotides, with MIP-1α increasing as early as 24 hours. miR-122 levels rose 4- to 10-fold after 48 hours in both monocultures and co-cultures, confirming hepatocyte-specific damage. No changes were observed with non-toxic oligonucleotides.

### Example 7

- describe miR-122 release in hepatocyte-NPC co-cultures

miR-122 release was comparable between monocultures and co-cultures, indicating that hepatocytes are the primary source of this biomarker. The presence of non-parenchymal cells did not amplify miR-122 release, supporting the conclusion that direct hepatocyte injury drives the observed toxicity.

### Example 8

- describe testing of 34 ASOs in in vitro hepatotoxicity assay

A library of 34 antisense oligonucleotides with diverse sequences and in vivo toxicity profiles was tested. LDH and ATP were measured after 72 hours. The assay correctly predicted hepatotoxicity in 32 of 34 compounds, achieving 94% concordance with in vivo ALT elevation. Two false negatives were attributed to low cellular uptake, not lack of intrinsic toxicity.

### Example 9

- outline cytotoxicity readouts
- describe testing of reference ASOs

Human primary hepatocytes from multiple donors were treated with the same seven reference oligonucleotides. LDH and ATP were measured after 72 hours. Five of seven compounds showed consistent toxicity patterns across species, with toxic oligonucleotides inducing elevated LDH and reduced ATP. Two compounds showed reduced sensitivity in human cells, possibly due to differences in uptake kinetics.
