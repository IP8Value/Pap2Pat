# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

- introduce random number generation  
Random number generation is a foundational process in modern computing systems, underpinning critical applications such as cryptographic key generation, secure communications, Monte Carlo simulations, statistical sampling, and randomized algorithms. The integrity of these systems depends fundamentally on the unpredictability and statistical uniformity of the numbers produced. In cryptographic contexts, for instance, the security of encryption protocols is directly compromised if the keys are derived from predictable or biased sequences. Traditional software-based methods, known as pseudo-random number generators (PRNGs), rely on deterministic mathematical algorithms initialized with a seed value. While efficient and reproducible, these methods cannot generate true randomness because their output is entirely determined by the initial state and internal logic, rendering them vulnerable to reverse-engineering, state prediction, and replay attacks under sufficient computational scrutiny. Consequently, the generation of cryptographically secure random numbers necessitates the extraction of entropy from physical phenomena that are inherently non-deterministic.

- motivate high-quality random numbers  
High-quality random numbers must exhibit statistical independence, uniform distribution across their domain, and resistance to any form of pattern recognition or predictive modeling, even under adversarial observation. The absence of these properties introduces systemic vulnerabilities that can be exploited to break encryption, forge digital signatures, or manipulate outcomes in financial, military, or scientific systems. The demand for such numbers has grown exponentially with the proliferation of networked devices, the expansion of quantum computing threats, and the increasing reliance on secure distributed systems. True random number generators (TRNGs) address these concerns by harvesting entropy from physical processes governed by quantum mechanics, where outcomes are fundamentally probabilistic and not governed by classical causality. Among these, photon-based processes offer particular advantages due to their isolation from classical environmental influences and their intrinsic connection to the probabilistic nature of quantum electrodynamics.

- describe limitations of deterministic algorithms  
Deterministic algorithms, despite their computational efficiency and ease of implementation, suffer from an inherent limitation: they cannot produce entropy. Their output is a function of a finite internal state, and given knowledge of the algorithm and its initial seed, every future output can be perfectly predicted. This renders them unsuitable for applications requiring long-term security or resistance to sophisticated adversaries. Furthermore, PRNGs are susceptible to state recovery attacks, where an observer who captures a sufficiently long sequence of outputs can reconstruct the internal state and thereby predict all prior and subsequent values. Even when seeded with external entropy, the deterministic transformation process itself introduces structural biases and periodicities that may be amplified over time or under specific operating conditions. These shortcomings make deterministic algorithms inadequate as standalone sources of cryptographic randomness, necessitating the integration of physical entropy sources.

- describe photon-emission-based RNGs  
Photon-emission-based random number generators exploit the quantum mechanical unpredictability of photon arrival times or counts in a controlled optical environment. When a weak light source emits photons at a rate low enough that the probability of multiple photons arriving within a single detection window is negligible, the detection events become statistically independent Bernoulli trials governed by Poisson statistics. By measuring the presence or absence of photons within discrete time intervals, binary outcomes can be extracted. These outcomes, when properly conditioned and post-processed, yield a stream of bits whose entropy is derived directly from the quantum vacuum fluctuations and the probabilistic nature of photon emission. Devices such as single-photon avalanche diodes (SPADs) and conventional CMOS image sensors have been employed to detect these photons and convert them into digital random bits. The physical origin of randomness in these systems—photon statistics—ensures that the output is not reproducible, even with identical initial conditions.

- summarize limitations of previous RNG technologies  
Despite their promise, prior photon-based RNG technologies face significant practical limitations. SPAD-based systems, while capable of single-photon detection, require high-voltage biasing beyond standard CMOS supply levels, leading to increased power consumption, thermal instability, and fabrication complexity. They also suffer from after-pulsing effects, where prior detection events artificially trigger subsequent false counts, introducing temporal correlations that degrade entropy quality. Conventional CMOS image sensors, though compatible with mass production and low-power operation, lack the sensitivity to resolve individual photons due to high read noise and low conversion gain, resulting in blurred photon-counting distributions and reduced per-bit entropy. As a consequence, these systems require aggressive post-processing and compression, which drastically reduce their output data rates. Moreover, both technologies are susceptible to environmental noise sources such as dark current, 1/f noise, and temperature drift, which introduce non-quantum entropy that compromises the purity and stability of the generated randomness. These limitations hinder scalability, reliability, and integration into consumer-grade electronic systems.

## SUMMARY OF SOME EMBODIMENTS

- introduce QRNG using Quanta Image Sensor (QIS)  
A quantum random number generator is disclosed that leverages the unique photon-counting capabilities of a Quanta Image Sensor to produce high-entropy, high-throughput random bitstreams derived from the quantum nature of photon emission. Unlike prior technologies, this system operates at standard CMOS voltages, achieves sub-electron read noise, and enables pixel-level binary decision-making without requiring external high-voltage circuitry or complex cryogenic cooling. The sensor captures discrete photon arrivals with high fidelity, converting each pixel’s response into a deterministic binary outcome based on a calibrated threshold, thereby transforming quantum uncertainty into a stream of statistically independent random bits.

- describe QIS configuration  
The Quanta Image Sensor comprises a two-dimensional array of microscopic photosensitive elements known as jots, each capable of resolving individual photoelectrons with sub-electron read noise. These jots are fabricated using standard CMOS processes and are arranged in a dense grid with pitch dimensions on the order of sub-microns, enabling high spatial resolution and scalability to gigapixel arrays. Each jot includes a photodiode, a charge-storage node, and a dedicated readout transistor configured to amplify and digitize the charge generated by incident photons. The sensor operates in a frame-based mode, where each exposure interval captures photon arrivals and outputs a digital representation of the accumulated photoelectrons per jot.

- describe single-photon counting  
The sensor is operated under conditions where the average number of photons per jot per frame is maintained below unity, ensuring that the probability of multiple photon arrivals within a single exposure is negligible. Under such conditions, the output of each jot reflects either zero or one photoelectron, with minor deviations due to read noise. The quantized nature of the photoelectron signal, combined with ultra-low read noise, enables the sensor to distinguish between empty and occupied quantum states with high confidence, thereby establishing a direct link between quantum events and binary output states.

- describe comparison circuitry  
Within each pixel or in a shared per-column circuit, a comparison circuit is implemented to evaluate the analog output voltage of the jot against a predefined threshold voltage. This threshold is selected to lie between the expected voltage levels corresponding to zero and one photoelectron, typically near the valley of the combined Poisson-Gaussian probability distribution. The comparator generates a binary output indicating whether the measured signal exceeds the threshold, effectively converting the continuous analog signal into a discrete bit representing the presence or absence of a quantum event.

- describe binary output data generation  
The binary outputs from all jots in the sensor array are collected simultaneously at the end of each exposure frame and transmitted through a parallel readout architecture to an output buffer. Each jot contributes one bit per frame, resulting in a high-bandwidth stream of random bits proportional to the total number of jots. The timing of frame acquisition is synchronized to ensure temporal independence between consecutive bits, and the output is structured to maintain bit-level uniformity and statistical integrity.

- describe optional photon source  
An optional coherent or incoherent photon source may be coupled to the sensor to provide a controlled and stable flux of photons. This source may be implemented using light-emitting diodes, laser diodes, or filtered ambient light, and is configured to emit photons at a rate such that the average quanta exposure per jot per frame is approximately 0.7 photoelectrons, a value empirically determined to maximize entropy while minimizing sensitivity to intensity fluctuations.

- describe optional optical conditioner  
An optional optical conditioner may be interposed between the photon source and the sensor to homogenize the illumination profile, suppress stray light, filter out non-target wavelengths, and attenuate intensity variations. This conditioner may include diffusers, bandpass filters, apertures, or micro-lens arrays to ensure uniform photon flux across the entire sensor array, thereby reducing spatial non-uniformities that could introduce bias into the output stream.

- describe optional randomness extractor  
An optional randomness extractor may be coupled to the binary output stream to compress and purify the raw bits into a final sequence of uniformly distributed random numbers. This extractor employs a cryptographic hash function, such as a Universal-2 family function, to map a larger number of lower-entropy input bits into a smaller number of higher-entropy output bits, ensuring that the final output satisfies the statistical requirements of cryptographic randomness.

- describe control circuitry  
Control circuitry is integrated to regulate the timing of exposure intervals, adjust the threshold voltage dynamically, modulate the intensity of the photon source, and coordinate the operation of the optical conditioner. This circuitry includes digital state machines, analog-to-digital converters for feedback, and memory registers for storing calibration parameters, enabling autonomous adaptation to environmental changes and long-term stability.

- describe threshold level adjustment  
The threshold level is programmatically adjusted based on real-time measurements of the sensor’s output distribution. By monitoring the cumulative probability of binary outcomes, the system identifies the optimal threshold position corresponding to the valley between quantized peaks in the photon-counting histogram, ensuring maximal entropy and minimal sensitivity to drift in photon flux or read noise.

- describe time interval adjustment  
The duration of each exposure frame is dynamically adjusted to maintain a consistent quanta exposure value despite variations in photon source intensity or sensor sensitivity. This adjustment is performed using feedback from the histogram of pixel outputs, ensuring that the average number of photoelectrons per jot remains within a narrow target range.

- describe photon source emission intensity adjustment  
The intensity of the photon source is modulated via a feedback loop that monitors the average output of the sensor array. If the measured quanta exposure deviates from the target value, the drive current to the light source is adjusted proportionally to restore equilibrium, thereby maintaining a stable entropy source independent of external illumination changes.

- describe optical conditioner adjustment  
The optical conditioner may include movable elements such as variable apertures or tunable filters whose transmission characteristics are adjusted in response to environmental conditions or sensor calibration data. These adjustments ensure that the spectral composition and spatial uniformity of the incident light remain optimal for entropy generation.

- describe randomness monitoring  
The system continuously monitors the statistical properties of the output stream using online entropy estimators and statistical test suites. Deviations from expected distributions trigger alerts or automatic recalibration procedures, ensuring that the output remains cryptographically secure at all times.

- describe in-pixel conversion gain  
Each jot is designed with a high in-pixel conversion gain, enabling the amplification of single-electron signals to measurable voltage levels without requiring external amplifiers. This gain is achieved through specialized charge-transfer architectures that minimize parasitic capacitance and maximize signal-to-noise ratio.

- describe read noise  
The read noise of the sensor is maintained below 0.25 electron root-mean-square, enabling clear discrimination between zero and one photoelectron states. This low noise is achieved through optimized transistor design, correlated double sampling, and low-noise amplifier topologies integrated directly within each pixel.

- define terms and phrases  
For the purposes of this disclosure, “quanta exposure” refers to the average number of photoelectrons collected per jot per exposure frame. “Read noise” refers to the root-mean-square deviation of the sensor’s output in the absence of incident photons. “Binary output” refers to a digital bit generated by comparing the sensor’s analog signal to a threshold. “Jot” refers to the smallest photosensitive unit in the Quanta Image Sensor. “Randomness extractor” refers to a cryptographic function that compresses a low-entropy bitstream into a high-entropy one. “Threshold” refers to the voltage level used to discriminate between binary states in the sensor output. “Stability” refers to the resilience of entropy output against fluctuations in environmental parameters such as temperature, light intensity, or supply voltage.
