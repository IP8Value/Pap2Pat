## TECHNICAL FIELD

The present invention relates to the field of noncontact physiological monitoring, specifically to systems and methods for detecting and analyzing cardiac rhythm using commodity smart speaker devices equipped with microphone arrays and audio transducers. More particularly, the invention pertains to the use of active sonar principles to detect minute chest wall motions induced by cardiac activity, and to extract clinically relevant heart rate and R–R interval metrics without requiring physical contact with the subject. The invention further encompasses signal processing techniques that enable the separation of cardiac motion signals from dominant respiratory artifacts and ambient noise using adaptive beamforming and self-supervised learning, thereby enabling accurate rhythm assessment in both regular and irregular cardiac conditions. This technology is particularly suited for integration into consumer-grade smart home devices, hospital monitoring environments, and telehealth platforms where continuous, unobtrusive, and privacy-preserving cardiac surveillance is desired.

## BACKGROUND

Accurate assessment of cardiac rhythm is essential for diagnosing arrhythmias, evaluating autonomic function, and monitoring patients with known cardiovascular disease. Conventional methods rely on electrocardiography, which provides direct measurement of electrical depolarization events but requires skin contact, electrode placement, and specialized equipment that limits usability in home, mobile, or infection-prone settings. While frequency domain analysis has been employed to estimate average heart rate from contactless signals such as photoplethysmography or Doppler radar, these approaches are fundamentally limited by their assumption of periodicity in the underlying rhythm. In pathological conditions such as atrial fibrillation, ventricular ectopy, or respiratory sinus arrhythmia, the R–R intervals vary significantly from beat to beat, causing spectral energy to spread across a broad bandwidth and rendering traditional spectral peak detection ineffective. Furthermore, optical methods introduce privacy concerns due to the necessity of visual surveillance, while radar-based systems require specialized hardware not commonly integrated into consumer electronics. The widespread deployment of smart speakers—devices equipped with multi-microphone arrays and high-fidelity audio transducers—presents a unique opportunity to leverage existing infrastructure for physiological sensing. However, prior attempts to extract cardiac signals from such platforms have failed to reliably distinguish the sub-millimeter chest wall displacements caused by individual heartbeats from the substantially larger and non-sinusoidal motions induced by respiration, particularly when operating under real-world constraints of limited bandwidth, low sampling rates, and environmental noise.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention provides a comprehensive system and method for contactless cardiac rhythm monitoring using commodity smart speakers as active sonar transceivers. The system operates by emitting inaudible frequency-modulated continuous wave signals in the 18–22 kHz range, which reflect off the human torso and are captured by a multi-element microphone array. These reflections contain minute displacements of the chest wall corresponding to the apical impulse of the heart and arterial pulsations, which are orders of magnitude smaller than the displacement caused by respiration. The invention introduces a novel signal processing pipeline that first filters out audible frequencies and computes the impulse response of the acoustic channel to isolate reflections originating from within one meter of the device. Echoes from distant objects are suppressed using a raised-cosine window applied in the time domain to eliminate spectral leakage while preserving temporal resolution. The resulting impulse responses are then processed using an adaptive maximum signal-to-interference-and-noise-ratio beamformer that combines signals across multiple microphones and frequency bands to enhance cardiac motion while suppressing respiratory artifacts and ambient noise. This beamformer is trained using a self-supervised optimization framework that does not require prior ground truth data or subject-specific calibration. The optimization function maximizes the energy of the cardiac signal within the 60–150 beats-per-minute frequency range while penalizing energy in respiratory bands and enforcing temporal stability of the cardiac envelope. To avoid convergence to local maxima caused by transient noise or abrupt motion, the algorithm employs probabilistic weight updates and a regularization term that constrains the maximum amplitude of the extracted cardiac signal. Following beamforming, the complex-valued cardiac waveform is subjected to a high-pass filter to preserve high-frequency components critical for temporal precision. The segmented heartbeats are then identified using a noniterative, rotation-invariant segmentation algorithm that compares adjacent beat segments after normalizing their durations and applying a complex-valued rotation transformation to account for residual respiratory modulation between the in-phase and quadrature components of the signal. The midpoint of each identified segment is used to compute R–R intervals and heart rate, with the system capable of distinguishing between regular sinus rhythms and irregular patterns such as atrial fibrillation. The system is designed to operate with the subject seated or lying still within one meter of the smart speaker, with no requirement for wearable sensors or user interaction beyond passive presence. The entire process is implemented using embedded signal processing units capable of running on standard smart speaker hardware architectures, with beamforming weights computed in real time over 30-second training intervals. The output is transmitted via a communication interface to a user interface that displays the extracted waveform, heart rate, R–R interval variability, and alerts for detected arrhythmias. The system is further configured to pause monitoring upon detection of significant motion and resume upon return to a stable posture, ensuring data integrity. The invention enables continuous, unobtrusive, and privacy-preserving cardiac surveillance without the need for cameras, skin contact, or external sensors.

## IMPLEMENTED EXAMPLES

A clinical validation study was conducted involving 26 healthy adult participants and 24 hospitalized cardiac patients recruited from the acute care cardiology unit of a tertiary medical center. Healthy participants had no history of cardiac disease, with a median age of 31 years and a body mass index of 22. Cardiac patients were stratified into regular rhythm (n=18) and irregular rhythm (n=6) cohorts based on telemetry review, with the latter group consisting entirely of patients with documented atrial fibrillation or variable atrial flutter. Ground truth data were acquired using FDA-cleared sensors, including Polar H10 ECG chest bands and CorSense PPG fingertip monitors, with R–R intervals synchronized to the smart speaker recordings through manual alignment of the first detected heartbeat. Participants underwent multiple 60-second measurement sessions under varying conditions, including different distances (40–60 cm), speaker orientations (frontal, angled, elevated), background noise (75 dB jazz music), and post-exercise states. For hospitalized patients, ambient noise was minimized, and family members were required to remain at least two meters away. The system achieved an intraclass correlation coefficient of 0.983 and a concordance correlation coefficient of 0.983 for heart rate estimation across healthy participants, with a median absolute error of 1 beat per minute and a 90th percentile error below 4 beats per minute. For R–R intervals, the intraclass correlation coefficient was 0.929 and the concordance correlation coefficient was 0.927, with a median absolute error of 28 milliseconds and a 90th percentile error of 75 milliseconds. In the cardiac patient cohort, the system maintained robust performance with a median absolute error of 2 beats per minute for heart rate and 30 milliseconds for R–R intervals, achieving intraclass and concordance correlation coefficients of 0.901 and 0.898, respectively. For patients with atrial fibrillation, the mean absolute R–R interval error was 35 milliseconds, with an intraclass correlation coefficient of 0.891, demonstrating that the system accurately captured beat-to-beat variability without misclassifying rhythm irregularity. Performance remained consistent across diverse clothing materials, ambient conditions, and participant demographics, including gender and body mass index. In five extreme obese patients with BMI exceeding 35, the system successfully extracted cardiac signals in only one case, consistent with known limitations of motion-based sensing in severe adiposity. The system demonstrated no false positives for arrhythmia detection in healthy subjects and correctly identified all cases of atrial fibrillation in the clinical cohort. The results confirm that the invention provides clinically viable, noncontact cardiac rhythm monitoring with accuracy comparable to established contact-based methods, while enabling deployment in home, hospital, and telehealth environments without compromising privacy or requiring user compliance beyond passive positioning.
