# DESCRIPTION

- introduce invention

The present invention relates to a novel catalytic process for the selective upgrading of Fischer-Tropsch wax fractions into high-quality middle distillates with enhanced cold flow properties and minimal yield loss due to overcracking. This process employs a uniquely structured hydrocracking and hydroisomerisation catalyst that combines a precisely engineered IZM-2-type crystalline solid as the acidic support with a balanced hydro-dehydrogenating function composed of noble and non-noble metals. Unlike conventional systems that rely on amorphous silica-alumina or standard zeolites such as USY or ZSM-5, the catalyst of this invention exhibits superior selectivity for primary cracking and skeletal isomerisation under mild operating conditions, enabling unprecedented yields of diesel-range hydrocarbons while suppressing methane and light gas formation. The invention further enables the integration of oxygenate-containing Fischer-Tropsch feedstocks without requiring prior extensive hydrotreatment, as the catalyst composition inherently tolerates and mitigates the deactivating effects of water and alcohols through a controlled acid site density and optimized metal-acid site proximity. The resulting process achieves a middle distillate yield exceeding 75% on a weight basis from heavy paraffinic feedstocks, surpassing the theoretical maximum achievable by Fischer-Tropsch synthesis alone and outperforming all known industrial hydrocracking systems in both efficiency and product quality.

## PRIOR ART

- describe Fischer-Tropsch process

The Fischer-Tropsch process is a well-established catalytic method for converting synthesis gas, derived from coal, natural gas, or biomass, into liquid hydrocarbons. Under controlled conditions using iron or cobalt-based catalysts, carbon monoxide and hydrogen react to form a broad distribution of linear aliphatic chains, predominantly n-paraffins, following the Anderson–Flory–Schulz polymerization kinetics. The resulting product slate spans from methane to heavy waxes with carbon numbers exceeding C₈₀, with the middle distillate fraction (C₁₀–C₂₀) typically accounting for no more than 40 weight percent of the total output under ideal conditions. While these hydrocarbons are inherently free of sulfur, nitrogen, and aromatics—making them environmentally superior to petroleum-derived fuels—their high linear paraffin content results in poor cold flow properties, limiting direct utility as diesel or jet fuel without further processing.

- limitations of Fischer-Tropsch products

The primary limitation of Fischer-Tropsch products lies in their narrow carbon number distribution and overwhelming predominance of linear paraffins, which crystallize at elevated temperatures, leading to high pour points and poor low-temperature fluidity. Direct use of the raw wax fraction in internal combustion engines is impractical without upgrading. Conventional refining methods such as solvent dewaxing are energy-intensive and result in significant yield losses. Catalytic hydrocracking, while effective, often suffers from secondary cracking when catalysts possess excessive acidity or poor metal-acid balance, leading to overproduction of light gases such as methane and ethane. Furthermore, the presence of trace oxygenates—alcohols, ketones, and carboxylic acids—commonly co-produced in low-temperature Fischer-Tropsch synthesis, can adsorb onto acidic sites, disrupt the hydro-dehydrogenation function, and shift the catalyst toward non-ideal behavior, thereby reducing middle distillate selectivity and accelerating deactivation.

- describe feedstocks from renewable sources

Renewable feedstocks for Fischer-Tropsch synthesis include biomass-derived syngas, waste-derived gases, and captured carbon dioxide combined with green hydrogen. These sources offer a pathway to carbon-neutral liquid fuels, but their chemical composition is often more complex than fossil-derived syngas, containing higher levels of impurities such as oxygenates, tars, and particulates. The variability in feedstock composition necessitates robust and adaptable upgrading technologies. Conventional hydrocracking catalysts, designed for clean, synthetic feedstocks, frequently fail under these conditions due to pore blockage, active site poisoning, or imbalance in the bifunctional mechanism. Therefore, a catalyst system capable of maintaining high selectivity and stability in the presence of variable and impure feedstocks is essential for the economic viability of renewable fuel production.

- describe feedstocks from oils and fats

Oils and fats, particularly those derived from vegetable or animal sources, can be converted into hydrocarbons via hydrotreated esters and fatty acids (HEFA) pathways, producing paraffinic streams similar in composition to Fischer-Tropsch waxes. These feedstocks are rich in even-numbered long-chain n-paraffins and contain residual oxygen functionalities that must be removed prior to upgrading. Although HEFA-derived streams are already low in sulfur and nitrogen, their oxygen content can interfere with catalyst function, particularly when processed using conventional silica-alumina-supported systems. The presence of water from dehydration reactions and carboxylic acid residues can lead to accelerated catalyst deactivation and reduced isomerisation selectivity, limiting the achievable yield of high-quality middle distillates.

- describe feedstocks from coal

Coal-derived Fischer-Tropsch wax remains a significant feedstock in regions with abundant coal reserves and limited access to crude oil. The wax fraction from coal-based synthesis is typically heavier and more linear than that from natural gas, with carbon numbers extending beyond C₄₀. Upgrading this material requires severe hydrocracking conditions, which, when applied to conventional catalysts, result in excessive cracking, low middle distillate yields, and rapid coke formation. The absence of natural inhibitors such as nitrogen compounds in coal-derived syngas allows for the use of noble metal catalysts, but these are economically prohibitive at scale. Base metal catalysts, while cost-effective, often exhibit uncontrolled hydrogenolysis, leading to high methane yields and poor product distribution.

- describe hydroisomerisation catalytic reactions

Hydroisomerisation is a catalytic process that rearranges the carbon skeleton of linear paraffins into branched isomers without significant carbon chain shortening. This reaction is critical for improving the cold flow properties of diesel and jet fuels. The process occurs on bifunctional catalysts comprising a hydrogenation/dehydrogenation site and a Brønsted acid site. The n-paraffin is first dehydrogenated to an olefin, which migrates to an acidic site where it is protonated to form a carbocation. This intermediate undergoes skeletal rearrangement via alkyl shifts or protonated cyclopropane intermediates to form branched isomers, which are then hydrogenated back to paraffins. The selectivity of this process is governed by the catalyst’s ability to promote isomerisation over cracking, which depends critically on the strength, density, and spatial distribution of acid sites, as well as the diffusion constraints imposed by the catalyst’s pore structure.

- describe bifunctional catalysts

Bifunctional catalysts are essential for both hydrocracking and hydroisomerisation, as they enable the sequential dehydrogenation, isomerisation/cracking, and hydrogenation steps to occur in close proximity. The hydrogenation/dehydrogenation function is typically provided by noble metals such as platinum or palladium, or non-noble transition metals such as nickel, cobalt, molybdenum, or tungsten, often in sulfided form. The acidic function is derived from solid acids such as zeolites, amorphous silica-alumina, or modified zirconias. The efficiency of the catalyst hinges on the balance between these two functions: an excess of hydrogenation activity suppresses cracking but limits conversion, while an excess of acidity promotes overcracking and coking. Achieving this balance is particularly challenging when processing complex or impure feedstocks.

- describe acid function of catalysts

The acid function of a hydrocracking or hydroisomerisation catalyst determines the rate and pathway of carbocation rearrangements. Strong Brønsted acid sites favor rapid cracking and secondary reactions, leading to light gas formation and reduced middle distillate yield. Weak to moderate acid sites, on the other hand, allow for selective isomerisation and primary cracking, which are desirable for maximizing diesel-range products. The distribution of acid sites—whether concentrated in narrow pores or dispersed across a mesoporous matrix—also influences molecular diffusion and residence time, thereby affecting selectivity. In conventional systems, amorphous silica-alumina offers moderate acidity and good tolerance to impurities but lacks shape selectivity, whereas zeolites provide shape selectivity but often suffer from excessive confinement and rapid deactivation.

- describe hydrogenating function of catalysts

The hydrogenating function facilitates the reversible dehydrogenation of paraffins to olefins and the subsequent hydrogenation of cracked or isomerised olefinic intermediates back to saturated hydrocarbons. Noble metals such as platinum and palladium offer high activity and stability under low-sulfur conditions, making them ideal for Fischer-Tropsch upgrading. Non-noble metals such as nickel and cobalt are less expensive but prone to hydrogenolysis, leading to excessive methane formation. The activity of the hydrogenating function must be finely tuned to match the acid function; too little hydrogenation results in olefin accumulation and coking, while too much suppresses carbocation formation and reduces conversion rates. The spatial distribution of metal particles relative to acid sites is critical to satisfy the Weisz intimacy criterion, ensuring rapid transfer of intermediates between functions.

- describe balance between acid and hydrogenating functions

The optimal performance of a bifunctional catalyst requires a precise equilibrium between the hydrogenating and acidic functions. This balance determines whether the catalyst operates in an “ideal” mode, where the rate-limiting step occurs on the acid site and secondary cracking is minimized, or in a “non-ideal” mode, where hydrogenation or diffusion limitations dominate. In ideal operation, primary cracking predominates, yielding a symmetrical distribution of middle distillates centered around half the carbon number of the feedstock. Deviations from this balance—due to metal sintering, acid site leaching, or impurity adsorption—result in skewed product distributions, increased light gas yield, and accelerated deactivation. Maintaining this balance is particularly difficult with oxygenate-containing feedstocks, as water and alcohols preferentially adsorb on acid sites, altering the effective acid strength and disrupting the reaction sequence.

- describe conventional catalysts

Conventional hydrocracking catalysts typically consist of a zeolite or amorphous silica-alumina support impregnated with Group VIII noble metals or Group VIB sulfides. Pt/USY and CoMo/Al₂O₃ are widely used in industrial settings, but both suffer from significant drawbacks when applied to Fischer-Tropsch waxes. Pt/USY exhibits high activity but suffers from excessive secondary cracking due to its large pore structure and high acid site density, leading to low middle distillate selectivity. CoMo/Al₂O₃, while more selective, requires continuous sulfiding to maintain activity and is ineffective in low-sulfur environments, necessitating costly sulfur additives. Neither system effectively tolerates oxygenates, and both exhibit poor stability over extended operation with real-world feedstocks.

- describe drawbacks of silica-alumina-based catalytic systems

Silica-alumina-based catalysts are valued for their moderate acidity and tolerance to impurities, but they lack the shape selectivity necessary to control isomerisation pathways. Their amorphous structure provides a broad distribution of acid site strengths and pore sizes, resulting in unselective cracking and poor control over branching position. This leads to a wide distribution of isomers, many of which are prone to further cracking, reducing the yield of desirable middle distillates. Additionally, silica-alumina supports are susceptible to hydrothermal degradation under prolonged operation, leading to loss of surface area and mechanical integrity. Their inability to suppress hydrogenolysis also results in higher methane yields compared to zeolite-based systems.

- describe zeolite-based catalytic systems

Zeolite-based catalysts offer superior shape selectivity due to their well-defined microporous structures, enabling control over molecular access and transition-state geometry. However, their narrow pores often restrict the diffusion of long-chain paraffins, leading to pore blocking and rapid deactivation. Large-pore zeolites like USY promote excessive multibranched isomerisation followed by cracking, while medium-pore zeolites like ZSM-22 and ZSM-23 favor terminal branching but exhibit low activity for long-chain molecules. Furthermore, the synthesis of zeolites with precise aluminum distribution and crystal morphology remains challenging, resulting in batch-to-batch variability that undermines reproducibility and scalability. Many zeolites also suffer from insufficient thermal stability under hydrocracking conditions, limiting their industrial applicability.

## Subject-Matter of the Invention

- introduce process for producing middle distillates

The invention introduces a novel catalytic process for the production of middle distillates from heavy Fischer-Tropsch wax fractions through a single-stage hydrocracking and hydroisomerisation reaction. This process employs a unique catalyst comprising an IZM-2-type crystalline solid as the acidic support, impregnated with a precisely controlled combination of Group VIII and Group VIB metals. The process operates under mild conditions of temperature and pressure, enabling high conversion of C₂₀₊ paraffins into branched middle distillates with minimal cracking to light gases. The catalyst’s structure promotes primary cracking and selective skeletal isomerisation, achieving middle distillate yields exceeding 79% by weight, while suppressing methane formation to less than 1.5% of total products. The process is uniquely capable of processing oxygenate-containing feedstocks without prior extensive hydrotreatment, leveraging the catalyst’s inherent tolerance to water and alcohols to maintain activity and selectivity.

- describe catalyst used in process

The catalyst employed in this process is a bifunctional hydrocracking and hydroisomerisation catalyst consisting of an IZM-2-type crystalline solid as the primary acidic support, combined with a hydro-dehydrogenating function provided by a combination of platinum and molybdenum. The IZM-2 solid possesses a unique crystalline structure characterized by a specific X-ray diffraction pattern with distinct dhkl values and a defined chemical composition that optimizes acid site density and pore geometry for long-chain paraffin conversion. The catalyst is prepared through a controlled synthesis and impregnation sequence that ensures intimate contact between the metal function and the acidic sites, satisfying the Weisz intimacy criterion. The resulting catalyst exhibits exceptional stability, high selectivity for middle distillates, and resistance to deactivation by oxygenates and water, making it uniquely suited for upgrading Fischer-Tropsch waxes derived from renewable, coal, or oil-based feedstocks.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

- define hydrocracking/hydroisomerisation catalyst

The hydrocracking and hydroisomerisation catalyst of the invention is a bifunctional solid material designed to simultaneously catalyze the cleavage of carbon-carbon bonds in long-chain n-paraffins and the rearrangement of their carbon skeletons into branched isomers. It comprises a crystalline IZM-2-type solid as the acidic component and a hydro-dehydrogenating metal function supported on its surface. The catalyst operates under conditions where the rate-limiting step occurs on the acidic sites, ensuring ideal hydrocracking behavior characterized by primary cracking and minimal secondary reactions. The IZM-2 solid provides a controlled distribution of Brønsted acid sites with moderate strength, preventing overcracking while enabling efficient carbocation rearrangement. The metal function facilitates rapid dehydrogenation and hydrogenation, ensuring the continuous regeneration of olefinic intermediates and the saturation of cracked products.

- specify operating conditions

The process is conducted at temperatures ranging from 280°C to 360°C, pressures between 3.0 MPa and 8.0 MPa, and hydrogen-to-hydrocarbon molar ratios from 8:1 to 20:1. Liquid hourly space velocities are maintained between 0.5 and 2.0 h⁻¹, depending on feedstock composition. Under these conditions, conversions of heavy paraffins (C₂₀₊) exceed 90%, with middle distillate yields reaching 75–82% by weight. The process is operated in a fixed-bed trickle-flow reactor, with the catalyst bed maintained in a fully wetted state to ensure optimal contact between the liquid-phase feedstock and the catalyst surface. The mild operating conditions reduce energy consumption and minimize catalyst coking, extending operational cycles and reducing regeneration frequency.

- provide chemical composition formula

The IZM-2-type crystalline solid has a chemical composition expressed by the empirical formula:  
XO₂ · Y₂O₃ · M₂/nO · R · H₂O  
where X is selected from silicon and aluminum, Y is selected from aluminum and gallium, M is selected from sodium, potassium, lithium, and tetraalkylammonium cations, R represents organic structure-directing agents, and H₂O denotes water of crystallization. The molar ratio of XO₂ to Y₂O₃ ranges from 10:1 to 50:1, and the M/Y atomic ratio is maintained between 0.01 and 0.15. The crystalline structure is stabilized through calcination and ammonium exchange, yielding an acid form with a controlled density of Brønsted acid sites.

### The Hydrocracking and Hydroisomerisation Catalyst

- define hydrocracking and hydroisomerisation catalyst

The hydrocracking and hydroisomerisation catalyst is a composite material comprising a crystalline IZM-2 solid as the acidic support and a hydro-dehydrogenating metal phase deposited on its external and internal surfaces. The catalyst is engineered to facilitate the sequential dehydrogenation of n-paraffins to olefins, protonation to carbocations, skeletal rearrangement via alkyl shifts or protonated cyclopropane intermediates, β-scission for chain cleavage, and subsequent hydrogenation to saturated branched hydrocarbons. The IZM-2 solid provides a uniform distribution of moderate-strength Brønsted acid sites that favor primary cracking and terminal branching, while the metal function ensures rapid hydrogen transfer, preventing olefin accumulation and coke formation. The catalyst’s architecture enables the reaction to proceed under ideal bifunctional conditions, where the acid site is rate-limiting and secondary cracking is suppressed.

- describe composition of catalyst

The catalyst composition consists of 5 to 40 weight percent of the IZM-2 crystalline solid dispersed within a matrix of alumina or silica-alumina binder, with the remainder comprising hydro-dehydrogenating metals. The IZM-2 solid is the primary source of acidity and shape selectivity, while the binder provides mechanical integrity and porosity for mass transport. The metal function is introduced via impregnation and calcination, ensuring uniform dispersion and strong interaction with the IZM-2 surface. The final catalyst contains no extraneous additives such as chlorides or fluorides, and is free of phosphorus or boron promoters that could alter acid site distribution.

- specify hydro-dehydrogenating metal content

The hydro-dehydrogenating metal content is maintained between 0.1 and 2.0 weight percent, with a preferred range of 0.3 to 1.2 weight percent. This range ensures sufficient hydrogenation activity to sustain the bifunctional cycle without inducing excessive hydrogenolysis. The metal loading is optimized to achieve a metal-to-acid-site ratio that maximizes primary cracking yield while minimizing methane formation.

- list group VIII metals

Group VIII metals include platinum, palladium, rhodium, iridium, osmium, nickel, cobalt, and iron. Among these, platinum and nickel are preferred for their high hydrogenation activity and compatibility with the IZM-2 support.

- list group VIB metals

Group VIB metals include molybdenum, tungsten, and chromium. Molybdenum is the preferred metal due to its synergistic interaction with platinum, enhancing sulfur tolerance and stabilizing the metal dispersion under reaction conditions.

- describe preferred metal combinations

The preferred metal combination is platinum and molybdenum, with a Pt/Mo atomic ratio between 1:1 and 3:1. This combination provides high hydrogenation activity, suppresses hydrogenolysis, and enhances catalyst stability under oxygenate-containing feed conditions. The presence of molybdenum also mitigates the deactivating effects of water by forming stable surface molybdate species that protect platinum sites from sintering.

- specify metal content range

The total hydro-dehydrogenating metal content is maintained between 0.3 and 1.2 weight percent, with platinum comprising 0.1 to 0.8 weight percent and molybdenum comprising 0.2 to 0.6 weight percent. This range ensures optimal balance between hydrogenation and acid-catalyzed reactions, maximizing middle distillate yield while minimizing light gas formation.

- describe substrate composition

The substrate is composed of the IZM-2 crystalline solid dispersed in an amorphous alumina or silica-alumina binder, with a binder content ranging from 60 to 95 weight percent. The substrate is shaped into extrudates or pellets to facilitate handling and packing in fixed-bed reactors. The binder provides mechanical strength, thermal stability, and mesoporosity to enhance mass transfer of long-chain paraffins to the active IZM-2 sites.

- specify oxide-type amorphous or poorly crystallised porous inorganic binder

The binder is an oxide-type amorphous or poorly crystallized inorganic material selected from alumina, silica-alumina, or titania-alumina, with a surface area between 150 and 400 m²/g and a pore volume of 0.3 to 0.8 cm³/g. The binder is prepared from aluminum hydroxide or boehmite precursors, calcined at temperatures between 500°C and 700°C to achieve optimal porosity and thermal stability without compromising the crystallinity of the IZM-2 component.

- describe IZM-2-type crystallised solid

The IZM-2-type crystallised solid is a novel aluminosilicate zeolite with a unique framework topology characterized by one-dimensional 10-membered ring channels and a low aluminum content. Its structure is distinct from known zeolites such as ZSM-22 or ZSM-48, exhibiting a higher degree of structural order and a more uniform distribution of acid sites. The IZM-2 solid is synthesized under hydrothermal conditions using a specific organic template, resulting in crystals with controlled morphology and minimal defects.

- specify IZM-2 solid content range

The IZM-2 solid is present in the catalyst in an amount ranging from 5 to 40 weight percent, with a preferred range of 15 to 30 weight percent. This range ensures sufficient acidity for carbocation formation while maintaining adequate porosity and mechanical stability provided by the binder matrix.

- describe hydro-dehydrogenating function

The hydro-dehydrogenating function is provided by the deposition of platinum and molybdenum on the surface of the IZM-2 crystalline solid and the surrounding binder. The metals are introduced via incipient wetness impregnation using aqueous solutions of platinum chloride and ammonium molybdate, followed by drying and calcination in air. The resulting metal phase is highly dispersed and strongly anchored to the support, preventing sintering during reaction.

- list group VIII noble metals

Group VIII noble metals include platinum, palladium, rhodium, and iridium. Platinum is the preferred noble metal due to its high hydrogenation activity, resistance to oxidation, and compatibility with the IZM-2 framework.

- list group VIII non-noble metals

Group VIII non-noble metals include nickel, cobalt, and iron. Nickel is optionally used in combination with platinum to reduce cost, but its use is limited to concentrations below 0.2 weight percent to avoid excessive hydrogenolysis.

- list preferred group VIB metals

The preferred Group VIB metal is molybdenum, due to its ability to form stable surface species that enhance metal dispersion, suppress hydrogenolysis, and improve catalyst longevity under hydrothermal conditions.

- describe metal combinations

The preferred metal combination is platinum and molybdenum, with a Pt/Mo atomic ratio between 1:1 and 3:1. This combination provides synergistic effects: platinum ensures rapid dehydrogenation and hydrogenation, while molybdenum stabilizes platinum against sintering and reduces the formation of methane through hydrogenolysis. The presence of molybdenum also enhances resistance to water-induced deactivation.

- specify metal content range

The total metal content is maintained between 0.3 and 1.2 weight percent, with platinum at 0.1 to 0.8 weight percent and molybdenum at 0.2 to 0.6 weight percent. This range ensures optimal bifunctional balance, maximizing middle distillate yield while minimizing light gas formation.

- describe crystallised IZM-2 solid

The crystallised IZM-2 solid is a highly ordered aluminosilicate with a unique framework structure defined by a specific X-ray diffraction pattern and chemical composition. It exhibits a high degree of crystallinity, with minimal amorphous content, and possesses a uniform distribution of Brønsted acid sites with moderate strength, ideal for selective isomerisation and primary cracking.

- specify IZM-2 solid chemical composition

The IZM-2 solid has the chemical composition expressed as XO₂ · Y₂O₃ · M₂/nO · R · H₂O, where X is silicon, Y is aluminum, M is a tetraalkylammonium cation, R is an organic template, and H₂O is water of crystallization. The SiO₂/Al₂O₃ molar ratio is between 10:1 and 50:1, and the M/Al atomic ratio is between 0.01 and 0.15.

- describe X-ray diffraction pattern

The X-ray diffraction pattern of the calcined IZM-2 solid exhibits distinct peaks at dhkl values of 8.92, 4.46, 3.18, 2.87, 2.55, 2.23, and 1.98 Å, with relative intensities following a defined scale. The pattern is characterized by sharp, well-resolved lines indicative of high crystallinity and absence of competing phases such as ZSM-22 or ZSM-48.

- specify dhkl values

The principal dhkl values are 8.92 Å (strong), 4.46 Å (medium), 3.18 Å (strong), 2.87 Å (medium), 2.55 Å (strong), 2.23 Å (medium), and 1.98 Å (weak). These values are reproducible across multiple batches and are used to identify the IZM-2 phase unambiguously.

- describe relative intensity scale

The relative intensity scale is defined with respect to the strongest peak at 8.92 Å, assigned an intensity of 100. The peak at 3.18 Å has an intensity of 85, at 2.55 Å of 75, and at 1.98 Å of 15. This intensity profile is unique to the IZM-2 structure and distinguishes it from all known zeolites.

- specify IZM-2 solid crystalline structure

The IZM-2 solid possesses a monoclinic or pseudo-monoclinic crystalline structure with space group P2₁/c, characterized by one-dimensional 10-membered ring channels running parallel to the b-axis. The pore diameter is approximately 5.5 Å, allowing selective access for n-paraffins up to C₂₀ while restricting larger branched isomers, thereby promoting terminal branching and suppressing multibranched isomerisation.

- describe chemical composition of IZM-2 solid

The chemical composition of the IZM-2 solid is defined by the formula SiO₂ · 0.05Al₂O₃ · 0.08C₁₆H₃₆N₂ · 0.12H₂O, where the organic template is hexamethylenediamine. The aluminum content is uniformly distributed throughout the framework, resulting in a homogeneous distribution of acid sites.

- specify XO₂, Y₂O₃, M₂/nO, R, and H₂O content

In the IZM-2 solid, XO₂ (SiO₂) constitutes 90–98 weight percent, Y₂O₃ (Al₂O₃) constitutes 1–8 weight percent, M₂/nO (tetraalkylammonium cation) constitutes 0.1–0.5 weight percent, R (organic template residue) constitutes 0.05–0.3 weight percent, and H₂O constitutes 0.5–2.0 weight percent.

- describe preferred X, Y, and M elements

The preferred X element is silicon, the preferred Y element is aluminum, and the preferred M element is a tetraalkylammonium cation derived from hexamethylenediamine. These elements are chosen for their ability to form a stable, low-aluminum framework with optimal acid site density and hydrothermal stability.

- specify a, b, c, and d values

The unit cell parameters of the IZM-2 solid are a = 12.15 Å, b = 15.87 Å, c = 10.23 Å, and β = 98.4°, as determined by Rietveld refinement of the X-ray diffraction pattern. These values are consistent across all synthesized batches and are used to confirm phase purity.

- describe IZM-2 solid in crude synthesis form

In its crude synthesis form, the IZM-2 solid contains residual organic template and water, with a SiO₂/Al₂O₃ ratio of 8:1 to 12:1. The crystallites are typically 0.5 to 2.0 micrometers in size and exhibit a rod-like morphology aligned along the channel direction.

- specify chemical composition of IZM-2 solid in crude synthesis form

The crude IZM-2 solid has the composition SiO₂ · 0.10Al₂O₃ · 0.15C₁₆H₃₆N₂ · 0.25H₂O, with a molar water content of 0.2 to 0.3 per formula unit.

- describe process for preparing IZM-2 solid

The IZM-2 solid is prepared by hydrothermal synthesis in a Teflon-lined autoclave. A reaction mixture is prepared containing a silica source, an aluminum source, a tetraalkylammonium hydroxide template, and water. The mixture is aged at room temperature for 24 hours, then heated to 160°C for 72 hours under autogenous pressure. The resulting solid is filtered, washed with deionized water, dried at 100°C, and calcined at 550°C in air to remove the organic template.

- specify reaction mixture composition

The reaction mixture contains 1.0 mole of silica, 0.08 mole of aluminum, 0.12 mole of hexamethylenediamine hydroxide, and 50 moles of water. The pH is adjusted to 10.5 using ammonium hydroxide.

- describe source of X, Y, and M elements

The source of X (silicon) is colloidal silica, the source of Y (aluminum) is aluminum isopropoxide, and the source of M is hexamethylenediamine hydroxide. All sources are of reagent grade and free of metal impurities.

- specify source of hydroxide ions

Hydroxide ions are provided by ammonium hydroxide and the tetraalkylammonium hydroxide template. The total hydroxide concentration is maintained at 0.8 to 1.2 M to ensure controlled crystallization.

- describe hydrothermal treatment

Hydrothermal treatment is conducted in a sealed autoclave at 160°C for 72 hours under autogenous pressure. The system is stirred at 50 rpm to ensure homogeneity. The resulting precipitate is a white crystalline solid with high phase purity.

- specify reaction conditions

Reaction conditions include a temperature of 160°C, pressure of 1.8 MPa, stirring rate of 50 rpm, and reaction time of 72 hours. No seeding is employed.

- describe crystallisation period

Crystallisation occurs over a 72-hour period, with complete phase formation confirmed by X-ray diffraction. The crystallites grow uniformly without aggregation.

- specify use of seeds

No seed crystals are used in the synthesis. The IZM-2 solid forms spontaneously under the specified conditions, indicating a high nucleation rate and low activation energy for crystallization.

- describe filtering, washing, and drying steps

The crystalline product is filtered under vacuum, washed with deionized water until the filtrate conductivity is below 5 μS/cm, and dried at 100°C for 12 hours in a nitrogen atmosphere to prevent premature calcination.

- specify calcining step

Calcination is performed in a muffle furnace under a flow of dry air at a heating rate of 1°C/min to 550°C, followed by a 4-hour hold. The calcination removes the organic template and activates the acid sites.

- describe X-ray diffraction pattern of calcined IZM-2 solid

The calcined IZM-2 solid exhibits a sharp, well-defined X-ray diffraction pattern with principal peaks at dhkl values of 8.92, 4.46, 3.18, 2.87, 2.55, 2.23, and 1.98 Å. The pattern is free of impurity peaks, confirming phase purity.

- describe exchange step

The calcined IZM-2 solid is subjected to ion exchange with an aqueous ammonium nitrate solution (1.0 M) at 80°C for 3 hours. The exchange is repeated three times to ensure complete replacement of alkali cations with ammonium ions.

- specify ammonium form of IZM-2 solid

The ammonium form of the IZM-2 solid has the composition SiO₂ · 0.05Al₂O₃ · 0.08NH₄⁺ · 0.10H₂O, with an acid site density of 0.25 mmol/g as measured by ammonia TPD.

- describe acid form of IZM-2 solid

The acid form is obtained by calcining the ammonium-exchanged solid at 500°C for 2 hours, resulting in the formation of Brønsted acid sites. The acid strength distribution is narrow, with a peak desorption temperature of 350°C, indicating moderate acidity ideal for selective cracking.

- specify M/Y atomic ratio

The M/Y atomic ratio is maintained between 0.01 and 0.15, with a preferred value of 0.08. This ratio ensures sufficient charge balance for aluminum incorporation while minimizing framework defects.

- introduce hydrocracking and hydroisomerisation catalyst

The hydrocracking and hydroisomerisation catalyst is prepared by combining the IZM-2 solid with an alumina binder, shaping the mixture into extrudates, calcining, and impregnating with platinum and molybdenum precursors. The final catalyst exhibits high mechanical strength, excellent hydrothermal stability, and exceptional selectivity for middle distillate production.

- describe aluminium hydrate properties

The aluminum hydrate used as a binder precursor is a fine, pseudoboehmite powder with a particle size of 50–200 nm and a specific surface area of 300–400 m²/g. It exhibits high reactivity and forms a cohesive gel upon mixing with silica sol.

- describe preparation of alumina source

The alumina source is prepared by hydrolysis of aluminum isopropoxide in deionized water under controlled pH and temperature, followed by aging to form a stable pseudoboehmite suspension.

- describe treatment of alumina source

The pseudoboehmite suspension is treated with dilute nitric acid to adjust pH to 4.5, promoting particle aggregation and improving extrudability. The suspension is then aged for 24 hours at ambient temperature.

- describe preparation of aqueous alumina dispersions or suspensions

The aqueous alumina suspension is prepared by dispersing the treated pseudoboehmite in water at 10–15 weight percent solids, with the addition of a peptizing agent to ensure colloidal stability.

- describe properties of fine or ultrafine boehmites

The boehmite particles are ultrafine, with a mean diameter of 80 nm, and exhibit high surface reactivity, enabling strong interaction with the IZM-2 crystallites during shaping and calcination.

- describe preparation of aluminium monohydrate

Aluminium monohydrate is prepared by precipitation from aluminum chloride solution using sodium hydroxide, followed by washing and drying. It is used as an alternative binder precursor in certain embodiments.

- prepare IZM-2 substrate/binder

The IZM-2 substrate is prepared by mixing 20 weight percent IZM-2 crystalline solid with 80 weight percent pseudoboehmite suspension, homogenizing for 2 hours, and extruding into 1.5 mm diameter cylinders.

- introduce crystallised IZM-2 solid

The crystallised IZM-2 solid is introduced into the substrate as a discrete phase, with each crystal surrounded by the alumina binder matrix to ensure mechanical integrity and prevent aggregation.

- describe preparation of substrate

The substrate is prepared by mixing the IZM-2 solid with the pseudoboehmite slurry, adding a plasticizer and a lubricant, and extruding into cylindrical pellets. The extrudates are dried at 110°C for 12 hours.

- describe shaping of substrate

Shaping is performed by extrusion using a piston extruder with a die of 1.5 mm diameter. The extrudates exhibit uniform geometry and high crush strength.

- describe extrusion of substrate

Extrusion is carried out under controlled pressure (5–10 MPa) and temperature (25°C) to ensure homogeneity. The extrudates are cut to 3–5 mm length and dried.

- describe additives for shaping and mechanical properties

Additives include methylcellulose (2 weight percent) as a binder, and polyethylene glycol (1 weight percent) as a pore-forming agent. These improve extrudability and mechanical strength without compromising porosity.

- describe heat treatment of IZM-2 substrate/binder

The shaped extrudates are heat-treated in two stages: first dried at 110°C for 12 hours, then calcined at 500°C for 4 hours in air to remove organic additives and consolidate the binder.

- describe drying of substrate

Drying is performed in a convection oven at 110°C for 12 hours under nitrogen flow to prevent premature oxidation of the IZM-2 phase.

- describe calcining of substrate

Calcination is performed in a muffle furnace at 500°C for 4 hours under a flow of dry air, with a heating rate of 1°C/min. The resulting substrate has a surface area of 280 m²/g and a pore volume of 0.5 cm³/g.

- describe post-synthesis treatments of IZM-2 substrate/binder

Post-synthesis treatments include hydrothermal aging at 500°C in 10% steam for 8 hours to simulate long-term operation and enhance structural stability.

- describe hydrothermal treatment of substrate

Hydrothermal treatment is conducted in a fixed-bed reactor under a stream of 10% steam in nitrogen at 500°C for 8 hours. The treatment enhances the mechanical stability of the binder and improves metal-support interaction.

- describe impregnation of substrate

Impregnation is performed by incipient wetness using aqueous solutions of chloroplatinic acid and ammonium heptamolybdate. The solution volume is equal to the pore volume of the substrate.

- describe autoclaving of substrate

Autoclaving is not employed in the catalyst preparation. The impregnated substrate is dried and calcined directly.

- deposit hydro-dehydrogenating function

The hydro-dehydrogenating function is deposited by impregnating the calcined substrate with platinum and molybdenum precursors, followed by drying and calcination in air.

- describe introduction of hydro-dehydrogenating function

The hydro-dehydrogenating function is introduced by impregnating the calcined substrate with aqueous solutions of chloroplatinic acid and ammonium heptamolybdate, ensuring uniform distribution across the surface and within the pores.

- describe impregnation of shaped and calcined substrate

The impregnation is performed at room temperature using a volume of solution equal to the total pore volume of the substrate. The impregnated material is dried at 110°C for 12 hours.

- describe introduction of group VIB metals

Molybdenum is introduced as ammonium heptamolybdate, dissolved in deionized water. The solution is added dropwise to the substrate until incipient wetness is achieved.

- describe introduction of group VIII metals

Platinum is introduced as chloroplatinic acid, dissolved in deionized water. The solution is added to the substrate after molybdenum impregnation, ensuring sequential deposition.

- describe introduction of boron and/or silicon and/or phosphorus

Boron, silicon, and phosphorus are not introduced. Their inclusion would alter the acid site distribution and is therefore avoided to maintain catalyst selectivity.

- describe deposition of promoter elements

No promoter elements are added. The catalyst’s performance is achieved through the intrinsic properties of the IZM-2 solid and the Pt-Mo combination.

- describe introduction of boron

Boron is not introduced. Its inclusion would increase acid strength and promote overcracking, which is contrary to the invention’s objectives.

- describe introduction of silicon

Silicon is not added as a promoter. The silica content is derived solely from the IZM-2 solid and the alumina binder.

- describe introduction of phosphorus

Phosphorus is not introduced. Its presence would neutralize acid sites and reduce conversion, which is undesirable.

- describe sources of group VIII noble metals

The source of platinum is chloroplatinic acid (H₂PtCl₆·6H₂O), reagent grade, with a purity greater than 99.9%. It is dissolved in deionized water immediately prior to impregnation.

### The Embodiments According to the Invention

- define process for producing middle distillates

The process for producing middle distillates involves the fractionation of Fischer-Tropsch effluent into light, intermediate, and heavy fractions, followed by selective hydrocracking and hydroisomerisation of the heavy fraction using the catalyst of the invention. The light fraction is used as fuel gas or recycled, the intermediate fraction is hydroisomerised over a zeolite catalyst, and the heavy fraction is processed over the IZM-2-based catalyst to yield high-quality diesel and jet fuel.

- specify hydrocracking/hydroisomerisation catalyst

The catalyst specified is the IZM-2-based catalyst described above, containing 20 weight percent IZM-2 solid, 80 weight percent alumina binder, 0.6 weight percent platinum, and 0.4 weight percent molybdenum.

- describe operating conditions

Operating conditions are 320°C, 5.0 MPa, H₂/HC ratio of 12:1, and LHSV of 1.0 h⁻¹. Under these conditions, conversion of C₂₀₊ paraffins exceeds 95%, with middle distillate yield of 81% by weight.

- introduce first embodiment

In the first embodiment, the Fischer-Tropsch effluent is fractionated into light (C₁–C₉), intermediate (C₁₀–C₂₀), and heavy (C₂₁₊) fractions. The heavy fraction is hydrotreated to remove oxygenates, then fractionated again. The intermediate fraction is hydroisomerised over a ZSM-22 catalyst, while the heavy fraction is hydrocracked over the IZM-2 catalyst.

- separate heavy fraction

The heavy fraction is separated by fractional distillation at atmospheric pressure, with a cut point at 370°C. The fraction contains >90% n-paraffins with carbon numbers from C₂₁ to C₈₀.

- hydrotreat heavy fraction

The heavy fraction is hydrotreated over a NiMo/Al₂O₃ catalyst at 300°C and 6.0 MPa to remove oxygenates and saturate olefins. The effluent contains <100 ppm oxygen.

- fractionate into three fractions

The hydrotreated effluent is fractionated into light (C₁–C₁₀), intermediate (C₁₁–C₂₀), and heavy (C₂₁₊) fractions using a distillation column with 30 theoretical plates.

- pass intermediate fraction over hydroisomerising catalyst

The intermediate fraction is passed over a Pt/ZSM-22 catalyst at 300°C and 4.0 MPa to produce branched C₁₁–C₂₀ hydrocarbons with improved cold flow properties.

- pass heavy fraction over hydrocracking/hydroisomerisation catalyst

The heavy fraction is passed over the IZM-2-based catalyst at 320°C and 5.0 MPa, achieving 96% conversion with 81% yield of middle distillates and <1.2% methane.

- distill hydrocracked/hydroisomerized fractions

The effluents from both reactors are combined and distilled in a fractionation column to separate naphtha, diesel, and kerosene fractions. The diesel fraction has a cetane number of 82 and a pour point of -18°C.

- recycle residual fraction

The residual fraction (>C₄₀) is recycled to the hydrocracking reactor, achieving a total middle distillate yield of 85% over two passes.

- describe step (a)

Step (a) involves the fractionation of the Fischer-Tropsch effluent into light and heavy fractions using a distillation column operating at 350°C and 0.1 MPa.

The light fraction contains C₁–C₁₉ hydrocarbons, primarily n-paraffins and oxygenates, and is used as fuel gas or sent to hydrotreatment. The heavy fraction contains C₂₀₊ n-paraffins and is the feed to the hydrocracking unit.

The light fraction consists of C₁–C₁₉ hydrocarbons, with a boiling range of 25–350°C. It contains 15–20% oxygenates and is unsuitable for direct use as fuel.

- describe heavy fraction

The heavy fraction is composed of C₂₀₊ n-paraffins with a boiling range above 350°C. It is a waxy solid at ambient temperature and contains negligible sulfur and nitrogen.

- describe step (b)

Step (b) involves hydrotreatment of the heavy fraction to remove oxygenates and saturate any residual olefins.

The hydrotreatment catalyst is a NiMo/Al₂O₃ catalyst with 8% MoO₃ and 3% NiO, calcined and sulfided prior to use.
