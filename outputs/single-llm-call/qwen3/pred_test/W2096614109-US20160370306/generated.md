# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

Serial femtosecond crystallography (SFX) is a revolutionary structural biology technique that enables the determination of protein structures from micro- and nanocrystals using ultrafast X-ray pulses generated by free-electron lasers. This method exploits the principle of diffraction-before-destruction, wherein femtosecond-duration X-ray pulses capture coherent diffraction patterns from protein crystals before they are obliterated by the intense radiation. The technique has proven particularly transformative for the study of proteins that are difficult to crystallize in large sizes or that are sensitive to cryogenic conditions, such as membrane proteins and transient molecular complexes. In conventional SFX implementations, crystals are delivered to the X-ray beam via gas-focused liquid jets, which propel the sample at high velocities to replenish the interaction volume between pulses. However, these liquid jets require substantial volumes of protein suspension—often in the range of 10 to 100 milligrams per data set—rendering them impractical for proteins that are expressed at low yields or are inherently scarce.

Membrane proteins represent a critical class of biological targets, accounting for approximately 60% of all current pharmaceutical interventions. Despite their therapeutic relevance, structural characterization of membrane proteins remains severely limited, with fewer than 550 unique structures deposited in public databases compared to over 100,000 for soluble proteins. The hydrophobic nature of membrane proteins necessitates their extraction from lipid bilayers using detergents, forming protein–detergent micelles that can be crystallized in aqueous solutions or within lipidic environments. The lipidic cubic phase (LCP), a self-assembled, three-dimensional network of lipid bilayers interwoven with aqueous channels, has emerged as a dominant method for crystallizing membrane proteins due to its biomimetic properties that promote favorable crystal packing. LCP has enabled the determination of numerous G-protein-coupled receptors, ion channels, and photosynthetic complexes. To adapt LCP for SFX, specialized injectors have been developed to deliver the viscous, gel-like material to the X-ray beam at slow flow rates, thereby reducing protein consumption by orders of magnitude compared to gas dynamic virtual nozzles.

Despite its success, LCP presents significant limitations when applied to large, multi-domain membrane protein complexes. The curvature of the lipid bilayer and the reduced diffusion coefficients of large protein assemblies within the LCP matrix hinder nucleation and crystal growth. Furthermore, the lipid components of LCP generate substantial background X-ray scattering, particularly in the 3–5 Å resolution range, which obscures weak diffraction signals from low-symmetry or large-unit-cell crystals. Alternative viscous media, such as mineral oil-based greases and petroleum jelly, have been proposed to circumvent these issues. These media offer reduced background scattering compared to LCP but introduce new challenges, including the formation of strong Debye–Scherrer rings at 3.77–5 Å, poor stream stability under vacuum, and incompatibility with many common crystallization buffers. Moreover, these media have not been successfully applied to the delivery of large membrane protein complexes or to experiments conducted under cryogenic conditions. The need for a universally compatible, inert, and low-background crystal delivery medium that accommodates a broad range of crystallization conditions, protein sizes, and operational environments remains unmet.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The invention discloses a novel method for preparing an inert crystal delivery medium for serial femtosecond crystallography, comprising the preparation of an agarose-based gel matrix that is chemically inert, optically transparent to X-rays, and compatible with a wide range of crystallization buffers and cryoprotectants. The method involves the dissolution of agarose in a solution containing a cryoprotectant and a crystallization buffer, followed by thermal equilibration and controlled mixing with a concentrated suspension of pre-formed protein crystals. The resulting mixture forms a viscous, stable medium suitable for extrusion through capillary injectors at flow rates as low as 160 nanoliters per minute. The agarose medium is prepared using ultralow-gelling-temperature agarose, dissolved at concentrations ranging from 5% to 9% (w/v) in aqueous solutions containing glycerol, sucrose, or other compatible cryoprotectants, and is compatible with precipitants such as polyethylene glycols, ammonium sulfate, magnesium chloride, and organic solvents like 2-methyl-2,4-pentanediol. The medium is designed to maintain crystal integrity during storage, mixing, and extrusion, and exhibits negligible X-ray scattering background compared to lipidic cubic phase, mineral oil, or petroleum jelly. The invention further encompasses a system for preparing and delivering the agarose-based medium, comprising dual syringes connected via a syringe coupler, a heating apparatus for dissolving agarose, and a capillary injector system compatible with high-velocity inert-gas sheathing and vacuum or ambient-pressure environments. The system allows for precise control over agarose concentration, cryoprotectant composition, and crystal-to-medium ratio, enabling optimization for diverse protein systems without altering the fundamental composition of the medium.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

The invention introduces an agarose-based crystal delivery medium for use in serial femtosecond crystallography, wherein protein crystals are embedded within a hydrogel matrix formed by agarose polymers dissolved in aqueous solutions containing cryoprotectants and crystallization buffer components. The agarose medium is prepared by first dissolving ultralow-gelling-temperature agarose powder in a heated solution of cryoprotectant and buffer, ensuring complete dissolution at temperatures above 85°C. The resulting agarose solution is then drawn into a syringe that has been pre-warmed to prevent premature gelation. Separately, a concentrated suspension of protein crystals in their native mother liquor is drawn into a second syringe. The two syringes are coupled via a precision syringe coupler, and the crystal suspension is homogenized into the agarose matrix through repeated back-and-forth plunger movements, typically exceeding 40 cycles, to ensure uniform crystal distribution without mechanical damage. The final agarose concentration after mixing is maintained between 5.6% and 7% (w/v), with cryoprotectant concentrations optimized for the specific crystallization conditions—glycerol at 30% for high-salt systems, sucrose at 0.6 M for low-ionic-strength systems, and other cryoprotectants as required. The medium is compatible with a broad spectrum of crystallization buffers, including those containing high concentrations of polyethylene glycols (PEG 2000–8000), ammonium sulfate, calcium chloride, and detergents such as β-dodecylmaltoside. The agarose gel forms a continuous, stable stream when extruded through a 50-micrometer capillary at flow rates between 100 and 200 nanoliters per minute, and is stabilized by a high-velocity inert-gas sheath, typically nitrogen or helium, which confines the stream and prevents dispersion. The medium exhibits minimal X-ray background scattering, with diffuse scattering peaks observed only near 3.3 Å, significantly lower than the 4.5 Å peak characteristic of lipidic cubic phase and the multiple rings observed in oil-based media. The low water content of the agarose matrix relative to lipidic systems—approximately 93% water versus 50% in LCP—combined with the absence of long-range lipid order, results in substantially reduced low-angle scattering, making the medium ideal for proteins with large unit cells. The agarose gel is stable across a temperature range of 4°C to 30°C, enabling compatibility with crystallization protocols conducted at ambient or chilled conditions. Cryoprotectants are incorporated to suppress ice formation during vacuum operation, and their selection is tailored to the specific buffer composition to avoid crystal dissolution or aggregation. The medium does not interfere with crystal morphology, and crystals embedded in agarose retain their diffraction quality, as demonstrated by the successful structure determination of phycocyanin, photosystem I, and photosystem II. The method is scalable, cost-effective, and utilizes commercially available reagents, eliminating the need for proprietary or complex formulations. The system for preparing the medium includes a heating block, dual syringes with precision plunger control, a syringe coupler for mixing, and a capillary injector compatible with standard SFX beamline infrastructure. The medium is compatible with both vacuum and ambient-pressure environments, though vacuum operation is preferred for optimal stream stability and reduced background noise. The invention further encompasses modifications including the use of alternative polysaccharides with similar gelling properties, the incorporation of stabilizing agents such as trehalose or sorbitol, and the adaptation of the medium for use in electrospinning or microfluidic delivery systems. All equivalents, alternatives, and modifications that fall within the spirit and scope of the disclosed invention are intended to be encompassed by the claims.

### EXAMPLE 1

The invention was demonstrated using the phycocyanin (PC) complex, a cyanobacterial light-harvesting protein that forms a disc-shaped trimer composed of α and β subunits, each bound to a phycocyanobilin chromophore. PC was purified from Thermosynechococcus elongatus via cell disruption, ultracentrifugation, and concentration using 100 kDa molecular weight cutoff filters. Crystallization was achieved by free interface diffusion using a precipitant solution of 1.0 M ammonium sulfate and 40 mM MES pH 6.4, resulting in crystals of 1–5 µm in size. Prior to embedding, crystals were filtered through a 10 µm stainless-steel filter to remove aggregates. An agarose solution was prepared by dissolving 7% (w/v) ultralow-gelling-temperature agarose in a mixture of 600 µl glycerol and 1.4 ml of the crystallization buffer, heated to boiling for 30 minutes. The solution was drawn into a pre-warmed 100 µl Hamilton syringe, and 5 µl of concentrated crystal suspension was introduced into a second syringe. The syringes were coupled and mixed via 40 reciprocal plunger movements to achieve a final agarose concentration of 5.6% and a crystal density of 2 × 10¹⁰ crystals per milliliter. The embedded crystals were extruded through a 50 µm capillary at 160 nl/min into the CXI instrument at the Linac Coherent Light Source. Diffraction patterns were collected over 72 minutes, yielding 41,100 hits from 513,848 frames. After background correction and hit filtering using Cheetah, 14,143 patterns were indexed in space group P63 with unit cell parameters a = b = 153.4 Å, c = 39.6 Å. Indexing ambiguity was resolved using ambigator, and data were integrated using CrystFEL. A resolution cutoff of 2.5 Å was selected based on CC* and multiplicity criteria. Molecular replacement with PDB entry 4gy3 yielded an electron density map of high quality, permitting refinement to Rwork = 18.7% and Rfree = 25.5%. Water molecules were added and refined, confirming the integrity of the chromophore binding sites. The same protocol was successfully applied to photosystem I and photosystem II, demonstrating the medium’s versatility across protein classes and crystallization conditions. Agarose outperformed LCP in background scattering, with a 2.3-fold reduction in diffuse intensity at 4–5 Å. No ice formation was observed under vacuum when glycerol was used, and stream stability was superior to that of petroleum jelly or mineral oil-based media. The agarose medium enabled the collection of high-resolution data from large complexes with minimal protein consumption, establishing its utility as a general-purpose SFX delivery system.
