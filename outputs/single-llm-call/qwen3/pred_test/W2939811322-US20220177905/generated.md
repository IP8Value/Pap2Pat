## TECHNICAL FIELD

- The present invention relates to the field of genetic engineering and synthetic biology, specifically to systems and methods for achieving super-Mendelian inheritance of specific allelic variants within populations of eukaryotic organisms. The invention provides novel gene drive architectures that enable the preferential transmission of selected genetic alleles—whether naturally occurring or engineered—through successive generations by exploiting targeted DNA cleavage and homology-directed repair mechanisms. These systems are particularly useful for altering allele frequencies in wild or laboratory populations of insects, plants, fungi, and other multicellular eukaryotes without requiring continuous external intervention. The technology is applicable to agricultural pest control, disease vector management, conservation biology, and the accelerated breeding of complex genotypes in model and crop organisms.

## BACKGROUND

- Gene drive systems have emerged as powerful tools for propagating engineered genetic traits through populations at rates exceeding Mendelian inheritance, offering transformative potential for addressing global challenges in public health, agriculture, and ecology. CRISPR-Cas9-based gene drives have demonstrated the ability to copy transgenic cassettes into homologous chromosomal loci with high efficiency in species such as Drosophila, mosquitoes, and yeast. However, these systems are largely limited to the dissemination of large DNA insertions, typically exceeding one kilobase in length, which restricts their utility in scenarios where the desired trait is conferred by a single nucleotide polymorphism or a small set of base changes. Many agriculturally and medically relevant traits—such as pesticide sensitivity in insect vectors, drought tolerance in crops, or resistance to pathogens in livestock—are governed by subtle allelic variants that cannot be effectively transmitted using conventional gene drive strategies. Furthermore, existing CRISPR-Cas9 systems face significant limitations due to the emergence of resistance alleles arising from error-prone non-homologous end joining repair, which frequently inactivates the target site and prevents further drive propagation. These resistance alleles accumulate rapidly in populations, undermining the long-term efficacy of gene drives and limiting their practical deployment. The inability to efficiently propagate small, naturally occurring allelic variants represents a critical gap in the field of active genetics.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

- The invention introduces a novel class of genetic systems termed allelic drive, which enables the super-Mendelian inheritance of specific allelic variants through targeted cleavage and repair mechanisms. The allelic drive system comprises a dual-guide RNA architecture in which one guide RNA directs the copying of a genetic element to a defined genomic locus, while a second guide RNA selectively cleaves a non-preferred allele of the same gene, thereby promoting repair using the preferred allele as a template. This dual-function design permits the coordinated propagation of both a transgenic marker and a beneficial allelic variant. Two distinct mechanisms are disclosed: copy-cutting allelic drive, in which a cleavage-sensitive allele is replaced by a homologous cleavage-resistant allele via homology-directed repair; and copy-grafting allelic drive, in which a short genomic interval encompassing a favored allele is copied from a donor chromosome to a recipient chromosome following a nearby double-strand break. The invention further introduces the concept of lethal mosaicism, a dominant, Cas9-dependent embryonic lethality that eliminates individuals carrying non-functional, non-cleavable alleles generated by non-homologous end joining, thereby purging drive-resistant variants from the population. These systems collectively enable the efficient assembly, dissemination, and stabilization of complex genotypes composed of multiple favorable alleles in a single genetic background.

The term “allele” refers to any variant form of a gene, including naturally occurring polymorphisms, induced mutations, or synthetically engineered sequences. The term “drive element” refers to a nucleic acid construct capable of promoting its own inheritance at a rate exceeding Mendelian expectations, typically through the action of a programmable endonuclease and one or more guide polynucleotides. The term “homology arm” refers to a nucleic acid sequence flanking a drive element that shares substantial sequence identity with a target genomic locus, enabling targeted integration via homologous recombination. Conventional techniques for transgenesis, including microinjection of plasmid DNA into embryos, transposon-mediated integration, and CRISPR-Cas9-mediated knock-in, are employed in the practice of this invention. The allelic-drive copy-cutting method involves the introduction of a first guide RNA that targets a cleavage-sensitive allele of a gene, and a second guide RNA that directs the insertion of a transgenic element into a linked locus. The cleavage-sensitive allele is repaired using a homologous template provided by a companion chromosome carrying a cleavage-resistant variant, resulting in the conversion of the sensitive allele to the resistant form. The allelic-drive copy-grafting method involves the design of a guide RNA that cleaves a genomic region adjacent to a favored allele, such that the repair process copies a short segment of DNA—including the favored allele—from the donor chromosome to the recipient chromosome. The allelic-drive element insertion is achieved through homology-directed repair using flanking homology arms that direct integration into a specific genomic locus. Allelic conversion efficiency is quantified as the percentage of progeny inheriting the desired allele on a previously non-carrier chromosome, and in the disclosed embodiments, efficiencies exceed 75% in both males and females. The Cas endonuclease is provided in trans via a separate transgenic locus expressing Cas9 under the control of a germline-specific promoter, such as vasa or nanos. Progeny survival is enhanced when the drive element is linked to a functional, non-cleavable version of the targeted gene, thereby preventing lethal mosaicism. Perduring Cas9-gRNA complexes, deposited maternally into the oocyte, can persist for one or more generations and mediate low-level drive activity in the absence of inherited Cas9 transgenes, a phenomenon termed shadow-drive. Lethal mosaicism occurs when a heterozygous individual inherits a cleavage-sensitive allele from one parent and a drive element from the other, resulting in somatic Cas9-mediated cleavage of the sensitive allele in a subset of cells, leading to biallelic loss of function and embryonic lethality. Essential gene modification is achieved by targeting genes critical for viability or reproduction, ensuring that non-functional alleles are eliminated by lethal mosaicism. Functional recoded sequences are engineered variants of essential genes that retain biological function but are refractory to cleavage by the guide RNA, allowing their stable inheritance alongside the drive element. Gene examples include Notch, voltage-gated sodium channels, and glutamate receptors, all of which are known to harbor allelic variants with phenotypic consequences relevant to pest control and disease transmission. Selectively eliminating alleles is accomplished by designing guide RNAs that target sequences unique to undesirable variants while sparing the desired allele. The lethal mosaicism technique exploits the dominant nature of loss-of-function mutations in essential genes to eliminate individuals carrying non-functional, non-cleavable alleles. Germline editing is enabled by the expression of Cas9 and guide RNAs in the developing germline, ensuring that cleavage and repair events occur prior to gamete formation. Repair of cleavage-sensitive alleles is mediated by homology-directed repair using a homologous template from the companion chromosome. Assembling alleles in plants is achieved by introducing the drive element into a heterozygous line carrying a desired allele and crossing it to a line expressing Cas9, enabling iterative stacking of multiple favorable alleles. Assembling alleles in insects follows a similar strategy, with the added advantage of rapid generation time and high fecundity.
