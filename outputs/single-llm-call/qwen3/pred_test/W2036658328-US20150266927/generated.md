# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNICAL FIELD

- relate to HPV VLP purification

The present invention relates to improved methods for the purification of human papillomavirus virus-like particles (VLPs), particularly those composed of the L1 capsid protein, produced in yeast expression systems. More specifically, the invention concerns a refined downstream processing protocol that enhances the structural integrity, purity, and immunogenicity of recombinant HPV L1 VLPs through a combination of controlled cell lysis, redox modulation, thermal cycling, and sequential chromatographic separation. The methods described herein are particularly suited for the large-scale production of high-quality VLPs for use in prophylactic vaccines against HPV-associated diseases, including cervical, oropharyngeal, and anogenital cancers. The invention addresses critical limitations in existing purification workflows by optimizing conditions that preserve the native conformational epitopes essential for eliciting potent neutralizing antibody responses.

## BACKGROUND ART

- introduce HPV and cervical cancer

Human papillomavirus (HPV) is a non-enveloped, double-stranded DNA virus that infects epithelial tissues and is causally linked to a significant proportion of human cancers, particularly cervical carcinoma, which remains a leading cause of cancer-related mortality among women in resource-limited settings. Persistent infection with high-risk HPV types, notably HPV16 and HPV18, accounts for approximately 70% of all cervical cancer cases globally. The development of prophylactic vaccines targeting these oncogenic strains has revolutionized preventive oncology, yet the efficacy of such vaccines remains contingent upon the structural fidelity and immunogenic potency of the antigenic components used.

- describe HPV types and risks

Over 200 distinct HPV genotypes have been identified, but only a subset—primarily types 16, 18, 31, 33, 45, 52, and 58—are classified as high-risk due to their strong association with malignant transformation. Among these, HPV16 is the most prevalent and oncogenic, responsible for nearly half of all cervical cancers. The immune system’s ability to recognize and neutralize these viruses is largely dependent on antibodies targeting conformational epitopes on the major capsid protein, L1, which forms the outer shell of the virion.

- explain L1 and L2 proteins

The L1 protein is the principal structural component of the HPV capsid, capable of self-assembling into pentameric units that further organize into icosahedral virus-like particles mimicking the native virion architecture. While the minor capsid protein L2 contributes to viral entry and genome packaging, it is not essential for VLP formation and plays a minimal role in eliciting neutralizing immunity. Consequently, recombinant L1-based VLPs serve as the cornerstone of current HPV vaccines, as they present repetitive, high-density arrays of neutralizing epitopes that effectively activate B-cell responses.

- discuss current vaccines and limitations

Current licensed HPV vaccines, including Gardasil and Cervarix, utilize L1 VLPs produced in yeast or insect cell systems and adjuvanted with aluminum salts. While these vaccines demonstrate high efficacy in clinical trials, their cost of goods remains prohibitive for widespread deployment in low-income countries. Furthermore, batch-to-batch variability in VLP quality, stemming from suboptimal purification protocols, can compromise immunogenic consistency and necessitate extensive quality control measures.

- describe downstream processing costs

Downstream processing represents the most expensive phase of VLP vaccine manufacturing, accounting for over 60% of total production costs. Traditional purification methods, such as cesium chloride gradient ultracentrifugation, are labor-intensive, low-yielding, and unsuitable for industrial scale-up. Alternative approaches relying on ammonium sulfate precipitation or single-step chromatography often result in co-purification of host cell proteins, nucleic acids, and misfolded L1 aggregates, thereby reducing antigenic potency and increasing the risk of adverse immunological reactions.

- summarize current purification methods

Current industry-standard purification protocols typically involve cell disruption, clarification, ammonium sulfate precipitation, and one or two rounds of ion-exchange or affinity chromatography. However, these methods frequently fail to remove contaminating proteins that interfere with VLP assembly or mask critical conformational epitopes. Moreover, the absence of controlled redox and thermal treatments during processing leads to partial disassembly or oxidation of L1 proteins, diminishing their ability to induce high-titer neutralizing antibodies.

- highlight limitations of current methods

A critical limitation of existing methods is the failure to account for the dynamic intracellular environment in which L1 proteins assemble into VLPs. Yeast expression systems, while cost-effective, produce L1 proteins under conditions that may not fully recapitulate the redox and chaperone environment necessary for optimal folding. As a result, purified VLPs often exhibit heterogeneous particle sizes, reduced thermal stability, and diminished reactivity with neutralizing monoclonal antibodies, even when protein yield appears adequate.

- discuss importance of VLP structure and immunogenicity

The immunogenicity of L1 VLPs is directly correlated with their structural integrity. Particles that retain native-like quaternary organization display repetitive surface epitopes that cross-link B-cell receptors, triggering robust germinal center formation and long-lived plasma cell differentiation. Conversely, partially disassembled or aggregated VLPs fail to stimulate high-affinity neutralizing antibodies, rendering them clinically suboptimal despite high protein concentrations.

- mention previous attempts at purification

Previous efforts to improve purification have included the use of detergents, chaotropes, and redox agents such as β-mercaptoethanol, but these have yielded inconsistent results, often destabilizing the VLP structure or introducing residual contaminants that interfere with downstream applications. Other approaches, such as temperature cycling or pH shifts, have been explored but not systematically optimized for yield, purity, and immunogenicity in tandem.

- emphasize need for efficient purification method

There remains a critical and unmet need for a scalable, reproducible, and cost-effective purification method that simultaneously maximizes L1 VLP yield, minimizes contaminant load, and preserves the conformational epitopes essential for eliciting potent and durable neutralizing immunity. The present invention fulfills this need by introducing a novel, multi-step purification protocol that integrates controlled reducing conditions, precise thermal modulation, and sequential chromatographic refinement to produce VLPs of superior quality.

## DISCLOSURE

### Technical Problem

- develop efficient HPV L1 protein purification method

The technical problem addressed by the present invention is the inability of conventional purification methods to consistently produce human papillomavirus L1 virus-like particles with high purity, structural homogeneity, and preserved immunogenic epitopes. Despite high expression levels of L1 protein in Saccharomyces cerevisiae, existing downstream processing protocols fail to remove non-specific host proteins, misfolded aggregates, and nucleic acid contaminants without compromising the native conformation of the VLPs. This results in vaccine batches with variable neutralizing antibody induction capacity, necessitating extensive quality control and limiting scalability for global vaccine distribution. The invention seeks to resolve this issue by establishing a standardized, reproducible purification methodology that enhances both the physical and immunological quality of HPV L1 VLPs.

### Technical Solution

- provide method of purifying HPV L1 proteins

The invention provides a novel method for purifying human papillomavirus L1 proteins from yeast expression systems that significantly improves the purity, structural integrity, and immunogenicity of resulting virus-like particles. The method comprises culturing transformed yeast cells under optimized carbon source conditions, followed by controlled disruption of the cellular matrix, addition of a reducing agent to the homogenate, thermal cycling through defined heating and chilling phases, and sequential chromatographic purification using ion-exchange resins.

- describe culturing transformed host cells

Host cells, specifically Saccharomyces cerevisiae strains transformed with a plasmid encoding the codon-optimized HPV L1 gene, are cultured in a defined medium containing a carbon source concentration between 6% and 8% (w/v), with glucose and galactose in a 1:1 ratio, for a duration of five to seven days at 30°C under continuous agitation. This extended culture period under elevated carbon availability promotes intracellular accumulation of properly folded L1 protein and enhances the yield of assembly-competent monomers.

- explain disrupting host cells

Following incubation, the yeast cells are harvested and disrupted using mechanical means, such as vortexing with acid-washed glass beads, to release intracellular contents. The resulting homogenate is centrifuged to remove cellular debris, yielding a clarified lysate containing soluble L1 protein and contaminating host components.

- add reducing agent to homogenate

To facilitate the reduction of non-native disulfide bonds and promote the refolding of misfolded L1 monomers, a reducing agent is added to the clarified lysate at a final concentration between 1 mM and 5 mM. The reducing agent is selected from the group consisting of dithiothreitol (DTT), β-mercaptoethanol (β-ME), or tris(2-carboxyethyl)phosphine (TCEP), and the mixture is incubated for a period of 30 to 90 minutes at room temperature.

- perform chromatography

The treated lysate is subjected to a two-step chromatographic purification process. In the first step, the sample is loaded onto a heparin-affinity chromatography column equilibrated with a low-salt buffer, and bound proteins are eluted using a linear or stepwise sodium chloride gradient. Fractions containing L1 protein are pooled, concentrated, and dialyzed prior to a second chromatographic step.

- describe alternative method with heating and chilling

An alternative embodiment of the invention includes a thermal modulation step following reduction. The reduced lysate is heated to a temperature between 40°C and 45°C for a duration of 15 to 30 minutes, followed by rapid chilling to a temperature between 4°C and 8°C for a minimum of 2 hours. This thermal cycling promotes the selective aggregation and removal of misfolded or denatured proteins while preserving the structural integrity of properly assembled VLPs.

- explain importance of reducing agent

The inclusion of a reducing agent is critical to the success of the method, as it disrupts aberrant intermolecular disulfide linkages that form during prolonged intracellular expression in yeast, thereby enabling the reorganization of L1 monomers into native-like pentamers and subsequent VLP assembly. Without this step, a significant proportion of L1 remains trapped in non-functional oligomeric states, reducing both yield and immunogenicity.

- describe types of reducing agents

Preferred reducing agents include β-mercaptoethanol, dithiothreitol, and tris(2-carboxyethyl)phosphine, each of which is compatible with downstream chromatographic processes and does not introduce toxic residues. β-Mercaptoethanol is particularly advantageous due to its volatility, allowing for easy removal during subsequent dialysis.

- specify reducing agent concentrations

The reducing agent is added at a concentration ranging from 1 mM to 5 mM, with a preferred concentration of 2.5 mM. Concentrations below 1 mM are insufficient to reduce non-native disulfides, while concentrations exceeding 5 mM may induce excessive protein unfolding and aggregation.

- discuss reaction time

The reduction reaction is conducted for a period of 30 to 90 minutes, with optimal results observed at 60 minutes. Shorter durations result in incomplete reduction, whereas prolonged incubation increases the risk of nonspecific protein degradation.

- explain heating and chilling process

The heating and chilling protocol is designed to exploit differential thermal stability between correctly assembled VLPs and misfolded or aggregated L1 species. Heating induces partial unfolding of non-native structures, while subsequent chilling promotes their precipitation, leaving the native VLPs in solution.

- specify heating temperatures

The lysate is heated to a temperature between 40°C and 45°C, with 42°C being the most effective for maximizing contaminant removal without compromising VLP integrity.

- specify heating times

The heating phase is maintained for a duration of 15 to 30 minutes, with 20 minutes providing the optimal balance between aggregate removal and structural preservation.

- specify chilling temperatures

Following heating, the sample is rapidly cooled to a temperature between 4°C and 8°C, with 4°C being preferred to ensure maximal precipitation of denatured proteins.

- specify chilling times

The chilling phase is maintained for a minimum of 2 hours, with 4 hours being optimal to allow complete sedimentation of non-native aggregates.

- describe chromatography-based purification

The purified lysate is subjected to two sequential chromatographic steps, both employing heparin-affinity resin, which selectively binds L1 VLPs through electrostatic interactions with negatively charged surface residues. The first chromatography removes the majority of host proteins and nucleic acids, while the second step further refines purity and eliminates residual contaminants.

- explain types of chromatography

Ion-exchange chromatography, particularly heparin-affinity chromatography, is preferred due to its high selectivity for L1 VLPs and compatibility with physiological buffers. Other methods, including hydrophobic interaction or size-exclusion chromatography, may be employed as supplementary steps but are less effective as primary purification modalities.

- highlight advantages of ion exchange chromatography

Heparin-affinity chromatography offers superior resolution for VLPs compared to other methods, as it exploits the unique surface charge distribution of intact L1 assemblies. This enables the separation of functional VLPs from monomeric or oligomeric L1 species that lack the correct quaternary structure.

- summarize purification method

In summary, the invention provides a method comprising: (a) culturing transformed yeast under elevated carbon source conditions; (b) disrupting cells and clarifying the lysate; (c) treating the lysate with a reducing agent; (d) applying a thermal cycling regimen of heating followed by chilling; and (e) performing two sequential heparin-affinity chromatography steps to yield highly pure, structurally intact HPV L1 VLPs with enhanced immunogenic properties.

### Advantageous Effects

- enhance purification purity and immunogenicity

The method of the invention significantly enhances both the purity and immunogenicity of HPV L1 virus-like particles. Purified VLPs exhibit greater than 95% homogeneity by SDS-PAGE and Western blot analysis, with minimal contamination from host proteins or nucleic acids. Electron microscopy confirms the presence of uniformly sized, intact particles with morphological similarity to native virions. Dynamic light scattering reveals a narrow size distribution centered at approximately 55 nm, indicative of structural homogeneity. Most critically, VLPs purified by the disclosed method demonstrate markedly enhanced reactivity with neutralizing monoclonal antibodies such as H16.V5 and H16.E70, and elicit significantly higher titers of neutralizing antibodies in immunized mice compared to VLPs purified by conventional methods. These effects are achieved without the need for expensive reagents, complex instrumentation, or extended processing times, making the method highly suitable for industrial-scale vaccine production.

## EXAMPLES

- describe cell culture method

Saccharomyces cerevisiae strain Y2805, transformed with the plasmid YEGα-MCS-HPV16 L1 gene-opt, was cultured in YPDG medium containing 6% or 8% total carbon source (glucose and galactose at a 1:1 ratio) for six days at 30°C with shaking at 230 rpm. Cell density was monitored daily, and cultures were harvested during the late stationary phase to maximize L1 accumulation.

- detail purification of HPV VLPs using T-1 method

Cells were harvested by centrifugation and resuspended in phosphate-buffered saline. Glass bead disruption was performed for 15 minutes at 4°C, followed by centrifugation at 14,000 × g for 10 minutes. The supernatant was treated with 2.5 mM β-mercaptoethanol for 60 minutes at room temperature.

- outline cell disruption and treatment with reducing agent

The lysate was then subjected to thermal cycling: heating at 42°C for 20 minutes, followed by chilling at 4°C for 4 hours. The sample was centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 10 minutes to remove precipitated aggregates.

- describe heating and chilling process

The supernatant was dialyzed against binding buffer (2.68 mM KCl, 1.47 mM KH₂PO₄, 8.1 mM Na₂HPO₄, 0.5 M NaCl, pH 7.2) for 3 hours at room temperature and loaded onto a heparin-affinity column (1.8 × 3 cm, 3 mL resin). The column was washed with five column volumes of binding buffer, and bound proteins were eluted with a stepwise gradient of NaCl (0.6 M to 1.0 M).

- detail first chromatography using heparin resin

Fractions containing L1 protein were pooled, concentrated using an Amicon Ultra-4 centrifugal filter, and dialyzed against storage buffer. A second heparin-affinity chromatography was performed under identical conditions to further purify the sample.

- describe second chromatography using heparin resin

The second chromatography step resulted in a single, sharp elution peak corresponding to L1 VLPs, with no detectable contaminant bands on SDS-PAGE. The final yield of purified VLPs was 12.4 mg per liter of culture.

- outline non-treatment method for purification

A control purification was performed without reduction or thermal cycling, using only ammonium sulfate precipitation and single-step heparin chromatography. This method yielded a product with 80% purity and significantly reduced reactivity with neutralizing antibodies.

- describe purification method using β-me treatment

A separate group of samples was treated with β-mercaptoethanol alone, without thermal cycling. While purity improved to 90%, immunogenicity remained suboptimal, indicating that reduction alone is insufficient.

- detail heating and chilling method

Samples subjected to heating and chilling without reduction showed partial aggregation and reduced VLP recovery, confirming that the combination of reduction and thermal cycling is essential.

- describe purification using size-exclusion chromatography

Size-exclusion chromatography was applied to some samples but resulted in lower recovery and co-elution of smaller contaminants, demonstrating inferior resolution compared to heparin affinity.

- outline purification using ammonium sulfate precipitation

Ammonium sulfate precipitation at 40% saturation was used as a traditional control method. This resulted in a highly impure product with multiple contaminant bands and poor immunogenicity.

- quantify protein concentration

Protein concentration was determined by Bradford assay, with bovine serum albumin as standard. Final concentrations ranged from 0.8 to 1.2 mg/mL for the inventive method.

- perform SDS-PAGE and Western blotting

SDS-PAGE under reducing conditions revealed a single dominant band at approximately 55 kDa corresponding to L1. Western blotting confirmed specificity using anti-L1 antibodies.

- analyze protein using electron microscopy

Transmission electron microscopy showed uniform, spherical particles with diameters of 50–60 nm, consistent with native HPV VLP morphology.

- perform dynamic light scattering for HPV VLPs

Dynamic light scattering analysis demonstrated a hydrodynamic diameter of 55.2 ± 2.1 nm, with a polydispersity index below 0.15, indicating high homogeneity.

- analyze monoclonal antibody reactivity towards HPV VLPs

ELISA using H16.V5 and H16.E70 monoclonal antibodies showed a 3.5-fold increase in binding signal compared to conventionally purified VLPs.

- immunize mice with purified HPV L1 protein

BALB/c mice were immunized subcutaneously three times at two-week intervals with 10 ng or 1000 ng of purified VLPs formulated with aluminum hydroxide adjuvant.

- evaluate immunogenicity of HPV L1 protein

Mice immunized with VLPs purified by the inventive method exhibited significantly higher anti-L1 IgG titers than controls.

- collect and store mouse serum

Serum was collected ten days after the third immunization and stored at −80°C until analysis.

Anti-L1 IgG titers were determined by ELISA, and neutralizing activity was assessed using HPV16 pseudovirus-based neutralization assays.
