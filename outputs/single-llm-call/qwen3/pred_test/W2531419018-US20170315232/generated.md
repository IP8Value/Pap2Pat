# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

- introduce tsunamis  
Tsunamis are long-wavelength, low-amplitude oceanic waves generated by the sudden displacement of large volumes of water, typically triggered by undersea earthquakes, submarine landslides, or atmospheric disturbances such as rapid pressure changes associated with severe storms. Unlike wind-driven waves, which are confined to the surface layer and have periods of seconds to minutes, tsunamis propagate through the entire water column with periods ranging from twenty to fifty minutes and wavelengths exceeding hundreds of kilometers. Their energy is distributed over vast horizontal dimensions, allowing them to traverse entire ocean basins with minimal attenuation. As these waves approach continental shelves and coastal regions, the reduction in water depth causes a dramatic transformation: the wave slows down, its wavelength shortens, and its amplitude increases significantly, often resulting in destructive inundation of coastal areas. The potential for catastrophic loss of life and infrastructure damage has been demonstrated repeatedly throughout history, most notably in the 2004 Indian Ocean and 2011 Tohoku events, where tsunamis caused hundreds of thousands of fatalities and billions of dollars in economic losses. The inherent unpredictability of tsunami generation—where not all seismic events produce significant waves—necessitates real-time detection systems capable of identifying the onset of a tsunami in the near-field region, well before it reaches populated shorelines.

- limitations of DART sensors  
The Deep-ocean Assessment and Reporting of Tsunamis (DART) system, developed and deployed by the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration, represents the primary operational network for detecting tsunamis in the open ocean. DART sensors consist of bottom-mounted pressure recorders connected to surface buoys that relay data via satellite to warning centers. While effective at measuring the vertical displacement of water as a tsunami passes overhead, DART systems are inherently limited by their sparse spatial distribution, typically positioned in deep ocean basins thousands of kilometers from coastlines. This geographic isolation results in significant delays between tsunami generation and detection, reducing the time available for coastal warnings. Furthermore, DART sensors cannot resolve the fine-scale spatial variations in tsunami height and velocity that occur in the near-field region—within fifty kilometers of the shore—where bathymetric complexity, coastal geometry, and local currents dramatically influence wave behavior. As a result, forecasts derived from DART data often lack the resolution needed to predict localized inundation, leading to either over-warning in areas unaffected or under-warning in vulnerable zones. Additionally, DART systems are vulnerable to sensor failure, communication outages, and signal degradation during extreme events, and they provide no information on the direction or orbital motion of the approaching wave, which are critical for assessing the timing and intensity of landfall.

- limitations of numerical models  
Numerical tsunami propagation models, such as the Method of Splitting Tsunami (MOST), rely on bathymetric data and initial conditions derived from seismic or buoy observations to simulate the evolution of tsunami waves across ocean basins. While these models are essential for generating broad-scale forecasts, they are fundamentally constrained by their reliance on coarse-resolution bathymetric datasets, simplified assumptions of water column behavior, and the inability to account for small-scale, nonlinear interactions that dominate near-shore dynamics. The computational grids used in these models typically have resolutions of several kilometers, which are insufficient to resolve abrupt changes in seafloor topography such as underwater cliffs, reef structures, or narrow inlets that significantly alter wave refraction, reflection, and amplification. Consequently, model predictions of arrival time and wave height at specific coastal locations often deviate substantially from observed values, particularly in regions with complex coastlines. Moreover, these models require substantial computational resources and time to execute, making real-time forecasting during an evolving event impractical. They also lack the capacity to incorporate real-time, in-situ measurements of surface current velocity, which are directly sensitive to tsunami propagation and provide a more immediate indicator of wave approach than pressure-based measurements. As a result, numerical models alone are inadequate for delivering precise, localized, and timely warnings necessary for effective emergency response.

## SUMMARY

- introduce tsunami detection system  
A novel tsunami detection system has been developed that leverages existing coastal high-frequency (HF) radar networks to provide real-time, near-field detection of approaching tsunamis through the measurement of surface orbital velocity anomalies. This system operates autonomously on radar infrastructure already deployed for ocean current monitoring, transforming passive observational platforms into active early-warning sensors. By analyzing the temporal and spatial coherence of radial current fluctuations across multiple range cells and directional beams, the system identifies the unique signature of tsunami-induced orbital motion, which manifests as correlated, low-frequency oscillations persisting over tens of minutes and propagating shoreward in a manner distinct from background tidal or wind-driven currents. The detection algorithm is calibrated to recognize the characteristic pattern of velocity synchronization across adjacent spatial bands, enabling discrimination between tsunami signals and transient noise or anthropogenic interference. This approach provides a direct, physical measurement of tsunami dynamics in the critical near-shore zone where traditional sensors are blind, offering lead times of up to forty-five minutes depending on local bathymetry.

- receive Bragg peak information  
The system receives raw Doppler spectra from HF radar transmissions reflected off ocean surface gravity waves that satisfy the Bragg resonance condition, wherein the radar wavelength is exactly twice the wavelength of the ocean waves. These reflections produce distinct spectral peaks known as Bragg peaks, whose frequency shift relative to the transmitted signal is directly proportional to the radial component of surface current velocity. The system processes unaveraged, high-temporal-resolution spectral data to extract the precise Doppler shift at each range cell and azimuthal direction, preserving the full dynamic range of surface motion without temporal smoothing that would obscure the slow, coherent signal of a tsunami. The Bragg peak information is continuously acquired at intervals of less than one minute, ensuring that even subtle velocity changes associated with the initial stages of tsunami approach are captured with sufficient fidelity.

- generate tsunami alert  
Upon detection of a statistically significant pattern of synchronized orbital velocity anomalies across multiple adjacent spatial bands, the system generates a tsunami alert. This alert is not triggered by a single threshold crossing but by the emergence of a spatially coherent, temporally persistent, and directionally consistent velocity signature that matches the theoretical and empirically observed characteristics of tsunami-induced surface motion. The alert is encoded with metadata including the time of detection, estimated radial velocity magnitude, spatial extent of the correlated signal, and the calculated direction of propagation relative to the radar site.

- determine credibility of alert  
The credibility of each generated alert is assessed through a multi-layered validation process that evaluates the consistency of the signal with known tsunami physics, the stability of the correlation pattern over successive time intervals, and the absence of confounding artifacts such as radio interference or biological scatter. A quantitative metric known as the q-factor is computed based on the degree of velocity correlation across bands, the temporal persistence of the anomaly, and the alignment of velocity direction with shoreward propagation. The q-factor is then compared against a site-specific, dynamically adjusted threshold that accounts for prevailing environmental conditions, including background current variability and ambient noise levels, to determine whether the alert meets the criteria for high credibility.

- transmit alert to warning center  
When an alert is determined to have high credibility, it is automatically transmitted in real time to regional tsunami warning centers via secure, redundant communication channels. The transmitted data includes the full q-factor time series, the spatial distribution of correlated velocity bands, the estimated propagation vector, and the predicted arrival time window derived from the radar’s coverage area and local bathymetry. The alert is formatted to integrate seamlessly with existing warning infrastructure, enabling rapid dissemination to emergency management agencies and public alert systems.

- ignore alert  
If the q-factor signal fails to meet the credibility threshold, or if corroborating evidence from other sources indicates a non-tsunami origin, the alert is automatically suppressed and logged as a false positive. The system does not transmit such alerts to warning centers, thereby minimizing alarm fatigue and preserving operational trust in the system. The suppression logic is adaptive, learning from historical false alarm events to refine future decision thresholds without compromising detection sensitivity.

- correlate with external sources  
The system continuously correlates its alerts with external data streams, including seismic network notifications, satellite-based atmospheric pressure anomaly reports, and real-time tide gauge readings. When a tsunami alert coincides in time and spatial context with a significant seismic event or a meteotsunami-inducing atmospheric disturbance, the system elevates the alert’s priority and refines its estimated arrival window. This multi-source fusion significantly enhances the reliability of the detection and reduces the likelihood of false alarms arising from isolated radar artifacts.

- estimate arrival time window  
Using the measured orbital velocity and the known bathymetric profile along the radar’s line of sight, the system computes an estimated arrival time window for the tsunami at the coastline. This estimation is derived from the shallow-water wave propagation equations, which relate phase velocity to water depth, and incorporates the observed rate of shoreward progression of the correlated velocity pattern. The arrival window is updated continuously as new data becomes available, narrowing over time as the tsunami approaches and the uncertainty in propagation speed diminishes.

- determine complexity of ocean currents  
The system evaluates the spatial and temporal complexity of the ambient ocean current field by analyzing the variance, spectral content, and spatial coherence of background velocities over the preceding several hours. Regions with highly variable or turbulent currents are flagged as having reduced detection confidence, and the system dynamically adjusts its sensitivity thresholds to maintain a stable false alarm rate. This assessment ensures that the detection algorithm remains robust even in environments where natural current fluctuations might otherwise mimic tsunami signatures.

- estimate height and arrival time  
By applying empirical relationships derived from the linear shallow-water wave equations, the system estimates the tsunami wave height at the coastline based on the magnitude of the observed orbital velocity and the local water depth. The relationship between velocity and height is calibrated using historical tsunami observations and validated against tide gauge measurements. Simultaneously, the system refines its arrival time estimate by integrating the observed velocity gradient across range cells with the bathymetric slope, producing a spatially resolved prediction of when the wave front will reach specific coastal points.

- resolve radial current information  
The system resolves radial current information from each radar’s directional beams, distinguishing between components of motion toward and away from the radar site. This directional resolution enables the reconstruction of the vector field of surface motion, allowing the system to determine whether the observed velocity pattern is consistent with a shoreward-propagating tsunami or an unrelated local phenomenon such as a rip current or wind surge.

- determine orbital velocity  
Orbital velocity is determined by combining radial velocity measurements from multiple radar sites, if available, or by inferring the total surface velocity vector from the spatial gradient of radial components within a single site’s coverage area. The system applies a least-squares fitting algorithm to the radial velocity data across a grid of range-azimuth cells, producing a two-dimensional map of surface current vectors that reveals the coherent, large-scale motion characteristic of a tsunami.

- generate Doppler spectra information  
The system continuously generates high-resolution Doppler spectra from each radar return, preserving the full spectral bandwidth and temporal resolution of the raw signal. These spectra are stored in a rolling buffer and analyzed in real time for the presence of anomalous energy at frequencies corresponding to tsunami periods (20–50 minutes), which lie far below the typical energy band of wind-driven waves.

- remove signal interference  
Signal interference from man-made radio sources, ionospheric reflections, or biological scatterers is identified through spectral anomaly detection and temporal coherence analysis. The system applies adaptive filtering techniques to suppress narrowband interference, while rejecting broadband artifacts that lack the spatial and temporal correlation expected of a genuine tsunami signal. Interference events are logged and used to update site-specific noise profiles, improving future discrimination.

- extract Bragg peak information  
The system isolates the Bragg peak from the Doppler spectrum using a peak detection algorithm that identifies the dominant spectral component at the expected frequency offset for surface wave resonance. The amplitude and frequency of the Bragg peak are extracted with sub-hertz precision, enabling accurate calculation of radial current velocity even under low signal-to-noise conditions.

- generate tsunami alert  
The system generates a tsunami alert when the extracted Bragg peak information exhibits a sustained, spatially correlated pattern of velocity change consistent with tsunami propagation. This alert is triggered only after multiple independent criteria are satisfied, including the persistence of the signal over multiple time steps, the alignment of velocity vectors with shoreward motion, and the absence of competing explanations such as interference or local current anomalies.

- determine credibility of alert  
The credibility of the alert is determined by evaluating the statistical significance of the q-factor, the spatial extent of correlated velocity bands, and the temporal stability of the signal. The system compares the observed pattern against a library of known tsunami signatures and false alarm events, assigning a confidence score that reflects the likelihood that the signal represents a genuine tsunami. Alerts with low confidence scores are discarded without transmission.

- correlate with stored correlations  
The system cross-references the detected signal against a database of previously observed tsunami events and known false alarm patterns. This stored correlation library, built from historical radar data collected during past tsunamis and non-tsunami events, enables the system to recognize subtle features unique to genuine tsunami signatures, improving discrimination accuracy over time.

- transmit alert to warning center  
When an alert is confirmed with high credibility, it is transmitted to national and regional tsunami warning centers using encrypted, low-latency communication protocols. The message includes the full set of processed data, including velocity maps, q-factor trends, estimated arrival times, and confidence metrics, enabling warning center operators to make informed decisions with minimal delay.

- ignore alert  
Alerts that fail to meet the credibility threshold, or that are contradicted by corroborating data from other sensors, are automatically ignored. The system does not generate public alerts or notifications in these cases, preserving the integrity of the warning system and preventing unnecessary public disruption.

- correlate with nearby radar systems  
The system evaluates whether similar tsunami signatures are detected simultaneously or sequentially by neighboring HF radar systems within a defined spatial and temporal window. If multiple radars detect a coherent, propagating signal aligned with tsunami physics, the system increases the alert’s credibility exponentially. Conversely, if a signal is detected by only one radar without corroborating evidence, it is downgraded or discarded as a local anomaly.

Credibility is assessed using a multi-dimensional scoring function that integrates the q-factor, signal-to-noise ratio, spatial coherence, and alignment with known tsunami dynamics.

- correlate with stored correlations  
The system compares the detected signal against a database of previously recorded tsunami and false alarm events to enhance classification accuracy.

- transmit alert to warning center  
High-credibility alerts are transmitted in real time to designated tsunami warning centers using secure, redundant communication channels.

- ignore alert  
Alerts that fail to meet predefined credibility thresholds are suppressed and logged for post-event analysis.

- correlate with nearby radar systems  
The system evaluates whether neighboring HF radar systems detect a similar signal within a defined spatial and temporal window, significantly increasing alert credibility when multiple systems concur.

- estimate arrival time window  
The system computes the estimated arrival time window using the observed propagation speed of the velocity anomaly and the detailed bathymetric profile along the radar’s line of sight.

- determine complexity of ocean currents  
The system quantifies the complexity of ambient ocean currents using statistical metrics derived from background velocity variability, dynamically adjusting detection thresholds to maintain optimal sensitivity.

- estimate height and arrival time  
The system estimates tsunami wave height using the empirically derived relationship between orbital velocity and wave amplitude under shallow-water conditions, and simultaneously refines the arrival time estimate based on the observed rate of shoreward progression.

- generate Doppler spectra information  
The system continuously generates and archives high-resolution Doppler spectra for every range cell and azimuthal direction, preserving the full dynamic range of surface motion for forensic analysis and algorithm training.

- remove signal interference  
Interference from man-made sources, ionospheric reflections, or biological scatterers is identified and removed using adaptive spectral filtering and spatial decorrelation techniques.

- extract Bragg peak information  
The system isolates the Bragg peak from each Doppler spectrum with sub-hertz precision, enabling accurate determination of radial current velocity even under low signal-to-noise conditions.
