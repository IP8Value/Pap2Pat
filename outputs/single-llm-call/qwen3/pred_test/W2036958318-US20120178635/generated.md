## BACKGROUND

Chromosomal translocations represent a class of structural genomic alterations in which segments of DNA from two distinct chromosomes are exchanged, often resulting in the formation of novel fusion genes with aberrant regulatory or functional properties. These rearrangements are frequently associated with the pathogenesis of hematologic malignancies and certain solid tumors, where the juxtaposition of normally unrelated genetic elements leads to constitutive activation of oncogenic pathways or loss of tumor suppressor function. One of the most well-characterized examples is the Philadelphia chromosome, a reciprocal translocation between chromosomes 9 and 22, denoted as t(9;22)(q34;q11). This translocation generates a chimeric BCR-ABL1 fusion gene, which encodes a constitutively active tyrosine kinase that drives uncontrolled proliferation of myeloid progenitor cells and is the molecular hallmark of chronic myeloid leukemia. The breakpoints within the ABL1 gene typically occur within a large intronic region upstream of the tyrosine kinase domain, while those in the BCR gene are clustered within a 5.8-kilobase segment known as the major breakpoint cluster region, which accounts for the majority of cases in chronic myeloid leukemia and a subset of Philadelphia chromosome-positive B-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia. Despite the clinical significance of this translocation, current diagnostic methods remain limited in their ability to provide precise, stable, and sensitive detection of the underlying DNA rearrangement. Conventional karyotyping requires actively dividing cells and is constrained by low resolution, typically detecting alterations larger than five megabases, and is therefore unsuitable for routine use with peripheral blood samples. Fluorescent in situ hybridization improves upon this by enabling analysis of non-dividing cells but lacks the resolution to pinpoint exact breakpoint locations and is subject to signal attenuation due to fixation artifacts. Real-time reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction, while highly sensitive for detecting fusion transcripts, is inherently dependent on RNA integrity and is vulnerable to degradation during sample handling, storage, or from transcriptional silencing in residual disease states. Furthermore, RNA-based assays cannot distinguish between transcriptionally active and inactive fusion alleles, nor can they reliably detect the presence of the fusion at the genomic level in cases where the transcript is absent despite the persistence of the DNA rearrangement. The genetic heterogeneity of translocation breakpoints among patients further complicates the development of universal diagnostic assays, as the precise location of the junction varies between individuals and may influence disease progression or response to therapy. There remains a critical unmet need for a robust, DNA-based method capable of identifying and characterizing translocation breakpoints with single-base-pair resolution, independent of cell viability or transcriptional activity, and amenable to high-throughput, multiplexed analysis across multiple patient samples.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The invention disclosed herein introduces a novel method for the detection, characterization, and monitoring of chromosomal structural variations through the use of Anchored Chromosomal Paired-End Tags, a technique that integrates targeted genomic enrichment, high-throughput paired-end sequencing, and computational breakpoint prediction to generate patient-specific DNA biomarkers of translocation events. The method begins with the selective capture of a genomic region of interest using a biotinylated RNA bait designed to hybridize specifically to sequences flanking a known translocation hotspot, such as the major breakpoint cluster region of the BCR gene. This enrichment step is followed by the construction of a chromosomal paired-end tag library from fragmented genomic DNA, wherein each DNA fragment is ligated to a Y-shaped adapter containing a unique molecular barcode that enables multiplexing of multiple samples within a single sequencing run. The resulting library is subjected to paired-end sequencing, generating short sequence reads from both termini of each fragment. Bioinformatic analysis then maps these paired reads to the reference genome, distinguishing between normal chromosomal linkages and aberrant junctional chromosomal paired-end tags that span the translocation breakpoint. A proprietary algorithm is employed to predict the precise location of the breakpoint by aggregating positional votes from each junctional tag, weighted according to the expected fragment size distribution derived from normal chromosomal linkages. This predictive model enables the design of patient-specific polymerase chain reaction primers that flank the identified breakpoint, allowing for highly sensitive and specific amplification of the fusion junction from minute quantities of genomic DNA. The stability of DNA relative to RNA ensures that this biomarker remains intact under conditions that degrade RNA, including formalin fixation, prolonged storage, or exposure to serum, making it uniquely suited for retrospective analysis of archived clinical specimens and for detection of circulating tumor DNA in body fluids. The method is scalable to any chromosomal region of interest and can be adapted for the detection of structural variations in solid tumors, microorganisms, or animal models. The invention further encompasses a diagnostic kit comprising the reagents necessary for targeted enrichment, library preparation, sequencing, and breakpoint-specific amplification, along with instructional material for implementation in clinical laboratories. The ability to generate a durable, sequence-defined DNA biomarker provides a foundation for precise diagnosis, longitudinal monitoring of minimal residual disease, early detection of recurrence, and personalized therapeutic decision-making, thereby overcoming the limitations of existing RNA- or cytogenetics-based approaches.

## EMBODIMENTS

Chromosomal translocations are structural rearrangements in which segments of DNA from two non-homologous chromosomes are exchanged, often resulting in the creation of novel fusion genes with oncogenic potential. The detection of such events has long been a cornerstone of cancer diagnostics, yet traditional methods such as karyotyping and fluorescence in situ hybridization are limited by low resolution, dependence on cell division, and inability to precisely define breakpoint locations. Paired-end mapping techniques offer a powerful alternative by simultaneously sequencing both ends of fragmented genomic DNA, enabling the identification of discordant linkages that indicate structural variation. The basic principle of paired-end-tag sequencing involves fragmenting genomic DNA, ligating adapters to both ends, and sequencing each terminus to generate a pair of short reads that, when mapped to a reference genome, reveal the distance and orientation of the original fragment. Aberrant ChromPETs are identified when the two ends map to non-contiguous regions of the genome, suggesting a translocation, inversion, or other rearrangement. To generate PCR primers for amplifying the junctional fragment, the coordinates of the paired-end tags that span the breakpoint are analyzed to determine the precise location of the fusion junction. This approach has been validated in the yeast genome, where ChromPET analysis successfully identified gross chromosomal rearrangements, including insertions of Ty retrotransposons and deletions within coding regions. A global approach to identifying structural variation involves the systematic analysis of millions of paired-end tags across the entire genome, filtering out artifacts such as chimeric products generated during library preparation. Bioinformatic analysis proceeds by extracting flanking sequences from each ChromPET, mapping them to the reference genome, and identifying those that exhibit non-canonical linkages. Unique ChromPETs are selected based on their ability to map uniquely to a single genomic location, and a profile of aberrant linkages is generated to highlight regions of recurrent structural disruption. To overcome the challenge of short read lengths and repetitive sequences, multiple independent paired-end tags are required to confidently define a breakpoint, and the density of abnormal tags is calculated across sliding windows of the genome. Regions exhibiting a statistically significant enrichment of abnormal tags are flagged as candidate breakpoints. Lower cutoff thresholds are applied to generate an initial list of candidate windows, and partner windows are tabulated to identify potential translocation partners. Abnormal linkages are further evaluated for biological plausibility, and thresholds are relaxed to include low-frequency events that may represent rare subclonal variants. The method is then applied to detect translocations in specific chromosomal regions, such as chromosome V in yeast models, and to identify insertions of mobile elements into coding sequences. In human applications, the ChromPET method is modified to focus on a region of interest, such as the major breakpoint cluster region of the BCR gene, by using array comparative genomic hybridization to define the target locus, followed by PCR purification and conversion into a biotinylated RNA bait. This bait is hybridized to a fragmented genomic DNA library, and the bound DNA is captured on streptavidin-coated beads, eliminating repetitive sequences through the inclusion of excess Cot-1 DNA. The enriched DNA is converted to double-stranded form, ligated to sequencing adaptors, and subjected to paired-end sequencing. Aberrant ChromPETs are identified computationally, and PCR primers are designed to flank the predicted breakpoint. Amplification of the junctional fragment from test DNA enables the detection of the translocation, and comparison of results across samples identifies patient-specific biomarkers. This method is applicable to the diagnosis of diseases associated with chromosomal structural variations, including chronic myeloid leukemia, and enables the design of personalized diagnostic kits containing reagents for enrichment, sequencing, and amplification. Primers can be tailored to specific cancer types, and the method supports the implementation of personalized medicine by providing a durable, sequence-defined biomarker for longitudinal monitoring.

## EXAMPLES

### Reagents

The reagents used in the practice of this invention included APex Heat-Labile Alkaline Phosphatase, Biotin-16-UTP, DNAZol reagent, Dynabeads M-280 streptavidin, End-It DNA End Repair Kit, human Cot-1 DNA, MAXIscript Kit, MinElute Reaction Cleanup Kit, pCR4-TOPO-TA vector, QIAquick Gel Extraction Kit, QIAquick PCR Purification Kit, QuickExtract FFPE DNA Extraction Kit, QuickExtract FFPE RNA Extraction Kit, Quick Ligation Kit, SuperScript III Reverse Transcriptase, TaKaRa Ex Taq DNA Polymerase, Taq DNA Polymerase, TRIzol, and TURBO DNase. Cell lines utilized included K562 and KU812, both derived from human chronic myeloid leukemia and obtained from the American Type Culture Collection. Patient samples were collected from individuals diagnosed with Philadelphia chromosome-positive chronic myeloid leukemia under institutional review board approval, with peripheral blood mononuclear cells isolated by Ficoll gradient centrifugation and genomic DNA extracted using the DNeasy Blood and Tissue Kit.

The percentage of chromPETs successfully assigned to each sample via barcode sequencing was approximately 5% for K562, 45% for KU812, 15% for PS1, 45% for PS2, and 6% for PS3, reflecting variability in barcoding efficiency.

### Prediction And Validation Of Translocation Breakpoints In Cml Cell Lines

The bioinformatics algorithm successfully predicted the breakpoint in the K562 cell line at the precise location previously documented in the literature. PCR primers designed to flank this region amplified a fragment exclusively from K562 genomic DNA and not from normal lung DNA. Sequencing of the amplified product confirmed the exact nucleotide sequence of the BCR-ABL1 fusion junction. Similarly, in KU812 cells, the algorithm predicted a breakpoint in the BCR locus that matched the known fusion junction, and a reciprocal ABL1-BCR translocation was also identified and validated by sequencing. These results demonstrate that Anchored ChromPET can accurately detect both primary and reciprocal translocation events in a targeted genomic region.

### Prediction And Validation Of Translocation Breakpoints In Patient Samples

In patient sample PS1, the algorithm predicted a breakpoint in the BCR locus at genomic position 23,632,758, which was confirmed by PCR amplification and sequencing of the junctional fragment. In PS2, the predicted breakpoint at position 23,632,892 was similarly validated. PCR amplification of the corresponding junctional region was successful in both patients but absent in normal controls. In PS3, despite the presence of a small number of junctional chromPETs, no single peak in the breakpoint prediction profile was observed, and no junctional fragment was amplified by PCR, indicating that these signals were likely due to contamination during library preparation. This underscores the robustness of the algorithm in distinguishing true translocations from artifacts.

### Comparison Of Sensitivity: Dna Or Rna

A dilution series of K562 cells mixed with HCT116 cells demonstrated that the DNA-based junctional biomarker could be detected at a sensitivity of 0.01% tumor cell content, equivalent to the sensitivity of RNA-based RT-PCR under ideal laboratory conditions. When analyzing formalin-fixed cells, the DNA biomarker was recovered at fivefold higher efficiency than the RNA transcript. In cell culture medium, the DNA junctional fragment remained detectable after 72 hours at 37°C, while the RNA transcript was undetectable, demonstrating superior stability of the DNA biomarker in extracellular environments.

### Advantages Of Anchored Chrompet

Anchored ChromPET provides a digital karyotype with resolution at the single-base-pair level, surpassing the five-megabase resolution of conventional karyotyping and the 50- to 100-kilobase resolution of interphase FISH. Unlike RT-PCR, which is vulnerable to RNA degradation and transcriptional silencing, Anchored ChromPET detects the genomic DNA rearrangement, which persists regardless of transcriptional activity. The method requires no cell culture and can be applied to peripheral blood, formalin-fixed tissues, or circulating cell-free DNA. With the ability to multiplex up to ten samples per sequencing lane, the cost per sample is significantly lower than whole-genome sequencing. The method is scalable to any chromosomal region and can be adapted for the detection of translocations in solid tumors, where cell culture is not feasible.

### Computational Prediction Of Breakpoint

The algorithm’s predictive accuracy was validated across all samples, with the predicted breakpoint matching the sequenced junction in four out of five cases. The maximum deviation was 144 base pairs, well within the range of PCR primer design tolerance. In the case of PS3, the absence of a consensus breakpoint despite the presence of junctional tags confirmed the algorithm’s ability to reject false positives arising from contamination.

### Other Methods For Sequencing The Dna Translocation Junction

Previous methods relied on exon-specific PCR with nested primers or extensive primer walking to identify BCR-ABL1 junctions, requiring prior knowledge of the breakpoint location and limiting detection to small regions. Anchored ChromPET eliminates this constraint by enabling unbiased interrogation of entire gene loci, such as the full 135-kilobase BCR gene, without prior knowledge of the breakpoint.

### Rna Bait Preparation

The RNA bait used in this invention was prepared by PCR amplification of the M-BCR region, followed by in vitro transcription with biotin-labeled UTP and DNase digestion to remove the DNA template. This method is cost-effective and scalable, enabling the preparation of baits for larger genomic regions without the prohibitive expense of synthetic oligonucleotides.

### Translocation Junctions

Analysis of the breakpoint regions revealed that DNA sequence was frequently lost at the ABL1 locus, likely due to exonuclease activity following chromosomal breakage. In contrast, duplication of BCR sequence was observed in KU812 and PS1, possibly due to the formation of a cruciform structure that stabilized the broken ends and facilitated error-prone repair. The presence of stem-loop structures in the BCR breakpoint region, predicted with a Gibbs free energy of -88.96 kcal/mol, supports the hypothesis that secondary DNA structures contribute to genomic instability at this locus.

## CONCLUSIONS

The detection of the BCR-ABL1 fusion gene remains essential for the diagnosis and monitoring of chronic myeloid leukemia. Current methods, including karyotyping, FISH, and RT-PCR, are limited by low resolution, dependence on cell viability, or susceptibility to RNA degradation. The invention of Anchored ChromPET overcomes these limitations by enabling the precise identification of translocation breakpoints at the DNA level, with single-base-pair resolution, from minute quantities of genomic material. The method is highly sensitive, capable of detecting one leukemic cell among ten thousand normal cells, and produces a stable, sequence-defined biomarker that persists in formalin-fixed tissues and circulating cell-free DNA. The ability to multiplex multiple samples in a single sequencing run reduces cost and increases throughput, making it suitable for clinical implementation. Anchored ChromPET is not limited to CML but is broadly applicable to any disease caused by chromosomal translocations, including solid tumors and rare genetic disorders. By providing a durable, patient-specific DNA biomarker, this method enables accurate diagnosis, reliable monitoring of minimal residual disease, early detection of recurrence, and personalized therapeutic strategies, establishing a new standard for molecular diagnostics in oncology.
