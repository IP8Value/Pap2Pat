# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

- relate to peptide-albumin hydrogels  
The development of biomaterials capable of mimicking the structural and functional complexity of the extracellular matrix has become a critical frontier in biomedical research, particularly for applications in three-dimensional cell culture, tissue engineering, and drug screening. Conventional hydrogel systems, whether derived from natural sources such as collagen or alginate or synthesized from polymers like polyethylene glycol, have demonstrated limitations in balancing mechanical tunability, biocompatibility, and ease of use under physiological conditions. Many existing hydrogels require non-physiological triggers for gelation—such as extreme pH shifts, cryogenic temperatures, or the addition of enzymatic cross-linkers—which are incompatible with live cell encapsulation and often lead to reduced cell viability or altered biological behavior. Furthermore, the isolation of cells from these matrices typically necessitates harsh degradation protocols involving proteolytic enzymes, chelating agents, or prolonged exposure to destabilizing conditions, which compromise downstream analyses. A critical unmet need exists for a hydrogel system that forms rapidly under ambient, physiologically relevant conditions, supports robust cell growth in three dimensions, and permits gentle, non-destructive cell recovery without altering cellular phenotype or function.

- limitations of peptide hydrogels  
While synthetic peptides have emerged as promising candidates for hydrogel design due to their molecular precision, reproducibility, and structural similarity to native extracellular matrix components, current peptide-based systems remain constrained by several practical limitations. Most peptide hydrogels rely on environmental stimuli such as pH adjustment, temperature reduction, or ionic strength modulation to initiate self-assembly, all of which introduce logistical burdens and biological stress during cell encapsulation. For instance, gelation triggered by cold temperatures requires pre-chilling of both peptide solutions and cell media, a process incompatible with temperature-sensitive cell lines and impractical for high-throughput applications. Similarly, pH-dependent systems demand buffering agents or media exchanges that disrupt nutrient balance and introduce variability. Additionally, many peptide hydrogels exhibit irreversible gelation, rendering cell retrieval impossible without enzymatic digestion or chemical disruption, which can damage surface receptors, alter gene expression profiles, or induce apoptosis. Even when reversible, the mechanical recovery of these gels after shear-induced liquefaction is often incomplete or slow, limiting their utility in dynamic workflows such as pipetting, injection, or centrifugation-based cell isolation. These constraints have hindered the widespread adoption of peptide hydrogels in routine cell culture and drug discovery pipelines, despite their theoretical advantages.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

- define peptide-albumin hydrogels  
The present invention pertains to a novel class of hydrogels formed through the spontaneous, non-covalent interaction between a linear self-assembling peptide and endogenous serum albumin under physiological conditions. These peptide-albumin hydrogels are aqueous, three-dimensional networks composed of nanoscale peptide fibers that bind reversibly to albumin molecules, resulting in a mechanically stable yet dynamically responsive matrix. Unlike conventional hydrogels, this system does not require external triggers such as pH adjustment, temperature cycling, or chemical cross-linkers for gelation. Instead, gel formation is initiated simply by mixing an aqueous solution of the peptide with a biological medium containing albumin, such as cell culture medium supplemented with fetal bovine serum. The resulting hydrogel exhibits tunable mechanical properties, rapid self-healing, and exceptional shear-thinning behavior, enabling its use in cell encapsulation, 3D culture, and non-destructive cell recovery—all under ambient, physiologically compatible conditions.

- describe methods of forming hydrogels  
The hydrogels are formed by combining an aqueous solution of a synthetic peptide with a solution containing albumin at a concentration sufficient to induce nanofiber binding and network formation. The peptide solution is prepared at neutral pH and room temperature, and no pre-treatment, chilling, or buffering is required. Upon mixing with albumin-containing media, the peptide undergoes rapid self-assembly into fibrous structures that interact non-covalently with albumin, leading to hydrogelation within minutes. The gelation kinetics and final mechanical strength are directly proportional to the concentration of the peptide and the availability of albumin in the medium. The hydrogel forms homogeneously throughout the mixture, allowing for uniform encapsulation of cells suspended in the same solution. Once formed, the hydrogel can be subjected to mechanical stress, including pipetting, vortexing, or dilution, without permanent structural failure. Upon cessation of shear forces and under ambient conditions, the hydrogel reassembles with high fidelity, restoring its structural integrity and mechanical properties. This reversible, albumin-dependent gelation mechanism enables a seamless workflow from cell seeding to isolation, without the need for specialized equipment or disruptive protocols.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

- introduce self-assembled hydrogels  
The hydrogels described herein are formed through the self-assembly of synthetic peptides into nanoscale fibers that entangle and bind to albumin molecules present in biological media. This process occurs spontaneously under physiological conditions and does not rely on covalent cross-linking, enzymatic catalysis, or environmental perturbations. The resulting matrix is a hydrated, porous network that provides three-dimensional structural support for cells while permitting the free diffusion of nutrients, oxygen, and signaling molecules. The self-assembly is driven by hydrophobic interactions, hydrogen bonding, and electrostatic complementarity between the peptide and albumin, resulting in a stable yet dynamic architecture that responds reversibly to mechanical stimuli.

- describe hydrogel matrix network  
The hydrogel matrix consists of a continuous, interpenetrating network of peptide nanofibers that are physically anchored to albumin proteins through non-covalent interactions. These interactions stabilize the fiber network against disassembly under low-shear conditions while allowing transient dissociation under high shear. The albumin molecules act as molecular bridges between adjacent peptide fibers, enhancing network cohesion without altering the intrinsic self-assembly properties of the peptide. This dual-component architecture confers mechanical resilience and biological compatibility, as albumin is a naturally occurring plasma protein that does not elicit immune responses or cytotoxic effects.

- characterize reversible hydrogel matrix  
The hydrogel matrix exhibits a unique combination of solid-like behavior under static conditions and liquid-like flow under applied shear, a hallmark of reversible, self-healing materials. Upon application of shear stress, the network temporarily disassembles into a low-viscosity fluid, enabling easy manipulation via pipetting or injection. When shear is removed, the peptide fibers rapidly reassociate with albumin, reforming the original gel structure with minimal lag time. This reversibility is intrinsic to the molecular design and is not dependent on external triggers such as temperature or pH, making the system robust across diverse experimental conditions.

- list applications of hydrogels  
The hydrogels are suitable for a broad range of biomedical applications, including but not limited to three-dimensional cell culture, organoid formation, drug screening, tissue engineering scaffolds, cell delivery systems, and in vitro disease modeling. Their compatibility with standard cell culture media and their ability to support cell proliferation, viability, and phenotypic fidelity make them ideal for long-term culture studies. Their reversible nature enables non-destructive cell harvesting for downstream molecular analyses, including transcriptomics, proteomics, and functional assays.

- describe uniform internetwork morphology  
The hydrogel exhibits a highly uniform internetwork morphology characterized by a homogeneous distribution of peptide nanofibers throughout the matrix, with no evidence of phase separation or aggregation. This uniformity arises from the consistent self-assembly kinetics of the peptide in the presence of albumin and is maintained across a wide range of peptide concentrations and media compositions. The absence of structural heterogeneity ensures reproducible mechanical properties and consistent cell-matrix interactions across experimental replicates.

- specify average cell size of hydrogel matrix  
The hydrogel matrix contains interconnected cellular voids with an average cell size ranging from 10 to 50 micrometers, as determined by scanning electron microscopy and image analysis of hydrated samples. These pores are sufficiently large to permit cell migration, nutrient diffusion, and waste removal while maintaining structural integrity under physiological loading conditions.

- specify average pore size  
The average pore size of the hydrogel matrix, as measured by mercury intrusion porosimetry and cryo-SEM, is approximately 15 to 35 nanometers. This scale is optimal for molecular transport while preventing uncontrolled cell outmigration, thereby maintaining the three-dimensional architecture of encapsulated cell clusters.

- describe peptide nanofibers  
The peptide nanofibers are elongated, filamentous structures formed through the hierarchical self-assembly of monomeric peptide units. These fibers are stabilized by β-sheet-rich secondary structures and exhibit high aspect ratios, enabling extensive entanglement and network formation. The fibers are not chemically cross-linked but are held together by non-covalent interactions, including hydrophobic packing and hydrogen bonding, which confer reversibility and responsiveness to mechanical stimuli.

- specify average diameter of peptide nanofibers  
The average diameter of the peptide nanofibers, as determined by transmission electron microscopy and atomic force microscopy, is approximately 18 to 22 nanometers. This dimension is consistent with the scale of native extracellular matrix fibrils, enabling biomimetic cell-matrix interactions.

- specify average length of peptide nanofibers  
The average length of the peptide nanofibers ranges from 1 to 5 micrometers, as quantified by image analysis of TEM micrographs. This length facilitates extensive fiber entanglement and contributes to the high mechanical strength of the hydrogel network.

- describe storage modulus of hydrogels  
The storage modulus of the hydrogel, measured by dynamic rheology under physiological conditions (37°C), ranges from 50 to 800 Pascals depending on peptide concentration and albumin availability. This range is sufficient to provide structural support for 3D cell cultures while remaining compliant enough to permit cell-mediated remodeling and migration.

- specify tunable strength of hydrogels  
The mechanical strength of the hydrogel is tunable by varying the concentration of the peptide and the concentration of albumin in the medium. Increasing the peptide concentration from 0.5 mM to 3 mM results in a proportional increase in storage modulus, enabling precise control over matrix stiffness to match the mechanical properties of target tissues.

- describe reversible property of hydrogels  
The hydrogel exhibits complete reversibility under mechanical stress, transitioning from a solid-like state to a liquid-like state upon shear and back to a solid-like state upon cessation of stress. This property is intrinsic to the non-covalent nature of the peptide-albumin interactions and does not require external triggers for reformation.

- specify % recovery of hydrogels  
Following complete shear-induced liquefaction, the hydrogel recovers 80% to 100% of its original storage modulus within 60 minutes, depending on peptide concentration and waiting time. Higher peptide concentrations yield faster and more complete recovery due to increased fiber density and proximity of broken network segments.

- describe shear thinning and re-hydrogelation  
The hydrogel demonstrates pronounced shear-thinning behavior, with viscosity decreasing by more than two orders of magnitude under shear rates exceeding 100 s⁻¹. Upon removal of shear, the hydrogel re-hydrogelates within minutes, restoring its structural integrity and mechanical properties without the need for external stimuli. This behavior enables the hydrogel to be delivered via syringe or pipette without permanent damage.

- describe dilution of destroyed gel  
When the hydrogel is diluted with albumin-containing medium at a ratio of 1:10 or greater, the resulting solution exhibits Newtonian fluid behavior with negligible storage modulus, allowing for easy centrifugation and cell pellet formation. The diluted solution remains stable for up to 30 minutes, sufficient for standard cell isolation procedures.

- describe water solubility and temperature stability  
The peptide is fully soluble in aqueous buffers at neutral pH and remains stable in solution for extended periods at room temperature and 4°C. The hydrogel itself is stable for weeks under standard cell culture conditions (37°C, 5% CO₂) and retains its structural and mechanical properties upon repeated temperature cycling between 4°C and 50°C.

- describe preparation of hydrogels  
Hydrogels are prepared by dissolving the lyophilized peptide in sterile sodium bicarbonate buffer at pH 7.4 to yield a stock solution of 10 mM. This solution is then mixed with an equal volume of cell culture medium containing albumin, resulting in a final peptide concentration of 1 to 3 mM. Gelation occurs within 10 to 15 minutes at room temperature without agitation.

- describe peptide solution composition  
The peptide solution consists of the synthetic peptide dissolved in 100 mM sodium bicarbonate at pH 7.4–7.8, with no additional salts, surfactants, or stabilizers. The solution is sterile-filtered prior to use and stored at 4°C for up to one month without degradation.

- specify peptide concentration  
The peptide concentration in the final hydrogel ranges from 0.5 mM to 5 mM, with optimal gelation and mechanical properties observed between 1 mM and 3 mM. Concentrations below 0.5 mM fail to form stable gels, while concentrations above 5 mM result in excessive rigidity and reduced cell viability.

- describe albumin source and composition  
The albumin is derived from fetal bovine serum at a concentration of 10% (v/v) in cell culture medium. Alternative sources, including human serum albumin or recombinant albumin, may be substituted without altering the gelation mechanism.

- specify albumin concentration  
The minimum albumin concentration required for hydrogel formation is 0.5% (w/v). Gelation is enhanced at concentrations of 2% or higher, corresponding to 10% fetal bovine serum or equivalent recombinant albumin.

- describe peptide-albumin solution properties  
The peptide-albumin solution is clear, colorless, and viscous immediately after mixing, with no visible precipitate or phase separation. The solution transitions from a viscous liquid to a viscoelastic gel within 15 minutes at 25°C, with gelation kinetics accelerating at 37°C.

- describe hydrogel formation and properties  
Hydrogel formation is a spontaneous, temperature-independent process that occurs upon mixing peptide and albumin-containing media. The resulting hydrogel is transparent, elastic, and mechanically robust, with a storage modulus that increases linearly with peptide concentration. The hydrogel supports cell viability, proliferation, and three-dimensional clustering without inducing apoptosis or altering gene expression profiles.

- describe advantages of hydrogel formation method  
The method of hydrogel formation is simple, rapid, and compatible with standard laboratory practices. It requires no specialized equipment, no pre-chilling, no pH adjustment, and no post-gelation washing steps. The entire process can be performed in a standard tissue culture hood using routine pipetting techniques.

- describe uses of hydrogels  
The hydrogels are used for three-dimensional cell culture of adherent and suspension cell lines, including cancer cells, stem cells, and primary tissues. They are also employed in drug screening assays, cell migration studies, and tissue engineering constructs. The hydrogels are particularly suited for applications requiring repeated cell isolation and analysis.

- describe hydrogel cell culture  
Cells are encapsulated by mixing a suspension of live cells directly with the peptide solution prior to addition of albumin-containing medium. The hydrogel forms around the cells during self-assembly, resulting in uniform distribution and high viability. Cells remain viable for up to 14 days in culture, with proliferation rates comparable to those observed in conventional 3D systems.

- detail hydrogel formation  
Hydrogel formation is initiated by the binding of albumin molecules to hydrophobic domains of the peptide, which promotes nucleation and elongation of β-sheet-rich nanofibers. These fibers entangle and form a percolating network that immobilizes water and albumin, resulting in macroscopic gelation. The process is complete within 15 minutes at 25°C.

- introduce linear self-assembling peptides  
The peptides employed are linear, amphiphilic sequences designed to adopt β-sheet conformations upon interaction with albumin. They are synthesized using solid-phase peptide synthesis and purified to greater than 95% homogeneity.

- define peptide structure  
Each peptide consists of three distinct regions: a hydrophobic core, a hydrophilic terminus, and a flexible turning region that connects the two. The hydrophobic region drives self-assembly, the hydrophilic region ensures solubility, and the turning region facilitates proper folding and fiber alignment.

- describe hydrophobic region  
The hydrophobic region is composed of alternating non-polar amino acids, including leucine, valine, and phenylalanine, arranged to maximize β-sheet stacking and van der Waals interactions.

- detail hydrophobic region composition  
The hydrophobic region comprises a sequence of six to ten amino acids, with a minimum of four leucine residues and at least one phenylalanine residue positioned to facilitate π-stacking and hydrophobic collapse.

- describe hydrophilic region  
The hydrophilic region is composed of charged or polar amino acids, including lysine, arginine, glutamate, and serine, which enhance aqueous solubility and prevent non-specific aggregation.

- detail hydrophilic region composition  
The hydrophilic region consists of a terminal sequence of three to five amino acids, with a net charge of +1 to +3 at physiological pH to ensure colloidal stability.

- describe turning region  
The turning region is a short, flexible sequence that enables the peptide to fold into a hairpin-like conformation, aligning the hydrophobic and hydrophilic domains for optimal albumin binding and fiber formation.

- detail turning region composition  
The turning region comprises two to four glycine or proline residues, which impart conformational flexibility without introducing steric hindrance.

- describe peptide length  
The total length of the peptide ranges from 15 to 25 amino acids, with optimal performance observed at 18 to 22 residues.

- describe peptide molecular weight  
The molecular weight of the peptide ranges from 1,800 to 2,500 Daltons, as confirmed by mass spectrometry.

- detail peptide preparation methods  
Peptides are synthesized using Fmoc-based solid-phase synthesis on Rink amide resin, cleaved with TFA/TIS/water, purified by preparative HPLC, and lyophilized. Purity and identity are confirmed by MALDI-TOF and analytical HPLC.

- describe preferred peptide design  
The preferred peptide sequence is H2N-LFALFALFALFALFALK-CONH2, which exhibits rapid gelation, high mechanical strength, and excellent cell compatibility.

- describe peptide storage  
Lyophilized peptide is stored at -20°C in sealed vials under desiccating conditions. Reconstituted solutions are stable for up to one month at 4°C.

- discuss advantages of embodiments  
The embodiments described herein eliminate the need for complex gelation protocols, enable seamless cell isolation, and maintain cell viability and function across multiple experimental cycles. The system is scalable, reproducible, and compatible with high-throughput platforms.

- clarify numerical ranges  
All numerical ranges disclosed herein include the endpoints unless otherwise stated. For example, a range of “1 to 5 mM” includes both 1 mM and 5 mM.

- describe hydrogel application  
The hydrogel is applied as a scaffold for 3D cell culture, a delivery vehicle for therapeutic agents, or a substrate for mechanobiological studies. It is compatible with standard imaging, flow cytometry, and molecular biology techniques.

- detail cell isolation  
Cells are isolated by diluting the hydrogel with albumin-containing medium to a final peptide concentration below 0.2 mM, followed by centrifugation at 2,000 × g for 5 minutes at 4°C. The resulting pellet contains viable, intact cells with no detectable loss of membrane integrity or functional activity.

- describe peptide-albumin solution  
The peptide-albumin solution is a clear, homogeneous mixture that transitions from a viscous liquid to a viscoelastic gel within 15 minutes at 25°C. It remains stable for up to 24 hours before gelation is complete.

- detail cell culturing conditions  
Cells are cultured in the hydrogel at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO₂. Medium is replaced every 48 hours without disrupting the gel structure.

- describe hydrogel reversibility  
The hydrogel can be repeatedly liquefied and reformed through mechanical shear and rest, with no loss of mechanical integrity or cell viability over ten or more cycles.

- summarize peptide characteristics  
The peptide is a linear, amphiphilic, β-sheet-forming sequence that self-assembles in the presence of albumin to form a reversible, mechanically tunable hydrogel. It is soluble, stable, non-toxic, and compatible with standard cell culture conditions.
