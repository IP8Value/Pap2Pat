# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD

- relate to photovoltaic array operation

The present invention relates to the operation of photovoltaic (PV) arrays in real-world environmental conditions, particularly those involving dynamic and partial shading events that degrade power output efficiency. It encompasses systems and methods for autonomously reconfiguring the electrical interconnections among individual photovoltaic panels within an array to maximize power generation under varying irradiance distributions. The invention integrates cyber-physical infrastructure, including panel-level sensors, relay-based switching mechanisms, and machine learning-driven decision-making, to enable real-time topology optimization without requiring physical relocation of panels or addition of external components. The system operates within a closed-loop control framework wherein real-time irradiance measurements are processed by a trained deep neural network to select the optimal electrical configuration from a predefined set of topologies, including series-parallel, bridge-link, honeycomb, and total-cross-tied arrangements. This approach is specifically designed for deployment in utility-scale, commercial, and residential photovoltaic installations where shading from trees, buildings, dust accumulation, or transient cloud cover frequently induces significant mismatch losses. The invention distinguishes itself by eliminating reliance on heuristic rules, exhaustive simulations, or additional hardware such as bypass diodes or auxiliary panels, instead leveraging learned patterns from synthetic irradiance data to predict the most efficient topology configuration with high accuracy and minimal computational overhead.

## BACKGROUND

- describe limitations of power production from PV systems

Photovoltaic energy systems are inherently susceptible to reductions in power output due to non-uniform irradiance conditions caused by partial shading, soiling, or environmental obstructions. When portions of a PV array are shaded while others remain fully illuminated, the resulting current and voltage mismatch among panels leads to substantial power losses, often exceeding 30% under moderate shading conditions. Conventional PV systems employ fixed electrical topologies—typically series-parallel configurations—that are optimized for uniform irradiance but perform poorly under dynamic shading. Even when alternative topologies such as bridge-link, honeycomb, or total-cross-tied are theoretically superior under specific shading patterns, their implementation in practice has been limited by the absence of automated, scalable, and computationally efficient methods for topology selection. Existing approaches to mitigate these losses include static reconfiguration strategies that rely on pre-defined rules or puzzle-based algorithms, which are incapable of adapting to real-time variations in shading. Other methods involve physically relocating panels or inserting additional unshaded modules into the array, which increases system cost, complexity, and maintenance burden. Dynamic reconfiguration techniques based on irradiance equalization or mismatch index minimization require continuous monitoring and iterative computation across all possible configurations, making them impractical for large-scale arrays due to prohibitive computational latency. Furthermore, classical machine learning models such as support vector machines, random forests, and k-nearest neighbors, while capable of classification, lack the capacity to generalize reliably across diverse and unseen shading scenarios due to their limited representational power and susceptibility to overfitting. These limitations collectively hinder the ability of modern photovoltaic systems to achieve consistent, high-efficiency power delivery under real-world operating conditions, necessitating a more intelligent, adaptive, and hardware-efficient solution.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

- motivate reconfiguring PV array connections

Reconfiguring the electrical connections within a photovoltaic array is a critical mechanism for mitigating power losses induced by partial shading. When individual panels experience dissimilar irradiance levels, the resulting current imbalance forces higher-performing panels to operate below their maximum power point, while shaded panels may enter reverse bias, leading to hot-spot heating and accelerated degradation. By dynamically altering the series-parallel interconnections among panels, the array can be restructured to minimize these imbalances, thereby restoring more uniform current flow and maximizing the aggregate power output. Unlike fixed topologies that are optimized for ideal conditions, reconfigurable architectures enable the system to adapt to the unique irradiance profile of each moment, effectively transforming a rigid electrical network into a responsive, intelligent energy harvesting system. The potential for power gain is substantial: under realistic shading conditions, switching from a standard series-parallel configuration to an optimized topology such as total-cross-tied or honeycomb can yield power improvements exceeding ten percent. This motivates the development of automated, real-time reconfiguration systems that can identify the optimal configuration without human intervention, computational delay, or additional hardware. The challenge lies not in the physical feasibility of switching, but in the intelligent selection of the correct topology from a combinatorial space of possibilities, a task that demands a model capable of learning complex, non-linear relationships between irradiance patterns and power outcomes.

- describe conventional approaches to mitigate shading

Conventional methods for mitigating shading-induced losses in photovoltaic arrays have historically relied on either passive or rule-based active strategies. Passive approaches include the use of bypass diodes, which allow current to circumvent shaded cells but do not reconfigure the overall array topology and offer no benefit beyond preventing hot-spot damage. Active strategies have included static reconfiguration schemes based on Sudoku-like algorithms that redistribute shaded panels spatially across the array, assuming that uniform distribution of shading minimizes losses. Other methods involve irradiance equalization techniques that adjust connections to ensure each string receives approximately the same total irradiance, though these approaches assume linear relationships between irradiance and power that do not hold under non-uniform shading. Some systems employ exhaustive simulation-based selection, where every possible topology is evaluated in real time using a power model, a process that becomes computationally intractable for arrays larger than 4×4 due to the exponential growth in configuration space. Additional methods utilize image processing to estimate shading patterns from camera inputs, or fuzzy logic controllers to infer optimal configurations from heuristic rules derived from limited datasets. These approaches suffer from poor scalability, high latency, reliance on external sensors beyond panel-level irradiance, or lack of generalization to unseen shading scenarios. Furthermore, many require additional hardware such as secondary arrays, extra panels, or specialized switching matrices that increase cost and reduce reliability. None of these methods have demonstrated the ability to learn and generalize optimal reconfiguration policies from data without manual feature engineering or domain-specific assumptions.

- introduce system for deep neural network-based topology reconfiguration

The present invention introduces a novel system for photovoltaic array topology reconfiguration based on a deep neural network trained to predict the optimal electrical configuration from real-time panel-level irradiance measurements. This system operates as a closed-loop cyber-physical controller that receives input data from sensors embedded at each panel, processes the data through a pre-trained deep neural network, and outputs a control signal to a reconfigurable switching matrix that alters the inter-panel connections. The neural network is trained exclusively on synthetic irradiance data generated under realistic conditions, including modeled wiring losses, and learns to map complex, high-dimensional irradiance patterns to the topology that maximizes power output. Unlike conventional methods, this system does not require exhaustive simulation during inference, nor does it rely on predefined rules or external imaging systems. Instead, it leverages the representational power of deep learning to capture non-linear, non-intuitive relationships between shading distributions and power outcomes, enabling accurate, low-latency topology selection even for large 5×5 or larger arrays. The system is designed for seamless integration into existing photovoltaic infrastructure, requiring only the addition of panel-level sensors and programmable relays, with no need for additional panels, batteries, or external computational units.

- describe key contributions of the system

The key contributions of this system include the first application of a regularized deep neural network architecture to the problem of photovoltaic topology selection under realistic wiring losses, the expansion of topology classification to four distinct configurations—series-parallel, bridge-link, honeycomb, and total-cross-tied—without reliance on simplifying assumptions, and the demonstration of consistent power improvements exceeding eleven percent through automated reconfiguration. The system introduces a novel training framework that generates labeled synthetic irradiance data using a binary mapping scheme and simulated power outputs, enabling supervised learning without the need for expensive field data collection. It further incorporates dropout and batch normalization layers to prevent overfitting and enhance generalization, achieving an average test accuracy of 81.1% and a macro F1 score of 0.74, significantly outperforming traditional machine learning algorithms such as support vector machines, random forests, and XGBoost. Crucially, the system requires no additional photovoltaic panels, bypass diodes, or auxiliary hardware to function, making it cost-effective and deployable in existing installations. The architecture is scalable to larger arrays, computationally efficient during inference, and compatible with standard industrial communication protocols, enabling real-time operation without introducing latency that would compromise system performance.

- outline system architecture and components

The system architecture comprises four primary components: a panel-level sensor network, a central processing unit, a reconfigurable switching matrix, and a trained deep neural network model. The sensor network consists of irradiance sensors embedded at each photovoltaic panel, capable of measuring local irradiance levels and transmitting this data wirelessly or via wired bus to the central processing unit. The central processing unit, implemented as a dedicated computing device, receives the irradiance data as a 25-dimensional vector for a 5×5 array and feeds it into the neural network model. The neural network, pre-trained offline on synthetic data, processes the input through multiple hidden layers with ReLU activations, dropout, and batch normalization, and outputs a probability distribution over the four possible topologies via a softmax activation function. The topology with the highest probability is selected and transmitted as a control signal to the reconfigurable switching matrix, which consists of solid-state relays or semiconductor switches positioned at strategic inter-panel connection points. These switches alter the electrical wiring to realize the selected topology, thereby reconfiguring the array’s current-voltage characteristics to maximize power output. The entire system operates autonomously, with periodic re-evaluation triggered by changes in irradiance or at fixed time intervals, ensuring continuous optimization under dynamic environmental conditions.

- describe system operation and functionality

The system operates in two distinct phases: training and inference. During the training phase, a large dataset of synthetic irradiance patterns is generated by randomly assigning binary shading states to each panel and assigning irradiance values drawn from uniform distributions representing shaded and unshaded conditions. Each irradiance pattern is simulated across all four topologies using a detailed electrical model that includes panel characteristics, bypass diodes, and wiring resistances. The topology yielding the highest power output for each pattern is designated as the ground truth label. This labeled dataset is used to train a six-layer deep neural network with dropout and batch normalization to minimize classification error. During inference, the system continuously monitors real-time irradiance data from the panel sensors. Upon receipt of a new irradiance vector, the neural network computes the probability of each topology being optimal and selects the one with the highest confidence. The selected topology is then communicated to the switching matrix, which activates or deactivates the appropriate relays to rewire the array accordingly. This process repeats at intervals determined by system latency and environmental variability, ensuring that the array remains optimally configured under changing shading conditions. The system requires no human intervention, does not rely on external weather forecasts, and adapts to previously unseen shading patterns due to its robust generalization capabilities derived from regularization and synthetic data diversity.

### Approach

- motivate need for automated methods for topology reconfiguration

The increasing scale and deployment density of photovoltaic systems in urban and semi-urban environments have rendered manual or rule-based reconfiguration methods obsolete. As shading patterns become more complex and unpredictable due to architectural obstructions, vegetation growth, and transient weather events, the need for an automated, real-time, and adaptive method for topology selection becomes paramount. Human operators cannot continuously monitor and reconfigure large arrays, and pre-programmed rules fail to account for the non-linear interactions between shading, wiring losses, and panel characteristics. Automated methods are essential not only for maximizing energy yield but also for ensuring system longevity, as prolonged operation under mismatched conditions can lead to hot-spot heating, cell degradation, and safety hazards. Furthermore, the economic viability of photovoltaic installations depends on consistent, high-efficiency power delivery, and any method that can reliably improve output by over ten percent without additional hardware represents a transformative advancement. The motivation for automation is therefore both technical and economic: to eliminate inefficiencies inherent in static configurations and to enable photovoltaic systems to perform at their theoretical maximum under real-world conditions.

- describe limitations of conventional approaches

Conventional approaches to photovoltaic reconfiguration are fundamentally constrained by their reliance on static heuristics, exhaustive computation, or external hardware. Rule-based methods such as Sudoku-based redistribution assume that uniform shading distribution minimizes losses, a premise that does not hold under asymmetric or localized shading. Irradiance equalization techniques require precise measurement of total string irradiance and assume linear power responses, which are invalidated by the non-linear I-V characteristics of photovoltaic cells under mismatch. Exhaustive simulation methods, while theoretically optimal, are computationally prohibitive for arrays larger than 4×4 due to the exponential number of possible topologies. Methods that require additional panels or secondary arrays increase system cost, complexity, and maintenance, while image-based approaches introduce dependency on external sensors, lighting conditions, and calibration stability. Classical machine learning models, though faster than simulation, lack the depth to capture complex interactions between irradiance patterns and power outcomes, leading to poor generalization and susceptibility to overfitting. None of these approaches have demonstrated the ability to learn from data, adapt to novel shading scenarios, or operate with the speed and reliability required for real-time control in commercial photovoltaic systems.

The system for reconfiguring a photovoltaic array to maximize power output operates by learning the mapping between spatial irradiance distributions and the corresponding optimal electrical topology through supervised deep learning. It does not rely on physical rearrangement of panels, external sensors, or additional hardware. Instead, it leverages existing panel-level irradiance sensors and programmable switching matrices to dynamically alter the series-parallel connections among panels. The system is trained offline on a synthetic dataset comprising thousands of irradiance patterns, each labeled with the topology that yields the highest simulated power output under realistic wiring losses. During operation, the system receives real-time irradiance data from the array, processes it through a deep neural network with regularization layers, and outputs a topology selection with high confidence. This selection is then executed by the switching matrix, which reconfigures the array’s internal wiring to realize the predicted optimal configuration. The entire process is fully automated, operates in near real-time, and continuously adapts to changing environmental conditions without requiring recalibration or human intervention.

- describe system components and architecture

The system comprises a distributed sensor network, a central processing unit, a reconfigurable switching matrix, and a deep neural network model. The sensor network includes individual irradiance sensors mounted on each photovoltaic panel, capable of measuring local irradiance levels with high temporal resolution and transmitting the data to a central controller via wired or wireless communication. The central processing unit is a low-power embedded computing device equipped with sufficient memory and processing capability to execute the neural network inference in under 10 milliseconds. The reconfigurable switching matrix consists of solid-state relays or MOSFET-based switches positioned at every potential inter-panel connection point, allowing the system to realize any of the four target topologies—series-parallel, bridge-link, honeycomb, or total-cross-tied—by selectively enabling or disabling specific electrical links. The deep neural network model is a six-layer feedforward architecture with ReLU activations, dropout regularization, and batch normalization, trained to map a 25-dimensional irradiance vector to a four-class probability distribution over topologies. The model is deployed as a static inference engine, requiring no online training during operation, ensuring deterministic and reliable performance.

- describe system operation and functionality

The system operates in a continuous cycle of sensing, inference, and actuation. At regular intervals—determined by environmental dynamics and system latency requirements—the central processing unit collects irradiance measurements from all panels and assembles them into a single input vector. This vector is passed through the pre-trained neural network, which applies a series of affine transformations, non-linear activations, dropout, and batch normalization to extract high-level features representative of the shading pattern. The final layer employs a softmax function to produce a normalized probability distribution over the four possible topologies. The topology with the highest probability is selected as the optimal configuration. A control signal is then transmitted to the switching matrix, which reconfigures the electrical connections among the panels to realize the selected topology. The system then waits for the next sampling interval, during which it may detect a new irradiance pattern and repeat the process. This closed-loop operation ensures that the array is always operating under the topology most suited to the current shading conditions, thereby maximizing power output without requiring physical panel movement or additional hardware.

The system is inherently scalable due to its modular architecture and reliance on distributed sensing and centralized inference. For larger arrays, such as 6×6 or 8×8 configurations, the input dimension of the neural network scales linearly with the number of panels, and the model architecture can be expanded with additional hidden layers or neurons without altering the fundamental design. The system’s ability to achieve significant power improvements without requiring additional photovoltaic panels is a critical innovation that distinguishes it from prior art. The neural network’s ability to generalize from synthetic data ensures that it can accurately select the best configuration even in the absence of temporal variation, avoiding unnecessary switching that could degrade relay lifespan. The system’s low inference latency and deterministic output allow it to operate reliably under static conditions without oscillation or instability. Furthermore, because the model is trained on a wide variety of irradiance distributions—including those that mimic static shading—it learns to recognize and respond to persistent patterns with high confidence, making it suitable for deployment in environments with predictable shading profiles.

### Neural Network Model

- introduce neural network model

The neural network model introduced in this invention is a deep feedforward architecture specifically designed to classify photovoltaic array topologies based on spatial irradiance patterns. Unlike traditional machine learning models that rely on handcrafted features or linear separability assumptions, this model learns hierarchical representations of shading distributions directly from raw panel-level irradiance measurements. It is trained as a supervised classifier to map a 25-dimensional input vector—representing the irradiance values of each panel in a 5×5 array—to one of four discrete topology classes: series-parallel, bridge-link, honeycomb, or total-cross-tied. The model’s architecture is optimized for generalization, robustness, and computational efficiency, enabling real-time inference on low-power embedded hardware without sacrificing accuracy.

- describe neural network model architecture

The neural network architecture comprises six fully connected layers with the following neuron counts: 64, 64, 128, 256, 64, and 4. The first layer receives the 25-dimensional irradiance input vector, and each subsequent layer applies an affine transformation followed by a non-linear activation function. The first five layers utilize the Rectified Linear Unit (ReLU) activation function to introduce non-linearity and enhance feature representation, while the final layer employs a softmax activation to produce a probability distribution over the four topology classes. Between the second and fifth layers, dropout and batch normalization operations are applied to mitigate overfitting and accelerate convergence. The model is fully connected, with no convolutional or recurrent components, ensuring simplicity and compatibility with embedded deployment. The total number of trainable parameters is approximately 58,000, making the model compact enough for real-time inference on microcontrollers with limited memory and processing capacity.

- describe input to neural network model

The input to the neural network model is a 25-dimensional vector, where each element corresponds to the irradiance level measured at a single photovoltaic panel in a 5×5 array. Each irradiance value is normalized to a range between 0 and 1, representing the relative intensity of sunlight incident on the panel, with 0 indicating complete shading and 1 indicating full irradiance. The values are derived from panel-level sensors and are aggregated into a single vector in row-major order, preserving the spatial structure of the array. This input format allows the model to learn spatial dependencies between neighboring panels, such as the effect of a shaded column on adjacent strings, without requiring explicit feature engineering or topological encoding.

- describe hidden layers of neural network model

The hidden layers of the neural network model consist of four intermediate layers, each performing an affine transformation followed by a ReLU activation function. These layers progressively extract higher-level features from the raw irradiance input, beginning with low-level patterns such as isolated shaded panels and progressing to complex configurations such as diagonal shading gradients or clustered shadow regions. The dimensions of the hidden layers—64, 64, 128, and 256 neurons—were selected through empirical hyperparameter search to balance representational capacity with computational efficiency. Each hidden layer is followed by batch normalization to stabilize the distribution of activations and dropout with a rate of 20% to prevent co-adaptation of neurons and enhance generalization. These mechanisms collectively enable the model to learn robust, invariant representations of shading patterns that generalize to unseen scenarios.

- describe output layer of neural network model

The output layer of the neural network model consists of four neurons, each corresponding to one of the four possible photovoltaic topologies: series-parallel, bridge-link, honeycomb, and total-cross-tied. The outputs of these neurons are passed through a softmax activation function, which transforms them into a probability distribution over the four classes. The softmax ensures that the output values are non-negative and sum to one, allowing the system to interpret the results as confidence scores for each topology. The topology with the highest probability is selected as the optimal configuration for the given irradiance pattern. This probabilistic output enables the system to quantify uncertainty and, in future iterations, to trigger re-evaluation when confidence falls below a threshold.

- describe softmax activation function

The softmax activation function is applied to the final layer of the neural network to convert the raw output logits into a valid probability distribution over the four topology classes. It is defined as the exponential of each logit divided by the sum of exponentials across all logits, ensuring that the resulting values lie between zero and one and sum to unity. This function is essential for classification tasks with mutually exclusive outcomes, as it allows the model to express relative confidence in each possible topology rather than producing arbitrary scalar outputs. The softmax enables the system to make informed decisions even when the distinction between topologies is subtle, by providing a calibrated measure of confidence that can be used for validation, logging, or triggering secondary verification mechanisms.

- describe regularization techniques used

The neural network employs two primary regularization techniques: dropout and batch normalization. Dropout is applied to the second through fifth hidden layers, randomly setting 20% of neuron outputs to zero during each training iteration. This prevents the model from relying on specific neurons and encourages distributed feature learning, reducing overfitting. Batch normalization is applied immediately after each affine transformation in the same layers, standardizing the activations of each mini-batch to have zero mean and unit variance. This stabilizes the training process, accelerates convergence, and reduces sensitivity to initialization and learning rate selection. Together, these techniques significantly improve the model’s ability to generalize to unseen irradiance patterns, a critical requirement for real-world deployment.

- describe dropout policy

The dropout policy is implemented with a fixed rate of 20% applied to the second, third, fourth, and fifth hidden layers during training. Neurons in these layers are independently and randomly deactivated with a probability of 0.2 during each forward pass, meaning that on average, 20% of the activations are set to zero. This forces the network to learn redundant representations and prevents any single neuron from dominating the decision-making process. During inference, dropout is disabled, and all neurons are active with their weights scaled by the dropout rate to maintain expected output magnitude. This policy was selected after extensive hyperparameter tuning, as lower rates failed to prevent overfitting, while higher rates degraded model performance due to excessive information loss.

- describe batchnorm

Batch normalization is applied to the output of each affine transformation in the second through fifth hidden layers. For each mini-batch of training data, the activations of each neuron are standardized by subtracting the batch mean and dividing by the batch standard deviation, followed by a learnable scale and shift parameter. This operation reduces internal covariate shift, allowing the network to train faster and with higher learning rates. It also introduces a mild regularization effect by introducing noise through batch statistics, further improving generalization. Batch normalization is disabled during inference, and the learned mean and variance parameters are replaced with running averages computed during training, ensuring deterministic and consistent behavior.

- describe training framework

The training framework is built upon a supervised learning paradigm using synthetic irradiance data generated through a binary mapping scheme and electrical simulation. A dataset of 8,000 irradiance instances is created, each labeled with the topology that yields the maximum power output under modeled wiring losses. The data is split into 90% for training and 10% for validation. The neural network is optimized using the Adam optimizer with a learning rate of 10⁻⁴ and categorical cross-entropy loss. Training proceeds for 200 epochs, with early stopping applied if validation loss does not improve for ten consecutive epochs. Data is normalized to zero mean and unit variance before training, and batch size is set to 64. The training process is fully automated and reproducible, requiring no manual feature extraction or domain-specific tuning.

- describe simulation module

The simulation module is responsible for generating labeled training data by simulating the electrical behavior of the photovoltaic array under various irradiance patterns. For each synthetic irradiance instance, the module computes the maximum power point (MPP) of the array under each of the four topologies—series-parallel, bridge-link, honeycomb, and total-cross-tied—using a detailed MATLAB-Simulink model that includes panel characteristics, bypass diodes, and wiring resistances. The topology with the highest MPP is assigned as the ground truth label for that irradiance pattern. The simulation is executed in parallel across multiple computing cores to generate the 8,000 labeled instances efficiently. The module ensures that all simulations are performed under identical environmental conditions, including a constant cell temperature of 25°C, to eliminate confounding variables.

- describe training module

The training module orchestrates the entire process of neural network training, including data loading, preprocessing, model initialization, optimization, and evaluation. It loads the labeled dataset, applies normalization, and partitions it into training and validation sets. The module initializes the neural network with random weights, configures the optimizer and loss function, and executes the training loop over 200 epochs. It monitors validation loss and accuracy, applies early stopping, and saves the model checkpoint with the highest validation accuracy. The module also logs training metrics, including loss curves and confusion matrices, for performance analysis. The training process is fully automated and requires no manual intervention, enabling reproducible and scalable deployment across multiple array sizes and configurations.

- describe synthetic data generation

Synthetic data generation involves the creation of irradiance patterns through a binary mapping scheme in which each panel is assigned a binary state: 0 for shaded and 1 for unshaded. For each state, an irradiance value is sampled independently from a uniform distribution: 100–586 W/m² for shaded panels and 586–1000 W/m² for unshaded panels, with 586 W/m² serving as the threshold between shaded and unshaded conditions. This results in 2⁴⁰ possible binary patterns, from which 8,000 are randomly sampled to ensure diversity. Each pattern is then simulated across all four topologies to determine the optimal configuration, which becomes the label. This method ensures that the training data covers a wide spectrum of shading scenarios, including clustered, striped, diagonal, and random patterns, while maintaining physical plausibility through the use of empirically grounded irradiance ranges.

- describe binary mapping scheme

The binary mapping scheme assigns a binary value to each panel in the photovoltaic array based on whether it is considered shaded or unshaded. A value of 0 indicates shading, and a value of 1 indicates full illumination. These binary states are not binary in the sense of on/off, but rather represent qualitative shading conditions derived from irradiance thresholds. Each binary state is then mapped to a continuous irradiance value drawn from a uniform distribution, with shaded panels assigned values between 100 and 586 W/m² and unshaded panels assigned values between 586 and 1000 W/m². This scheme preserves the spatial structure of shading while introducing variability in intensity, allowing the neural network to learn both the location and severity of shading effects. The scheme is computationally efficient and scalable, enabling the generation of large, diverse datasets without requiring real-world measurements.

- describe irradiance distributions

The irradiance distributions used in this invention are derived from empirical analysis of global horizontal irradiance data from the National Solar Radiation Database (NSRDB) over a one-year period. The distributions are modeled as uniform between 100–586 W/m² for shaded panels and 586–1000 W/m² for unshaded panels, with 586 W/m² selected as the threshold based on observed irradiance variability under partial cloud cover. These ranges reflect realistic conditions encountered in field installations, including diffuse irradiance under thin clouds and direct irradiance under clear skies. The use of uniform distributions ensures that all irradiance values within these ranges are equally likely, preventing bias toward specific intensities and promoting generalization across diverse climates and seasons.

- describe dataset construction

The dataset is constructed by generating 8,000 unique irradiance patterns using the binary mapping scheme, each corresponding to a distinct spatial arrangement of shaded and unshaded panels in a 5×5 array. For each pattern, the electrical performance of the array is simulated under all four topologies using a detailed Simulink model that includes wiring resistances, bypass diodes, and panel characteristics. The topology yielding the highest maximum power point (MPP) is assigned as the label. The resulting dataset contains 8,000 input-output pairs, where each input is a 25-element irradiance vector and each output is a class label from {1, 2, 3, 4}. The dataset is normalized to zero mean and unit variance, and split into 7,200 training samples and 800 validation samples to ensure robust evaluation.

- describe supervised classification problem

The problem of photovoltaic topology reconfiguration is formulated as a supervised multi-class classification task, in which the goal is to predict the optimal electrical configuration from a set of four possible topologies based solely on a vector of panel-level irradiance measurements. The input space consists of 25-dimensional irradiance vectors, and the output space consists of four discrete classes. The model is trained to minimize the categorical cross-entropy loss between predicted probabilities and true labels, effectively learning a decision boundary that separates the irradiance patterns associated with each topology. This formulation enables the use of powerful deep learning techniques to discover complex, non-linear relationships between shading patterns and power outcomes without requiring domain-specific knowledge or heuristic rules.

- describe label assignment

Label assignment is performed by simulating the electrical behavior of the photovoltaic array under each of the four topologies for a given irradiance pattern and selecting the topology that produces the highest maximum power point (MPP). The label is encoded as an integer from 1 to 4, corresponding to series-parallel, bridge-link, honeycomb, and total-cross-tied, respectively. This assignment is deterministic and based solely on simulated power output, ensuring that the ground truth reflects the true optimal configuration under realistic conditions, including wiring losses. The label is not based on heuristic rules, irradiance equalization, or manual selection, but on physics-based simulation, making it a reliable and objective target for training.

- describe simulation setup

The simulation setup utilizes a detailed MATLAB-Simulink model of a 5×5 photovoltaic array, with each panel modeled as a single-diode equivalent circuit with parameters calibrated to commercial monocrystalline silicon cells. Bypass diodes are connected in parallel with each panel to prevent reverse bias damage. Wiring losses are modeled using resistors: 0.01 ohms between panels in adjacent strings and 0.005 ohms between panels within the same string. All simulations are conducted at a constant cell temperature of 25°C to eliminate thermal effects as confounding variables. The maximum power point is computed using a perturb-and-observe algorithm for each topology, ensuring accurate determination of the optimal configuration for each irradiance pattern.

- describe PV array simulation model

The PV array simulation model is a physics-based electrical circuit representation of a 5×5 photovoltaic array, where each panel is modeled as a current source in parallel with a diode, shunt resistance, and series resistance. The panels are interconnected according to the topology being simulated—series-parallel, bridge-link, honeycomb, or total-cross-tied—with wiring resistances inserted at every connection point. The model accounts for the non-linear I-V characteristics of photovoltaic cells, including the effects of partial shading, bypass diode activation, and series resistance. The model is validated against experimental data from commercial panels and is capable of accurately predicting maximum power point under a wide range of irradiance and temperature conditions.

- describe wiring losses modeling

Wiring losses are modeled by inserting resistive elements at every inter-panel connection point within the array. Specifically, a resistance of 0.01 ohms is assigned to connections between panels in adjacent strings, and a resistance of 0.005 ohms is assigned to connections within the same string. These values are derived from empirical measurements of copper wiring in commercial photovoltaic installations and reflect the resistive losses incurred during current flow between panels. The inclusion of wiring losses in the simulation is critical, as it ensures that the neural network learns to select topologies that are optimal not only in terms of irradiance distribution but also under realistic electrical constraints, making the model applicable to real-world systems.

- describe re-configurable links

Re-configurable links refer to the programmable electrical connections between photovoltaic panels that can be enabled or disabled by solid-state relays or semiconductor switches. These links allow the array to be reconfigured into any of the four target topologies—series-parallel, bridge-link, honeycomb, or total-cross-tied—by altering the path of current flow between panels. Each link is controlled by a binary signal from the central processing unit, which determines whether the connection is active or open. The system supports all possible combinations of links required to realize the four topologies, ensuring full configurability without physical constraints.

- describe string connections

String connections refer to the series connections between photovoltaic panels that form vertical or horizontal current paths within the array. In the series-parallel topology, panels are connected in series to form strings, and these strings are connected in parallel. In the bridge-link and honeycomb topologies, additional connections are made between panels of different strings to create cross-connections that mitigate current mismatch. In the total-cross-tied topology, every panel in a row is connected to the corresponding panel in adjacent rows, forming a fully interconnected grid. The system’s switching matrix is designed to enable or disable these string connections dynamically, allowing the array to transition between topologies without physical reconfiguration.

- describe resistance values used

The resistance values used in the simulation are 0.01 ohms for inter-string connections and 0.005 ohms for intra-string connections. These values are selected based on empirical measurements of copper wiring commonly used in photovoltaic installations, with inter-string connections being longer and thus having higher resistance. The inclusion of these resistances in the simulation ensures that the neural network learns to account for real-world electrical losses when selecting topologies, preventing the selection of configurations that appear optimal in lossless simulations but perform poorly under actual conditions.

- describe activation and deactivation of simulated linkages

Activation and deactivation of simulated linkages are performed during the simulation phase by programmatically enabling or disabling specific resistive connections between panels. For each topology, the set of active linkages is defined by a connectivity matrix that specifies which panels are electrically connected. During simulation, the model applies Kirchhoff’s laws to compute the resulting current and voltage distribution across the array, and the maximum power point is calculated for each configuration. The activation and deactivation of linkages are performed in a manner consistent with the physical constraints of the switching matrix, ensuring that only feasible topologies are considered during training and inference.

- describe topology configurations

The four topology configurations considered in this invention are series-parallel (SP), bridge-link (BL), honeycomb (HC), and total-cross-tied (TCT). The SP configuration connects panels in series to form strings, and the strings are connected in parallel. The BL configuration introduces diagonal connections between panels of adjacent strings, resembling a bridge rectifier pattern. The HC configuration creates a comb-like structure with connections between every other panel in adjacent strings. The TCT configuration fully interconnects all panels in adjacent rows, forming a grid-like structure that minimizes current mismatch. These topologies are selected for their known performance advantages under partial shading and their feasibility for implementation with existing switching technology.

- describe wire loss modeling

Wire loss modeling is achieved by inserting resistive elements between every pair of connected panels in the simulation model. The resistance values are assigned based on the physical length and cross-section of the wiring, with higher resistance assigned to connections between strings and lower resistance to connections within strings. The resistive elements are modeled as ideal resistors in series with the panel connections, and their impact on current flow is computed using Ohm’s law during each simulation step. This modeling ensures that the neural network learns to select topologies that are not only efficient in terms of irradiance distribution but also robust to the inherent resistive losses of real-world wiring.

- describe adaptability of the system

The system is highly adaptable to different array sizes, shading patterns, and environmental conditions due to its data-driven nature and modular architecture. The neural network can be retrained on new datasets generated for larger arrays, such as 6×6 or 8×8 configurations, by simply expanding the input dimension and adjusting the model architecture accordingly. The training process does not require domain-specific knowledge, making it applicable to any photovoltaic system with panel-level sensors and reconfigurable switches. The system can also be adapted to new topologies by expanding the label space and retraining the model, allowing for continuous innovation in array design. Its reliance on synthetic data generation ensures that it can be deployed in any geographic region without requiring field data collection.

- describe design of regularized neural network

The design of the regularized neural network is centered on the integration of dropout and batch normalization to enhance generalization and prevent overfitting. The network is structured as a deep feedforward architecture with six layers, where dropout is applied to the second through fifth layers at a rate of 20%, and batch normalization is applied after each affine transformation in the same layers. The use of ReLU activations ensures non-linearity, while the final softmax layer provides calibrated probability outputs. The model is trained using a large, diverse synthetic dataset and optimized with the Adam algorithm to minimize categorical cross-entropy loss. This design balances representational capacity with computational efficiency, enabling high accuracy on unseen data while remaining deployable on low-power embedded systems.

- describe neural network architecture

The neural network architecture consists of six fully connected layers with neuron counts of 64, 64, 128, 256, 64, and 4, respectively. The first layer receives a 25-dimensional irradiance input vector, and each subsequent layer applies an affine transformation followed by a ReLU activation. Dropout with a rate of 20% and batch normalization are applied after the first four hidden layers. The final layer uses a softmax activation to produce a probability distribution over four topology classes. The architecture is designed to be deep enough to capture complex spatial patterns in irradiance data while remaining shallow enough to ensure fast inference and low memory usage. The total number of parameters is approximately 58,000, making it suitable for deployment on embedded processors with limited computational resources.

- describe training and optimization of neural network model

The neural network model is trained using a supervised learning framework with a dataset of 8,000 labeled irradiance patterns. The model is optimized using the Adam optimizer with a learning rate of 10⁻⁴ and categorical cross-entropy loss. Training proceeds for 200 epochs, with early stopping applied if validation loss does not improve for ten consecutive epochs. The data is normalized to zero mean and unit variance, and batch size is set to 64. The model is validated using a held-out test set, and performance is evaluated using accuracy, macro F1 score, and confusion matrix analysis. The training process is fully automated and reproducible, requiring no manual intervention. The resulting model achieves an average test accuracy of 81.1% and a macro F1 score of 0.74, demonstrating its ability to generalize to unseen shading scenarios.

## Methods

- introduce method 600 for photovoltaic topology reconfiguration

Method 600 is a systematic procedure for autonomously reconfiguring a photovoltaic array to maximize power output under partial shading conditions. The method begins with the continuous acquisition of panel-level irradiance data from a distributed sensor network. This data is aggregated into a single input vector and fed into a pre-trained deep neural network model that has been trained to classify irradiance patterns into one of four topology configurations. The model outputs a probability distribution over the topologies, and the configuration with the highest probability is selected as the optimal arrangement. A control signal is then transmitted to a reconfigurable switching matrix, which alters the electrical connections among the panels to realize the selected topology. The method operates in a closed loop, repeating the process at regular intervals to adapt to changing environmental conditions without human intervention.

- receive operating data as input to neural network model

Operating data is received as a 25-dimensional vector representing the irradiance levels measured at each panel in a 5×5 photovoltaic array. Each element of the vector corresponds to a single panel and is normalized to a range between 0 and 1, where 0 indicates complete shading and 1 indicates full irradiance. The data is transmitted from panel-level sensors via wired or wireless communication to the central computing device, where it is buffered and formatted for input to the neural network. The vector preserves the spatial arrangement of the panels, allowing the model to learn correlations between neighboring irradiance values.

- generate prediction probability of topology configuration

The neural network model processes the input irradiance vector through a series of affine transformations, non-linear activations, dropout, and batch normalization layers to extract high-level features representative of the shading pattern. The final layer applies a softmax activation function to convert the raw outputs into a probability distribution over the four possible topologies. Each probability value represents the model’s confidence that the corresponding topology will yield the highest power output under the given irradiance conditions. The probabilities are normalized to sum to one, ensuring that the output is a valid probability distribution.

- provide neural network model and train

The neural network model is provided as a pre-trained, static computational graph with fixed weights and architecture. The model is trained offline using a synthetic dataset generated by simulating irradiance patterns across all four topologies and labeling each with the topology that maximizes power output. Training is performed using the Adam optimizer with categorical cross-entropy loss and includes dropout and batch normalization for regularization. The training process is fully automated and requires no manual feature engineering. Once trained, the model is deployed as a firmware component on the computing device, where it operates in inference mode without further training.

- receive operating data at first input layer

Operating data is received at the first input layer of the neural network as a 25-dimensional vector, with each element corresponding to the normalized irradiance value of a single photovoltaic panel. The input layer performs no transformation but passes the data directly to the first hidden layer. The data is pre-normalized to zero mean and unit variance to ensure consistent scaling during inference. The input vector is processed in real time, with no delay introduced by preprocessing or filtering.

- receive output of previous layer at hidden layer

Each hidden layer receives the output of the preceding layer as its input. The output from the first hidden layer is a 64-dimensional vector of ReLU-activated features, which is then passed to the second hidden layer. This process continues through four hidden layers, with each layer applying an affine transformation followed by ReLU activation, batch normalization, and dropout. The output of each layer serves as the input to the next, with the final hidden layer producing a 64-dimensional feature vector that is passed to the output layer.

- apply affine transformation and non-linear activation function

Each hidden layer applies an affine transformation to its input by multiplying it with a learned weight matrix and adding a learned bias vector. The result is then passed through a non-linear activation function, specifically the Rectified Linear Unit (ReLU), which sets all negative values to zero. This operation introduces non-linearity into the model, enabling it to learn complex, non-linear relationships between irradiance patterns and topology performance. The affine transformation and ReLU activation are applied sequentially in each hidden layer, with the output of one layer serving as the input to the next.

- apply dropout policy

The dropout policy is applied after each affine transformation and ReLU activation in the second through fifth hidden layers. During training, a random subset of neurons—20% of the total—is set to zero with a probability of 0.2, effectively removing their contribution to the forward pass. This prevents the model from relying on specific neurons and encourages distributed feature learning. During inference, dropout is disabled, and all neurons are active, with their weights scaled by the dropout rate to maintain the expected output magnitude.

- apply batchnorm operation

The batch normalization operation is applied after each affine transformation in the second through fifth hidden layers. For each mini-batch of data, the activations of each neuron are standardized by subtracting the batch mean and dividing by the batch standard deviation. A learnable scale and shift parameter are then applied to restore representational capacity. This operation stabilizes the distribution of activations across layers, accelerates convergence, and reduces sensitivity to initialization. During inference, the batch mean and variance are replaced with running averages computed during training, ensuring deterministic behavior.

- receive output of final hidden layer at output layer

The output of the final hidden layer is a 64-dimensional feature vector that has been processed through four layers of non-linear transformation and regularization. This vector is passed to the output layer, which consists of four neurons, each corresponding to one of the four topology classes. The output layer applies a final affine transformation to project the 64-dimensional features into a four-dimensional space of logits, which are then passed through the softmax activation function to produce a probability distribution.

- apply softmax activation function

The softmax activation function is applied to the four logits produced by the output layer. It computes the exponential of each logit and normalizes the result by dividing by the sum of all exponentials, ensuring that the output values are non-negative and sum to one. The resulting vector represents the probability distribution over the four topology classes, with each element indicating the model’s confidence that the corresponding topology is optimal for the given irradiance pattern. The topology with the highest probability is selected as the output of the model.

- generate prediction probabilities for each topology configuration

The softmax activation function generates four prediction probabilities, one for each of the four topology configurations: series-parallel, bridge-link, honeycomb, and total-cross-tied. Each probability is a value between 0 and 1, representing the model’s confidence that the corresponding topology will yield the highest power output under the current irradiance conditions. The probabilities are ordered consistently with the label encoding used during training, ensuring that the output can be directly mapped to a control signal for the switching matrix.

- determine topology selection

The topology selection is determined by identifying the topology class with the highest predicted probability. The system selects the class with the maximum value in the softmax output vector and assigns it as the optimal configuration for the current irradiance pattern. This selection is deterministic and based solely on the model’s learned mapping between irradiance inputs and topology outputs. The selected topology is then transmitted as a control signal to the switching matrix.

- communicate topology selection to photovoltaic array

The topology selection is communicated to the photovoltaic array via a digital control signal transmitted to the reconfigurable switching matrix. The signal is encoded as a binary sequence that specifies which electrical connections should be enabled or disabled to realize the selected topology. The communication protocol is designed for low latency and high reliability, with error detection and retransmission mechanisms to ensure accurate execution. The switching matrix responds by activating or deactivating solid-state relays to rewire the array accordingly.

- configure linkages of photovoltaic array

The linkages of the photovoltaic array are configured by the switching matrix, which activates or deactivates programmable relays to establish or break electrical connections between panels. The relays are positioned at strategic inter-panel junctions and are controlled by the topology selection signal. The configuration process takes less than 100 milliseconds, ensuring that the array can be reconfigured in near real time without disrupting power delivery. The system ensures that only valid topologies are implemented, preventing electrical shorts or open circuits.

- introduce method 700 for generating labeled synthetic irradiance data

Method 700 is a procedure for generating a labeled dataset of synthetic irradiance patterns to train the neural network model for photovoltaic topology reconfiguration. The method begins by generating a binary matrix representing the shading state of each panel in a 5×5 array, where 0 indicates shading and 1 indicates illumination. For each binary pattern, irradiance values are assigned to shaded and unshaded panels by sampling from uniform distributions between 100–586 W/m² and 586–1000 W/m², respectively. Each irradiance pattern is then simulated across all four topologies using a physics-based electrical model that includes wiring losses. The topology with the highest maximum power point is assigned as the label, and the resulting input-output pair is added to the dataset.

- generate synthetic irradiance data

Synthetic irradiance data is generated by randomly sampling binary shading patterns for a 5×5 array and assigning irradiance values to each panel based on its shading state. Shaded panels are assigned values uniformly distributed between 100 and 586 W/m², and unshaded panels are assigned values uniformly distributed between 586 and 1000 W/m². This results in a 25-dimensional irradiance vector for each pattern. The process is repeated 8,000 times to create a diverse dataset covering a wide range of shading scenarios, including clustered, striped, diagonal, and random patterns.

- apply synthetic irradiance data to PV array simulation model

Each synthetic irradiance vector is applied to a detailed photovoltaic array simulation model that includes panel characteristics, bypass diodes, and wiring resistances. The model simulates the electrical behavior of the array under each of the four topologies—series-parallel, bridge-link, honeycomb, and total-cross-tied—and computes the maximum power point for each configuration. The simulation is performed at a constant cell temperature of 25°C to eliminate thermal effects. The results are stored as a set of four power values for each irradiance pattern.

- identify simulated topology configuration with maximum power output

The simulated topology configuration with the maximum power output is identified by comparing the four computed maximum power points for each irradiance pattern. The topology yielding the highest power value is selected as the ground truth label for that pattern. This selection is deterministic and based solely on the physics-based simulation, ensuring that the label reflects the true optimal configuration under realistic conditions, including wiring losses.

- assign label to synthetic irradiance data

A label is assigned to each synthetic irradiance data point based on the topology that produced the highest maximum power point in the simulation. The label is encoded as an integer from 1 to 4, corresponding to series-parallel, bridge-link, honeycomb, and total-cross-tied, respectively. This label is paired with the 25-dimensional irradiance vector to form a supervised training sample. The labeling process is fully automated and applied to all 8,000 generated patterns, resulting in a complete dataset for training the neural network.

- introduce method 800 for training neural network

Method 800 is a procedure for training the deep neural network model to classify photovoltaic array topologies based on synthetic irradiance data. The method begins by loading the labeled dataset of 8,000 irradiance patterns and their corresponding topology labels. The data is normalized to zero mean and unit variance. The neural network architecture is initialized with random weights, and the model is trained using the Adam optimizer with categorical cross-entropy loss. Training proceeds for 200 epochs, with early stopping applied if validation loss does not improve for ten consecutive epochs. The model is validated on a held-out test set, and performance is evaluated using accuracy and F1 score metrics. The final trained model is saved as a static inference engine for deployment on the computing device.
