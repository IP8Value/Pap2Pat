# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND OF THE DISCLOSURE

Autism spectrum disorder (ASD) is a neurodevelopmental condition characterized by persistent deficits in social communication and social interaction, alongside restricted, repetitive patterns of behavior, interests, or activities. These behavioral manifestations typically emerge in early childhood and persist across the lifespan, significantly impacting cognitive, emotional, and adaptive functioning. The disorder exists along a broad continuum, with individuals exhibiting varying degrees of impairment and support needs, and is often accompanied by co-occurring conditions such as intellectual disability, epilepsy, anxiety, and gastrointestinal disturbances. The global prevalence of ASD has risen substantially over the past two decades, with recent estimates indicating that approximately one in fifty-four children are diagnosed, reflecting a complex interplay of genetic susceptibility and environmental influences. Despite extensive research efforts, no definitive biological marker has been validated for clinical diagnosis, and current diagnostic protocols rely exclusively on behavioral observation and standardized psychometric assessments, which are subjective, time-intensive, and often delayed until symptoms become pronounced. This reliance on behavioral criteria introduces significant variability in diagnostic accuracy, particularly in younger children and those with milder presentations, and impedes early intervention, which is critical for optimizing long-term outcomes. Genetic factors contribute substantially to ASD risk, with heritability estimates exceeding 80%, and include rare de novo mutations, copy number variations, and polygenic risk architectures affecting synaptic function, neuronal connectivity, and gene regulation. Environmental factors such as prenatal exposure to certain medications, maternal immune activation, advanced parental age, prematurity, and perinatal hypoxia have also been associated with increased risk, although their mechanisms remain incompletely understood. While numerous studies have explored potential biochemical correlates of ASD, including alterations in neurotransmitter systems, oxidative stress markers, mitochondrial function, and lipid metabolism, none have yielded a robust, reproducible, and clinically actionable diagnostic signature. Many prior investigations have focused on group-level statistical differences in metabolite concentrations, such as fatty acids or amino acids, without adequately evaluating their capacity to distinguish individual subjects. This approach frequently overstates the diagnostic utility of biomarkers by conflating population-level trends with individual-level predictability, leading to misleading conclusions that hinder translational progress. Consequently, there remains an urgent and unmet need for an objective, quantifiable, and biologically grounded method capable of accurately classifying individuals as having ASD or being neurotypical based on measurable physiological data.

## SUMMARY OF THE DISCLOSURE

A system is disclosed for determining autism state in an individual by analyzing a data array derived from concentrations of specific metabolites in biological samples. The system comprises a data processing apparatus configured to receive, process, and classify such data arrays using a multivariate statistical model trained to distinguish between autism and neurotypical classes. The data array structure consists of a fixed-length vector of metabolite concentration values, each corresponding to a defined biochemical entity measured in a standardized biological matrix such as plasma or erythrocyte membranes. The system calculates a discriminant score for each input data array by applying a linear or nonlinear transformation derived from training data representing confirmed autism and neurotypical cases. This score is computed by evaluating the relative position of the input array within a multidimensional feature space defined by the joint distribution of metabolite concentrations. The calculated score is then compared against a predetermined classification threshold, which is established through statistical optimization to maximize the separation between the two classes while minimizing misclassification. Individuals are classified as having autism if their score exceeds the threshold, and as neurotypical if it falls below. The metabolite concentrations used in the data array include arachidonic acid, dihomo-γ-linolenic acid, docosahexaenoic acid, eicosapentaenoic acid, linoleic acid, oleic acid, palmitic acid, stearic acid, and their respective ratios, each normalized to total fatty acid content. Machine learning techniques including Fisher Discriminant Analysis, Kernel Fisher Discriminant Analysis, and Kernel Partial Least Squares are employed to derive the classification model, with cross-validation used to ensure generalizability and prevent overfitting. The classification threshold is defined as the point on the score distribution that maximizes the sum of sensitivity and specificity, or equivalently, the point closest to the top-left corner of the receiver operating characteristic curve. The method for determining autism state involves acquiring a biological sample from the subject, quantifying the concentrations of the specified metabolites using standardized analytical methods, constructing the data array from these values, computing the discriminant score using the trained model, and assigning a classification based on comparison with the threshold. This approach enables objective, quantitative, and reproducible classification independent of behavioral observation, thereby providing a novel diagnostic tool with clinical utility.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The system is implemented as a computational platform comprising hardware and software components designed to process metabolite data arrays and generate autism state classifications. Biomarkers for autism diagnosis are selected based on their ability to form a multivariate signature that discriminates between individuals with autism and neurotypical controls, rather than on univariate group differences. The fatty acid metabolism pathways, particularly the omega-6 and omega-3 biosynthetic cascades involving the FOCM and TS enzymatic systems, are implicated in neurodevelopmental processes such as membrane fluidity, synaptic signaling, and inflammatory regulation, and their dysregulation may contribute to the pathophysiology of autism. Mutations in genes encoding enzymes involved in these pathways, as well as epigenetic modifications affecting their expression, may alter metabolite profiles in ways that are detectable at the individual level. Environmental factors such as dietary intake of essential fatty acids, exposure to toxins, and maternal metabolic status during gestation can further modulate these profiles, contributing to phenotypic heterogeneity. Latent variable techniques are employed to account for unobserved biological variability that may confound direct measurement of metabolites. Fisher Discriminant Analysis is used to project the multidimensional metabolite space onto a single discriminant axis that maximizes the ratio of between-class variance to within-class variance, thereby enhancing separability. Kernel Fisher Discriminant Analysis extends this approach by mapping the input space into a higher-dimensional feature space via a kernel function, enabling detection of nonlinear relationships among metabolites. Partial Least Squares and its kernel variant are used for regression modeling to predict continuous measures of symptom severity, although classification remains the primary objective. A cross-validatory approach is applied to assess model performance on unseen data, ensuring that results are not artifacts of overfitting. Metabolite data analysis begins with normalization of concentrations to total fatty acid content to account for variations in sample composition. FDA is applied to compute a discriminant score for each individual, and the within-cluster variation is quantified as the average squared distance of points within each class from their respective centroids, while between-cluster variation is measured as the squared distance between the centroids of the two classes. A probability distribution function is estimated for each class using kernel density estimation with a Gaussian kernel, yielding a smoothed representation of the score distribution. The estimated density function is used to determine the optimal classification threshold by identifying the point of maximum discriminative power. The scoring engine receives the data array and applies the trained model coefficients to compute a scalar score, which is then compared to the threshold to assign a classification. The classifier functionality includes model storage, score computation, and decision logic, while class template generation involves deriving the mean vectors and covariance matrices from training data. The data array structure is standardized to contain exactly twelve metabolite values in a fixed order, ensuring consistency across samples. Metabolite concentrations are measured using gas chromatography with flame ionization detection following derivatization to methyl esters. The FDA calculation involves matrix operations that project the data onto the optimal linear discriminant direction. The kernel density estimation employs a bandwidth parameter selected via cross-validation to balance bias and variance. The Gaussian kernel is defined as a radial basis function that assigns higher weight to nearby data points. The estimated density function provides a continuous probability surface over the score space. The scoring engine computes the discriminant score using a weighted sum of metabolite concentrations, where weights are determined by the training phase. The method for diagnosing autism involves receiving a data array from a biological sample, calculating the score using the trained classifier, and classifying the subject based on the comparison with the threshold. The system receives a test data array, computes its score, and groups the result into one of two classes: autism or neurotypical, based on the predefined decision boundary.

### EXAMPLES

An autism study was conducted using blood samples collected from 63 individuals diagnosed with autism spectrum disorder and 49 neurotypical controls, all aged between 3 and 17 years. Data collection included standardized behavioral assessments, dietary questionnaires, and blood draws performed under controlled conditions. ASD and neurotypical controls were matched for age, sex, and socioeconomic status, and excluded if they had taken nutritional supplements or consumed unusual diets in the prior two months. Metabolite measurements were performed by a CLIA-certified laboratory using standardized protocols for red blood cell fatty acid extraction and quantification. Blood samples were collected in EDTA tubes, centrifuged to isolate erythrocytes, washed, and stored at −80°C until processing. Plasma was separated and stored under identical conditions. DNA was extracted using commercial kits, purified via ethanol precipitation, and quantified spectrophotometrically. FDA scores were calculated for each subject using the twelve metabolite concentrations as inputs. The resulting FDA scores were plotted, revealing a clear but incomplete separation between the two groups. Classifier performance was evaluated using the C-statistic, which reached 0.76, indicating moderate discriminative ability. SIB class evaluation demonstrated that the two groups could be separated with statistical significance, and the separation was illustrated graphically using kernel density plots. Simultaneous use of multiple metabolite measurements improved classification over any single analyte. Variable combinations were systematically evaluated for separability, and the optimal subset was selected based on cross-validated performance. A receiver operating characteristic curve was generated, and the C-statistic was calculated to quantify overall classification accuracy. The maximum C-statistic plot identified the most informative metabolite combinations. Final minimal classifier variables were selected to balance simplicity and performance, and their separation was illustrated in a two-dimensional projection. A cross-validated confusion matrix confirmed classification accuracy with high specificity and moderate sensitivity. Metabolites were investigated for their ability to predict adaptive behavior scores using nonlinear regression via KPLS. Variable combinations were evaluated for predictive power, and a cross-validated R² plot demonstrated modest but significant associations. Model predictions versus actual data were visualized to assess fit. The importance of FOCM and TS pathway dysfunction was discussed in the context of altered lipid metabolism observed in autism.
