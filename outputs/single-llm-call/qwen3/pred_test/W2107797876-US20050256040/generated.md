# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates to methods for disrupting biological membranes through the targeted application of a synthetically engineered small globular protein. This invention specifically encompasses the use of a de novo designed polypeptide capable of inducing rapid and irreversible structural compromise of lipid bilayers in cell membranes, thereby triggering non-apoptotic, non-necrotic cell death. The method is particularly useful in therapeutic contexts where selective disruption of malignant cell membranes is required, and where conventional cytotoxic agents exhibit limited efficacy or unacceptable systemic toxicity. The invention further includes formulations and delivery systems that preserve the structural integrity of the protein during administration while enabling localized membrane disruption in tumor tissues. The utility of this invention extends beyond direct cytolytic applications to include adjuvant therapies for metastatic disease, palliative interventions in anatomically inaccessible tumors, and combinatorial regimens with conventional chemotherapeutics. The membrane-disrupting mechanism is distinct from detergent-based solubilization or pore formation by bacterial toxins, instead relying on a precisely arranged helical architecture that inserts into and destabilizes phospholipid bilayers through hydrophobic and electrostatic interactions. This physical disruption leads to immediate loss of membrane potential, osmotic imbalance, and cellular collapse without triggering classical apoptotic or necrotic signaling pathways.

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

Protein design has emerged as a powerful discipline for creating molecules with tailored structural and functional properties, enabling the construction of biomolecules that do not exist in nature. Among the most extensively studied classes of synthetic proteins are coiled-coil proteins, which derive their stability from the interhelical packing of alpha-helices through heptad repeat motifs. Helix bundle proteins, composed of multiple amphipathic helices arranged in defined spatial orientations, have been engineered to mimic natural membrane-inserting domains such as those found in viral fusion proteins. β-structural proteins, characterized by antiparallel or parallel β-sheets, have been utilized to form stable pores or channels in lipid bilayers, though often with limited control over selectivity and kinetics. De novo design of proteins has advanced significantly with computational modeling tools that predict folding pathways, stability, and surface electrostatics, allowing researchers to construct proteins from first principles rather than by modifying natural templates. A small globular protein (SGP) represents a novel class of such designed molecules, engineered to adopt a compact, water-soluble tertiary structure in aqueous environments while undergoing a conformational transition upon contact with lipid bilayers. Despite the promise of such designs, prior SGPs have been limited by poor stability, low solubility, or insufficient membrane insertion efficiency. Many early designs failed to maintain structural integrity under physiological conditions or exhibited off-target cytotoxicity due to non-specific interactions with non-malignant cells. Furthermore, previous attempts to translate membrane-disrupting peptides into clinical applications have been hampered by rapid proteolytic degradation, poor tissue penetration, and lack of selectivity between healthy and diseased cells. The present invention overcomes these limitations by introducing an SGP with optimized helical content, charge distribution, and hydrophobic moment, resulting in a molecule that is both stable in circulation and highly effective at disrupting target membranes under controlled conditions.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention introduces a novel small globular protein (SGP) engineered for the specific purpose of disrupting biological membranes through direct physical interaction with lipid bilayers. Unlike naturally occurring pore-forming toxins, SGP is entirely synthetic, designed de novo to adopt a stable, monomeric globular fold in solution that undergoes a conformational change upon membrane contact, leading to rapid insertion and structural destabilization of the lipid bilayer. The membrane-disrupting properties of SGP are not mediated by pore formation in the classical sense but rather by a cooperative disruption of lipid packing, resulting in instantaneous loss of membrane integrity and osmotic collapse. This mechanism leads to a form of cell death that is neither apoptotic nor necrotic, as evidenced by the absence of caspase activation, chromatin condensation, or plasma membrane rupture in the initial phase of exposure. The anti-tumor activity of SGP arises from its ability to induce this rapid membrane disruption in a wide range of malignant cell types, including carcinomas, sarcomas, and melanomas, both in vitro and in vivo. The proposed mechanism of action involves the insertion of hydrophobic helical segments into the lipid core of the membrane, followed by reorganization of adjacent phospholipids that compromises barrier function. Crucially, SGP exhibits selectivity not at the level of cellular identity but at the level of microenvironmental context: while it disrupts membranes of both normal and malignant cells in culture, its activity is effectively neutralized in vivo by extracellular matrix components such as matrigel and fibronectin, which bind and sequester the protein, preventing access to cell membranes in healthy tissues. In contrast, tumor tissues, which often exhibit disrupted or sparse extracellular matrices, permit SGP to reach and disrupt malignant cell membranes. This spatial selectivity enables potent anti-tumor effects without systemic toxicity. Compared to other pore-forming peptides such as magainin, cecropin, or granulysin, SGP demonstrates superior in vivo efficacy, greater stability, and a more predictable mechanism of action. Therapeutic applications of SGP include direct intratumoral injection for localized tumor ablation, combination with targeted delivery systems such as liposomes, and use in palliative settings for unresectable or metastatic lesions. The invention further encompasses functional derivatives and conservative variants of SGP that retain membrane-disrupting capacity while enhancing pharmacokinetic properties or reducing immunogenicity.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

The small globular protein (SGP) is a synthetic polypeptide composed of 127 amino acid residues, designed to adopt a stable, monomeric tertiary structure in aqueous solution while possessing the capacity to undergo a conformational transition upon interaction with lipid bilayers. The structure of SGP consists of three α-helical domains arranged in a triangular bundle, with a central hydrophobic core formed by leucine, alanine, and valine residues, and a surface enriched with lysine and aspartic acid residues that confer solubility and electrostatic guidance to negatively charged membrane surfaces. Functional derivatives of SGP include variants in which one or more amino acids are substituted, deleted, or appended without altering the core helical architecture or membrane-disrupting function. The term “functional derivative” refers to any polypeptide sequence that retains the ability to induce rapid, non-apoptotic, non-necrotic membrane disruption in mammalian cells, as measured by loss of membrane integrity within 60 seconds of exposure. Examples of functional derivatives include SGP-L, in which the central alanine-rich helix is replaced with a leucine-rich sequence, and SGP-E, in which lysine residues are substituted with glutamic acid, both of which exhibit diminished membrane activity. Conservative variations of SGP involve substitutions of amino acids with chemically similar residues, such as replacing leucine with isoleucine or valine, or lysine with arginine, provided that the overall hydrophobic moment and charge distribution are preserved. A conservative variation is defined as an amino acid substitution that maintains the side chain volume, polarity, and charge characteristics of the original residue, thereby preserving the structural and functional integrity of the protein. Examples include the substitution of phenylalanine with tyrosine, aspartic acid with glutamic acid, or serine with threonine. Substantially identical or highly conserved polypeptides are those that share at least 85% sequence identity with the reference SGP sequence over a defined comparison window of 100 contiguous residues, as determined by pairwise alignment algorithms. Sequence homology and identity are evaluated using the BLAST algorithm, with parameters set to a word size of 3, BLOSUM62 scoring matrix, gap opening penalty of 11, and gap extension penalty of 1. Statistical significance is assessed by the smallest sum probability (P(N)), with a threshold of less than 1×10⁻⁵ considered significant. High-scoring segment pairs are identified as contiguous regions of alignment with scores exceeding the expected random match threshold. Scoring matrices such as PAM250 and BLOSUM62 are employed to quantify evolutionary relationships and residue compatibility. An effective amount of SGP is defined as the quantity sufficient to induce membrane disruption in at least 80% of target cells within 60 minutes under physiological conditions, typically ranging from 0.1 μM to 1 mM depending on the cell type and delivery method. A biological membrane is understood to include the phospholipid bilayer of the plasma membrane, mitochondrial membrane, or other intracellular membranes composed of amphipathic lipids. Methods for disrupting membrane architecture involve the direct application of SGP to cells or tissues, resulting in physical perturbation of lipid packing, loss of membrane potential, and osmotic lysis. Membrane architecture of a cell refers to the organized arrangement of lipids, proteins, and cholesterol that maintains compartmentalization and selective permeability. Cell lysis is defined as the rupture of the plasma membrane leading to uncontrolled release of intracellular contents. Methods for selectively disrupting cell membranes rely on the differential accessibility of SGP to target cells, mediated by the presence or absence of extracellular matrix components. Selectively disrupting cell membrane refers to the preferential disruption of membranes in tumor cells over normal cells due to microenvironmental differences, not intrinsic cellular properties. Methods for treating a tumor involve the administration of SGP directly into the tumor mass or surrounding tissue, resulting in localized cell death and inhibition of tumor growth. Treating encompasses any therapeutic intervention that reduces tumor volume, inhibits metastasis, prolongs survival, or alleviates symptoms. Examples of tumors for treatment include breast carcinoma, Kaposi’s sarcoma, prostate adenocarcinoma, non-small cell lung cancer, melanoma, and glioblastoma. Administering refers to the delivery of SGP via injection, implantation, or infusion into the tumor site or adjacent tissue. Forms of administration include intratumoral, intraperitoneal, intrathecal, and interstitial infusion. Non-toxic, pharmaceutically acceptable carriers include saline, phosphate-buffered solutions, dextrose, and albumin, which maintain protein stability without inducing aggregation or denaturation. Targeted-delivery systems such as liposomes are employed to enhance bioavailability and reduce systemic exposure. Liposomes are formed by encapsulating SGP within phospholipid vesicles composed of phosphatidylcholine, cholesterol, and polyethylene glycol-modified lipids, which protect the protein from proteolysis and extend circulation time. Delivery approaches include intrathecal injections for central nervous system tumors, surgical implants for localized delivery, and interstitial infusion via catheters for diffuse tumors. Omega-3 fatty acid derivatization enhances membrane integration and reduces immunogenicity. Agent absorption is optimized by conjugating SGP to peptide transport vectors such as TAT or penetratin, which facilitate cellular uptake. Site-specific macromolecular carriers, including antibody fragments or ligand-conjugated nanoparticles, direct SGP to tumor-associated antigens. Covalent bonding between SGP and targeting moieties ensures stable conjugation during circulation. Cationic macromolecular carriers enhance binding to negatively charged tumor cell surfaces. Anti-cancer drugs may be co-administered with SGP to synergize with membrane disruption. Acid-sensitive intermediate molecules enable controlled release of SGP in the acidic tumor microenvironment. A therapeutically effective amount is the dose sufficient to achieve tumor regression or stabilization without inducing systemic toxicity. Non-necrotic, non-apoptotic cell death refers to a rapid form of cell demise characterized by membrane disruption without activation of caspases, DNA fragmentation, or inflammatory signaling. A cell population refers to a group of cells of homogeneous or heterogeneous origin exposed to SGP under defined conditions. Apoptosis is a programmed form of cell death involving caspase activation, chromatin condensation, and membrane blebbing. Caspase activation is measured by cleavage of caspase-3 substrates using fluorogenic assays. Primary tumor growth inhibition is demonstrated by reduction in tumor volume compared to untreated controls. Metastatic tumor growth inhibition is confirmed by absence of distant lesions in histological sections of organs. Formulations include lyophilized powders, aqueous suspensions, and polymer-based depots for sustained release.

### EXAMPLE 1

The small globular protein (SGP), its leucine-substituted variant (SGP-L), and its glutamic acid-substituted variant (SGP-E) were synthesized using solid-phase peptide synthesis with Fmoc chemistry on PEG-bound resin. Synthesis was performed using a Miligen automatic peptide synthesizer (Model 9050), with deprotection monitored by UV absorbance at 210 nm. After cleavage from the resin using trifluoroacetic acid, crude peptides were purified by reverse-phase high-performance liquid chromatography on an ODS column (20 × 250 mm) using a water/acetonitrile gradient containing 0.1% trifluoroacetic acid. Fractions were collected, lyophilized, and analyzed by amino acid analysis following acid hydrolysis in 5.7 M HCl at 110 °C for 24 hours. Molecular weight was confirmed by fast atom bombardment mass spectrometry using a JEOL JMX-HX100 instrument, yielding a base peak at 7555.1 m/z, consistent with the calculated mass of 7554.8 for C₃₆₇H₆₃₉O₇₇N₉₁·H⁺. Peptide concentrations were determined spectrophotometrically using an extinction coefficient of 8000 M⁻¹cm⁻¹ at 280 nm, derived from the presence of three tyrosine residues and one tryptophan. Gel filtration HPLC was performed on a COSMOSIL 5DIOL-300 column using Tris buffer (10 mM Tris, 150 mM NaCl, pH 7.4) to confirm monomeric state and absence of aggregates.

### EXAMPLE 2

A computer-generated three-dimensional model of SGP was constructed using the Insight II software suite running on an Octane SSE workstation (Silicon Graphics). The initial structure was generated from a de novo helical bundle design, followed by energy minimization and molecular dynamics simulation in explicit solvent over 5 nanoseconds. The final model exhibited a compact globular fold with three amphipathic helices arranged in a triangular bundle, with hydrophobic residues oriented inward and charged residues exposed on the surface. Electrostatic potential mapping revealed a positively charged surface patch complementary to the anionic phospholipid head groups of mammalian membranes. Hydrophobicity profiling confirmed a continuous hydrophobic surface spanning the helical interface, consistent with membrane insertion capability.

### EXAMPLE 3

Human cell lines including KS1767 (Kaposi’s sarcoma-derived), MDA-MB-435 (breast carcinoma-derived), PC-3 (prostate carcinoma-derived), H358 (lung carcinoma-derived), and dermal microvessel endothelial cells were obtained from commercial sources and cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and antibiotics. Cells were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere of 5% CO₂ and passaged every 48 hours. All experiments were conducted using cells in logarithmic growth phase, with confluency maintained below 80%.

### EXAMPLE 4

Cells were treated with SGP, SGP-L, or SGP-E at concentrations ranging from 0.1 μM to 1 mM for periods of 1 minute to 24 hours. Viability was assessed by dual staining with acridine orange and ethidium bromide, followed by fluorescence microscopy. Cells exhibiting nuclear condensation or membrane permeability were scored as non-viable. LC₅₀ values were calculated from dose-response curves. SGP exhibited an LC₅₀ of approximately 3 μM across all cell lines tested, while SGP-L and SGP-E showed LC₅₀ values exceeding 30 μM. Membrane disruption was confirmed by Trypan Blue exclusion assays, which demonstrated immediate uptake of dye in SGP-treated cells. Control peptides showed no cytotoxicity. These results confirmed that the helical integrity of SGP is essential for membrane-disrupting activity and that SGP lacks selectivity between malignant and non-malignant cells in vitro.

### EXAMPLE 5

Human tumor xenografts were established in nude mice by subcutaneous injection of 10⁶ tumor cells (MDA-MB-435, KS1767, PC-3, or H358) in serum-free medium. Tumors were allowed to grow to 100–600 mm³ before treatment. SGP was administered directly into the tumor mass via 40 μL injections delivered in 5 μL increments at concentrations of 100 μM or 1 mM. Injections were performed weekly for four weeks. Tumor volumes were measured using calipers and calculated as (length × width²)/2. SGP-treated mice exhibited a five-fold reduction in tumor volume compared to PBS-treated controls. No significant difference was observed between the two SGP doses. Metastatic spread to lungs and liver was absent in SGP-treated animals. Tumor-free survival exceeded four months in treated mice. Histological analysis confirmed complete ablation of tumor cells without residual viable tissue.

### EXAMPLE 6

Tumors and major organs from treated mice were fixed in Bouin’s solution, embedded in paraffin, sectioned, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin. SGP-treated tumors displayed widespread cell death with condensed nuclei and loss of cellular architecture, while the extracellular matrix remained intact. No evidence of apoptosis was observed in the initial phase of treatment; however, delayed caspase-3 activation was detected at 24 hours, suggesting secondary apoptotic events following membrane disruption. No pathological changes were observed in liver, kidney, spleen, or heart tissue.

### EXAMPLE 7

Female nude mice received intradermal injections of 10 μL of 100 μM SGP or PBS into the dorsal skin. Injection sites were monitored daily for two weeks. No erythema, edema, necrosis, or ulceration was observed in SGP-treated animals. Histological examination confirmed normal skin architecture with no inflammatory infiltrate or tissue damage, demonstrating the absence of systemic or local toxicity.

### EXAMPLE 8

A matrigel-based assay was developed to mimic the extracellular matrix of solid tumors. KS1767 cells were seeded and coated with a thin layer of matrigel or polymeric fibronectin prior to SGP exposure. In the absence of matrigel, SGP induced rapid cell death within 10 minutes. In the presence of matrigel or fibronectin, SGP failed to induce any cytotoxicity, even at 1 mM concentration. Ethanol and doxorubicin retained cytotoxic activity under the same conditions, indicating that the matrix physically sequesters SGP rather than inhibiting its mechanism. These findings explain the discrepancy between in vitro non-selectivity and in vivo tumor selectivity, as the extracellular matrix in healthy tissues prevents SGP access to cell membranes, while the disrupted matrix in tumors permits localized activity.
