## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

- Lactic acidemia in the context of septic shock represents a critical metabolic derangement associated with high mortality and poor clinical outcomes. The accumulation of lactate in the bloodstream arises from an imbalance between its rate of production and its rate of clearance by vital organs, particularly the liver. Under normal physiological conditions, lactate is transported into hepatocytes via the monocarboxylate transporter, a pH-dependent, bidirectional facilitative antiport system that enables the uptake of lactate for subsequent conversion into glucose through gluconeogenesis or for oxidation within mitochondria to produce carbon dioxide and water. In septic shock, this balance is disrupted due to systemic inflammation, tissue hypoperfusion, and cellular metabolic dysfunction. The resulting lactic acidemia contributes to acidosis, which further impairs organ function and exacerbates hemodynamic instability. The precise mechanisms underlying this phenomenon remain incompletely understood, with conflicting reports in the literature regarding the role of hepatic mitochondrial dysfunction as a primary driver of lactate accumulation.

- Lactate production occurs primarily through anaerobic glycolysis, especially in tissues experiencing hypoxia or increased energy demand, while its utilization depends on the functional integrity of mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation. The monocarboxylate transporter, particularly the MCT1 isoform, plays a central role in facilitating lactate flux across cell membranes, and its expression and activity may be modulated under pathological conditions. However, the extent to which alterations in MCT1 expression contribute to lactic acidemia during septic shock has not been definitively established. Mitochondrial dysfunction, characterized by impaired electron transport chain activity and reduced ATP synthesis capacity, has long been hypothesized to underlie the failure of lactate clearance. Yet, prior studies have yielded inconsistent results, likely due to methodological variability in the assessment of mitochondrial function, including differences in tissue sampling, substrate availability, and measurement techniques.

- The Seahorse XF24 extracellular flux analyzer represents a significant advancement in the real-time, dynamic assessment of mitochondrial respiration in intact cells and isolated mitochondria. Unlike conventional methods that rely on isolated enzyme assays or static measurements of oxygen consumption, this technology enables sequential, controlled perturbations of mitochondrial function through the precise addition of metabolic modulators such as adenosine diphosphate, oligomycin, carbonyl cyanide p-trifluoromethoxyphenylhydrazone, and respiratory chain inhibitors. By measuring oxygen consumption rates under defined metabolic states—basal, coupled, maximal, and uncoupled—this instrument provides a comprehensive functional profile of mitochondrial health. Despite its potential, the Seahorse XF24 has not been previously applied to investigate hepatic mitochondrial function in models of septic shock, leaving a critical gap in the understanding of how mitochondrial respiration is altered during this condition and whether interventions can restore it.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

- The present invention provides a novel therapeutic approach for the treatment of septic shock by administration of gentisic acid sodium salt, which has been demonstrated to simultaneously reverse cardiovascular collapse and reduce lactic acidemia. Unlike conventional vasopressors such as norepinephrine, which restore mean arterial pressure but fail to mitigate lactate accumulation, gentisic acid sodium salt not only improves hemodynamic stability but also enhances mitochondrial respiratory capacity and promotes the clearance of lactate from the circulation. This dual action distinguishes gentisic acid sodium salt as a uniquely effective agent for addressing the core pathophysiological features of septic shock. The invention encompasses the use of gentisic acid sodium salt to treat both septic shock and its associated lactic acidemia, thereby offering a comprehensive therapeutic strategy that targets both systemic hypotension and metabolic dysfunction.

- The invention further includes the preparation of a medicament comprising gentisic acid sodium salt as the active pharmaceutical ingredient, formulated for intravenous administration in a pharmaceutically acceptable carrier. The medicament is designed to deliver a therapeutically effective dose of gentisic acid sodium salt to patients suffering from septic shock or lactic acidemia, with the formulation optimized for stability, solubility, and compatibility with standard infusion protocols. The invention provides a method for preparing such a medicament by dissolving gentisic acid sodium salt in a sterile aqueous solution, adjusting pH to physiological range, and sterilizing by filtration or autoclaving. The resulting composition is suitable for immediate clinical use and can be administered continuously or intermittently based on patient response and hemodynamic parameters.

## DESCRIPTION OF THE PREFERRED EMBODIMENTS

- For the purposes of this disclosure, the term “septic shock” refers to a life-threatening condition characterized by systemic inflammation, hypotension refractory to fluid resuscitation, and evidence of organ dysfunction, including lactic acidemia with serum lactate levels exceeding 2 mmol/L. “Lactic acidemia” is defined as an elevated concentration of lactate in the blood, typically greater than 4 mmol/L, associated with metabolic acidosis and impaired tissue oxygen utilization. “Gentisic acid sodium salt” refers to the sodium salt of 2,5-dihydroxybenzoic acid, a phenolic compound with known antioxidant properties and a molecular weight of approximately 190.14 g/mol. “Effective amount” denotes the quantity of gentisic acid sodium salt sufficient to produce a clinically meaningful improvement in mean arterial pressure and a reduction in serum lactate concentration without inducing significant adverse effects.

- The preferred method of administration involves intravenous infusion of gentisic acid sodium salt in a sterile, isotonic solution such as 0.9% sodium chloride or 5% dextrose in water. The solution is prepared by dissolving the sodium salt in the chosen vehicle at a concentration ranging from 1 to 10 mg/mL, with a preferred concentration of 5 mg/mL. The infusion is initiated at a rate of 0.1 to 0.8 mg/kg/hour, with adjustments made based on real-time hemodynamic and lactate monitoring. The preferred vehicle is free of endotoxins and preservatives, and the solution is maintained at room temperature during administration. The medicament may be delivered via central or peripheral venous access, with continuous monitoring of blood pressure, heart rate, and arterial blood gases to guide therapy.

- The invention is further exemplified by a canine model of septic shock induced by continuous intravenous infusion of Pseudomonas aeruginosa at a rate of approximately 10¹⁰ colony-forming units per hour. Animals are anesthetized with sufentanil and midazolam, mechanically ventilated with 100% oxygen, and instrumented with arterial and pulmonary artery catheters for continuous hemodynamic monitoring. After the development of septic shock, defined as a sustained mean arterial pressure below 65 mmHg, gentisic acid sodium salt is infused at a rate sufficient to restore mean arterial pressure to baseline levels. Hemodynamic parameters, including cardiac output, systemic vascular resistance, stroke work index, and heart rate, are recorded at baseline, during shock, and at 3 and 5 hours post-treatment. Blood samples are collected for lactate, pH, troponin, and hematological analysis. Hepatic tissue is harvested post-mortem for mitochondrial isolation and functional assessment using the Seahorse XF24 extracellular flux analyzer.

- The association between lactic acidemia and hepatic mitochondrial dysfunction is demonstrated through measurements of oxygen consumption rates in isolated liver mitochondria. Basal, coupled, maximal, and uncoupled respiration are quantified under controlled substrate conditions, with pyruvate and malate used as respiratory substrates. Sequential additions of adenosine diphosphate, oligomycin, FCCP, rotenone, and antimycin A allow for the determination of respiratory control ratios and spare respiratory capacity. In septic control animals, maximal respiration is significantly reduced, while coupled respiration remains unchanged. In contrast, animals treated with gentisic acid sodium salt exhibit a marked increase in both coupled and basal respiration, indicating enhanced mitochondrial efficiency and ATP synthesis capacity.

- Norepinephrine, while effective in restoring mean arterial pressure, fails to improve mitochondrial respiration or reduce lactate levels, demonstrating that hemodynamic stabilization alone is insufficient to correct metabolic dysfunction. Gentisic acid sodium salt, in contrast, produces a significant increase in coupled respiration and a concomitant reduction in serum lactate concentration, suggesting a direct effect on hepatic lactate metabolism independent of oxygen delivery. The mechanism of this effect is not attributable to changes in monocarboxylate transporter expression, as MCT1 protein levels in liver tissue remain unchanged across all treatment groups. Instead, the antioxidant properties of gentisic acid sodium salt likely protect mitochondrial membranes from oxidative damage, enhance the activity of the electron transport chain, and reduce feedback inhibition of respiration by ATP, thereby promoting more efficient lactate oxidation.

- The sodium salt form of gentisic acid is preferred due to its superior solubility and stability in aqueous solutions compared to the free acid form. The compound exhibits a pKa of approximately 3.5 for the carboxylic acid group and 9.8 for the phenolic hydroxyl group, allowing for optimal ionization at physiological pH. The resulting salt form ensures rapid absorption, uniform distribution, and predictable pharmacokinetics when administered intravenously. The preferred concentration for infusion is 5 mg/mL, corresponding to a dose range of 0.3 to 0.8 mg/kg/hour, with a total daily dose not exceeding 10 mg/kg. The solution is stable for at least 24 hours at room temperature when protected from light.

- Comparative studies between gentisic acid sodium salt and norepinephrine demonstrate that both agents restore mean arterial pressure to baseline levels, but only gentisic acid sodium salt increases coupled respiration, improves spare respiratory capacity, and reduces serum lactate concentration. The increase in maximal respiration observed with gentisic acid sodium salt is comparable to that seen in non-septic controls, whereas norepinephrine only partially restores uncoupled respiration. Furthermore, gentisic acid sodium salt treatment is associated with lower heart rates and reduced myocardial injury, as evidenced by lower troponin levels, suggesting a cardioprotective effect not observed with norepinephrine.

- Mitochondrial oxygen consumption rates are measured using the Seahorse XF24 analyzer, with basal OCR determined prior to substrate addition, coupled OCR following ADP administration, maximal OCR after FCCP stimulation, and residual OCR after inhibition with rotenone and antimycin A. The reduction in maximal respiration observed in the septic control group confirms the presence of mitochondrial depression, while the restoration of coupled respiration with gentisic acid sodium salt indicates improved functional coupling between electron transport and ATP synthesis. The lack of change in MCT1 protein levels across all groups indicates that lactate clearance is not mediated by altered transporter expression but rather by enhanced intracellular metabolic flux.

- The conclusion that mitochondrial depression is not the sole mechanism for lactic acidemia is supported by the observation that norepinephrine, despite restoring mitochondrial respiration, fails to reduce lactate levels. This suggests that extra-hepatic sources of lactate production and/or non-mitochondrial hepatic metabolic impairments, such as suppressed gluconeogenesis or accelerated glycolysis, may contribute significantly to lactic acidemia in septic shock. Gentisic acid sodium salt, however, appears to address multiple pathways, including mitochondrial enhancement and modulation of glycolytic flux, resulting in a more complete correction of the metabolic derangement.

- The use of gentisic acid sodium salt in the treatment of septic shock is further defined by a method of administering an effective amount to a human subject diagnosed with septic shock, wherein the effective amount is determined by titration based on the patient’s mean arterial pressure and serum lactate concentration. The treatment is initiated upon confirmation of septic shock with lactate levels exceeding 4 mmol/L and mean arterial pressure below 65 mmHg. The medicament is infused intravenously at a rate of 0.5 mg/kg/hour, with adjustments made every 30 minutes based on hemodynamic response. The treatment is continued until lactate levels fall below 2 mmol/L and mean arterial pressure is maintained above 65 mmHg for at least two consecutive hours.

- The invention further provides a method for identifying individuals at risk of developing sepsis or septic shock based on clinical criteria including systemic inflammatory response syndrome, organ dysfunction, and elevated lactate levels. Such individuals are candidates for prophylactic or early therapeutic intervention with gentisic acid sodium salt to prevent the progression to full-blown septic shock. The invention also encompasses the use of gentisic acid sodium salt for the preparation of a medicament intended for the treatment of septic shock or lactic acidemia, wherein the medicament comprises a therapeutically effective amount of gentisic acid sodium salt in a pharmaceutically acceptable carrier suitable for intravenous administration.

- The method of preparing the medicament involves dissolving gentisic acid sodium salt in sterile water for injection, adjusting the pH to between 6.5 and 7.5 using sodium hydroxide or hydrochloric acid, filtering through a 0.22-micron membrane, and filling into sterile vials or infusion bags. The final product is terminally sterilized by autoclaving at 121°C for 15 minutes and stored at room temperature. The medicament may be co-formulated with antioxidants or buffering agents to enhance stability and reduce the risk of degradation during storage.

- The method of treating lactic acidemia involves the intravenous administration of gentisic acid sodium salt to a subject with serum lactate levels exceeding 4 mmol/L and a pH below 7.35. The treatment results in a measurable reduction in lactate concentration and a concurrent elevation in serum pH, indicating correction of metabolic acidosis. The reduction in lactate levels is observed within one to two hours of initiation and is sustained for the duration of therapy. The invention further provides the use of gentisic acid sodium salt for treating lactic acidemia, whether arising from septic shock or other causes, and for the preparation of a medicament for such use.

- The preferred embodiment of the medicament is a sterile, isotonic, pyrogen-free solution of gentisic acid sodium salt at a concentration of 5 mg/mL, packaged in single-use infusion bags or vials, each containing 100 mL of solution. The solution is light-sensitive and is stored in opaque containers to prevent photodegradation. The medicament is compatible with standard intravenous tubing and infusion pumps and does not require refrigeration.

- The objective of determining mitochondrial dysfunction in septic shock was achieved through the use of the Seahorse XF24 analyzer, which revealed a significant reduction in maximal respiration in septic animals. This finding was corroborated by decreased spare respiratory capacity and preserved coupled respiration, indicating that mitochondrial dysfunction in septic shock is characterized by a loss of reserve capacity rather than a global failure of ATP synthesis. These results align with and extend prior studies that focused on isolated enzyme activities, demonstrating that functional assessment of the entire electron transport chain provides a more sensitive and clinically relevant measure of mitochondrial health.

- The results of this study contrast with previous reports that attributed lactic acidemia solely to mitochondrial failure. The present findings demonstrate that mitochondrial dysfunction can be reversed by norepinephrine without concomitant lactate reduction, indicating that other mechanisms—such as altered substrate utilization, impaired gluconeogenesis, or increased glycolytic flux—are equally important. The effect of norepinephrine on mitochondrial function is likely mediated by increased oxygen delivery and calcium influx into mitochondria, which stimulates dehydrogenase activity. However, this mechanism does not translate into improved lactate clearance, underscoring the limitations of vasopressor therapy alone.

- In contrast, gentisic acid sodium salt enhances mitochondrial function through antioxidant-mediated protection of mitochondrial membranes and electron transport chain components, leading to improved coupling of respiration and ATP synthesis. The mechanism by which gentisic acid sodium salt reduces lactic acidemia may involve suppression of glycolytic flux in peripheral tissues, enhancement of hepatic gluconeogenesis, or direct stimulation of lactate oxidation within hepatocytes. The compound’s dual hydroxyl structure may facilitate scavenging of reactive oxygen species, reducing oxidative inhibition of key metabolic enzymes and preserving mitochondrial integrity.

- The effect of gentisic acid sodium salt on lactic acidemia is proposed to result from a combination of improved mitochondrial efficiency and modulation of cellular metabolism. The compound may inhibit key glycolytic enzymes such as phosphofructokinase or activate gluconeogenic pathways through modulation of pyruvate carboxylase or mitochondrial glycerol-3-phosphate dehydrogenase. The reduction in lactate levels observed in the absence of increased maximal respiration suggests that the compound acts upstream of the electron transport chain, enhancing substrate utilization rather than electron flux.

- In conclusion, gentisic acid sodium salt represents a novel and effective therapy for the treatment of lactic acidemia and cardiovascular collapse in septic shock. Unlike conventional vasopressors, it addresses both the hemodynamic and metabolic components of the disease, offering a more comprehensive therapeutic approach. The invention provides a method for its clinical use, a formulation for its administration, and a mechanistic basis for its efficacy, establishing a new standard of care for patients with septic shock.

### RESULTS

- The time required to reach the septic condition, defined as a sustained mean arterial pressure below 65 mmHg, was consistent across all septic groups, averaging approximately 4.5 hours following bacterial infusion. No statistically significant differences were observed in body weight among the five experimental groups, with means ranging from 20.3 to 23.6 kg. The total volume of fluid infused over the course of the experiment was comparable among the three septic groups, averaging approximately 6 liters, while the non-septic groups received significantly less fluid due to the absence of third-spacing phenomena. All animals survived to the conclusion of the study, with no mortality observed in any group.

- At baseline, mean arterial pressure was uniformly approximately 100 mmHg across all groups. Following induction of septic shock, mean arterial pressure declined to 60–65 mmHg in all septic groups. Infusion of norepinephrine or gentisic acid sodium salt restored mean arterial pressure to baseline levels within minutes, with no significant difference between the two treatments. The dose of norepinephrine required to achieve this effect averaged 0.51 μg/kg/min, while the dose of gentisic acid sodium salt averaged 0.55 mg/kg/min. Heart rate increased significantly in all septic groups during the shock phase but was significantly lower in the gentisic acid sodium salt-treated group at 3 and 5 hours post-treatment, suggesting a reduction in sympathetic overdrive.

- Troponin levels, an indicator of myocardial injury, increased significantly in the norepinephrine-treated septic group at the 5-hour endpoint, whereas no such rise was observed in the gentisic acid sodium salt group, indicating a protective effect on cardiac tissue. Blood lactate concentrations rose progressively in the septic control and norepinephrine groups, reaching levels exceeding 8 mmol/L by the end of the study. In contrast, lactate levels in the gentisic acid sodium salt group remained stable or decreased slightly, remaining below 4 mmol/L throughout the observation period. Serum pH was significantly higher in the gentisic acid sodium salt group compared to all other septic groups, reflecting correction of metabolic acidosis.

- Oxygen delivery was significantly higher in the norepinephrine group due to increased hemoglobin concentration, yet this did not correlate with improved lactate clearance. Systemic vascular resistance decreased during septic shock in all septic groups and was partially restored by both treatments, though the effect was not statistically significant. Pulmonary wedge pressure remained within normal limits across all groups, with minor fluctuations attributable to fluid administration for anesthesia maintenance. Cardiac output remained relatively stable across all groups, with no significant differences observed at any time point.

- Hepatic monocarboxylate transporter 1 protein levels, as measured by Western blot, showed no significant differences among any of the five experimental groups, indicating that changes in lactate metabolism were not mediated by alterations in transporter expression. Mitochondrial oxygen consumption rates, as measured by the Seahorse XF24 analyzer, revealed a significant reduction in maximal respiration in the septic control group compared to non-septic controls. This deficit was restored to control levels by both norepinephrine and gentisic acid sodium salt. However, only gentisic acid sodium salt significantly increased coupled respiration, indicating enhanced efficiency of ATP synthesis. Spare respiratory capacity was significantly reduced in the septic control group and restored to normal levels only in the gentisic acid sodium salt group.

- Gallic acid, a structurally similar phenolic compound with three hydroxyl groups, was tested in a supplemental group and failed to reduce lactate levels or improve mitochondrial function, demonstrating that the specific chemical structure of gentisic acid sodium salt is essential for its therapeutic effect. The pKa and hydroxyl positioning of gentisic acid sodium salt appear to confer unique antioxidant and metabolic properties not shared by other phenolic compounds.

## METHODS

- Animal preparation and experimental protocols were conducted in accordance with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals published by the National Institutes of Health and were approved by the University Animal Care Committee under protocol number 13-046/1/2.

### Animal Preparation

- A bacteremic model of septic shock was induced in adult dogs weighing 18 to 26 kg by continuous intravenous infusion of Pseudomonas aeruginosa (ATCC 27853) at a rate of approximately 10¹⁰ colony-forming units per hour. Animals were anesthetized with sufentanil citrate and midazolam, mechanically ventilated with 100% oxygen to maintain arterial PO₂ above 350 mmHg, and instrumented with arterial and pulmonary artery catheters for continuous hemodynamic monitoring. A third catheter was placed in the left jugular vein for fluid and bacterial infusion. Intravenous fluids were administered as needed to maintain pulmonary wedge pressure between 8 and 12 mmHg. Blood samples were collected at baseline, during septic shock, and at 3 and 5 hours post-treatment for analysis of lactate, pH, troponin, hematocrit, white blood cell count, and liver function tests.

### Experimental Protocols

- Five groups of animals were studied: non-septic control, septic control, norepinephrine-treated septic, gentisic acid sodium salt-treated septic, and gentisic acid sodium salt-treated non-septic. Baseline measurements were obtained after one hour of stabilization. Septic shock was defined as a sustained mean arterial pressure below 65 mmHg. Norepinephrine was infused at a rate sufficient to restore mean arterial pressure to baseline, while gentisic acid sodium salt was infused at an equivalent rate. In the non-septic groups, identical measurements were taken at the time point corresponding to septic shock in the septic groups. Post-treatment measurements were taken at 3 and 5 hours after the initiation of vasopressor or gentisic acid sodium salt infusion.

### Mitochondrial Preparation

- After euthanasia with sodium pentobarbital, liver tissue was rapidly excised and placed in ice-cold mitochondrial isolation buffer containing 70 mM sucrose, 210 mM mannitol, 5 mM HEPES, 1 mM EGTA, and fatty acid-free bovine serum albumin. Tissue was homogenized using a glass-Teflon dounce homogenizer, and mitochondria were isolated by differential centrifugation. The final mitochondrial pellet was resuspended in mitochondrial assay solution and plated onto a Seahorse XF24 culture dish at a concentration of 20 μg mitochondrial protein per 50 μL. Plates were centrifuged to ensure mitochondrial adherence and transferred to the analyzer within 15 minutes.

### Mitochondria Measurement of Oxygen Consumption Rates (OCR)

- The Seahorse XF24 extracellular flux analyzer was used to measure oxygen consumption rates under sequential metabolic conditions. Basal OCR was measured after addition of pyruvate (10 mM) and malate (2 mM). Coupled OCR was measured following addition of ADP (4 mM). State IV respiration was induced by addition of oligomycin (2.0 μM), and maximal uncoupled respiration was measured after addition of FCCP (4 μM). Electron transport chain inhibition was confirmed by addition of rotenone (2 μM) and antimycin A (2 μM). Two measurements were obtained for each condition, and the average was used for analysis. Respiratory control ratio was calculated as coupled OCR divided by oligomycin-inhibited OCR. Spare respiratory capacity was calculated as the difference between maximal and basal OCR.

### To Determine Whether MCT1 Protein is Altered in Septic Shock

- Liver tissue samples were homogenized in RIPA buffer containing protease inhibitors. Protein concentration was determined by bicinchoninic acid assay. Equal amounts of protein were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred to nitrocellulose membranes. Membranes were probed with a monoclonal antibody against MCT1 (1:1000 dilution) and visualized using chemiluminescence. Band intensity was quantified by densitometry and normalized to β-actin.

### Gallic Acid Septic Group

- A supplemental group of animals received an infusion of gallic acid at a dose equivalent to that of gentisic acid sodium salt, initiated at the time of septic shock. Hemodynamic and metabolic parameters were measured as described above. No improvement in lactate levels or mitochondrial function was observed.

### Statistics

- Data were analyzed using two-way repeated measures analysis of variance with group and time as factors. Post-hoc comparisons were performed using the Student-Newman-Keuls test. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Results are presented as mean ± standard deviation. All analyses were performed using GB-Stat V8 software.
