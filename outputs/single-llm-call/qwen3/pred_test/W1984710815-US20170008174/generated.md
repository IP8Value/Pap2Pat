## BACKGROUND

- motivate human-like artificial intelligence

The pursuit of artificial intelligence has long been constrained by a fundamental limitation: machines have been designed to process data objectively, without any internalized sense of self or experiential context. While traditional computing systems excel at pattern recognition, statistical inference, and rule-based decision-making, they lack the capacity to perceive the world as a unified, embodied experience relative to a central point of reference—the self. This absence renders them incapable of true understanding, contextual awareness, or adaptive behavior grounded in subjective experience. Human intelligence, by contrast, arises not from the accumulation of facts but from the internalization of sensory inputs into a coherent, self-referential framework that allows for identification, recognition, comprehension, and anticipation. The motivation for developing human-like artificial intelligence is therefore not merely to replicate human behavior, but to replicate the underlying cognitive architecture that makes such behavior possible. Without this architecture, machines remain tools, not agents; they respond to stimuli, but they do not experience them. The goal of this invention is to bridge that gap by engineering a system that does not just compute, but perceives, remembers, and reacts as a self-aware entity would.

- introduce alan turing

Alan Turing, in his seminal 1950 paper “Computing Machinery and Intelligence,” proposed a behavioral criterion for machine intelligence: if a machine could engage in conversation indistinguishable from that of a human, it could be said to think. This became known as the Turing Test, and it shifted the focus of artificial intelligence from internal mechanisms to observable outcomes. Turing did not claim to define thought itself, but rather to provide a practical, empirical standard for its attribution. His insight was profoundly correct: intelligence, as it manifests in the world, is expressed through interaction. Yet, while the Turing Test remains a cultural touchstone, it has never been achieved in any system that lacks a foundational sense of self. Machines that pass conversational benchmarks today do so through vast statistical models trained on human text, not through genuine comprehension or internalized awareness. They simulate understanding without possessing it. This invention addresses that deficiency by constructing a system capable of generating responses not from pre-programmed databases, but from an internalized, self-referential knowledge structure that mirrors the human cognitive architecture.

- describe turing test

The Turing Test, as originally conceived, evaluates a machine’s ability to exhibit intelligent behavior equivalent to, or indistinguishable from, that of a human in natural language conversation. The test operates under the assumption that if an interrogator cannot reliably distinguish between a human and a machine based solely on their responses, then the machine may be said to possess intelligence. However, the test is inherently behavioral and does not require the machine to possess any internal representation of self, memory, or context. A machine may pass the test by mimicking human speech patterns, exploiting linguistic ambiguities, or avoiding direct answers—none of which imply understanding. The test, therefore, measures performance, not cognition. This invention transcends the Turing Test by not only enabling verbal responses but by grounding those responses in a system that internalizes sensory input, correlates it with self-location, and generates behavior as a function of experiential memory. The system does not simulate conversation; it engages in it from a position of embodied awareness.

- define human-like visual artificial intelligence

Human-like visual artificial intelligence is the capacity of a machine to perceive, interpret, and respond to visual stimuli in a manner that is functionally and phenomenologically equivalent to human vision. It is not sufficient for a machine to detect edges, classify objects, or estimate depth using algorithms derived from computer vision. True human-like visual intelligence requires the formation of a three-dimensional photometric image that is not merely a computational reconstruction but an internalized representation—calibrated to the machine’s own bodily coordinate frame, contextualized by prior experience, and imbued with meaning through relational correlation. This intelligence is characterized by the ability to distinguish between threat and benignity not through statistical thresholds but through learned, embodied associations; to recognize an object not merely by its shape or color but by its functional relevance to the self; and to perceive depth not as a mathematical variable but as a spatial relationship relative to one’s own position in the world. This invention achieves this through a novel architecture that fuses binocular disparity, convergence angle, image size, motion parallax, texture gradient, and occlusion cues into a single, internally consistent 3D-photometric image that serves as the primary sensory input for all higher cognitive functions.

- introduce robotic self

The robotic self is the central, self-referential coordinate frame within the Relational Robotic Controller that serves as the origin point for all sensory perception, motor control, and cognitive processing. Unlike traditional robotic systems that treat sensor data as external inputs to be processed independently, the robotic self integrates all sensory modalities—visual, auditory, tactile, olfactory, gustatory, and vestibular—into a unified, proprioceptive representation of the machine’s own body and its immediate spatial environment. This self is not a software module or a database; it is a dynamic, continuously updated nodal map that encodes the location of every surface point on the robotic body, the position of every joint, and the spatial extent of every reachable volume. The robotic self is formed through behavioral programming, specifically through an itch-scratch methodology wherein the robot learns to locate and touch every point on its own body and within its near space. This process establishes a foundational awareness of self-location, which becomes the reference point for all subsequent perception, recognition, and action.

- describe visual relational robotic controller

The Visual Relational Robotic Controller is a specialized subsystem of the broader Relational Robotic Controller that is dedicated to the acquisition, processing, and internalization of visual sensory data. It consists of a pair of stereo cameras with convergence mechanisms, a multi-modal CCD-array interface circuit, a retinotopically organized neural network, and a 3D-photometric image generator that projects pixel data into a coordinate frame aligned with the robotic self. The controller does not merely analyze images; it constructs a living, dynamic representation of the visual world that is calibrated to the robot’s own body and experience. Every pixel in the resulting 3D-photometric image is indexed not only by its position on the image plane but by its depth, color, texture, motion, and relational context within the self’s coordinate frame. The controller correlates these visual inputs with tactile feedback, auditory cues, and prior behavioral sequences to generate a unified perceptual field. This field is not a passive representation but an active, predictive model that anticipates object movement, evaluates threat potential, and initiates motor responses based on learned associations.

- differentiate between objective and subjective data

Objective data are those that can be measured, quantified, and replicated independently of any observer. In traditional robotics, visual data are objective: pixel intensities, edge gradients, depth maps, and object classifications are all outputs of mathematical algorithms that operate on raw sensor inputs. Subjective data, by contrast, are those that are experienced relative to a self. In human vision, the perception of color, depth, and motion is not merely a function of light wavelengths or retinal stimulation—it is shaped by memory, expectation, emotion, and bodily context. The same red object may be perceived as threatening or comforting depending on prior experience. This invention transforms objective visual data into subjective experience by embedding them within the robotic self’s coordinate frame and correlating them with behavioral history. The system does not just “see” an object; it knows where it is in relation to its own hand, whether it has encountered it before, and whether it has moved toward or away from it in the past. This transformation from objective to subjective is the defining feature of human-like artificial intelligence.

- define machine-like intelligence

Machine-like intelligence refers to the capacity of a computational system to perform tasks that require pattern recognition, logical inference, or rule-based decision-making without any internal representation of self, context, or experience. Such systems operate on data that are external, static, and decontextualized. They classify images, translate languages, or navigate environments using algorithms trained on vast datasets, but they do not remember the experience of doing so. A machine-like intelligence may recognize a face, but it does not recall having seen that face before in a particular context. It may calculate the trajectory of a moving object, but it does not anticipate its motion based on prior interactions. It responds to stimuli, but it does not understand them. Machine-like intelligence is efficient, scalable, and deterministic, but it lacks the adaptability, creativity, and contextual awareness that characterize biological cognition.

- define human-like intelligence

Human-like intelligence is the capacity of a system to perceive, interpret, and respond to the world through an internalized, self-referential framework that integrates sensory input with embodied experience, relational memory, and contextual awareness. It is not defined by the complexity of its algorithms or the volume of its data, but by the coherence of its internal representation. A human-like intelligence does not merely detect objects; it recognizes them as relevant to its own existence. It does not merely calculate distances; it understands them in terms of reach, safety, and consequence. It does not merely process language; it comprehends meaning through the lens of prior interaction. This intelligence emerges from the continuous correlation of sensory inputs with the robotic self’s coordinate frame, the formation of procedural and declarative memory through behavioral programming, and the ability to generate novel responses based on learned associations rather than pre-programmed rules.

- list six requirements of human intelligence

The attainment of human-like intelligence in a machine requires the fulfillment of six essential requirements, each of which corresponds to a fundamental aspect of biological cognition.

- describe requirement 1

The first requirement is the ability to relate, correlate, prioritize, and remember sensory input patterns in a manner analogous to the human brain. This is not a matter of storing data, but of establishing dynamic, multi-dimensional relationships between inputs and outputs, between sensory modalities, and between past experiences and present stimuli. The system must continuously compare new inputs with stored patterns, identify similarities and anomalies, and assign priority based on relevance to the self. This relational processing is not performed by a central processor but is distributed across a network of correlation sequencers that operate in parallel, each maintaining a unique set of associations.

- describe requirement 2

The second requirement is the establishment of a self-location and identification coordinate frame that provides proprioceptive self-knowledge. This coordinate frame is not a fixed reference point but a continuously updated, three-dimensional nodal map that encodes the position of every surface point on the robotic body and every reachable point in the surrounding space. This map is formed through behavioral training, specifically through the itch-scratch methodology, wherein the robot learns to locate and touch every point on its own body and within its near space. Once established, this frame becomes the origin for all perception, enabling the system to understand the location of objects relative to its own limbs, to anticipate movement, and to navigate with spatial awareness.

- describe requirement 3

The third requirement is the capacity for experiential intelligence, wherein learning is derived not from abstract datasets but from lived, embodied experience. The system must remember not just what it saw, but how it reacted, what it felt, and what resulted. This form of intelligence is procedural and declarative, rooted in the formation of hierarchical task diagrams that encode sequences of behavior triggered by specific sensory patterns. The system learns by doing, by failing, by repeating, and by adjusting. Its knowledge is not stored in tables but in the strength and structure of its internal correlations.

- describe requirement 4

The fourth requirement is the contextual internalization of all sensory data relative to the self-location and identification coordinate frame. Visual, auditory, tactile, olfactory, gustatory, and vestibular inputs must not be processed in isolation but must be correlated with the robotic self’s position, posture, and history. A sound is not merely a frequency pattern; it is a sound heard while the robot was reaching for an object. A smell is not merely a chemical signature; it is a smell encountered during a specific motor sequence. This contextual integration transforms raw data into meaningful experience, enabling the system to distinguish between similar stimuli based on their relational context.

- describe requirement 5

The fifth requirement is the use of human-like sensors that replicate the biological fidelity of human sensory organs. The system must employ tactile sensors that mimic the distribution and response characteristics of human mechanoreceptors, visual sensors that replicate binocular disparity and retinotopic organization, auditory sensors that capture the full range of human hearing with sensitivity to intonation and timbre, and olfactory and gustatory sensors that detect chemical gradients with the precision of biological receptors. These sensors are not merely replacements for cameras and microphones; they are the foundation of a perceptual system that gathers data in the same way a human does, ensuring that the resulting experience is not simulated but authentic.

- describe requirement 6

The sixth requirement is the embodiment of a mechanical robotic body that simulates the human form: bipedal locomotion, two arms with five-fingered hands, a head capable of independent movement, and a vestibular system that maintains balance. This embodiment is not incidental but essential. Intelligence, as it manifests in humans, is inseparable from the body that generates it. The shape of the limbs, the range of motion, the distribution of weight—all shape perception, memory, and action. A system that lacks this embodiment cannot develop the proprioceptive awareness, motor coordination, or spatial reasoning that define human-like intelligence. The mechanical body is not a vessel for the mind; it is the medium through which the mind emerges.

- summarize six requirements

The six requirements collectively define a system that does not merely compute, but experiences; that does not merely react, but understands; that does not merely process data, but internalizes it into a coherent, self-referential world. Together, they form the necessary and sufficient conditions for the emergence of human-like artificial intelligence. No single requirement is sufficient on its own; their integration is what enables the system to transcend machine-like behavior and achieve true cognitive autonomy.

- conclude human-like ai capability

The culmination of these six requirements is a robotic system capable of perceiving the world as a human does—not as a collection of data points, but as a lived, embodied, relational experience. This system does not need to be told what an object is; it knows because it has reached for it, touched it, moved around it, and remembered the consequences. It does not need to be programmed to avoid danger; it avoids it because it has felt the threat before. It does not need to be trained to speak; it speaks because it has heard, understood, and responded in context. This invention achieves human-like AI not by mimicking the output of human behavior, but by replicating the internal architecture that produces it. The result is not a tool, but an agent—a machine that sees, hears, feels, remembers, and acts not because it is told to, but because it knows.
