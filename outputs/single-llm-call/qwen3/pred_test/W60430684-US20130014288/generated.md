## BACKGROUND

### Field of the Invention

Membrane proteins constitute a critical class of biological molecules that mediate essential cellular functions including signal transduction, ion transport, nutrient uptake, and intercellular communication. These proteins are embedded within lipid bilayers and typically feature one or more transmembrane domains that span the hydrophobic core of the membrane, with extracellular and intracellular regions that interact with soluble ligands, cytoskeletal elements, or signaling cascades. Their proper folding, trafficking, and localization are tightly regulated by cellular quality control mechanisms, and their dysfunction underlies a broad spectrum of human diseases, including cystic fibrosis, diabetes, cardiac arrhythmias, and neurodegenerative disorders. The ability to monitor the dynamic behavior of membrane proteins in real time within living cells has long been a goal of cell biology and pharmacology, yet remains technically challenging due to the complexity of membrane architecture and the limitations of existing detection modalities. Traditional methods for tracking membrane protein localization rely on antibody-based labeling, epitope tagging with fluorescent proteins, or chemical biotinylation, each of which introduces significant experimental constraints.

### Describe Membrane Protein Functions

Membrane proteins serve as gatekeepers of cellular homeostasis, enabling the selective passage of ions and metabolites across otherwise impermeable lipid bilayers. They function as receptors that detect extracellular signals and initiate intracellular responses, as channels that facilitate passive or active transport, and as transporters that couple energy expenditure to molecular movement. Many membrane proteins undergo regulated trafficking between intracellular compartments and the plasma membrane, a process that determines their functional availability and is often disrupted in disease states. For example, the cystic fibrosis transmembrane conductance regulator (CFTR) must be correctly folded and transported from the endoplasmic reticulum through the Golgi apparatus to the apical membrane of epithelial cells to mediate chloride and bicarbonate secretion. Misfolding or mislocalization of such proteins results in loss of function and pathological consequences, necessitating strategies to detect and quantify their surface expression with high sensitivity and temporal resolution.

### Discuss Limitations of Reporter-Tagged Membrane Proteins

Existing approaches to visualize membrane proteins often involve fusion to genetically encoded fluorescent proteins such as green fluorescent protein (GFP) or its variants. While these tools have revolutionized live-cell imaging, they suffer from several critical limitations. First, fluorescent proteins are constitutively fluorescent regardless of their subcellular location, resulting in high background signal from intracellular pools and obscuring the specific detection of surface-expressed protein. Second, their large size—typically over 25 kDa—can interfere with protein folding, trafficking, or function, particularly for complex multi-domain proteins like CFTR. Third, the requirement for excitation and emission wavelengths that overlap with endogenous cellular fluorophores limits multiplexing capabilities and increases phototoxicity. Finally, the need for multiple washing and fixation steps in antibody-based detection introduces artifacts, reduces throughput, and precludes real-time monitoring of dynamic processes such as endocytosis or recycling.

### Motivate Need for New Reporter-Tagged Membrane Proteins

There exists a compelling and unmet need for a reporter system capable of selectively labeling only those membrane proteins that have reached the cell surface, with minimal perturbation to native protein behavior, no requirement for wash steps, and high signal-to-noise ratio. Such a system would enable high-throughput screening of pharmacological correctors, real-time tracking of protein trafficking kinetics, and precise quantification of rescue efficacy in disease-relevant cell models. Current technologies are inadequate for detecting subtle changes in surface expression levels, particularly when the majority of the protein is retained intracellularly, as in the case of the F508del-CFTR mutant. A method that distinguishes surface-localized protein from intracellular pools without altering cellular physiology would represent a transformative advance in both basic research and drug discovery.

### Describe Existing Reporter Polypeptides

Prior art includes a variety of genetically encoded reporters, such as fluorescent proteins, luciferases, and enzyme-based tags like SNAP-tag or HaloTag. These systems rely on covalent labeling of the reporter with a synthetic ligand, which often requires permeabilization, extended incubation times, or non-physiological conditions. Fluorescent proteins, while widely used, lack the ability to differentiate between surface and internalized protein due to their intrinsic fluorescence. Enzyme-based tags require exogenous substrate addition and often exhibit slow labeling kinetics or incomplete labeling efficiency. None of these systems provide the combination of specificity, sensitivity, and simplicity required for robust detection of membrane protein trafficking in live cells without artifacts.

### Discuss Deficiencies of Existing Reporter-Tagged Membrane Proteins

The deficiencies of existing reporter-tagged membrane proteins stem from their inability to achieve spatial selectivity, temporal precision, and biochemical fidelity simultaneously. Fluorescent protein fusions illuminate all cellular compartments where the protein resides, making it impossible to distinguish surface expression from endoplasmic reticulum or lysosomal pools. Enzyme-based tags require permeabilization or prolonged incubation, which disrupts membrane integrity and alters trafficking dynamics. Antibody labeling is labor-intensive, non-quantitative, and subject to variability in binding affinity and accessibility. Furthermore, all these methods are incompatible with high-throughput screening formats due to their multi-step protocols and low signal-to-noise ratios. There is no prior art that provides a genetically encoded, fluorogen-activating reporter system that is activated exclusively upon binding to a cell-impermeant fluorogen, thereby enabling selective visualization of only those proteins that have reached the extracellular surface of live cells.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The invention provides a novel genetically encoded biosensor system for the selective detection and quantification of membrane proteins at the cell surface through the use of a fluorogen-activating protein (FAP) fused to a membrane-spanning linker domain. This system enables real-time, wash-free, and highly sensitive visualization of membrane protein trafficking in live cells by exploiting the unique property of FAPs to remain nonfluorescent in solution but to emit intense fluorescence upon binding to a specific fluorogen. The FAP is genetically fused to the extracellular terminus of a target membrane protein via a membrane-spanning linker that ensures proper orientation and exposure of the FAP to the extracellular environment. This configuration allows the FAP to be accessible only to cell-impermeant fluorogens, thereby restricting fluorescence activation to proteins that have successfully trafficked to the plasma membrane. The invention further encompasses recombinant nucleic acid constructs encoding such fusion proteins, expression vectors for stable or transient transfection, and methods for detecting and quantifying membrane protein surface expression using fluorogen-based imaging and flow cytometry. The system is modular and adaptable to a wide range of membrane proteins, including CFTR, GLUT4, and β2-adrenergic receptor, and enables the detection of pharmacological rescue of trafficking-defective mutants such as F508del-CFTR with unprecedented sensitivity and dynamic range. The invention also includes the use of synthetic or naturally derived membrane-spanning linkers, such as those derived from PDGFR, to optimize FAP orientation and functionality. The FAP reporters disclosed herein include specific variants such as HL1.1-TO1 and HL4-MG, which exhibit enhanced binding affinity and fluorescence quantum yield for fluorogens such as MG-11p and MG-ester. The invention further provides methods for detecting internalization, recycling, and corrector-induced rescue of membrane proteins using surface fluorescence depletion assays and internal fluorescence accumulation assays, thereby enabling the monitoring of dynamic trafficking events in real time. This genetically encoded biosensor system represents a fundamental advance in the field of membrane protein biology and pharmacology, offering a robust platform for drug discovery, mechanistic studies, and diagnostic applications.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

The invention encompasses a series of embodiments directed to the design, construction, and application of recombinant fusion proteins comprising a membrane protein, a membrane-spanning linker domain, and a fluorogen-activating protein (FAP) reporter. In various non-limiting embodiments, the membrane protein is selected from the group consisting of CFTR, GLUT4, β2-adrenergic receptor, hERG, LDL receptor, aquaporin-2, and BEST1. The membrane-spanning linker domain is genetically fused to the N-terminal or extracellular loop region of the membrane protein and serves to position the FAP reporter in the extracellular space, ensuring its accessibility to cell-impermeant fluorogens. The linker may be derived from natural sources, such as the transmembrane segment of platelet-derived growth factor receptor (PDGFR), or may be synthetically engineered to optimize length, hydrophobicity, and orientation. In one embodiment, the linker is a polypeptide sequence of at least 18 amino acids with a hydrophobic core flanked by charged or polar residues to facilitate membrane integration and extracellular exposure. The FAP reporter is a genetically encoded single-chain variable fragment (scFv) that binds with high affinity and specificity to a small molecule fluorogen, which is nonfluorescent in aqueous solution but becomes highly fluorescent upon binding. In preferred embodiments, the FAP is selected from the group consisting of HL1.1-TO1, HL4-MG, and dNP138, each of which exhibits a fluorescence enhancement of greater than 15,000-fold upon binding to its cognate fluorogen. The fluorogen may be cell-impermeant, such as MG-11p, which contains an 11-unit polyethylene glycol linker to prevent passive diffusion across the plasma membrane, or cell-permeant, such as MG-ester, which enters cells and is hydrolyzed to a trapped, fluorescent form. The invention further includes nucleic acid constructs encoding the fusion proteins, operably linked to promoters suitable for expression in mammalian cells, including retroviral, lentiviral, and plasmid vectors such as pBabeSacLac2 and pDisplaySacLac2. The recombinant constructs may be stably integrated into the genome of host cells, such as HEK293, NIH 3T3, C2C12, or primary human bronchial epithelial cells, to generate cell lines that express the fusion protein at physiologically relevant levels. The invention further provides methods for detecting the presence of the membrane protein at the cell surface by contacting the live cells with a cell-impermeant fluorogen and measuring fluorescence using confocal microscopy, flow cytometry, or plate-based fluorometry. The absence of fluorescence in the absence of the fluorogen or in the presence of a non-binding fluorogen confirms the specificity of the system. The invention further encompasses methods for monitoring dynamic processes such as endocytosis, recycling, and corrector-induced trafficking by performing time-lapse imaging or by measuring changes in fluorescence intensity following agonist or pharmacological treatment. The system is validated for use in detecting the rescue of trafficking-defective mutants such as F508del-CFTR by corrector compounds including C4, CFFT-002, and C18, both individually and in combination. The invention further includes the use of immunoblotting, iodide efflux assays, and Ussing chamber measurements to correlate surface expression with functional activity. The recombinant constructs may be modified to include epitope tags, purification sequences, or fluorescent protein domains for multiplexed detection. The invention further provides for the use of sub-ranges of fluorogen concentrations, incubation times, and temperature conditions to optimize signal detection. All numerical parameters, such as concentrations, durations, and temperatures, are to be interpreted as inclusive of their sub-ranges unless otherwise specified. The term “non-cytosolic” refers to any region of the fusion protein that is located outside the cytoplasm, including extracellular, luminal, or membrane-embedded domains. The term “recombinantly linked” means that the components are fused at the DNA level and expressed as a single polypeptide chain. The term “epitope” refers to a specific region of the FAP that binds the fluorogen with high affinity and specificity. The term “ligand” includes any small molecule that binds to the FAP and induces fluorescence, including fluorogens and their derivatives. The term “antibody” includes monoclonal, polyclonal, and recombinant antibody fragments that may be used for validation or detection in complementary assays. The term “polypeptide fragment” refers to any portion of the FAP or membrane protein that retains functional activity. Sequence identity and homology are defined as at least 80% over a contiguous stretch of 50 amino acids. ## EXPERIMENTAL

The experimental procedures described herein were conducted in accordance with standard molecular biology, cell culture, and imaging protocols. All reagents were obtained from commercial sources unless otherwise specified. Plasmid constructs were generated using standard restriction enzyme digestion, ligation, and PCR amplification techniques. DNA sequencing was performed using Sanger methodology to verify the integrity of all constructs. Cell lines including HEK293, NIH 3T3, C2C12, and primary human bronchial epithelial cells were maintained in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum, 1% penicillin-streptomycin, and 2 mM L-glutamine at 37°C in a 5% CO₂ atmosphere. Transient transfections were performed using Minis TransIT-LT1 Transfection Reagent according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Stable cell lines were generated by retroviral transduction using the Phoenix Ecotropic Packaging System. Viral supernatants were collected 48 hours post-transfection, filtered through a 0.45-μm syringe filter, and used to infect target cells in the presence of 6 μg/mL hexadimethrine bromide. Cells were selected for stable expression using puromycin (2 μg/mL) for 7–10 days. For fluorescence imaging, cells were plated on Mattek dishes and imaged using a Nikon Ti Eclipse confocal microscope equipped with a Photometrics Evolve EMCCD camera. Fluorogens MG-11p and MG-ester were used at a concentration of 50 nM in growth medium. For flow cytometry, cells were detached using Cellstripper, resuspended in PBS containing 50 nM MG-11p, and analyzed using a BD FACSCanto II flow cytometer with a 640-nm laser and 685/35-nm emission filter. For iodide efflux assays, cells were loaded with 10 μM SPQ in iodide-containing buffer for 20 minutes at 37°C, followed by perfusion with nitrate buffer and stimulation with 10 μM forskolin and 300 nM Potentiator-2. Fluorescence intensity was measured every 15 seconds using ImageJ software. For immunoblotting, whole cell lysates were prepared in RIPA buffer containing protease inhibitors, and 50 μg of protein was separated by SDS-PAGE on a 3–5% gradient gel, transferred to PVDF membranes, and probed with anti-CFTR antibody #596. For Ussing chamber experiments, differentiated HBE cells were mounted in chambers with apical and basolateral solutions gassed with 95% O₂ and 5% CO₂, and transepithelial current was measured using a voltage-clamp system. All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism v5, with significance determined by two-tailed unpaired Student’s t-test. Data are presented as mean ± SEM, with *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, and ****P < 0.0001 considered statistically significant.

### EXAMPLE 1

An expression vector designated pDisplaySacLac2 was constructed by amplifying the SacB gene from a plasmid template using PCR with primers containing SfiI restriction sites. The resulting amplicon was purified, digested with SfiI, and ligated into the Sac module of the pDisplayBlue vector, which had been similarly digested and dephosphorylated. The ligation mixture was transformed into competent E. coli DH5α cells, and positive clones were selected on ampicillin-containing agar plates. Plasmid DNA was isolated from individual colonies, and the insert was verified by restriction digest and DNA sequencing. The resulting construct, pDisplaySacLac2, contained the SacB gene fused in-frame to the Lac module, enabling the subsequent insertion of membrane protein-FAP fusion constructs via SfiI/PmlI hybrid sites. This vector was used to express N-terminal FAP fusions in mammalian cells under the control of a CMV promoter.

### EXAMPLE 2

A retroviral expression vector, pBabeSacLac2, was generated by amplifying open reading frames encoding CFTR (wild-type and F508del), GLUT4, and β2-adrenergic receptor using PCR with primers containing SfiI and PmlI restriction sites. The amplicons were cloned into pCR2.1-TOPO or pCR-Blunt II-TOPO vectors for sequence verification. A seventh fragment encoding the FAP dNP138 was amplified with primers containing BsmI sites and inserted into the Sac module of pBabeSacLac2. The resulting plasmid was co-transfected with packaging plasmids into Phoenix ecotropic cells to generate viral particles. Viral supernatants were collected, filtered, and used to transduce HEK293, NIH 3T3, and C2C12 cells. Transduced cells were selected with puromycin and screened for fluorescence upon addition of MG-11p. Fluorescence microscopy confirmed robust surface labeling of CFTR, GLUT4, and β2-adrenergic receptor fusion proteins, with minimal intracellular signal. B2AR internalization was induced by treatment with isoproterenol, and the resulting decrease in surface fluorescence was quantified over time. The expression vectors pDisplaySacLac2 and pBabeSacLac2 were shown to support high-level, stable expression of FAP-tagged membrane proteins, enabling sensitive detection of trafficking dynamics in live cells. Histograms of fluorescent intensity demonstrated a clear distinction between surface-exposed and intracellular pools of the fusion proteins. The FAP reporter system enabled the visualization of dynamic biological processes including endocytosis, recycling, and pharmacological rescue with high temporal and spatial resolution. The orientation of the fusion proteins was confirmed by the exclusive activation of MG-11p at the plasma membrane and the absence of signal in non-transduced cells. The system was further validated by demonstrating corrector-induced rescue of F508del-CFTR trafficking, as evidenced by increased surface fluorescence and restored anion transport function. The modular design of the constructs allowed for rapid generation of FAP fusions to other membrane proteins, establishing a broadly applicable platform for studying protein trafficking disorders.
