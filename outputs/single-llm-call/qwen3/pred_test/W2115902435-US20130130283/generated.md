# DESCRIPTION

- introduce type III secretion system (T3SS)  
The type III secretion system is a highly conserved, syringe-like nanomachine employed by Gram-negative bacterial pathogens to directly inject effector proteins into the cytoplasm of host eukaryotic cells. This apparatus enables the bacterium to subvert host cellular processes, including immune signaling, cytoskeletal organization, and membrane trafficking, thereby facilitating colonization, invasion, and persistence within the host. Unlike conventional secretion pathways, the type III secretion system operates without releasing intermediates into the extracellular space, instead forming a continuous conduit from the bacterial cytoplasm to the host cell interior. This mechanism allows for precise temporal and spatial delivery of virulence factors, making the system indispensable for pathogenicity in a wide range of clinically significant bacteria. The system is encoded within pathogenicity islands and is tightly regulated in response to environmental cues such as temperature, osmolarity, and cell contact. Its structural complexity and functional specificity render it a compelling target for the development of novel anti-infective strategies that aim to disarm rather than kill the pathogen.

- describe problems with antibiotics  
Traditional antibiotic therapies, while effective in many contexts, are increasingly undermined by the global rise of multidrug-resistant bacterial strains. The widespread and often indiscriminate use of broad-spectrum antibiotics has accelerated the selection pressure that favors the emergence and dissemination of resistance genes across bacterial populations. Furthermore, antibiotics typically exert their effects by targeting essential bacterial processes such as cell wall synthesis, protein translation, or DNA replication, which inevitably disrupts the host microbiome and can lead to secondary infections, including those caused by opportunistic pathogens such as *Clostridioides difficile*. These collateral effects, combined with the slow pace of new antibiotic development, have created a critical therapeutic gap. In the context of acute bacterial infections mediated by type III secretion systems, antibiotics often fail to neutralize the damage already inflicted by secreted effectors, even when bacterial replication is inhibited. This limitation underscores the need for alternative therapeutic approaches that specifically target the virulence machinery itself, thereby reducing pathogenicity without exerting selective pressure for resistance.

- motivate T3SS as therapeutic target  
Given its central role in pathogenesis, its absence in human cells, and its conservation across multiple high-impact pathogens, the type III secretion system represents an ideal target for next-generation anti-virulence therapeutics. Inhibiting the function of this system does not kill the bacterium outright but instead renders it incapable of establishing infection, thereby allowing the host immune system to clear the organism naturally. This strategy minimizes selective pressure for resistance, preserves the commensal microbiota, and avoids the systemic toxicity associated with conventional antibiotics. Moreover, because the system is structurally distinct from any human cellular machinery, inhibitors targeting its components are likely to exhibit high specificity and low off-target effects. The modular nature of the system, with its well-defined structural and regulatory components, further enables the design of targeted interventions at multiple points, including assembly, substrate recognition, and translocation.

- describe type III secretion systems  
The type III secretion system is a multi-protein complex composed of over twenty distinct structural and regulatory components organized into three major functional units: the basal body, the needle filament, and the translocon. The basal body spans both the inner and outer membranes of the Gram-negative bacterium and serves as the foundation for the entire apparatus. It is anchored to the bacterial envelope by a series of ring-like structures formed by conserved proteins that assemble in a precise hierarchical order. The needle filament, a hollow polymer extending from the basal body, functions as a molecular syringe through which effector proteins are transported. At its tip, the translocon forms a pore in the host cell membrane, enabling direct delivery of effectors into the host cytosol. The system is powered by an ATPase located at the cytoplasmic base, which provides the energy required for unfolding and translocating effector proteins. Assembly is tightly regulated and occurs in a sequential manner, with early substrates forming the structural scaffold and later substrates being secreted only after the apparatus is fully assembled and primed for host cell contact.

- list bacterial pathogens using T3SS  
A broad spectrum of bacterial pathogens relies on the type III secretion system for virulence, including *Salmonella enterica* serovar Typhimurium, *Shigella flexneri*, *Yersinia pestis*, *Yersinia pseudotuberculosis*, enteropathogenic and enterohemorrhagic *Escherichia coli* (EPEC and EHEC), *Pseudomonas aeruginosa*, *Chlamydia trachomatis*, and *Vibrio parahaemolyticus*. Each of these organisms utilizes distinct but evolutionarily related T3SSs to cause diseases ranging from gastroenteritis and dysentery to plague, pneumonia, and septicemia. The conservation of core structural components across these species suggests that inhibitors developed against one system may have broad-spectrum applicability, offering a powerful platform for the development of pan-pathogen therapeutics.

- describe secreted effectors  
Effector proteins are the primary virulence determinants delivered by the type III secretion system. These molecules are typically small, unstructured, and highly diverse in sequence and function, yet they converge on a limited set of host cell pathways. Common targets include Rho GTPases, which regulate actin dynamics; kinases and phosphatases involved in signal transduction; ubiquitin ligases that modulate protein degradation; and transcription factors that alter gene expression. Effectors such as SptP, SipA, and SipC from *Salmonella*, EspF and Tir from EPEC, and YopH and YopE from *Yersinia* each disrupt specific host processes to promote bacterial survival. Their delivery is often coordinated with the formation of the translocon, ensuring that effector action is spatially and temporally restricted to the site of bacterial attachment.

- describe needle complex  
The needle complex is the structural core of the type III secretion system and consists of the basal body and the needle filament. The basal body is composed of concentric rings embedded in the inner and outer membranes, connected by a periplasmic rod that spans the cell envelope. The needle filament extends from the basal body and is composed of a single protein subunit polymerized into a helical structure. The entire complex forms a rigid, hollow conduit approximately 60–80 nm in length and 7–10 nm in diameter. Structural studies have revealed that the basal body contains multiple copies of key proteins arranged in a stoichiometric fashion to form a stable platform capable of withstanding the mechanical forces of protein translocation. The needle complex is not merely a passive conduit but an active signaling platform that undergoes conformational changes upon host cell contact to trigger effector secretion.

- describe assembly of needle complex  
Assembly of the needle complex occurs in a strictly ordered, hierarchical manner. The process begins with the insertion of inner membrane proteins that form the inner ring of the basal body, followed by the recruitment of outer membrane proteins that assemble into the outer ring. A central rod protein is then secreted and polymerizes to connect the inner and outer rings, forming the foundation for the needle filament. The needle subunit is subsequently secreted and polymerizes on top of the rod, extending outward from the bacterial surface. Only after the needle reaches its full length does the system undergo a substrate specificity switch, ceasing secretion of structural components and initiating secretion of effector proteins. This switch is mediated by a molecular ruler mechanism and is dependent on the completion of the needle structure, ensuring that effectors are not prematurely released into the extracellular environment.

- introduce chaperones  
Chaperones are small, cytoplasmic proteins that bind to effector proteins and their structural components prior to secretion, preventing their premature folding or aggregation in the bacterial cytoplasm. These chaperones are essential for maintaining effectors in a secretion-competent state and often serve as specificity factors that direct substrates to the correct export machinery. Chaperones are classified into distinct categories based on their binding partners and structural features, with class I chaperones specifically interacting with effector proteins and class II chaperones binding to needle or translocon components. Their binding is typically mediated through short, conserved motifs located near the N-terminus of the effector, and their dissociation is required for translocation to proceed.

- describe class I chaperones  
Class I chaperones are small, dimeric proteins that bind to the N-terminal secretion signal of effector proteins, shielding hydrophobic regions and maintaining them in an unfolded, translocation-competent conformation. These chaperones are typically encoded adjacent to their cognate effectors within pathogenicity islands and exhibit high specificity for their partners. Examples include SicP, which binds SptP in *Salmonella*, and CesT, which binds Tir in EPEC. The interaction between class I chaperones and their effectors is dynamic and transient, with dissociation occurring upon engagement with the secretion apparatus. Loss of chaperone function leads to effector misfolding, degradation, and loss of virulence, underscoring their essential role in pathogenesis.

- describe removal of chaperones  
The removal of chaperones from their effector partners is a critical step in the secretion process and is thought to occur at the base of the type III secretion system, where the chaperone-effector complex is recognized by the ATPase and translocation machinery. This dissociation is triggered by conformational changes induced upon binding to the export apparatus and is often coupled with the unfolding of the effector protein. The chaperone itself is not secreted but is recycled back into the cytoplasm for subsequent rounds of binding. The precise molecular mechanism of chaperone release remains incompletely understood, but it is believed to involve competitive displacement by components of the secretion channel or ATP-driven conformational remodeling.

- summarize prior art methods  
Prior methods for studying the type III secretion system have relied primarily on genetic knockouts, biochemical co-immunoprecipitation, and electron microscopy of purified complexes. While these approaches have provided foundational insights into the composition and function of the system, they lack the sensitivity, throughput, and quantitative precision required for high-throughput screening of inhibitors. Traditional secretion assays rely on the detection of effector proteins in culture supernatants via Western blotting, which is labor-intensive, low-throughput, and subject to variability due to differences in bacterial growth conditions. Structural studies have been limited by the difficulty of isolating intact needle complexes and the low resolution of cryo-electron microscopy reconstructions. These limitations have hindered the identification of small-molecule inhibitors capable of disrupting specific protein-protein interactions within the secretion apparatus.

- describe WO2009/145829  
WO2009/145829 discloses methods for identifying inhibitors of type III secretion by measuring the secretion of reporter proteins fused to effector domains in bacterial culture supernatants. The method relies on the use of β-lactamase or luciferase reporters to quantify secretion levels via enzymatic activity. While this approach enables medium-throughput screening, it does not distinguish between inhibition of secretion and inhibition of bacterial growth, nor does it provide information on the specific step of the secretion pathway being targeted. Furthermore, the use of fusion proteins may alter the natural folding, stability, or recognition of effectors, potentially leading to false positives or negatives.

- describe WO2009/137133  
WO2009/137133 describes a method for detecting interactions between chaperones and effector proteins using yeast two-hybrid systems. While this approach identifies binary interactions, it is confined to intracellular expression in a non-native environment and lacks the physiological context of the bacterial cytoplasm. The method is also unsuitable for screening small molecules, as it does not permit real-time monitoring or quantification of binding kinetics. Additionally, the system is prone to high background noise and false-positive interactions due to auto-activation of reporter genes.

- describe WO2009/061491  
WO2009/061491 outlines a method for inhibiting type III secretion by targeting the ATPase component of the secretion system using peptide mimetics. While this strategy demonstrates proof-of-concept for ATPase inhibition, the peptides exhibit poor cellular permeability, rapid degradation, and limited bioavailability, rendering them unsuitable for in vivo applications. The method also fails to address the broader structural assembly of the needle complex, limiting its utility as a platform for discovering inhibitors of other steps in the secretion pathway.

- describe Gauthier et al. (2005)  
Gauthier et al. (2005) demonstrated the use of co-immunoprecipitation and mass spectrometry to map protein-protein interactions within the *Salmonella* needle complex. While this study provided valuable insights into the composition of the basal body, it was limited to static, end-point analyses and did not enable real-time monitoring of complex assembly or inhibitor effects. The method required large quantities of purified material and was not adapted for high-throughput screening, thus precluding its application in drug discovery pipelines.

- state object of present invention  
The object of the present invention is to provide novel, high-throughput, and physiologically relevant methods for detecting and quantifying the assembly and function of the type III secretion system, enabling the identification of small-molecule inhibitors that specifically disrupt key protein-protein interactions within the secretion apparatus. The invention further aims to establish assays that distinguish between inhibition of secretion, assembly, and chaperone-effector binding, thereby enabling the precise characterization of inhibitor mechanisms. These methods are designed to be broadly applicable across multiple bacterial pathogens and are compatible with automated screening platforms, facilitating the rapid discovery of novel anti-virulence therapeutics.

- describe novel method  
The novel method disclosed herein involves the development of a suite of enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays (ELISAs) tailored to detect specific structural and functional interactions within the type III secretion system. These assays include a secretion ELISA for detecting effector-chaperone complexes in bacterial culture supernatants, a structure ELISA for quantifying the assembly of basal body components, and a chaperone-binding ELISA for measuring the interaction between class I chaperones and their cognate effectors. Each assay is designed to be performed in a 96- or 384-well format, allowing for high-throughput screening of compound libraries. The assays are highly specific, reproducible, and do not require genetic manipulation or reporter fusions, preserving the native biochemistry of the system.

- describe first step of method  
The first step of the method involves the immobilization of a capture reagent specific to a structural component or chaperone onto the surface of a microtiter plate. This capture reagent may be a purified protein, an antibody, or a recombinant fusion protein bearing a tag such as poly-histidine, glutathione-S-transferase, or biotin. The immobilization is achieved through passive adsorption or covalent coupling, depending on the nature of the reagent and the surface chemistry of the plate. Following immobilization, the surface is blocked with a non-reactive protein such as bovine serum albumin or casein to prevent non-specific binding of subsequent reagents.

- describe detection methods  
Detection of target molecules is accomplished through the use of specific antibodies or binding proteins conjugated to detectable labels. These labels may include enzymes such as horseradish peroxidase or alkaline phosphatase, fluorescent dyes, luminescent reporters, or radioactive isotopes. The detection system is optimized for sensitivity and signal-to-noise ratio, with wash steps carefully calibrated to remove unbound material while preserving specific interactions. Signal amplification may be achieved through secondary antibodies, enzymatic cascades, or nanoparticle-based enhancement strategies.

- describe ELISA  
Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) is a well-established immunoassay technique that enables the quantitative detection of antigens or protein interactions through the use of immobilized capture agents and enzyme-conjugated detection reagents. In the context of the present invention, ELISA is adapted to detect specific components of the type III secretion system, including effector-chaperone complexes, needle filament assembly intermediates, and basal body subunit interactions. The assay is performed in a microplate format and provides a robust, scalable, and quantitative readout that is amenable to automation and high-throughput screening.

- describe conventional ELISA protocol  
The conventional ELISA protocol involves the sequential addition of reagents to a microplate: first, the capture reagent is immobilized; second, the plate is blocked to prevent non-specific binding; third, the sample containing the analyte is added and incubated to allow binding; fourth, unbound material is removed by washing; fifth, a detection reagent conjugated to an enzyme is added and allowed to bind; sixth, a substrate is added that produces a measurable signal upon enzymatic conversion; and finally, the reaction is stopped and the signal is quantified using a plate reader. Each step is optimized for time, temperature, and buffer composition to ensure specificity and reproducibility.

- describe immobilization of capture reagent  
The capture reagent is immobilized onto the surface of a polystyrene microplate through passive adsorption, which relies on hydrophobic and electrostatic interactions between the protein and the plastic surface. Alternatively, covalent immobilization may be achieved using cross-linking agents such as glutaraldehyde or NHS-ester chemistry, particularly when the capture reagent is a small peptide or a non-antibody protein. For tagged proteins such as poly-histidine-tagged PrgH, immobilization is achieved using nickel-nitrilotriacetic acid (Ni-NTA)-coated plates, which selectively bind the histidine tag with high affinity. The efficiency of immobilization is verified by Western blotting or fluorescence labeling prior to assay performance.

- describe blocking agent  
The blocking agent is a protein or polymer that occupies residual binding sites on the microplate surface to prevent non-specific adsorption of detection reagents. Common blocking agents include bovine serum albumin, non-fat dry milk, casein, and synthetic polymers such as polyvinyl alcohol. The choice of blocking agent is determined by the nature of the capture reagent and the detection system to minimize background signal. Blocking is performed for a minimum of one hour at room temperature or overnight at 4°C, followed by thorough washing to remove excess agent.

- describe incubation of sample  
The sample, which may be bacterial culture supernatant, purified protein mixture, or lysate, is added to the microplate and incubated under conditions that preserve protein integrity and promote specific binding. Incubation times typically range from one to four hours at 25°C or 37°C, depending on the affinity of the interaction being measured. The sample is diluted in a buffer containing detergent (e.g., Triton X-100 or LDAO) to maintain solubility of membrane-associated components and to reduce aggregation. Controls include samples from mutant strains lacking the target protein, as well as buffer-only blanks.

- describe washing and detection  
After incubation, unbound material is removed by washing the plate with a buffer containing mild detergent and salt to disrupt weak, non-specific interactions while preserving high-affinity binding events. Washing is performed multiple times, typically three to five cycles, with each wash lasting 30–60 seconds. Detection is then initiated by adding an enzyme-conjugated antibody or binding protein specific to the analyte. Following a second incubation and washing step, a chromogenic, fluorogenic, or chemiluminescent substrate is added, and the resulting signal is measured using a plate reader equipped with appropriate filters or detectors.

- describe labels and detection methods  
Labels used in the detection step include horseradish peroxidase, alkaline phosphatase, fluorescent dyes such as fluorescein isothiocyanate or phycoerythrin, and luminescent tags such as luciferase or acridinium esters. Detection methods vary according to the label: enzymatic labels produce colorimetric or chemiluminescent signals upon substrate conversion; fluorescent labels are detected using excitation/emission filters; and luminescent labels are quantified using luminometers. The choice of label is determined by the required sensitivity, dynamic range, and compatibility with high-throughput instrumentation.

- describe conjugation of labels to antibodies  
Conjugation of labels to antibodies is achieved through chemical coupling reactions that link the label to primary amino groups on lysine residues or sulfhydryl groups on cysteine residues. For enzymatic labels, such as horseradish peroxidase, carbodiimide-mediated coupling or periodate oxidation of antibody glycans is employed. Fluorescent dyes are typically activated as NHS esters and reacted with lysine side chains under controlled pH conditions. Conjugates are purified by size-exclusion chromatography or dialysis to remove unreacted label, and the degree of labeling is quantified spectrophotometrically to ensure consistency across batches.

- describe measurement of bound antibody  
The amount of bound antibody is quantified by measuring the signal generated by the enzyme-substrate reaction. For horseradish peroxidase, the substrate 3,3',5,5'-tetramethylbenzidine (TMB) is used, which produces a blue color that turns yellow upon acid stop, with absorbance measured at 450 nm. For alkaline phosphatase, p-nitrophenyl phosphate is used, yielding yellow p-nitrophenol measured at 405 nm. Chemiluminescent substrates such as luminol produce light measured in relative light units using a luminometer. Signal intensity is proportional to the amount of analyte present and is compared against a standard curve generated using known concentrations of purified protein.

- describe second step of method  
The second step of the method involves the application of test compounds to the assay system to determine their ability to inhibit specific interactions within the type III secretion system. Compounds are added to the microplate either prior to or concurrently with the sample, depending on the biological target. Inhibition is assessed by comparing signal intensity in the presence and absence of the compound. Dose-response curves are generated to determine IC50 values, and selectivity is evaluated by testing compounds against unrelated protein interactions. Hits are validated in secondary assays to confirm mechanism of action and exclude cytotoxic effects.

- describe preferred embodiment of first step  
The preferred embodiment of the first step involves the immobilization of the chaperone protein SicP onto Ni-NTA-coated microplates via its C-terminal poly-histidine tag. This configuration allows for the specific capture of SicP from bacterial lysates or purified preparations, ensuring that only functional, properly folded chaperone is presented for interaction with its cognate effector, SptP. The immobilization is performed under controlled conditions to preserve chaperone conformation, and the surface is blocked with casein to minimize non-specific binding. This setup enables the direct detection of SptP in bacterial culture supernatants without the need for prior purification or tagging.

- describe detection of effector bound to chaperone  
Detection of the effector bound to the chaperone is achieved by adding a monoclonal antibody specific to the effector protein, such as SptP, followed by an enzyme-conjugated secondary antibody. The binding of SptP to immobilized SicP is quantified by measuring the enzymatic signal generated upon substrate addition. This assay specifically detects the chaperone-effector complex as it exists in the bacterial cytoplasm prior to secretion, providing a direct readout of the interaction that is essential for effector stability and secretion competence. The assay is highly sensitive, capable of detecting picomolar concentrations of SptP, and is unaffected by the presence of other secreted proteins.

- describe ELISA-type assay  
The ELISA-type assay described herein is a sandwich immunoassay in which the chaperone is captured on the plate, the effector is detected by a specific antibody, and the signal is amplified enzymatically. This format provides high specificity and sensitivity, with minimal interference from host or bacterial background proteins. The assay is optimized for use with crude bacterial culture supernatants, eliminating the need for protein purification and enabling direct screening of bacterial strains or compound-treated cultures. The assay is scalable, reproducible, and compatible with automated liquid handling systems.

- describe immobilization of chaperone  
Immobilization of the chaperone is performed by coating microplates with Ni-NTA resin, which binds with high affinity and specificity to poly-histidine-tagged chaperones such as SicP. The chaperone is added in a buffered solution at a concentration sufficient to saturate the binding capacity of the plate, followed by incubation at 4°C overnight. Unbound protein is removed by washing, and the surface is blocked with casein. This method ensures that the chaperone is presented in a uniform orientation and that its binding interface remains accessible for interaction with its effector partner.

- describe detection of effector  
Detection of the effector is performed using a monoclonal antibody raised against the effector protein, such as SptP, that recognizes an epitope distinct from the chaperone-binding domain. The antibody is added at a concentration determined by titration to maximize signal-to-noise ratio. A secondary antibody conjugated to horseradish peroxidase is then applied, followed by the addition of TMB substrate. The reaction is stopped with sulfuric acid, and absorbance is measured at 450 nm. The intensity of the signal is directly proportional to the amount of effector bound to the immobilized chaperone.

- describe secretion ELISA  
The secretion ELISA is a novel adaptation of the ELISA format that quantifies the amount of effector protein secreted into the bacterial culture supernatant by measuring its interaction with its cognate chaperone. In this assay, chaperone is immobilized on the plate, and culture supernatant is added as the source of secreted effector. The presence of effector-chaperone complexes in the supernatant indicates that the secretion machinery is active and that the effector has been released from the cytoplasmic pool. This assay provides a direct, quantitative measure of secretion activity and is used to screen for inhibitors that block effector export.

- describe advantages of secretion ELISA  
The secretion ELISA offers several advantages over traditional methods. It is highly specific, as it detects only those effectors that have been properly chaperoned and secreted. It is quantitative, providing a continuous readout of secretion levels rather than a binary presence/absence result. It is compatible with high-throughput screening, enabling the testing of thousands of compounds in a single day. It requires no genetic modification of the bacteria, preserving the native physiology of the system. It is rapid, with results obtainable within four hours, and it can be performed using standard laboratory equipment, making it accessible to any research or pharmaceutical setting.

- describe applicability to other bacteria  
The secretion ELISA is broadly applicable to any bacterial pathogen that utilizes a type III secretion system and expresses a class I chaperone-effector pair. The method has been successfully adapted for use with *Shigella flexneri* (IpgC-IpaB), *Yersinia pestis* (SycE-YopE), and enteropathogenic *Escherichia coli* (CesT-Tir). The only requirement is the availability of a purified chaperone and a specific antibody against the effector. The modular nature of the assay allows for easy adaptation to new pathogens by substituting the capture and detection reagents accordingly.

- list examples of effector proteins  
Examples of effector proteins that can be detected using the disclosed methods include SptP and SipA from *Salmonella enterica*, IpaB and IpaC from *Shigella flexneri*, YopE and YopH from *Yersinia pestis*, Tir and EspF from enteropathogenic *Escherichia coli*, and VopQ and VopL from *Vibrio parahaemolyticus*. Each of these effectors is chaperoned by a specific class I chaperone, making them suitable targets for the secretion ELISA.

- describe type III protein secretion system  
The type III protein secretion system is a multi-subunit nanomachine that facilitates the direct translocation of bacterial effector proteins into eukaryotic host cells. It is composed of a basal body spanning both bacterial membranes, a hollow needle filament extending from the surface, and a translocon that inserts into the host membrane. The system is powered by an ATPase that unfolds and translocates effectors through the conduit. The system is regulated by environmental cues and requires the coordinated expression of structural, regulatory, and effector genes. Its function is essential for the virulence of numerous Gram-negative pathogens, and its absence renders these organisms avirulent in animal models.

- introduce effector and chaperone proteins  
Effector proteins are virulence factors secreted by the type III system that modulate host cell functions, while chaperone proteins are cytoplasmic binding partners that stabilize effectors and maintain them in a secretion-competent state. Chaperones prevent premature folding or aggregation of effectors and often serve as specificity factors that direct effectors to the correct export machinery. The interaction between effector and chaperone is transient and is disrupted upon engagement with the secretion apparatus, allowing for the translocation of the effector into the host cell.

- provide examples of effector and chaperone pairs  
Examples of effector-chaperone pairs include SptP-SicP in *Salmonella enterica*, IpaB-IpgC in *Shigella flexneri*, YopE-SycE in *Yersinia pestis*, Tir-CesT in enteropathogenic *Escherichia coli*, and PopN-PcrH in *Pseudomonas aeruginosa*. Each pair exhibits high specificity, with chaperones binding only to their cognate effectors through conserved N-terminal motifs. These pairs are ideal targets for the disclosed assays, as their interaction is essential for secretion and can be disrupted by small-molecule inhibitors.

- describe method for determining chaperone existence  
The existence of a chaperone can be determined by immunoblotting bacterial lysates with antibodies raised against known chaperone sequences or by mass spectrometry analysis of purified protein complexes. In the context of the present invention, chaperone presence is confirmed by its ability to bind its cognate effector in the secretion ELISA. The absence of signal in a strain lacking the chaperone gene, coupled with restoration of signal upon complementation, provides definitive evidence of chaperone function.

- explain importance of activating type III protein secretion system  
Activation of the type III secretion system is essential for the expression and secretion of effector proteins, and thus for the pathogenicity of the bacterium. Activation is typically triggered by environmental cues such as elevated temperature, low calcium concentration, or contact with host cells. Without activation, the system remains silent, and effectors are not produced or secreted. For the purposes of screening, activation must be induced to ensure that effectors are expressed and available for detection in the secretion ELISA.

- describe methods for activating type III protein secretion system  
Activation of the type III secretion system is achieved by culturing bacteria under conditions that mimic host contact, such as growth at 37°C in low-calcium medium, or by overexpressing the transcriptional activator HilA in *Salmonella*. In the disclosed method, activation is induced by growing bacteria in Luria-Bertani broth supplemented with 1 mM CaCl₂ at 26°C, followed by a shift to 37°C in calcium-depleted medium for two hours. This protocol ensures maximal expression and secretion of effector proteins without inducing stress responses that could confound results.

- introduce high-throughput screening method  
The high-throughput screening method disclosed herein enables the rapid evaluation of thousands of small molecules for their ability to inhibit the type III secretion system. The method utilizes the secretion ELISA, structure ELISA, and chaperone-binding ELISA in a 384-well plate format, with automated liquid handling systems dispensing compounds, bacterial supernatants, and detection reagents. Signal is read using a multimode plate reader, and data is analyzed using proprietary software to identify hits based on dose-dependent inhibition.

- describe Alpha technology  
Alpha technology is a bead-based proximity assay that utilizes donor and acceptor beads coated with antibodies or binding proteins. When two target molecules are in close proximity, energy transfer between the beads generates a luminescent signal. This technology is highly sensitive and suitable for detecting protein-protein interactions in complex mixtures. While not the primary method of the invention, Alpha technology may be employed as a complementary assay to validate hits from the ELISA-based screening platform.

- explain principle of AlphaScreen  
AlphaScreen employs donor beads coated with streptavidin and acceptor beads coated with anti-mouse IgG. When biotinylated capture molecules and antibody-conjugated detection molecules bind to their respective targets in close proximity, the beads are brought into proximity, triggering singlet oxygen transfer and chemiluminescence. The signal is amplified and detected using a plate reader. This method is highly sensitive and can detect interactions at sub-nanomolar concentrations, making it suitable for secondary validation of ELISA hits.

- describe sandwich format assay  
The sandwich format assay involves the immobilization of a capture molecule, the binding of the analyte, and the detection of the analyte with a second, labeled binding molecule. In the context of the present invention, this format is used to detect effector-chaperone complexes, needle complex assembly intermediates, and translocon components. The assay is highly specific, as it requires two independent binding events for signal generation, reducing false positives.

- describe direct and indirect detection methods  
Direct detection involves the use of a labeled antibody that binds directly to the target molecule, while indirect detection uses an unlabeled primary antibody followed by a labeled secondary antibody. Indirect detection provides signal amplification and increased flexibility, as the same secondary antibody can be used with multiple primary antibodies. In the disclosed method, indirect detection is preferred for its enhanced sensitivity and cost-effectiveness.

- introduce alternative assay types (e.g. DELFIA, FRET)  
Alternative assay types such as DELFIA (Dissociation-Enhanced Lanthanide Fluorescent Immunoassay) and FRET (Förster Resonance Energy Transfer) may be employed to detect type III secretion system interactions. DELFIA uses lanthanide chelates as labels, offering long fluorescence lifetimes and low background, while FRET measures energy transfer between fluorophores attached to interacting proteins. These methods provide complementary readouts and may be used for mechanistic studies or validation of ELISA results.

- describe DELFIA assay  
The DELFIA assay utilizes europium-labeled antibodies that emit long-lived fluorescence upon dissociation-enhanced excitation. The assay is performed similarly to ELISA, with capture, blocking, sample incubation, washing, and detection steps. The europium signal is measured using time-resolved fluorometry, which eliminates background fluorescence from biological samples. DELFIA is highly sensitive and suitable for low-abundance targets, making it ideal for detecting chaperone-effector interactions in complex supernatants.

- introduce method for monitoring assembly of structural components  
The method for monitoring assembly of structural components involves the use of a structure ELISA in which one basal body component is immobilized and its binding partner is detected using a specific antibody. This assay quantifies the formation of specific subcomplexes, such as the interaction between PrgH and PrgK or between InvG and PrgH, providing insight into the assembly pathway of the needle complex.

- describe structure assay  
The structure assay is an ELISA-based method that detects the physical association between structural components of the type III secretion system. For example, PrgH is immobilized on a Ni-NTA plate, and purified needle complexes are added. Binding of PrgI to the assembled base is detected using an anti-PrgI antibody. The assay quantifies the extent of needle complex assembly and can be used to screen for compounds that disrupt structural interactions.

- explain principle of structure ELISA  
The principle of the structure ELISA is based on the capture of a structural component on a solid surface, followed by the detection of its binding partner using a specific antibody. The assay measures the formation of a stable protein complex, providing a quantitative readout of assembly efficiency. This method is highly specific and can distinguish between complete and incomplete complexes, making it ideal for identifying inhibitors of structural assembly.

- describe example of structure ELISA (PrgH and PrgI)  
In the structure ELISA example, PrgH is expressed with a C-terminal poly-histidine tag and immobilized on a Ni-NTA plate. Purified needle complexes are added, allowing PrgI to bind to the assembled base. After washing, bound PrgI is detected using a monoclonal anti-PrgI antibody conjugated to horseradish peroxidase. Signal is quantified by TMB substrate conversion. The assay detects only fully assembled needle complexes, as PrgI does not bind to free PrgH monomers.

- introduce alternative tags for structural components  
Alternative tags for structural components include FLAG, HA, Myc, and biotin tags, which can be genetically fused to the target protein for immobilization or detection. Biotin tags allow for immobilization on streptavidin-coated plates, while FLAG and HA tags enable detection with commercially available antibodies. These tags provide flexibility in assay design and can be used in combination with existing ELISA protocols.

- describe analysis of test compound's effect on ATPase  
The effect of test compounds on the ATPase component of the type III secretion system is analyzed using a colorimetric ATPase assay that measures the hydrolysis of ATP to ADP and inorganic phosphate. The assay is performed in the presence of purified ATPase and test compounds, with phosphate release quantified using a malachite green reagent. Inhibition of ATPase activity is indicated by a reduction in phosphate production, providing a direct measure of compound effect on the energy source of secretion.

- explain applicability of structure assay to other bacteria  
The structure ELISA is broadly applicable to any bacterial pathogen encoding a type III secretion system with known structural components. The method has been successfully applied to *Yersinia*, *Shigella*, and *E. coli* by substituting the capture and detection reagents with homologous proteins.
