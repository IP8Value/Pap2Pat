# DESCRIPTION

- introduce malaria  
Malaria is a life-threatening infectious disease caused by protozoan parasites of the genus Plasmodium, which are transmitted to humans through the bite of infected female Anopheles mosquitoes. The disease remains one of the most significant global health burdens, particularly in tropical and subtropical regions where access to healthcare, sanitation, and preventive infrastructure is limited. Each year, hundreds of millions of clinical cases are reported, resulting in over half a million deaths, the majority of which occur among children under five years of age in sub-Saharan Africa. The clinical manifestations of malaria range from uncomplicated febrile illness to severe complications including cerebral malaria, acute respiratory distress syndrome, severe anemia, and multi-organ failure. Without timely diagnosis and treatment, infection can rapidly progress to fatal outcomes. The parasite’s complex life cycle, involving both human and mosquito hosts, contributes to its persistence and challenges in eradication. The transition from sporozoite to liver-stage parasite, followed by asexual replication in erythrocytes, underpins the pathogenesis of disease and represents critical windows for intervention.

- describe malaria as a serious disease  
Malaria is not merely an acute illness but a chronic public health crisis that perpetuates cycles of poverty, impedes economic development, and overwhelms healthcare systems in endemic regions. Its impact extends beyond mortality to include long-term cognitive and developmental deficits in survivors of severe disease, reduced school attendance, decreased labor productivity, and increased household expenditures on treatment and prevention. The burden is disproportionately borne by vulnerable populations, including pregnant women, young children, and immunocompromised individuals. Even in regions where transmission has been reduced, the risk of resurgence remains high due to factors such as insecticide resistance in mosquito vectors, drug resistance in parasites, climate variability, and population mobility. The World Health Organization has classified malaria as one of the top three infectious diseases requiring urgent global attention, and its elimination remains a central pillar of international health policy. The absence of a broadly effective vaccine has hindered progress toward sustainable control, making the development of novel preventive strategies an urgent scientific and humanitarian priority.

- discuss preventative medicines  
Preventative measures against malaria have historically relied on chemoprophylaxis, insecticide-treated bed nets, indoor residual spraying, and environmental management to reduce mosquito breeding sites. Chemoprophylactic drugs such as chloroquine, mefloquine, doxycycline, and atovaquone-proguanil have been widely used by travelers and in mass drug administration programs in high-risk areas. These medications act primarily by inhibiting the asexual replication of the parasite in the bloodstream, thereby preventing the onset of clinical symptoms. In pregnant women and infants, intermittent preventive treatment with sulfadoxine-pyrimethamine has demonstrated efficacy in reducing maternal anemia and low birth weight. Additionally, seasonal malaria chemoprevention using amodiaquine and sulfadoxine-pyrimethamine has been implemented successfully in the Sahel region during peak transmission seasons. While these interventions have contributed to a decline in malaria incidence over the past two decades, their long-term sustainability is compromised by the emergence of drug-resistant parasite strains and logistical challenges in delivering consistent coverage across remote and underserved communities.

- highlight limitations of current medicines  
The efficacy of existing antimalarial drugs is increasingly undermined by the evolution and spread of multidrug-resistant Plasmodium falciparum strains, particularly in Southeast Asia and emerging in parts of Africa. Resistance to artemisinin and its combination therapies, now the global standard for treatment, has been documented in multiple countries, raising fears of a potential global health emergency if resistance becomes widespread. Furthermore, chemoprophylactic agents often require daily or weekly dosing over extended periods, leading to poor adherence and reduced protective efficacy. Many of these drugs also carry significant side effect profiles, including neuropsychiatric disturbances, gastrointestinal intolerance, and contraindications in pregnancy or pediatric populations. The cost and infrastructure required for continuous drug distribution, especially in low-resource settings, further limit scalability. Importantly, none of the current chemoprophylactic agents target the liver stage of the parasite, meaning they do not prevent initial infection or establish sterile immunity, and thus cannot interrupt transmission at its source.

- discuss vaccination programs  
Vaccination remains the most promising strategy for achieving durable, population-wide protection against malaria. The RTS,S/AS01 vaccine, the first and only WHO-recommended malaria vaccine, targets the circumsporozoite protein (CSP) and has demonstrated modest efficacy in reducing clinical episodes in young children, with protection waning over time. A second vaccine, R21/Matrix-M, has shown comparable results in phase III trials and has recently received WHO prequalification. Both vaccines are administered in a multi-dose regimen and provide partial, transient protection, primarily through antibody-mediated inhibition of sporozoite invasion of hepatocytes. While these vaccines represent historic milestones, their limited efficacy profile—ranging from 30% to 70% over the first year—underscores the need for next-generation candidates capable of inducing broader, longer-lasting, and more robust immune responses. Current vaccination efforts are largely confined to the pre-erythrocytic stage, with limited focus on antigens that elicit potent T cell responses capable of eliminating infected hepatocytes.

- introduce RTS, S and ChAd63-MVA ME-TRAP vaccines  
The RTS,S vaccine is a recombinant protein-based construct consisting of a fusion between the central repeat and C-terminal region of the Plasmodium falciparum circumsporozoite protein and the hepatitis B surface antigen, formulated with the AS01 adjuvant system to enhance immunogenicity. The ChAd63-MVA ME-TRAP vaccine is a heterologous prime-boost viral vector platform, wherein chimpanzee adenovirus serotype 63 (ChAd63) primes the immune system and modified vaccinia virus Ankara (MVA) boosts it, both encoding the ME-TRAP antigen—a multiepitope string fused to thrombospondin-related anonymous protein. This platform has demonstrated the ability to induce strong CD8+ T cell responses in clinical trials, with evidence of partial protection against controlled human malaria infection. While both platforms have advanced to field implementation, their protective efficacy remains suboptimal, and neither provides sterile immunity in a majority of recipients. The reliance on a single antigenic target in both cases limits the breadth of immune recognition and increases vulnerability to parasite escape variants.

- describe limitations of current vaccines  
The principal limitation of current malaria vaccines lies in their narrow antigenic focus and inability to induce a comprehensive immune response capable of targeting multiple stages of the parasite’s life cycle. RTS,S and R21 primarily elicit antibody responses against CSP, which may be insufficient to neutralize all sporozoite variants or prevent infection in individuals with pre-existing immunity. The ChAd63-MVA ME-TRAP platform, while effective at generating T cell responses, targets a single fusion antigen with limited epitope diversity, and its efficacy is constrained by the immunodominance of TRAP and ME epitopes, which may not reflect the most protective targets in natural infection. Furthermore, both platforms exhibit declining efficacy over time, necessitating booster doses that complicate delivery in resource-limited settings. Neither vaccine has demonstrated consistent sterile protection across diverse genetic backgrounds, and their performance is significantly reduced in areas of high transmission intensity. The absence of robust liver-stage immunity, which is critical for eliminating parasites before they enter the bloodstream, remains a fundamental gap in current vaccine design.

- discuss irradiated sporozoites as a 'gold-standard'  
Irradiated sporozoites, delivered via mosquito bites or intravenous injection, represent the most effective form of malaria immunization known to date, consistently inducing sterile protection in human volunteers. This approach exploits the natural biology of the parasite: irradiated sporozoites invade hepatocytes but fail to complete liver-stage development, thereby presenting a broad array of antigens to the immune system without causing disease. The resulting immune response includes potent CD8+ T cell activation, liver-resident memory T cells, and humoral immunity, collectively capable of clearing infected hepatocytes before parasite release into the bloodstream. Despite its proven efficacy, irradiated sporozoite vaccination is not scalable due to the logistical complexity of producing, storing, and delivering live parasites under strict sterile conditions. It also requires multiple doses and specialized medical infrastructure, rendering it impractical for widespread public health use. Nevertheless, it serves as the benchmark against which all subunit vaccines are measured, highlighting the necessity of replicating its immunological profile through recombinant technologies.

- highlight the need for better targets  
The success of irradiated sporozoite immunization underscores that protection against malaria is not conferred by a single antigen but by a coordinated immune response targeting multiple liver-stage proteins. Current vaccines, by focusing narrowly on CSP or TRAP, fail to recapitulate this breadth. A substantial body of evidence suggests that antigens expressed during the intracellular liver stage, particularly those localized to the parasitophorous vacuole membrane or secreted into the host hepatocyte cytoplasm, are more effectively presented to CD8+ T cells and thus more likely to confer sterile immunity. The identification of such antigens has been hindered by the lack of robust in vitro systems for studying human liver-stage parasites and the absence of a small animal model capable of accurately predicting human immunogenicity and efficacy. Consequently, the field has been constrained to testing only a handful of candidate antigens, many of which have failed to translate from preclinical models to clinical success.

- introduce the problem of identifying suitable antigens  
The challenge of identifying protective antigens lies in the complexity of the parasite’s genome, the diversity of its life cycle stages, and the difficulty of distinguishing immunogenic from protective targets. Many proteins are highly expressed during the liver stage and elicit strong immune responses in natural infection or vaccination, yet fail to confer protection when tested individually in animal models. This discrepancy arises because immune recognition does not always equate to functional elimination of infected cells. Moreover, antigenic variation, post-translational modifications, and stage-specific expression patterns further complicate target selection. Traditional approaches relying on orthologous rodent parasite models are inadequate for P. falciparum, as many of its key liver-stage proteins lack conserved counterparts in murine species, rendering them invisible to conventional preclinical screening. Without a systematic method to prioritize antigens based on their capacity to induce protective immunity, the pipeline for new vaccine candidates remains slow and inefficient.

- discuss the lack of a suitable small animal model  
The absence of a small animal model that faithfully recapitulates human malaria infection has been a major bottleneck in vaccine development. Mice are not naturally susceptible to P. falciparum, and the use of rodent malaria species such as P. berghei or P. yoelii does not accurately reflect the immunobiology of human infection. While transgenic parasites expressing P. falciparum antigens in a murine background have been developed, their utility has been limited by inconsistent expression levels, altered parasite fitness, and immunological differences between mouse and human immune systems. These limitations have led to false negatives, where promising human antigens are dismissed due to poor performance in mice, and false positives, where antigens protective in mice fail in human trials. The development of chimeric P. berghei parasites expressing full-length P. falciparum antigens under native regulatory elements has begun to address this gap, enabling the direct evaluation of human antigens in a physiologically relevant context. This approach has revealed that antigens previously overlooked due to lack of rodent orthologs can be among the most protective.

- introduce LSA-1 as a liver-stage protein  
LSA-1, or liver-stage antigen 1, is a large, highly conserved protein expressed abundantly during the late liver stage of Plasmodium falciparum development. It is localized within the parasitophorous vacuole and is secreted as flocculent material surrounding developing merozoites, suggesting a role in the structural organization of the parasite during its transition from liver to blood stages. LSA-1 is not present in the sporozoite or blood stages, making it an ideal target for liver-stage-specific immunity. It contains multiple repetitive domains that are subject to immune recognition, and its expression is tightly regulated, peaking just prior to merozoite egress. Antibodies and T cells specific to LSA-1 have been detected in individuals exposed to natural infection and in volunteers immunized with irradiated sporozoites, indicating its relevance in naturally acquired immunity. Despite this, recombinant protein vaccines based on LSA-1 have failed to induce protection in clinical trials, likely due to their inability to generate the CD8+ T cell responses necessary for eliminating infected hepatocytes.

- describe the function of LSA-1  
The precise molecular function of LSA-1 remains incompletely understood, but its localization and timing of expression suggest a critical role in the maturation and egress of liver-stage parasites. It is believed to interact with host hepatocyte components to facilitate the formation of the parasitophorous vacuole membrane and the organization of merozoite budding structures. Its absence in other Plasmodium species, including rodent models, indicates that LSA-1 may have evolved specifically to support the unique biology of P. falciparum in human hepatocytes. This species-specificity renders LSA-1 an ideal candidate for a human-targeted vaccine, as immune responses against it are unlikely to be cross-reactive with non-pathogenic organisms, reducing the risk of off-target effects. Furthermore, its conservation across diverse P. falciparum isolates suggests that antigenic drift is unlikely to compromise vaccine efficacy, making it a stable target for long-term protection.

- discuss the association of LSA-1 with protection  
Epidemiological studies have consistently linked the presence of LSA-1-specific T cell responses with protection against clinical malaria in naturally exposed populations. Individuals who develop high-frequency, polyfunctional CD8+ T cells targeting LSA-1 are significantly less likely to experience symptomatic infection following subsequent exposure. In controlled human malaria infection trials, volunteers immunized with irradiated sporozoites who remained sterilely protected exhibited robust LSA-1-specific T cell reactivity, whereas those who developed parasitemia showed minimal responses. These findings strongly suggest that LSA-1 is not merely immunogenic but is a bona fide target of protective immunity. However, prior attempts to deliver LSA-1 as a recombinant protein or DNA vaccine failed to induce comparable T cell responses, indicating that the mode of antigen presentation is critical. The discovery that viral vector delivery of LSA-1 elicits potent liver-resident CD8+ T cells and confers sterile protection in murine models represents a paradigm shift in malaria vaccine design.

- introduce clinical trials of LSA-1  
Early clinical trials of recombinant LSA-1 protein formulated with alum or other adjuvants were conducted in malaria-naïve adults and demonstrated safety but failed to induce significant T cell responses or confer protection against controlled human malaria infection. These trials were conducted using subunit delivery methods incapable of promoting endogenous antigen processing and MHC class I presentation, which are essential for CD8+ T cell priming. As a result, the immune response was predominantly humoral, with low-titer antibodies that did not correlate with protection. These negative outcomes led to the premature dismissal of LSA-1 as a viable vaccine candidate, despite compelling evidence of its importance in natural immunity. The failure of these trials highlighted a critical flaw in vaccine design: the assumption that immunogenicity alone is sufficient, without regard to the quality, magnitude, and cellular context of the immune response elicited.

- discuss the failures of previous trials  
The failures of prior LSA-1 vaccine trials were not due to an inherent lack of antigenic value but rather to the inappropriate delivery platform. Recombinant protein vaccines are optimized for antibody induction via MHC class II presentation, whereas protection against liver-stage malaria requires CD8+ T cell-mediated killing of infected hepatocytes, which depends on MHC class I presentation. The absence of cross-presentation mechanisms in protein-based formulations rendered LSA-1 invisible to the cytotoxic T cell compartment. Similarly, DNA vaccines, while capable of inducing some T cell responses, suffer from low transfection efficiency and poor immunogenicity in humans. These limitations underscore a broader issue in vaccinology: the mismatch between antigen selection and delivery technology. The success of LSA-1 as a protective antigen is contingent upon its presentation in a context that mimics natural infection—namely, through intracellular expression and endogenous processing.

- highlight the need for alternative antigens and delivery methods  
The limitations of existing vaccines and the failures of prior antigen candidates necessitate a reevaluation of both target selection and delivery modalities. The identification of novel antigens must be guided not by immunogenicity alone but by their capacity to induce functional, liver-resident CD8+ T cells capable of eliminating infected hepatocytes. Viral vector platforms, particularly heterologous prime-boost regimens using adenovirus and poxvirus vectors, have demonstrated an unparalleled ability to drive robust, durable T cell responses. These systems enable endogenous expression of the antigen within antigen-presenting cells, facilitating MHC class I presentation and the generation of cytotoxic T lymphocytes. Furthermore, the use of chimeric parasite models has revealed that antigens previously considered non-protective due to lack of rodent orthologs, such as LSA-1 and LSAP2, are among the most efficacious when delivered via viral vectors. This paradigm shift opens the door to a new generation of malaria vaccines that are not constrained by the limitations of traditional animal models.

- introduce the invention  
The invention comprises a novel class of malaria vaccines based on the delivery of multiple Plasmodium falciparum liver-stage antigens using viral vector platforms to elicit potent, protective CD8+ T cell responses. Specifically, the invention provides immunogenic compositions comprising nucleic acid sequences encoding PfLSA1, PfLSAP2, PfUIS3, PfI0580c, and PfSPECT-1, each operably linked to a mammalian promoter and packaged within recombinant adenovirus or modified vaccinia virus Ankara vectors. These compositions are designed to be administered in prime-boost regimens to induce systemic and liver-resident T cell immunity capable of conferring sterile protection against sporozoite challenge. The invention further encompasses pharmaceutical compositions, kits, and methods for the prevention and treatment of malaria using these antigens in combination or as standalone components, with demonstrated efficacy in both inbred and outbred murine models and a clear mechanistic basis rooted in CD8+ T cell-mediated elimination of infected hepatocytes.

- describe the antigenic composition or vaccine  
The antigenic composition of the invention comprises one or more nucleic acid sequences encoding Plasmodium falciparum liver-stage antigens, including PfLSA1, PfLSAP2, PfUIS3, PfI0580c, and PfSPECT-1, each codon-optimized for expression in human cells and fused to a tissue plasminogen activator leader sequence to enhance secretion and immunogenicity. These sequences are cloned into recombinant adenovirus or modified vaccinia virus Ankara vectors under the control of a cytomegalovirus immediate-early promoter or a poxvirus p7.5 promoter, respectively. The vectors are engineered to be replication-deficient and devoid of immunomodulatory genes that may interfere with antigen presentation. When administered intramuscularly, the vectors transduce host cells, leading to endogenous expression of the encoded antigens, processing via the proteasome, and presentation on MHC class I molecules. This results in the activation and expansion of antigen-specific CD8+ T cells, which traffic to the liver and eliminate hepatocytes infected with sporozoites, thereby preventing the establishment of blood-stage infection.

- introduce PfLSA1 as a Plasmodium protein  
PfLSA1 is a 220-kilodalton protein encoded by the PF3D7_1472300 gene in Plasmodium falciparum, expressed exclusively during the late liver stage of infection. It is localized to the parasitophorous vacuole and is secreted as a flocculent matrix surrounding developing merozoites. PfLSA1 is highly conserved across global isolates, with minimal sequence variation, and lacks orthologs in rodent malaria species. Its expression is tightly regulated, peaking 48 to 72 hours post-invasion of hepatocytes, coinciding with the period of maximal parasite replication and prior to merozoite egress. The protein contains multiple repetitive domains that are targeted by T cells in naturally immune individuals, and its deletion in transgenic parasites impairs liver-stage development, indicating a functional role in parasite maturation. When delivered via ChAd63-MVA viral vectors, PfLSA1 induces high-frequency, polyfunctional CD8+ T cells that localize to the liver and confer sterile protection in over 85% of vaccinated mice.

- introduce PfLSAP2 as a Plasmodium protein  
PfLSAP2, or liver-stage associated protein 2, is a 110-kilodalton protein encoded by the PF3D7_1438800 gene, expressed during the mid-to-late liver stage and localized to the parasitophorous vacuole membrane. It is not expressed in sporozoites or blood stages, making it an ideal target for liver-stage-specific immunity. PfLSAP2 is conserved across P. falciparum strains and lacks homologs in rodent Plasmodium species. Immunological studies have demonstrated that CD8+ T cells specific to PfLSAP2 are enriched in individuals protected by irradiated sporozoite immunization. When delivered via heterologous viral vectors, PfLSAP2 elicits robust CD8+ T cell responses characterized by high levels of IFNγ, TNFα, and CD107a expression, and confers sterile protection in 70 to 87.5% of vaccinated mice across multiple strains, including outbred CD-1 mice. Depletion of CD8+ T cells abolishes protection, confirming the mechanism of action.

- introduce PfUIS3 as a Plasmodium protein  
PfUIS3, or upregulated in sporozoite 3, is a 45-kilodalton transmembrane protein encoded by the PF3D7_1439400 gene, expressed in sporozoites and throughout liver-stage development. It is localized to the parasitophorous vacuole membrane and is essential for the formation and maintenance of the vacuolar structure that supports parasite growth. PfUIS3 is conserved across Plasmodium species and has been shown to be a target of T cell responses in naturally exposed individuals. When delivered via viral vectors, PfUIS3 induces moderate CD8+ T cell responses and provides partial protection, primarily manifesting as a delay in the time to patent parasitemia. While sterile protection is less consistent than with PfLSA1 or PfLSAP2, PfUIS3 contributes to overall efficacy when combined with other antigens, suggesting a synergistic role in multi-antigen vaccines.

- introduce PfI0580c as a Plasmodium protein  
PfI0580c, encoded by the PF3D7_0925800 gene, is a 60-kilodalton protein of unknown function, expressed during the liver stage and localized to the parasitophorous vacuole. It is conserved across P. falciparum isolates and lacks orthologs in rodent parasites. Immunogenicity studies reveal that PfI0580c induces moderate CD8+ T cell responses in mice, with detectable IFNγ production and partial protection against sporozoite challenge. While not as potent as PfLSA1 or PfLSAP2, PfI0580c contributes to enhanced efficacy when used in combination with other antigens, suggesting it may broaden the immune response and reduce the likelihood of parasite escape.

- introduce PfSPECT-1 as a Plasmodium protein  
PfSPECT-1, or sporozoite microneme protein essential for cell traversal 1, is a 75-kilodalton protein encoded by the PF3D7_1343700 gene, expressed in sporozoites and early liver stages. It is involved in motility and host cell invasion and is localized to micronemes. PfSPECT-1 is conserved across Plasmodium species and has been shown to be a target of antibody and T cell responses in natural infection. When delivered via viral vectors, PfSPECT-1 induces moderate CD8+ T cell responses and confers partial protection in murine models, particularly in outbred strains. Its inclusion in multi-antigen formulations enhances overall immune breadth and contributes to sustained protection.

- describe the method of identifying key candidate antigens  
The method of identifying key candidate antigens involves the systematic evaluation of Plasmodium falciparum liver-stage proteins using a chimeric parasite model in which P. falciparum genes are expressed in P. berghei under the control of the UIS4 promoter. This approach enables direct assessment of antigen immunogenicity and protective efficacy in vivo, circumventing the limitations of rodent ortholog models. Candidate antigens are selected based on their expression during the liver stage, conservation across isolates, and association with protective immunity in naturally exposed individuals. Antigen constructs are cloned into viral vectors and administered to mice in a heterologous prime-boost regimen. Cellular and humoral immune responses are quantified using flow cytometry, ELISpot, and luminescence immunoprecipitation assays. Protective efficacy is determined by challenge with chimeric sporozoites and monitoring for sterile protection or delay in parasitemia. Antigens that consistently induce high-frequency CD8+ T cell responses and confer sterile protection in multiple mouse strains are prioritized for further development.

- discuss the use of viral vectors  
Viral vectors are employed as delivery vehicles to ensure endogenous expression of the encoded antigens, thereby facilitating MHC class I presentation and the generation of cytotoxic CD8+ T cells. Adenovirus serotype 63 and modified vaccinia virus Ankara are selected for their ability to transduce dendritic cells and hepatocytes, induce strong innate immune activation, and elicit durable T cell memory without causing disease. The vectors are replication-deficient and engineered to minimize pre-existing immunity interference. Heterologous prime-boost regimens using ChAd63 followed by MVA maximize T cell expansion and longevity, as demonstrated by the induction of liver-resident memory T cells that persist for months. The use of viral vectors enables the delivery of full-length, native-like antigens, preserving conformational epitopes and post-translational modifications critical for immune recognition.

- introduce the results of the method  
The results of this method demonstrate that PfLSA1 and PfLSAP2, when delivered via ChAd63-MVA vectors, confer sterile protection in over 85% of vaccinated mice across multiple strains, including outbred CD-1 mice. PfUIS3 and PfI0580c induce moderate protection, primarily delaying parasitemia, while PfSPECT-1 contributes to immune breadth without conferring sterile protection alone. These antigens elicit high-frequency, polyfunctional CD8+ T cells expressing IFNγ, TNFα, and CD107a, and these cells are detectable in the liver, where they colocalize with infected hepatocytes. Depletion of CD8+ T cells abolishes protection, confirming the mechanism of action. No protection is observed in mice vaccinated with control antigens or with recombinant protein formulations, underscoring the necessity of viral vector delivery.

- highlight the protective efficacy of PfLSA1 and LSAP2  
PfLSA1 and PfLSAP2 consistently demonstrate the highest level of protective efficacy among all tested antigens, achieving sterile protection in 87.5% of BALB/c mice and 70 to 87.5% of CD-1 outbred mice following a single prime-boost regimen. This level of efficacy surpasses that of the leading clinical vaccine candidates, RTS,S and ME-TRAP, and is comparable to that achieved by irradiated sporozoites. The protection is durable, lasting at least six months post-vaccination, and is associated with the induction of liver-resident memory CD8+ T cells. The antigens are highly conserved, with no evidence of immune escape in challenge experiments, and their efficacy is maintained across diverse genetic backgrounds, supporting their potential for broad population coverage.

- discuss the moderate protective efficacy of PfUIS3 and PfI0580c  
PfUIS3 and PfI0580c induce moderate but statistically significant protection, primarily manifesting as a delay in the time to patent parasitemia rather than sterile immunity. In BALB/c mice, PfUIS3 reduces parasitemia onset by 1.4 days, while PfI0580c delays it by 0.9 days. These antigens elicit CD8+ T cell responses of intermediate magnitude and are associated with lower levels of liver-resident memory cells compared to PfLSA1 and PfLSAP2. However, when combined with PfLSA1 or PfLSAP2, they enhance overall immune breadth and contribute to sustained protection, suggesting a complementary role in multi-antigen formulations. Their inclusion mitigates the risk of parasite escape and broadens the immune response to multiple liver-stage targets.

- introduce the term "protective immune response"  
A protective immune response in the context of this invention refers to an immune reaction capable of preventing the establishment of blood-stage infection following sporozoite challenge, as evidenced by sterile protection or a significant delay in parasitemia. This response is characterized by the induction of high-frequency, polyfunctional CD8+ T cells that express IFNγ, TNFα, and the cytotoxic marker CD107a, and that localize to the liver where they recognize and eliminate hepatocytes infected with Plasmodium falciparum. The response is durable, long-lasting, and independent of antibody-mediated mechanisms, as demonstrated by the complete loss of protection upon CD8+ T cell depletion. It is not merely immunogenic but functionally effective in clearing infected cells prior to parasite egress.

- describe the characteristics of a protective immune response  
The protective immune response elicited by the invention is defined by its specificity, magnitude, functionality, and localization. It is directed against multiple liver-stage antigens, including PfLSA1 and PfLSAP2, and is dominated by CD8+ T cells with a memory phenotype characterized by CD62L− CD127+ expression. These cells produce multiple cytokines simultaneously, indicating polyfunctionality, and express cytotoxic markers that enable direct killing of infected hepatocytes. The response is sustained in the liver, where antigen-specific T cells persist as resident memory populations, enabling rapid recall upon sporozoite invasion. The response is not dependent on high-titer antibodies, as passive transfer of serum fails to confer protection, and is effective across genetically diverse mouse strains, suggesting potential for broad human applicability.

- discuss the use of redundant codons  
The nucleic acid sequences encoding the Plasmodium falciparum antigens are optimized for expression in human cells through the use of redundant codons that correspond to the most frequently used codons in the human genome. This optimization enhances translational efficiency, increases protein yield, and minimizes the risk of mRNA instability or misfolding. Codon usage bias is adjusted to match human tRNA abundance, ensuring rapid and accurate translation following transduction by viral vectors. This modification does not alter the amino acid sequence of the encoded proteins but significantly enhances their immunogenicity by increasing expression levels in antigen-presenting cells, thereby amplifying the magnitude of the T cell response.

- introduce the viral vector  
The viral vector used in the invention is a replication-deficient chimpanzee adenovirus serotype 63 or a modified vaccinia virus Ankara, each engineered to carry a single expression cassette encoding one or more Plasmodium falciparum liver-stage antigens. The adenovirus vector is deleted in the E1 and E3 regions to prevent replication and is propagated in complementing HEK 293T cells. The MVA vector is derived from the attenuated vaccinia virus strain and is rendered replication-incompetent in mammalian cells by deletion of the thymidine kinase gene. Both vectors are purified by ultracentrifugation and formulated in endotoxin-free phosphate-buffered saline for intramuscular administration.

- describe the modification of the viral vector  
The viral vectors are modified to contain a mammalian cytomegalovirus immediate-early promoter or a poxvirus p7.5 promoter to drive high-level expression of the encoded antigen. Each antigen is fused at its N-terminus to a tissue plasminogen activator leader sequence to facilitate secretion and enhance immunogenicity. The coding sequence is flanked by Kozak and STOP sequences to ensure efficient translation initiation and termination. The vectors are devoid of any sequences that could trigger unintended immune modulation, including Vaccinia virus early transcription termination signals. All constructs are sequence-verified and tested for purity and potency prior to use in immunization studies.

- introduce the composition  
The composition of the invention comprises a pharmaceutical formulation containing a therapeutically effective amount of one or more viral vectors encoding PfLSA1, PfLSAP2, PfUIS3, PfI0580c, or PfSPECT-1, optionally in combination with an adjuvant. The composition is formulated as a sterile, pyrogen-free solution suitable for intramuscular injection and is stable for at least 24 months when stored at 2–8°C. The viral vectors are present at a concentration of 1×10⁸ infectious units per milliliter for adenovirus and 1×10⁷ plaque-forming units per milliliter for MVA, with each dose administered in a volume of 50 microliters.

- discuss the use of two or more different viral vectors  
The invention employs a heterologous prime-boost regimen utilizing two or more distinct viral vectors, specifically a chimpanzee adenovirus serotype 63 vector for priming and a modified vaccinia virus Ankara vector for boosting. This strategy maximizes T cell expansion and memory formation by circumventing vector-specific immunity and enhancing antigen presentation. The prime-boost interval is optimized at eight weeks to allow for the peak of CD8+ T cell responses. The use of two different vectors prevents immune dominance by a single vector backbone and ensures robust, durable immunity even in individuals with pre-existing immunity to human adenoviruses.

- introduce the pharmaceutical composition  
The pharmaceutical composition of the invention comprises a sterile, isotonic, non-pyrogenic aqueous solution containing a therapeutically effective amount of one or more viral vectors encoding Plasmodium falciparum liver-stage antigens, formulated in phosphate-buffered saline with 0.01% polysorbate 80 as a stabilizer. The composition is free of live infectious Plasmodium parasites and contains no adjuvants unless specifically indicated. It is packaged in single-dose vials or pre-filled syringes and is suitable for intramuscular administration in the deltoid or quadriceps muscle.

The PCR procedure involved the amplification of target genes using high-fidelity DNA polymerase, with initial denaturation at 95°C for 5 minutes, followed by 35 cycles of 95°C for 30 seconds, 58°C for 30 seconds, and 72°C for 1 minute, with a final extension at 72°C for 10 minutes. In-Fusion HD Enzyme Premix was used for seamless cloning of amplified antigen sequences into viral vector backbones. Plasmid DNA was transformed into electrocompetent E. coli DH5α cells by electroporation, followed by recovery in SOC medium at 37°C for 1 hour. Plasmid DNA was prepared using a commercial miniprep kit and quantified by spectrophotometry. - motivate transfection of HEK 293A cells  
Transfection of HEK 293A cells was performed to produce recombinant adenovirus particles. Lipofectamine 2000 was diluted in Opti-MEM medium at a 1:1 ratio and incubated for 5 minutes at room temperature. Plasmid DNA was added to the diluted lipofectamine and incubated for an additional 20 minutes to form lipid-DNA complexes. The mixture was then added dropwise to HEK 293A cells.

The DNA-lipofectamine mixture was prepared by combining 2 μg of plasmid DNA with 6 μL of Lipofectamine 2000 in 100 μL of Opti-MEM. The mixture was vortexed gently and incubated for 20 minutes at room temperature to allow complex formation. The complexes were then added to HEK 293A cells in 6-well plates.

- describe transfection procedure  
HEK 293A cells were seeded at 80% confluency in 6-well plates and transfected with the DNA-lipofectamine mixture. After 6 hours, the medium was replaced with complete α-MEM. Cells were monitored for cytopathic effect and harvested 72 hours post-transfection for virus collection.

Harvest of cellular lysate was performed to assess the expression of encoded antigens and to quantify viral titers. Lysates were used in Western blotting and luciferase assays to confirm antigen expression and to determine the infectious titer of recombinant adenovirus.

Lysis buffer was prepared by dissolving 1% Triton X-100, 150 mM NaCl, 50 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.5, and protease inhibitors in distilled water. The buffer was filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane and stored at 4°C. It was used to lyse transfected cells for protein extraction.

Cells were washed with PBS and lysed by addition of lysis buffer followed by incubation on ice for 30 minutes. Lysates were centrifuged at 14,000 × g for 15 minutes, and supernatants were collected and stored at −80°C for downstream analysis.

- describe luciferase activity quantification  
Luciferase activity was quantified using the Renilla Luciferase Assay System. Lysates were mixed with coelenterazine substrate and luminescence was measured using a luminometer. Results were normalized to total protein concentration and expressed as relative light units per microgram of protein.

Genotyping of HHD mice was performed to confirm the presence of human HLA-A2 transgenes, which are required for the evaluation of human-restricted T cell epitopes. This ensures that the immune responses observed are relevant to human immunology and not confounded by murine MHC restriction.

Ear punch biopsies were incubated in Ear Punch Buffer at 56°C overnight with proteinase K. DNA was extracted using phenol-chloroform and precipitated with ethanol. The pellet was washed, dried, and resuspended in TE buffer. DNA concentration was measured by spectrophotometry.

Animal and immunization procedures were performed to evaluate the immunogenicity and protective efficacy of the vaccine candidates in vivo. - describe animal housing and handling  
All mice were housed in specific pathogen-free facilities with controlled temperature, humidity, and light cycles. Animals were provided with sterile food and water ad libitum. All procedures were conducted under anesthesia and in accordance with institutional ethical guidelines.

- outline immunization and injection procedures  
Mice were immunized intramuscularly in the tibialis anterior muscle with 1×10⁸ infectious units of ChAd63 followed by 1×10⁷ plaque-forming units of MVA eight weeks later. Control groups received PBS or empty vector. Blood was collected at weekly intervals for immunological analysis.

- describe vaccine formulation and administration  
Vaccine formulations were prepared in endotoxin-free PBS at concentrations of 1×10⁸ IFU/mL for adenovirus and 1×10⁷ PFU/mL for MVA. Each dose was administered in a volume of 50 μL using a 27-gauge needle. Injection sites were marked for consistency, and animals were monitored for adverse reactions.

- dissect liver  
Livers were dissected from euthanized mice by incising the abdominal cavity and removing the organ with sterile scissors. Tissues were placed in cold PBS for further processing.

- tease apart liver cells  
Liver tissue was mechanically disrupted using a syringe plunger and passed through a 70 μm cell strainer to generate a single-cell suspension. Cells were washed and resuspended in ACK lysis buffer to remove erythrocytes.

- pass through strainer  
The liver cell suspension was passed through a 70 μm nylon cell strainer to remove debris and obtain a homogeneous single-cell suspension. The strainer was rinsed with PBS to recover all cells.

- spin and resuspend cells  
Cells were centrifuged at 480 × g for 7 minutes, the supernatant was discarded, and the pellet was resuspended in complete medium for flow cytometry or ELISpot assays.

- count and viability of cells  
Cell counts and viability were determined using a hemocytometer and trypan blue exclusion. Only samples with >90% viability were used for downstream assays.

- resuspend cells in culture medium  
Cells were resuspended in RPMI 1640 supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum, 1% penicillin-streptomycin, and 2 mM L-glutamine for stimulation assays.

- collect mouse sera  
Blood was collected from the retro-orbital sinus into serum separator tubes and allowed to clot at room temperature for 30 minutes. Serum was separated by centrifugation at 1,500 × g for 10 minutes and stored at −80°C.

- store sera at -20°C  
Serum samples were aliquoted and stored at −20°C for short-term use or at −80°C for long-term storage. Samples were thawed on ice prior to use in LIPS or ELISA assays.

- synthesize peptides  
Synthetic peptides corresponding to predicted CD8+ T cell epitopes of PfLSA1, PfLSAP2, PfUIS3, PfI0580c, and PfSPECT-1 were synthesized by solid-phase peptide synthesis with >95% purity. Peptides were dissolved in DMSO and stored at −80°C.

- reconstitute peptides in DMSO  
Peptides were reconstituted in dimethyl sulfoxide at a concentration of 10 mg/mL and stored in aliquots at −80°C. Working solutions were prepared in PBS immediately before use.

- combine peptides into sub-pools  
Peptides were combined into sub-pools of 10–15 overlapping 20-mers to cover the entire antigen sequence. Each pool was tested independently in ELISpot and intracellular cytokine staining assays.

- perform intracellular cytokine staining  
Splenocytes or liver mononuclear cells were stimulated with peptide pools for six hours in the presence of GolgiPlug. Cells were stained for surface markers, fixed, permeabilized, and stained for intracellular cytokines using fluorescently labeled antibodies.

- stimulate cells with peptides  
Cells were incubated with peptide pools at a final concentration of 5 μg/mL for six hours at 37°C in 5% CO₂. GolgiPlug was added after one hour to inhibit cytokine secretion.

- stain cells with antibodies  
Cells were stained with fluorochrome-conjugated antibodies against CD4, CD8, IFNγ, TNFα, and CD107a for 30 minutes at 4°C. Unbound antibody was removed by washing with FACS buffer.

- fix and permeabilize cells  
Cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 20 minutes and permeabilized with Perm/Wash buffer for 15 minutes at room temperature. Staining for intracellular cytokines was performed after permeabilization.

- stain for memory cell markers  
Cells were stained with antibodies against CD62L, CD127, and CD44 to identify memory subsets. LIVE/DEAD Aqua was used to exclude dead cells.

- acquire and analyze data  
Flow cytometry data were acquired using a BD LSRFortessa and analyzed using FlowJo software. Gating strategies were applied to identify CD8+ T cells and quantify cytokine production.

- perform ELISpot assay  
ELISpot plates were coated with anti-IFNγ antibody overnight at 4°C. Splenocytes were added with peptide pools and incubated for 18–20 hours. Spots were developed using enzyme-conjugated detection antibodies and counted using an automated ELISpot reader.

- coat plates with antibody  
ELISpot plates were coated with 10 μg/mL anti-mouse IFNγ antibody in coating buffer and incubated overnight at 4°C. Plates were washed and blocked with 1% BSA in PBS.

- add splenocytes and peptides  
Splenocytes were added at 2×10⁵ cells per well in the presence of peptide pools at 5 μg/mL. Controls included media alone and PMA/ionomycin.

- incubate and wash plates  
Plates were incubated at 37°C for 18–20 hours, washed five times with PBS/Tween, and incubated with biotinylated anti-IFNγ detection antibody for 2 hours.

- add detection antibody  
Biotinylated anti-mouse IFNγ antibody was added at 1 μg/mL and incubated for two hours at room temperature. Plates were washed and incubated with streptavidin-horseradish peroxidase.

- develop and count spots  
Spots were developed using AEC substrate and counted using an automated ELISpot counter. Results were expressed as spot-forming units per million cells.

- isolate CD4+ and CD8+ T cells  
CD4+ and CD8+ T cells were isolated from spleens using magnetic-activated cell sorting with anti-CD4 and anti-CD8 microbeads. Cells were passed through a MACS column and collected as positive fractions.

- perform positive selection of CD4+ T cells  
CD4+ T cells were positively selected using anti-CD4 microbeads and a MACS separator. Purity was confirmed by flow cytometry and was consistently >95%.

- perform negative selection of CD8+ T cells  
CD8+ T cells were negatively selected by depleting CD4+ and other non-CD8+ cells using a cocktail of biotinylated antibodies and anti-biotin microbeads. Purity was >90%.

- enrich CD8+ T cells  
CD8+ T cells were further enriched by magnetic separation and resuspended in complete medium for adoptive transfer or in vitro killing assays.

- deplete T cells in vivo  
Mice were injected intraperitoneally with 100 μg of anti-CD4 or anti-CD8 monoclonal antibodies on days −2, −1, and 0 relative to challenge. Depletion was confirmed by flow cytometry of peripheral blood.

- perform luminescence immunoprecipitation assay  
LIPS assays were performed by incubating serum with antigen-Renilla luciferase fusion proteins, capturing immune complexes on protein A/G beads, and measuring luminescence. Background was subtracted using naïve serum controls.

- perform enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay  
ELISAs were performed by coating plates with recombinant antigens, blocking with BSA, incubating with serum, and detecting with HRP-conjugated anti-mouse IgG. Absorbance was read at 450 nm.

- describe materials and methods  
All materials and methods were performed in accordance with institutional guidelines and ethical approvals. Reagents were sourced from commercial vendors and validated for use in immunological assays.

- detail antibody detection  
Antibody detection was performed using LIPS and ELISA assays. Serum samples were serially diluted and incubated with antigen-rluc fusion proteins. Luminescence was measured and fold-change over naïve controls was calculated.

- outline whole IgG passive transfer  
Whole IgG was purified from serum of vaccinated mice using protein G affinity chromatography. Purified IgG was injected intraperitoneally into naïve mice 24 hours prior to sporozoite challenge to assess antibody-mediated protection.

- introduce parasitology  
Parasitological methods were used to assess parasite burden in mice following sporozoite challenge. Thin blood smears were stained with Giemsa and examined microscopically for parasitemia.

- describe parasite strains  
The P. falciparum 3D7 strain and P. berghei ANKA strain were used in all experiments. Chimeric parasites expressing P. falciparum antigens were generated by homologous recombination at the 230p locus.

- detail preparation of thin blood smears  
Thin blood smears were prepared by spreading 2 μL of tail vein blood on glass slides, air-drying, and fixing in methanol. Slides were stained with Giemsa for 20 minutes and examined under oil immersion.

- outline sporozoite production  
Mosquitoes were fed on infected mice and maintained at 21°C for 14–21 days. Salivary glands were dissected and sporozoites released by mechanical disruption. Sporozoites were counted using a hemocytometer.

- describe cryopreservation of pRBCs  
Infected red blood cells were cryopreserved in freezing medium containing 10% DMSO and stored in liquid nitrogen. Vials were thawed rapidly and used for mouse infections.

- detail sporozoite challenge  
Mice were injected intravenously with 1,000 sporozoites in 100 μL of PBS. Challenge was performed eight days after the final immunization.

- outline in vivo imaging using IVIS system  
Mice were injected intravenously with luciferin and imaged using an IVIS Spectrum system to quantify liver-stage parasite burden. Bioluminescence was measured 44 hours post-challenge.

- describe immunofluorescence antibody test  
Immunofluorescence was performed on fixed sporozoites using anti-PfLSA1 and anti-PfLSAP2 sera. Bound antibodies were detected with Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated secondary antibodies and visualized by fluorescence microscopy.

- introduce murine in vitro T cell killing assay  
Murine in vitro T cell killing assays were performed by co-culturing splenocytes from vaccinated mice with Hepa1-6 hepatoma cells infected with chimeric sporozoites. Killing was assessed by flow cytometry for GFP-positive hepatocytes.

- detail preparation of hepatoma cells  
Hepa1-6 cells were cultured in complete α-MEM and infected with chimeric sporozoites at a ratio of 10:1. Cells were incubated for 48 hours before addition of splenocytes.

- outline infection of hepatoma cells with murine parasites  
Hepa1-6 cells were infected with chimeric P. berghei sporozoites expressing GFP and luciferase. Infection was confirmed by fluorescence microscopy and flow cytometry.

- describe addition of cytokines, drugs or splenocytes  
Splenocytes from vaccinated mice were added to infected hepatoma cells at a 10:1 ratio. Cytokines such as IFNγ and IL-12 were added to assess enhancement of killing.

- detail assessment of infectivity by flow cytometry  
Infected hepatoma cells were analyzed by flow cytometry for GFP expression. Percent inhibition was calculated as the reduction in GFP+ cells in the presence of splenocytes compared to controls.

- calculate percentage inhibition  
Percentage inhibition was calculated as [(GFP+ cells in control – GFP+ cells in test) / GFP+ cells in control] × 100. Results were averaged across triplicates.

- describe monitoring of parasitaemia  
Parasitaemia was monitored daily by thin blood smear and Giemsa staining. The time to reach 0.5% or 1% parasitemia was recorded and used to calculate survival curves.

- detail exflagellation measurement  
Exflagellation was assessed by incubating gametocyte cultures in exflagellation medium and counting microgametes under a microscope. Rates were expressed as the number of exflagellation centers per field.

- outline mosquito maintenance  
Mosquitoes were maintained at 27°C and 80% humidity with access to 10% sucrose. Infected mice were fed to mosquitoes using membrane feeders.

- describe salivary gland dissection  
Salivary glands were dissected from infected mosquitoes in PBS and homogenized. Sporozoites were released by pipetting and counted using a hemocytometer.

- detail sporozoite counting  
Sporozoites were counted in a Neubauer hemocytometer under a phase-contrast microscope. Counts were performed in triplicate and averaged.

- outline blood film analysis  
Blood films were prepared daily and stained with Giemsa. Parasitemia was calculated as the percentage of infected red blood cells among 5,000 cells counted.

## 2 ASSESSING IMMUNOGENICITY AND EFFICACY OF EIGHT CANDIDATE P. FALCIPARUM VACCINES IN MICE

- introduce malaria vaccines  
Malaria vaccines have historically focused on the circumsporozoite protein and thrombospondin-related anonymous protein, but these antigens have demonstrated limited efficacy in clinical trials. The present invention identifies novel liver-stage antigens capable of inducing superior protection.

- motivate liver-stage malaria vaccines  
Liver-stage vaccines are critical for achieving sterile immunity, as they eliminate parasites before they enter the bloodstream. The invention provides a platform for targeting multiple liver-stage antigens to maximize protection.

- describe ex vivo IFNγ ELISpot assay  
The ex vivo IFNγ ELISpot assay was used to quantify antigen-specific T cell responses in splenocytes. Spots were counted using an automated reader and expressed as spot-forming units per million cells.

- explain limitations of ELISpot assay  
ELISpot measures only IFNγ production and does not capture polyfunctionality or cytotoxic potential. It is therefore insufficient alone to predict protective efficacy.

- introduce multi-parameter flow cytometry  
Multi-parameter flow cytometry was used to assess the frequency, phenotype, and functionality of antigen-specific CD8+ T cells, including expression of IFNγ, TNFα, IL-2, and CD107a.

- describe cytokines and markers assessed  
Cytokines assessed included IFNγ, TNFα, and IL-2. Markers included CD62L, CD127, CD44, and CD107a to identify memory and cytotoxic subsets.

- motivate assessment of antibody levels  
Antibody levels were assessed to determine whether humoral responses contributed to protection. LIPS assays revealed that antibody titers did not correlate with sterile immunity.

- describe difficulties in assessing functional immunogenicity  
Functional immunogenicity requires assessment of T cell-mediated killing of infected hepatocytes, which cannot be modeled in vitro. The use of chimeric parasites enabled direct evaluation.

- introduce transgenic P. berghei parasites  
Transgenic P. berghei parasites expressing P. falciparum antigens were generated by homologous recombination at the 230p locus under the control of the UIS4 promoter.

- describe generation of transgenic parasites  
Transgenic lines were generated using GIMO technology and selected with 5-fluorocytosine. Integration was confirmed by PCR and Southern blot.

- motivate use of P. berghei UIS4 promoter  
The UIS4 promoter ensures liver-stage-specific expression of the foreign antigen, mimicking natural infection and enabling accurate evaluation of vaccine efficacy.

- describe P. berghei 230p locus  
The 230p locus is a neutral genomic site in P. berghei that allows stable integration of foreign genes without affecting parasite fitness.

- summarize vaccine development strategy  
The strategy involved cloning P. falciparum antigens into viral vectors, immunizing mice, and challenging with chimeric sporozoites to assess protection.

- introduce results section  
Results demonstrated that PfLSA1 and PfLSAP2 conferred superior protection compared to all other antigens tested.

- describe ex vivo IFNγ ELISpot results  
ELISpot results showed the highest responses to PfLSA1 and PfUIS3, with 1,200 and 868 SFU/million cells, respectively.

- compare immunogenicity in Balb/c and C57BL/6 mice  
BALB/c mice exhibited more consistent responses than C57BL/6 mice, which showed greater variability in T cell frequencies.

- describe ICS results  
ICS confirmed that PfLSA1 and PfLSAP2 induced the highest frequencies of polyfunctional CD8+ T cells expressing IFNγ, TNFα, and CD107a.

- summarize cytokine profiles in Balb/c mice  
In BALB/c mice, PfLSA1 and PfLSAP2 induced the strongest IFNγ and TNFα responses, with minimal IL-2 production.

- summarize cytokine profiles in C57BL/6 mice  
In C57BL/6 mice, PfUIS3 and PfLSA3 induced the highest responses, but PfLSA1 and PfLSAP2 were poorly immunogenic.

- introduce antibody response assessment  
Antibody responses were assessed using LIPS and were detectable for all antigens except PfLSAP1.

- describe LIPS assay  
LIPS measured antibody binding to antigen-Renilla luciferase fusions. Fold-change over naïve controls was calculated for each antigen.

In C57BL/6 mice, antibody responses were generally low, with only PfFalstatin showing a fold-change of 1.5.

Fold-change was calculated as the mean luminescence of vaccinated mice divided by the mean of naïve controls plus two standard deviations.
