# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD

- The present invention relates to the field of gene therapy and genetic medicine, specifically to compositions and methods for the treatment of Duchenne muscular dystrophy through in vivo genome editing of satellite cells. The invention encompasses novel vector systems, promoter configurations, and delivery strategies that enable persistent correction of the dystrophin gene in muscle stem cells, thereby providing long-term restoration of dystrophin expression and functional muscle regeneration. This technology is particularly directed toward the use of adeno-associated viral vectors encoding CRISPR-Cas9 systems under the control of muscle-specific regulatory elements to achieve targeted, durable, and safe genomic correction within the endogenous satellite cell compartment of skeletal muscle tissue.

## INTRODUCTION

- Duchenne muscular dystrophy is a severe, X-linked recessive genetic disorder characterized by progressive degeneration of skeletal and cardiac muscle due to mutations in the dystrophin gene, most commonly large deletions that disrupt the reading frame and prevent production of a functional dystrophin protein. The absence of dystrophin compromises sarcolemmal integrity, leading to recurrent cycles of muscle fiber necrosis, inflammation, fibrosis, and eventual replacement of muscle with adipose and connective tissue. Although supportive therapies such as corticosteroids and exon-skipping oligonucleotides have modestly slowed disease progression, they do not address the underlying genetic defect and require lifelong administration. Current gene replacement strategies using viral vectors to deliver micro-dystrophin cDNAs are limited by transient expression, immune responses, and inability to target the self-renewing satellite cell population responsible for lifelong muscle maintenance. The persistent loss of episomal vector genomes during satellite cell division further undermines the durability of such interventions. Therefore, a therapeutic approach that enables permanent correction of the endogenous dystrophin locus within satellite cells represents a transformative opportunity to halt or reverse disease progression by establishing a renewable source of dystrophin-expressing myofibers.

## SUMMARY

- The invention provides a composition comprising a first nucleic acid vector encoding a site-specific nuclease and a second nucleic acid vector encoding at least one guide RNA, wherein both vectors are packaged within an adeno-associated virus serotype selected from AAV8 or AAV9, and wherein the nuclease is operably linked to a muscle-specific promoter selected from the group consisting of MHCK7, CK8e, or SPc5-12. The guide RNA is configured to target intronic sequences flanking exon 23 of the murine dystrophin gene or the homologous region in human DMD, thereby enabling excision of the mutated exon and restoration of the open reading frame. In a preferred embodiment, the nuclease is a Staphylococcus aureus Cas9 protein, codon-optimized for expression in mammalian cells and fused to a nuclear localization sequence. The vectors may be administered simultaneously or sequentially via intramuscular or systemic routes, and the composition may further include a pharmaceutically acceptable carrier, excipient, or stabilizing agent. In alternative embodiments, the system may be delivered as a single vector encoding both the nuclease and multiple guide RNAs, or as a dual-vector system wherein one vector encodes the nuclease and the other encodes a single guide RNA targeting a distal intronic site to facilitate alternative repair outcomes. The invention further encompasses kits containing the vectors, instructions for administration, and reagents for detection of gene editing. The method of treatment involves administering the composition to a subject diagnosed with Duchenne muscular dystrophy, resulting in the correction of the dystrophin gene in satellite cells, sustained expression of dystrophin protein following muscle injury, and long-term regeneration of dystrophin-positive myofibers through the contribution of edited stem cells.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

- The invention discloses a comprehensive system for the in vivo genome editing of satellite cells to achieve durable correction of the dystrophin gene in Duchenne muscular dystrophy. The approach leverages the unique biology of satellite cells as the primary reservoir for muscle regeneration and their capacity for self-renewal, enabling the establishment of a permanent, endogenous source of corrected myogenic progenitors. The disclosed compositions and methods overcome prior limitations in vector tropism, promoter specificity, and editing efficiency by combining optimized AAV serotypes with muscle-restricted transcriptional control elements and dual-guide RNA configurations that maximize exon excision while minimizing off-target effects. The invention further demonstrates that gene editing in satellite cells, even at low frequencies, can yield clinically meaningful levels of dystrophin restoration over time due to the proliferative and regenerative capacity of these cells.

### 2. Dystrophin Gene

- The dystrophin gene is one of the largest known human genes, spanning over 2.4 million base pairs and comprising 79 exons. It encodes a 427-kDa cytoskeletal protein critical for maintaining the structural integrity of muscle fibers during contraction. Mutations in this gene, particularly large deletions or duplications affecting exons 45 through 55, are responsible for approximately 70% of Duchenne muscular dystrophy cases. These deletions frequently disrupt the reading frame, leading to the introduction of a premature stop codon and the absence of functional dystrophin protein. The mdx mouse, a widely used model of DMD, carries a point mutation in exon 23 that creates a premature stop codon, resulting in a truncated, non-functional dystrophin protein and a phenotype characterized by muscle necrosis, inflammation, and progressive weakness. Correction of the reading frame by excision of exon 23 in mdx mice or homologous exons in humans restores the open reading frame and permits production of a partially functional, internally deleted dystrophin protein analogous to the milder Becker muscular dystrophy phenotype. The restoration of even low levels of dystrophin expression has been shown to significantly improve muscle histology, reduce fibrosis, and extend lifespan in animal models. The dystrophin gene contains multiple introns of varying sizes, many of which serve as ideal targets for CRISPR-mediated excision due to their length and conservation. The intronic regions flanking exon 23, particularly those in intron 22 and intron 23, contain highly conserved sequences suitable for guide RNA design. The wild-type dystrophin gene is expressed in skeletal, cardiac, and smooth muscle, as well as in the brain and retina, but the pathogenic consequences of its loss are most pronounced in skeletal muscle. The mutant dystrophin gene, in contrast, produces a non-functional or absent protein, leading to membrane instability, calcium influx, and activation of proteolytic pathways. Restoration of dystrophin expression, even in a truncated form, has been demonstrated to confer substantial functional benefits, including improved force generation, reduced serum creatine kinase levels, and enhanced resistance to eccentric contraction-induced damage. The goal of gene editing in DMD is therefore not to restore the full-length protein, but to reframe the coding sequence to permit expression of a functional, internally deleted isoform. This approach is particularly advantageous because it leverages the endogenous regulatory elements of the dystrophin gene, ensuring appropriate spatiotemporal expression, tissue-specific splicing, and post-translational modifications that are not achievable with cDNA-based gene replacement strategies.

### 3. CRISPR/Cas9-Based Gene Editing System

- The CRISPR/Cas9-based gene editing system employed in the invention utilizes a dual-vector configuration wherein one vector encodes the Cas9 nuclease and the other encodes two guide RNAs designed to flank the target exon. The Cas9 protein is derived from Staphylococcus aureus (SaCas9), which is smaller than the commonly used Streptococcus pyogenes Cas9 (SpCas9), thereby enabling efficient packaging into adeno-associated viral vectors with limited cargo capacity. SaCas9 is codon-optimized for expression in mammalian cells and fused to a bipartite nuclear localization sequence to ensure its transport into the nucleus following transduction. The guide RNAs are expressed from separate U6 promoters and are designed to target conserved intronic sequences approximately 100–300 base pairs upstream and downstream of exon 23 in the murine Dmd gene, or the homologous regions in human DMD. Upon simultaneous binding of both guide RNAs to their respective genomic targets, Cas9 introduces two double-strand breaks, enabling the excision of the intervening exon through non-homologous end joining. The resulting repair event rejoins intron 22 directly to intron 23, restoring the reading frame and permitting translation of a functional, internally deleted dystrophin protein. The system may also be configured to utilize a single guide RNA targeting a single intronic site to induce a frameshift mutation that bypasses the original mutation, or to employ a donor template to insert a precise correction via homology-directed repair. The Cas9 protein may be further modified to include mutations that inactivate its nuclease activity, rendering it a transcriptional activator or repressor when fused to effector domains such as VP64, KRAB, or p300. The guide RNA scaffold is engineered for maximal stability and Cas9 binding efficiency, and may include modifications such as 2′-O-methyl or phosphorothioate linkages to enhance resistance to nucleases. The PAM sequence recognized by SaCas9 is NNGRRT, which occurs frequently in the intronic regions flanking exon 23, enabling multiple potential target sites. The system may also be adapted to utilize alternative Cas proteins such as Cas12a or engineered variants with altered PAM specificities, including VQR, EQR, or VRER variants, to expand targeting range. The Cas9 protein may be expressed under the control of a constitutive promoter such as CMV, or under the control of a muscle-specific promoter such as MHCK7, CK8e, or SPc5-12, to restrict expression to myogenic cells and minimize off-target editing in non-muscle tissues. The use of muscle-specific promoters reduces the risk of immune recognition of Cas9 and limits potential genotoxicity in non-target organs. The guide RNAs may be expressed from a single transcript using a tRNA or ribozyme-based processing system, or from separate promoters to allow independent regulation. The system may further include a fluorescent reporter or selectable marker to facilitate tracking of transduced cells. The CRISPR/Cas9 system may be delivered as a single vector or as multiple vectors, depending on the size of the components and the packaging capacity of the chosen viral vector. The system may also be combined with small molecules or peptides that enhance homology-directed repair or inhibit non-homologous end joining to increase the frequency of precise edits. The system may be further optimized by incorporating intronic enhancers, splice acceptors, or polyadenylation signals to improve expression and stability of the transgene cassette. The system may be administered as a single dose or as a series of doses to achieve cumulative editing in a expanding pool of satellite cells. The system may be used in combination with immunosuppressive regimens to prevent immune clearance of transduced cells or Cas9-expressing myofibers. The system may be adapted for use in human subjects by replacing murine guide RNA sequences with those targeting the homologous human dystrophin locus, and by selecting AAV serotypes with proven tropism for human muscle and satellite cells.

### 4. Genetic Constructs

- The genetic constructs of the invention comprise nucleic acid sequences encoding the components of the CRISPR/Cas9 system, operably linked to transcriptional regulatory elements that ensure efficient expression in muscle and satellite cells. The constructs are typically delivered as recombinant adeno-associated viral vectors, with the transgene cassette flanked by inverted terminal repeats derived from AAV serotype 2. The first construct encodes the SaCas9 nuclease under the control of a muscle-specific promoter, such as MHCK7, CK8e, or SPc5-12, followed by a bovine growth hormone polyadenylation signal. The second construct encodes two guide RNA expression cassettes, each driven by a human U6 promoter, separated by a short linker sequence to prevent transcriptional interference. Each guide RNA is preceded by a 20-nucleotide target sequence complementary to the intronic region flanking exon 23, followed by a scaffold sequence that forms the hairpin structure required for Cas9 binding. The constructs may be designed as linear minichromosomes containing additional elements such as origins of replication, centromeres, or telomeres for stable episomal maintenance, although this is not required for the intended application. The constructs may also include introns to enhance mRNA stability and nuclear export, such as the synthetic intron from the human β-globin gene. The coding sequences for SaCas9 and the guide RNAs may be optimized for GC content, codon usage, and secondary structure to maximize expression in human or murine cells. The constructs may be packaged into AAV particles using standard methods involving co-transfection of producer cells with helper plasmids and packaging plasmids. The AAV capsid may be derived from serotypes AAV8 or AAV9, which have been shown to exhibit superior tropism for skeletal muscle and satellite cells. The constructs may further include a selectable marker such as GFP or mCherry under the control of a constitutive promoter to facilitate identification of transduced cells. The constructs may be formulated as a single vector encoding both SaCas9 and the two guide RNAs, or as two separate vectors to accommodate larger payloads. The constructs may be produced in large-scale bioreactors using suspension cell cultures and purified by iodixanol gradient centrifugation or affinity chromatography. The final product is formulated in a buffered saline solution containing albumin or polysorbate 80 to stabilize the viral particles. The constructs may be stored at −80°C for long-term preservation or lyophilized for room-temperature stability. The constructs may be administered in a single injection or in multiple doses to achieve cumulative editing in satellite cells over time. The constructs may be combined with agents that promote satellite cell proliferation, such as hepatocyte growth factor or follistatin, to enhance the pool of editable cells. The constructs may be co-administered with immunomodulatory agents to reduce the immune response to Cas9 or the viral capsid. The constructs may be tested for purity, potency, and sterility according to regulatory guidelines for gene therapy products. The constructs may be used in preclinical models of DMD, including mdx mice, golden retriever muscular dystrophy dogs, or humanized mouse models, to evaluate safety and efficacy prior to clinical translation.

### 8. Methods

- The invention provides a method for correcting a mutant dystrophin gene in a cell of a subject diagnosed with Duchenne muscular dystrophy. The method comprises the steps of: (a) obtaining a sample of muscle tissue or satellite cells from the subject; (b) determining the presence of a mutation in the dystrophin gene that is amenable to exon skipping, wherein the mutation is located within exons 45 through 55; (c) administering to the subject a composition comprising a first nucleic acid vector encoding a Cas9 nuclease operably linked to a muscle-specific promoter selected from the group consisting of MHCK7, CK8e, or SPc5-12, and a second nucleic acid vector encoding at least two guide RNAs targeting intronic sequences flanking exon 23 of the dystrophin gene, wherein the vectors are packaged within an adeno-associated virus serotype selected from AAV8 or AAV9; (d) allowing the vectors to transduce satellite cells and myofibers in vivo; (e) permitting the Cas9 nuclease to cleave the dystrophin gene at the target sites; (f) permitting non-homologous end joining to repair the double-strand breaks, resulting in excision of exon 23 and restoration of the open reading frame; (g) allowing the corrected dystrophin gene to be transcribed and translated into a functional, internally deleted dystrophin protein; and (h) monitoring the subject for sustained expression of dystrophin in skeletal muscle following muscle injury or regeneration.
