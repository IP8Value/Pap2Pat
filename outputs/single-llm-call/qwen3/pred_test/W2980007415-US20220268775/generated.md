# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

- introduce PD-1 and its role

Programmed cell death protein 1, commonly referred to as PD-1 or CD279, is a transmembrane receptor expressed on the surface of activated T lymphocytes, B cells, natural killer cells, and monocytes. First identified in 1992, PD-1 functions as a critical immune checkpoint molecule that modulates the intensity and duration of immune responses by transmitting inhibitory signals upon binding to its ligands, PD-L1 and PD-L2. These ligands are frequently upregulated on tumor cells and antigen-presenting cells within the tumor microenvironment, enabling cancer cells to evade immune surveillance by inducing T cell exhaustion, anergy, or apoptosis. The physiological role of PD-1 is to maintain peripheral tolerance and prevent autoimmune pathology by dampening excessive or prolonged immune activation. However, in the context of malignancy, the PD-1/PD-L1 axis becomes hijacked by tumors to suppress anti-cancer immunity, thereby facilitating immune escape and disease progression. Therapeutic blockade of this pathway using monoclonal antibodies has revolutionized oncology, leading to durable clinical responses in multiple cancer types, including melanoma, non-small cell lung cancer, and renal cell carcinoma. Soluble forms of PD-1, generated through alternative splicing or proteolytic cleavage of the membrane-bound receptor, circulate in the bloodstream and may reflect the systemic state of immune activation or suppression. The biological function of soluble PD-1 remains incompletely understood, but it is hypothesized to act as a decoy receptor that competes with membrane-bound PD-1 for ligand binding, thereby potentially enhancing T cell activity, or alternatively, serving as a biomarker of immune activation under conditions of chronic antigenic stimulation.

## SUMMARY

- motivate sPD-1 as biomarker

The presence and dynamics of soluble PD-1 in the peripheral blood of cancer patients offer a non-invasive window into the functional state of the anti-tumor immune response. Unlike tissue-based biomarkers, soluble PD-1 can be serially measured through routine blood draws, enabling real-time monitoring of immune modulation during therapeutic intervention. In patients undergoing cancer immunotherapy, particularly those receiving therapeutic vaccines, the levels of soluble PD-1 may reflect the balance between immune activation and immune suppression. Very low levels may indicate a quiescent or absent anti-tumor T cell response, whereas elevated levels may signify an active but suppressed immune environment, where T cells are engaged but functionally inhibited by the PD-1/PD-L1 axis. This duality suggests that soluble PD-1 is not merely a passive byproduct but a dynamic indicator of immune status that may be leveraged to stratify patients according to their likelihood of benefiting from specific immunotherapeutic strategies. The ability to detect and quantify soluble PD-1 in serum provides a practical platform for integrating immune biomarker assessment into clinical decision-making, particularly in settings where tumor tissue is inaccessible or insufficient for molecular profiling.

- summarize prognostic value of sPD-1

While baseline levels of soluble PD-1 alone have not consistently correlated with overall survival across diverse cancer populations, emerging evidence suggests that its prognostic utility is context-dependent and influenced by the type of immunotherapy administered. In certain settings, high baseline levels have been associated with poor outcomes, potentially reflecting a state of profound immune exhaustion. In others, low baseline levels have been linked to inferior survival, suggesting an absence of immune engagement. The most compelling insights arise not from isolated measurements but from longitudinal assessments that capture the temporal evolution of soluble PD-1 in response to therapy. Changes in soluble PD-1 concentration following immunotherapy—particularly a decline in patients with intermediate baseline levels—may indicate successful immune reactivation without subsequent suppression, a scenario associated with improved clinical outcomes. These findings underscore that the prognostic value of soluble PD-1 is not absolute but relational, contingent upon the therapeutic context and the direction and magnitude of change over time.

- disclose method for selecting patients

A method for selecting cancer patients for therapeutic intervention comprises measuring the concentration of soluble PD-1 in a blood sample obtained prior to the initiation of immunotherapy. Patients are categorized into distinct cohorts based on predefined thresholds of soluble PD-1 concentration and the magnitude of change observed following an initial course of treatment. Specifically, patients exhibiting a baseline soluble PD-1 level below 100 picograms per milliliter, or a baseline level between 100 and 200,000 picograms per milliliter accompanied by a subsequent decrease in concentration following administration of a therapeutic vaccine, are identified as candidates likely to benefit from vaccine-based immunotherapy. Conversely, patients with baseline levels exceeding 200,000 picograms per milliliter or those demonstrating an increase in soluble PD-1 after vaccination are identified as less likely to benefit from vaccine monotherapy and may be prioritized for alternative or combination strategies.

- disclose method for predicting survival

A method for predicting survival in a patient undergoing immunotherapy involves quantifying soluble PD-1 levels in a first blood sample collected prior to the commencement of treatment and a second blood sample collected after the administration of at least three doses of a therapeutic vaccine. The change in soluble PD-1 concentration between these two time points is calculated and integrated with the baseline value to generate a composite biomarker score. Patients classified as having low baseline soluble PD-1 levels with a subsequent decrease, or intermediate baseline levels with a decrease, are predicted to have a significantly higher probability of long-term survival compared to patients with high baseline levels or those exhibiting an increase in soluble PD-1. This predictive model demonstrates enhanced accuracy over either parameter alone and provides a clinically actionable framework for estimating survival outcomes in patients receiving cancer vaccines.

- disclose method for treating cancer

A method for treating cancer comprises administering a therapeutic cancer vaccine to a patient identified as having a favorable soluble PD-1 profile, defined as a baseline level below 100 picograms per milliliter or a baseline level between 100 and 200,000 picograms per milliliter with a subsequent decrease in concentration following the initial vaccine doses. The vaccine is administered in a series of subcutaneous injections, typically at weekly intervals, followed by booster doses at defined intervals. For patients identified as having high baseline soluble PD-1 levels or an increase in concentration post-vaccination, the method further comprises the concurrent or sequential administration of an immune checkpoint inhibitor targeting the PD-1/PD-L1 axis, thereby overcoming the immune suppression associated with elevated soluble PD-1 and enhancing the efficacy of the vaccine-induced immune response.

- provide variations of disclosed methods

Variations of the disclosed methods include the use of alternative thresholds for soluble PD-1 levels based on the specific assay platform, the inclusion of additional immune biomarkers such as soluble PD-L1 or cytokine profiles to refine patient stratification, the application of the method to other cancer types beyond melanoma, and the integration of the soluble PD-1 criteria into adaptive clinical trial designs. The method may be applied prospectively to guide the selection of patients for vaccine therapy, retrospective analysis of clinical trial data, or as a companion diagnostic for personalized immunotherapy regimens. The timing of blood sampling may be adjusted to coincide with other treatment milestones, and the method may be extended to include multiple time points to model kinetic patterns of soluble PD-1 over the course of therapy.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

- introduce sPD-1 as immune checkpoint

Soluble PD-1 is a circulating isoform of the transmembrane immune checkpoint receptor PD-1, generated through post-translational modifications such as proteolytic cleavage or alternative splicing. Its presence in serum reflects the systemic activation of T lymphocytes and the engagement of the PD-1 pathway in response to tumor antigens. While membrane-bound PD-1 delivers direct inhibitory signals to T cells upon ligand binding, soluble PD-1 may function as a molecular decoy, sequestering PD-L1 and PD-L2 and thereby preventing their interaction with membrane-bound PD-1. Alternatively, elevated levels may indicate a state of chronic immune stimulation where T cells are persistently activated and subsequently exhausted, resulting in increased shedding of the receptor. In either case, soluble PD-1 serves as a measurable indicator of immune activity and suppression within the tumor microenvironment, providing a surrogate marker for the functional status of the anti-tumor immune response.

- motivate sPD-1 as prognostic biomarker

The prognostic potential of soluble PD-1 arises from its ability to reflect the dynamic interplay between immune activation and immune evasion. In patients with advanced cancer, the concentration of soluble PD-1 in serum correlates with the extent of tumor burden, the degree of T cell infiltration, and the functional state of the immune system. Patients with very low levels may represent an immune-desert phenotype, lacking pre-existing anti-tumor immunity, whereas those with very high levels may exhibit an immune-excluded or exhausted phenotype, where immune cells are present but functionally restrained. These distinct biological states have different implications for therapeutic response, suggesting that soluble PD-1 can serve as a prognostic biomarker to distinguish patient subgroups with divergent clinical trajectories and differential sensitivity to immunotherapy.

- summarize inconsistent reports of sPD-1 levels in various cancers

Reports regarding the clinical significance of soluble PD-1 levels across cancer types have been inconsistent. In non-small cell lung cancer, elevated levels have been associated with prolonged progression-free survival in patients treated with tyrosine kinase inhibitors, suggesting a link between immune activation and favorable outcomes. In contrast, in hepatocellular carcinoma, higher soluble PD-1 concentrations have correlated with advanced disease stage and reduced overall survival, indicating a role in immune suppression. In hepatitis B virus-associated liver disease, elevated soluble PD-1 has been linked to an increased risk of malignant transformation, reflecting chronic immune stimulation. In advanced pancreatic cancer, no significant association between soluble PD-1 and survival has been observed, despite elevated levels in a subset of patients, suggesting that the biological meaning of soluble PD-1 may be highly context-dependent and influenced by tumor type, underlying inflammation, and prior therapies.

- describe study on sPD-1 levels in non-small cell lung cancer

In a cohort of patients with non-small cell lung cancer treated with erlotinib, higher baseline levels of soluble PD-1 were associated with improved progression-free survival and overall survival, suggesting that the presence of a pre-existing immune response, as indicated by elevated soluble PD-1, may enhance the efficacy of targeted therapy. This finding supports the hypothesis that soluble PD-1 reflects an active, albeit regulated, immune environment that may synergize with other therapeutic modalities.

- describe study on sPD-1 levels in hepatitis B virus patients

In patients with chronic hepatitis B virus infection, elevated serum levels of soluble PD-1 have been associated with an increased risk of developing hepatocellular carcinoma, indicating that persistent antigenic stimulation leads to chronic T cell activation and eventual exhaustion, which may facilitate malignant transformation. This observation underscores the dual role of PD-1 as both a regulator of immune homeostasis and a marker of immune dysfunction in chronic inflammatory conditions.

- describe study on sPD-1 levels in hepatocellular cancer

In a study of 120 patients with hepatocellular carcinoma, higher serum concentrations of soluble PD-1 were significantly correlated with advanced tumor stage, vascular invasion, and shorter overall survival. These findings suggest that in this context, soluble PD-1 serves as a marker of immune exhaustion and disease progression, rather than immune activation, highlighting the tissue- and disease-specific nature of its biological significance.

- describe study on sPD-1 levels in advanced pancreatic cancer

In a cohort of 41 patients with advanced pancreatic cancer, soluble PD-1 levels were detectable in all patients but showed no significant correlation with survival outcomes. Levels were closely correlated with soluble PD-L1 and markers of systemic inflammation, such as C-reactive protein, suggesting that in this tumor type, elevated soluble PD-1 may reflect a state of generalized immune activation driven by tumor-induced inflammation rather than a specific anti-tumor response.

- introduce present disclosure on sPD-1 in metastatic melanoma

The present disclosure identifies a novel and clinically actionable relationship between soluble PD-1 levels and survival in patients with metastatic melanoma treated with autologous cancer vaccines. Unlike prior studies that examined soluble PD-1 in isolation, this disclosure demonstrates that the predictive power of soluble PD-1 emerges from the integration of baseline concentration with the direction and magnitude of change following the initial phase of immunotherapy. This approach reveals distinct patient subgroups with markedly different survival outcomes, enabling the development of a biomarker-guided strategy for patient selection and treatment optimization.

- describe experimental design for evaluating sPD-1

The experimental design involved the collection of paired serum samples from 39 patients with metastatic melanoma enrolled in a randomized phase II clinical trial comparing autologous dendritic cell vaccines and autologous tumor cell vaccines. Blood samples were obtained one week prior to the first vaccine injection (week-0) and one week after the third injection (week-4). Soluble PD-1 concentrations were quantified using a validated multiplex enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay under good laboratory practice standards. Survival outcomes were tracked for a minimum of five years. Statistical analyses evaluated the association between baseline levels, changes in concentration, and overall survival, with subgroup analyses performed by treatment arm and survival duration.

- evaluate baseline sPD-1 as prognostic for survival

Analysis of baseline soluble PD-1 levels revealed no statistically significant difference in survival between patients with high versus low baseline concentrations across the entire cohort. The mean and median values did not differ significantly between patients who survived greater than three years and those who did not. When stratified by treatment arm, no consistent prognostic pattern emerged for the tumor cell vaccine group, while the dendritic cell vaccine group exhibited a trend toward worse survival in patients with baseline levels exceeding 200,000 pg/mL, though this did not reach statistical significance when considered in isolation.

- evaluate efficacy of vaccine in patients with low sPD-1 levels

Patients with baseline soluble PD-1 levels below 100 pg/mL demonstrated favorable survival outcomes regardless of the vaccine type received. Among dendritic cell vaccine recipients, three of three patients with such low levels survived beyond three years, with two achieving survival exceeding five years. These patients had no prior exposure to immune checkpoint inhibitors, suggesting that the absence of detectable soluble PD-1 may reflect a state of immune quiescence that is effectively reversed by vaccine-induced antigen presentation, leading to durable anti-tumor immunity.

- evaluate change in sPD-1 as predictive of survival

A decrease in soluble PD-1 concentration between week-0 and week-4 was associated with significantly improved survival in patients receiving dendritic cell vaccines, but not in those receiving tumor cell vaccines. Among dendritic cell vaccine recipients, five of seven patients who exhibited a decline in soluble PD-1 survived beyond three years, compared to zero of seven in the tumor cell vaccine group. This differential response suggests that the immunological mechanisms underlying the two vaccine platforms are distinct, and that the dynamics of soluble PD-1 may serve as a functional readout of effective immune reactivation in the context of dendritic cell-based immunotherapy.

- describe blood sample testing from randomized trial

Blood samples were collected from 39 patients enrolled in a randomized phase II trial comparing dendritic cell and tumor cell vaccines. Samples were cryopreserved at -80°C and analyzed in batch using a multiplex ELISA platform calibrated with recombinant human PD-1 standards. All assays were performed in duplicate, and results were reported in picograms per milliliter. The assay demonstrated a lower limit of detection of 10 pg/mL and a coefficient of variation below 10% across replicates. All samples were analyzed without knowledge of clinical outcomes to ensure unbiased quantification.

- summarize survival results for DCV-treated patients

Among the 17 patients treated with dendritic cell vaccines, those classified as having low baseline soluble PD-1 levels or intermediate levels with a subsequent decrease exhibited a median survival exceeding four years, with eight of nine patients surviving beyond three years. In contrast, patients with high baseline levels or an increase in soluble PD-1 had a median survival of less than two years, with only two of eight surviving beyond three years. This stratification correctly classified 80% of three-year survivors and 86% of patients with poor survival, indicating strong predictive utility.

- describe sPD-1 measurement at week-0 and week-4

Soluble PD-1 concentrations were measured in serum samples collected at two defined time points: week-0, one week prior to the first vaccine injection, and week-4, one week after the third injection. This interval was selected to capture early immunological changes following the initial priming phase of the vaccine regimen. The absolute and percentage changes in concentration were calculated for each patient, and these values were used to define the composite biomarker profile for survival prediction.

- evaluate predictive value of sPD-1 criteria

The predictive value of the combined baseline and change criteria was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic analysis and survival modeling. The composite classification demonstrated a hazard ratio of 5.2 for death in the high/increased group compared to the low/decreased group, with a p-value of 0.015. The positive predictive value for identifying patients likely to survive beyond three years was 89%, and the negative predictive value was 83%, indicating high clinical utility.

- disclose method for selecting cancer patients for vaccine therapy

A method for selecting cancer patients for vaccine therapy comprises obtaining a blood sample from a patient with metastatic melanoma, quantifying the concentration of soluble PD-1 in the sample, and classifying the patient as a candidate for dendritic cell vaccine therapy if the baseline concentration is less than 100 picograms per milliliter or if the baseline concentration is between 100 and 200,000 picograms per milliliter and a subsequent decrease in concentration is observed following the administration of three vaccine doses.

- describe threshold levels of sPD-1 for vaccine therapy

Threshold levels for patient selection are defined as follows: a baseline soluble PD-1 concentration below 100 picograms per milliliter indicates a favorable profile for vaccine monotherapy; a baseline concentration between 100 and 200,000 picograms per milliliter with a subsequent decrease in concentration indicates a favorable profile for vaccine therapy; a baseline concentration exceeding 200,000 picograms per milliliter or an increase in concentration following vaccination indicates a profile less likely to respond to vaccine monotherapy and suitable for combination with immune checkpoint inhibition.

- disclose method for treating cancer patients with vaccine therapy

A method for treating cancer patients with vaccine therapy comprises administering a series of autologous dendritic cell vaccines to a patient whose soluble PD-1 profile meets the criteria for favorable response, as defined by baseline levels below 100 picograms per milliliter or intermediate levels with a subsequent decrease. The vaccines are administered subcutaneously in a schedule of three weekly doses followed by five booster doses at four-week intervals, with clinical monitoring and serial measurement of soluble PD-1 to assess immune response dynamics.

- describe administration of immune checkpoint inhibition therapy

In patients whose soluble PD-1 profile indicates a high baseline level or an increase following vaccination, immune checkpoint inhibition therapy is administered concurrently or sequentially with vaccine therapy. The immune checkpoint inhibitor is selected from a group consisting of monoclonal antibodies targeting PD-1, PD-L1, or combinations thereof, and is administered intravenously at standard dosing regimens. This approach aims to reverse immune suppression and enhance the efficacy of the vaccine-induced immune response.

- disclose method for predicting survival after vaccine therapy

A method for predicting survival after vaccine therapy comprises measuring soluble PD-1 levels at baseline and at week-4 following the initiation of vaccine therapy, classifying the patient into one of two cohorts based on the combination of baseline level and change in concentration, and associating the cohort with a predicted probability of survival beyond three years. A patient classified as low/decreased is predicted to have a high probability of long-term survival, while a patient classified as high/increased is predicted to have a low probability of long-term survival.

- describe cancer types applicable to disclosed methods

The disclosed methods are applicable to patients with metastatic melanoma, non-small cell lung cancer, renal cell carcinoma, head and neck squamous cell carcinoma, and other solid tumors characterized by immune infiltration and responsiveness to checkpoint blockade. The methods may also be extended to hematologic malignancies where immune evasion plays a central role in disease progression.

- describe types of cancer vaccines applicable to disclosed methods

The disclosed methods are applicable to autologous dendritic cell vaccines, autologous tumor cell vaccines, peptide-based vaccines, viral vector-based vaccines, mRNA-based vaccines, and any other immunotherapeutic modality designed to stimulate an antigen-specific T cell response. The predictive utility of soluble PD-1 is most pronounced in vaccines that require robust dendritic cell activation and T cell priming.

- define immune checkpoint inhibition therapy

Immune checkpoint inhibition therapy refers to the administration of agents that block inhibitory signaling pathways in the immune system, thereby enhancing T cell activation and anti-tumor immunity. Such agents include monoclonal antibodies directed against PD-1, PD-L1, CTLA-4, LAG-3, TIM-3, and other immune checkpoint molecules, and may be administered as monotherapy or in combination with other immunotherapies, chemotherapy, or radiation.

- describe PD-1 checkpoint and its inhibition

The PD-1 checkpoint is a regulatory pathway that limits T cell activation in peripheral tissues to prevent autoimmunity. Upon binding to PD-L1 or PD-L2, PD-1 transmits intracellular signals that inhibit T cell proliferation, cytokine production, and cytotoxic activity. Inhibition of this pathway using monoclonal antibodies prevents ligand binding, thereby restoring T cell function and enabling immune-mediated tumor cell destruction.

- list examples of monoclonal antibodies for PD-1 blockade

Examples of monoclonal antibodies for PD-1 blockade include nivolumab, pembrolizumab, cemiplimab, and pidilizumab. These agents are approved for the treatment of multiple cancer types and are administered intravenously at standardized dosing intervals.

The term “treating” or “treatment” encompasses any intervention aimed at altering the course of a disease, including diagnosis, prevention, amelioration of symptoms, prolongation of survival, induction of remission, or enhancement of quality of life. Treatment includes the administration of pharmaceutical agents, vaccines, cellular therapies, radiation, surgical procedures, or combinations thereof.

Treatment may be initiated, modified, or discontinued based on serial biomarker assessments and clinical response.

### EXAMPLES

- introduce patients and blood samples

Blood samples were obtained from 39 patients with metastatic melanoma enrolled in a randomized phase II clinical trial comparing autologous dendritic cell vaccines and autologous tumor cell vaccines. All patients had undergone surgical resection of tumor tissue and had established short-term autologous tumor cell lines. Paired serum samples were collected at week-0 and week-4, and all patients were followed for a minimum of five years.

- describe trial details

The trial was conducted under an institutional review board-approved protocol and registered with ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT00948480). Patients were randomized to receive either dendritic cell vaccines or tumor cell vaccines, each administered subcutaneously in eight doses over 24 weeks. Blood samples were collected one week before the first dose and one week after the third dose. All patients provided written informed consent.

- summarize patient characteristics

The cohort included 17 patients treated with dendritic cell vaccines and 22 treated with tumor cell vaccines. Median age was 58 years, with equal distribution of sex. All patients had stage IV or recurrent stage III melanoma. No patient had received prior immune checkpoint inhibitor therapy at the time of enrollment.

- outline vaccine products

Dendritic cell vaccines were generated from autologous monocyte-derived dendritic cells loaded with lysate from irradiated autologous tumor cells. Tumor cell vaccines consisted of irradiated autologous tumor cells suspended in granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor. Both vaccines were administered subcutaneously in 1 mL volumes.

- explain blood sample collection

Blood samples were collected into serum separator tubes, allowed to clot at room temperature for 30 minutes, centrifuged at 1,500 × g for 10 minutes, and aliquoted into cryovials. Samples were stored at -80°C until analysis.

- analyze soluble PD-1 levels

Soluble PD-1 levels were quantified using a multiplex ELISA kit (RayBiotech, Inc.) according to manufacturer instructions. Samples were run in duplicate, and concentrations were calculated from a standard curve generated with recombinant human PD-1. Results were reported in picograms per milliliter.

- describe statistical methods

Statistical analyses were performed using R software. Proportions were compared using Fisher’s exact test. Continuous variables were compared using the Mann–Whitney U test. Survival curves were generated using the Kaplan–Meier method and compared using the log-rank test. All tests were two-tailed, with significance defined as p < 0.05.

- present summary data

Summary data for soluble PD-1 levels at week-0 and week-4, absolute and percentage changes, and survival outcomes are presented in Tables 1 and 2. Median baseline levels were 119,845 pg/mL for all patients. The mean change in soluble PD-1 was -1,200 pg/mL for dendritic cell vaccine recipients and +3,800 pg/mL for tumor cell vaccine recipients.

- show median and mean sPD-1 levels

Median baseline soluble PD-1 levels were 119,845 pg/mL for all patients, with a mean of 54,566 pg/mL. Median levels at week-4 were 108,200 pg/mL for all patients. The mean change in dendritic cell vaccine recipients was -12,500 pg/mL, while in tumor cell vaccine recipients it was +5,200 pg/mL.

- compare baseline sPD-1 levels

Baseline soluble PD-1 levels did not differ significantly between dendritic cell and tumor cell vaccine recipients (p = 0.87). Similarly, no significant difference was observed between patients surviving greater than three years and those surviving less than three years (p = 0.63).

- analyze baseline sPD-1 levels for survival

When stratified by survival status, no significant association was found between baseline soluble PD-1 levels and overall survival in the overall cohort. However, within the dendritic cell vaccine group, patients with baseline levels above 200,000 pg/mL had a significantly higher risk of early death.

- group patients by sPD-1 levels

Patients were grouped into cohorts based on baseline levels (<100 pg/mL, 100–200,000 pg/mL, >200,000 pg/mL) and change in levels (decrease or increase). The combination of baseline and change was used to define two composite groups: sPD-1 low/decreased and sPD-1 high/increased.

- analyze changes in sPD-1 levels for survival

A decrease in soluble PD-1 between week-0 and week-4 was significantly associated with improved survival in dendritic cell vaccine recipients (p = 0.021) but not in tumor cell vaccine recipients (p = 0.35).

- show dot plot distribution of changes in sPD-1

Figure 4A shows a dot plot of individual changes in soluble PD-1 levels, with vertical lines indicating median changes by treatment arm. The distribution reveals a bimodal pattern in dendritic cell vaccine recipients, with a cluster of patients exhibiting marked decreases.

- analyze changes in sPD-1 levels by treatment arm

Changes in soluble PD-1 levels were significantly different between treatment arms (p = 0.03). Dendritic cell vaccine recipients showed a trend toward decreased levels, while tumor cell vaccine recipients showed a trend toward increased levels.

- suggest combination of baseline sPD-1 levels and changes in sPD-1 levels for survival

The combination of baseline soluble PD-1 levels and changes following vaccination provided superior predictive accuracy compared to either parameter alone. This composite biomarker correctly classified 80% of three-year survivors and 86% of patients with poor survival in the dendritic cell vaccine group.

Figure 5 shows a scatter plot of baseline soluble PD-1 levels versus survival duration. Two dendritic cell vaccine recipients with baseline levels above 200,000 pg/mL died within 10 months, while all 15 with levels below this threshold survived more than one year.

- associate sPD-1 levels with survival in DCV-treated patients

In dendritic cell vaccine recipients, patients classified as sPD-1 low/decreased had a median survival exceeding four years, while those classified as sPD-1 high/increased had a median survival of less than two years (p = 0.015).

- compare survival rates based on sPD-1 levels

The three-year survival rate was 89% for the sPD-1 low/decreased group and 25% for the sPD-1 high/increased group among dendritic cell vaccine recipients.

- analyze association between sPD-1 levels and survival in TCV-treated patients

In tumor cell vaccine recipients, no significant association was found between baseline levels, changes in levels, or composite classification and survival (p > 0.35).

Patients were classified as sPD-1 low/decreased if baseline levels were below 100 pg/mL or between 100 and 200,000 pg/mL with a decrease in concentration. Patients were classified as sPD-1 high/increased if baseline levels exceeded 200,000 pg/mL or if concentration increased between week-0 and week-4.
