# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

### 1. Field of the Invention

The present invention relates to biological sensors and wireless sensor systems for detecting antibiotic susceptibility in bacterial populations. Specifically, it concerns a wirelessly magnetically coupled LC sensor platform capable of monitoring real-time changes in the complex permittivity of bacterial cultures to determine antimicrobial susceptibility within minutes following inoculation. The system is designed for integration into standard microplate formats and operates without requiring labeled reagents, fluorescent tags, or species-specific surface modifications. It enables rapid, high-throughput phenotypic antimicrobial susceptibility testing directly from diluted clinical or laboratory cultures, circumventing the need for prolonged incubation periods or complex instrumentation typically associated with conventional diagnostic methods.

### 2. Description of the Related Art

Drug-resistant infections represent a growing global health crisis, with millions of cases occurring annually and hundreds of thousands of deaths attributed to delayed or inappropriate antibiotic therapy. In intensive care units, the prevalence of antimicrobial resistance exceeds thirty percent in high-income nations and is significantly higher in resource-limited settings. The standard of care often involves empirical administration of broad-spectrum antibiotics while awaiting culture-based susceptibility results, which typically require 12 to 48 hours to yield actionable data. This delay compromises patient outcomes, particularly in sepsis, where a mere three-hour extension in antibiotic administration has been shown to reduce survival rates by more than eighty percent. Traditional phenotypic methods such as broth microdilution, agar diffusion, and gradient strip assays remain the gold standard for antimicrobial susceptibility testing but are inherently slow, labor-intensive, and unsuitable for point-of-care or high-throughput applications. Genotypic approaches, while faster, rely on pre-existing knowledge of resistance genes and are incapable of detecting novel or uncharacterized resistance mechanisms. Emerging technologies including optical imaging, pH sensing, bioluminescent ATP assays, magnetic particle detection, and electrochemical biosensors have demonstrated promise in accelerating detection but often require extensive sample preparation, specialized coatings, complex data processing, or expensive instrumentation that limits their clinical utility. Many of these systems are also sensitive to interference from host proteins, cellular debris, or complex matrices, rendering them unsuitable for direct analysis of minimally processed clinical specimens. Furthermore, single-cell detection methods struggle with polymicrobial infections, where mixed populations obscure susceptibility profiles. There remains a critical unmet need for a rapid, scalable, label-free, and robust phenotypic antimicrobial susceptibility testing platform that operates without species-specific reagents, requires minimal sample handling, and delivers clinically actionable results within thirty minutes of inoculation.

## BRIEF SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention introduces a wirelessly magnetically coupled LC sensor system designed for rapid phenotypic antimicrobial susceptibility testing. The system exploits the capacitive properties of bacterial cells and their metabolic byproducts to detect changes in the complex permittivity of a culture medium in real time. A flexible, etched LC sensor, comprising a planar interdigitated capacitor and a multi-turn coil, is inserted into individual wells of a standard 96-well plate. The sensor operates passively, drawing no internal power, and communicates wirelessly with an external impedance analyzer via magnetic coupling across the well plate substrate. As bacterial growth proceeds, the accumulation of cellular material and associated dielectric changes near the sensor surface alters the resonant frequency and impedance characteristics of the LC circuit. These changes are quantified through electromagnetic coupling analysis and converted into a time-dependent permittivity profile. The slope of the initial permittivity increase over a thirty-minute window serves as a quantitative susceptibility score, enabling discrimination between antibiotic-sensitive and antibiotic-resistant strains. The system requires no prior knowledge of bacterial species, no surface functionalization, and no sample enrichment, making it broadly applicable across Gram-positive and Gram-negative pathogens. It is compatible with standard laboratory infrastructure and can be readily scaled for high-throughput clinical diagnostics.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

The invention comprises a complete system for monitoring bacterial growth and determining antimicrobial susceptibility through wireless electromagnetic sensing. The sensor is fabricated on a thin polyimide flexible circuit substrate using conventional photolithographic techniques, incorporating a spiral inductor coil and an interdigitated capacitor array. The interdigitated capacitor consists of multiple conductive fingers arranged in alternating polarity, with precise dimensions of finger width, gap spacing, and total number of digits optimized to maximize sensitivity to dielectric changes in the surrounding medium. The sensor is coated with a uniform layer of oil-based polyurethane to prevent direct biofouling while permitting the passage of dielectric signals from the culture. The sensor is inserted vertically into the wall of a standard 96-well plate, ensuring that the sensing region remains in close proximity to the liquid interface without interference from sedimentation. A separate receiver coil, connected to an impedance analyzer, is positioned beneath the plate and wirelessly couples with the sensor coil through the polystyrene bottom of the well, which has been confirmed to be magnetically transparent. The system operates by applying a swept frequency signal from one megahertz to twelve megahertz, measuring the absolute impedance and phase response of the coupled circuit. The background impedance of the system is subtracted using built-in calibration routines, leaving only the contribution from the sensor and its surrounding medium. The complex permittivity of the bacterial culture is derived from the measured resonant frequency and zero-reactance frequency using a mathematical model that incorporates the physical parameters of the interdigitated capacitor, the inductance of the coil, and the known permittivity of the substrate. The resulting permittivity values are plotted over time, and the initial linear slope, calculated over the first thirty minutes following inoculation, is used as the primary metric for antimicrobial susceptibility. This slope is normalized against control wells containing no antibiotic and against sterile medium to account for baseline drift and nonspecific adsorption effects.

### Example

The bacterial culture medium used in this system is a low-salt lysogeny broth formulation containing 0.5 grams per liter sodium chloride, 10 grams per liter tryptone, and 5 grams per liter yeast extract, selected for its high electrical resistivity to enhance signal-to-noise ratio. Overnight bacterial cultures are diluted to an initial optical density at 600 nanometers of 0.001 and aliquoted into wells containing the sensor inserts. Antibiotics are added at predetermined concentrations at the time of inoculation. The 96-well plate is incubated at 30 degrees Celsius without agitation to minimize turbulence-induced signal artifacts. The impedance analyzer performs a 1600-point frequency sweep every five minutes over a one-megahertz bandwidth centered on the initial resonant frequency. The raw impedance data are processed using a custom MATLAB script that identifies the resonant frequency and zero-reactance frequency for each time point. These values are substituted into a derived equation that calculates the complex permittivity of the culture based on the sensor’s geometric constants, including the number of digits, digit width, gap spacing, and coil inductance. The equation incorporates the permittivity of free space, the permittivity of the polyimide substrate, and the zero-inductance frequency of the system, allowing for precise quantification of dielectric changes attributable to bacterial growth. The resulting permittivity-time curve exhibits a characteristic profile: an initial transient rise due to nonspecific protein adsorption, followed by a sustained linear increase corresponding to exponential bacterial growth, and eventual saturation as the sensor surface becomes occupied by cells and extracellular material. The slope of the linear phase, measured between 15 and 30 minutes post-inoculation, provides a robust and reproducible indicator of growth rate. Experiments with Escherichia coli MG1655, Staphylococcus aureus ALC2085, and Pseudomonas aeruginosa PAO1 demonstrate consistent detection of growth within thirty minutes, with distinct slopes observed between antibiotic-sensitive and resistant strains. When exposed to ampicillin, the sensitive strain exhibits a near-zero slope, while the resistant strain maintains a slope comparable to the untreated control. Similar results are obtained with ofloxacin, vancomycin, ciprofloxacin, and tobramycin across all tested species. The system’s sensitivity is validated against traditional microdilution minimum inhibitory concentration assays, with correlation coefficients exceeding 0.98. The sensor functions reliably in the presence of up to two percent fetal bovine serum, indicating compatibility with diluted clinical samples such as blood cultures or urine. Coating thickness and material composition can be optimized to extend functionality to higher serum concentrations, with saturation thickness modeled using empirical equations dependent on digit geometry. The system’s design permits miniaturization through photolithographic scaling, integration with microfluidic channels, and adaptation to automated plate-handling systems for high-throughput deployment. A reference library of slope profiles for known strains and antibiotics enables automated identification of minimum inhibitory concentrations without requiring prior species identification. Compared to existing automated phenotypic systems, this method achieves comparable accuracy in one-tenth the time, with no need for optical imaging, fluorescent labeling, or mass spectrometry. Future iterations will incorporate machine learning algorithms for strain classification and real-time MIC prediction, further enhancing clinical utility.
