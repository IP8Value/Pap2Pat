# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNICAL FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates to genetically engineered microorganisms, specifically eukaryotic host cells, that have been systematically modified to enable the efficient biosynthesis and accumulation of L-ornithine and its downstream polyamine derivatives. These modified organisms are designed for industrial-scale production of L-ornithine, putrescine, spermidine, spermine, and agmatine through optimized metabolic pathways that overcome native regulatory constraints, subcellular compartmentalization barriers, and carbon flux limitations inherent in wild-type strains. The invention further encompasses the use of such engineered cells as microbial cell factories for the sustainable and cost-effective synthesis of amino acids and nitrogen-rich metabolites from renewable carbon sources.

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

L-Ornithine is a non-proteinogenic amino acid that serves as a central metabolic intermediate in the urea cycle and as a precursor for the biosynthesis of polyamines, including putrescine, spermidine, and spermine—molecules essential for cell proliferation, stress response, and nucleic acid stabilization. Industrially, L-ornithine is of significant interest as a precursor for the production of nutraceuticals, pharmaceuticals, and bio-based polymers. Current methods for its production rely primarily on chemical synthesis or whole-cell biotransformation using prokaryotic hosts such as Corynebacterium glutamicum and Escherichia coli. However, these approaches are limited by low yields, the requirement for expensive supplementation, and the inability to efficiently channel carbon from glucose under high substrate concentrations due to metabolic overflow phenomena. Whole-cell biotransformation systems often suffer from poor carbon efficiency, accumulation of inhibitory by-products such as ethanol, and insufficient control over intracellular metabolite trafficking. While thermophilic enzymes have been explored for their stability under industrial conditions, their integration into microbial hosts has not resolved the fundamental issues of pathway compartmentalization, feedback inhibition, and competing metabolic fluxes. Existing bacterial strains, despite their utility, are constrained by their limited capacity to regulate gene expression with precision, their susceptibility to metabolic burden, and their inability to perform complex post-translational modifications required for optimal enzyme function. There remains a critical need for a microbial platform that can efficiently convert glucose into L-ornithine at high titers without reliance on exogenous supplements, while simultaneously enabling the biosynthesis of valuable polyamine derivatives through coordinated pathway engineering.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The invention introduces a eukaryotic cell factory based on Saccharomyces cerevisiae that has been genetically reconfigured to overproduce L-ornithine and its derivatives through a series of precisely coordinated modifications. The host organism has been engineered to attenuate the activity of ornithine carbamoyltransferase, thereby reducing the consumption of L-ornithine for arginine biosynthesis while maintaining sufficient arginine levels for cell viability. This is achieved through promoter replacement strategies that fine-tune gene expression rather than complete gene deletion. The endogenous L-ornithine biosynthetic pathway from α-ketoglutarate has been enhanced through the overexpression of key mitochondrial enzymes, including ARG5,6, ARG7, ARG8, and ARG2, and the re-localization of the entire pathway to the cytoplasm using heterologous genes from bacterial sources. Carbon flux toward α-ketoglutarate has been dramatically improved by attenuating the Crabtree effect through the overexpression of a truncated form of MTH1, which reduces glucose uptake and suppresses ethanol fermentation. Additionally, the activity of the mitochondrial respiratory chain has been augmented through the expression of alternative NADH oxidases, thereby increasing TCA cycle flux. The invention further enables the overproduction of polyamines by enhancing ornithine decarboxylase activity, deleting regulatory antizymes, and overexpressing enzymes involved in spermidine and spermine biosynthesis. The resulting strains are capable of producing L-ornithine at titers exceeding five grams per liter under fed-batch conditions and can be further modified to directly synthesize putrescine, spermidine, spermine, and agmatine without the need for external supplementation.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

Saccharomyces cerevisiae is selected as the preferred chassis host due to its well-characterized genetics, robustness under industrial fermentation conditions, capacity for high-density growth, and native ability to metabolize a wide range of carbon sources. Unlike prokaryotic systems, yeast offers compartmentalized metabolism that, when properly engineered, allows for spatial separation of biosynthetic steps and minimizes toxic intermediate accumulation. The biosynthesis of L-ornithine in wild-type yeast is compartmentalized across the mitochondria and cytoplasm, with key enzymatic steps occurring in distinct organelles, necessitating the coordinated transport of intermediates such as L-glutamate, N-acetyl-L-ornithine, and L-ornithine itself. This compartmentalization presents a major challenge for metabolic engineering, as mislocalization or insufficient transporter activity can severely limit pathway flux. Conventional techniques employed in this invention include homologous recombination, CRISPR-Cas9-mediated genome editing, plasmid-based overexpression, promoter swapping, and gene deletion using selectable markers. A modified host organism refers to a eukaryotic cell in which one or more endogenous genes have been altered in sequence, expression level, or subcellular localization to enhance the production of L-ornithine. An open reading frame (ORF) denotes a continuous sequence of nucleotides that encodes a polypeptide, beginning with a start codon and ending with a stop codon. Recombinant refers to nucleic acid molecules or proteins that are artificially constructed through the combination of genetic elements from different sources. Recombinant eukaryotic cells are those that contain heterologous nucleic acids introduced through genetic engineering, whereas recombinant prokaryotic cells are similarly modified bacterial hosts. Endogenous nucleic acids or proteins are those naturally encoded by the host genome, while heterologous nucleic acids or proteins originate from a different species or are synthetically designed. A gene is a functional unit of heredity that encodes a specific product, typically a protein or RNA. The genome encompasses the complete set of genetic material in a cell. A nucleic acid is a polymer composed of nucleotide monomers, including DNA and RNA. A peptide is a short chain of amino acids, while a polypeptide is a longer unbranched chain, and a protein is a functional macromolecule composed of one or more polypeptides folded into a specific three-dimensional structure. An enzyme is a biological catalyst that accelerates a specific biochemical reaction. A promoter is a DNA sequence that initiates transcription of a gene, and a terminator is a sequence that signals the end of transcription. Overexpression refers to the increased transcription and translation of a gene beyond its native level, while overproducing describes the resulting accumulation of the encoded product. The Crabtree effect is the phenomenon in which yeast, under high glucose concentrations, ferments glucose to ethanol even in the presence of oxygen, diverting carbon away from the TCA cycle. A vector is a DNA molecule used as a vehicle to transfer genetic material into a host cell. Sequence identity refers to the degree of similarity between two nucleotide or amino acid sequences, expressed as a percentage of aligned residues that are identical. The modified eukaryotic cell for L-ornithine overproduction contains a combination of genetic alterations designed to reduce consumption, enhance biosynthesis, and improve precursor supply. Genetic modifications include the attenuation of ARG3 through replacement of its native promoter with weaker, glucose-regulated promoters such as HXT1 or KEX2, the deletion of CAR2 to block ornithine transamination, the overexpression of CAR1 to enhance arginase-mediated conversion of arginine to ornithine, and the overexpression of ORT1 and AGC1 to facilitate mitochondrial export of ornithine and import of glutamate. The yeast genus and species preferred for this invention is Saccharomyces cerevisiae, although other species such as Saccharomyces bayanus, Saccharomyces pastorianus, and Kluyveromyces lactis may also be utilized. Prokaryotic cell types suitable for complementary applications include gram-negative bacteria such as Escherichia coli, Pseudomonas putida, and Salmonella enterica, as well as gram-positive bacteria such as Corynebacterium glutamicum, Bacillus subtilis, and Lactobacillus plantarum. The modified yeast cell exhibits reduced ornithine consumption through the downregulation of ornithine carbamoyltransferase and ornithine aminotransferase activities, while simultaneously enhancing flux from α-ketoglutarate through the overexpression of pyruvate carboxylase, citrate synthase, aconitase, isocitrate dehydrogenase, and the E1α subunit of pyruvate dehydrogenase. Carbon channeling is further improved by overexpressing MTH1-ΔT to reduce glucose uptake and suppress ethanol formation, and by introducing NADH alternative oxidase to enhance respiratory capacity. The L-ornithine biosynthetic pathway is reconstituted in the cytoplasm using heterologous genes from Escherichia coli and Corynebacterium glutamicum, including argA, argB, argC, argD, and argE, thereby bypassing mitochondrial transport limitations. The strain is further engineered to overproduce polyamines by increasing ornithine decarboxylase activity through SPE1 overexpression, deleting the OAZ1 gene to reduce antizyme-mediated degradation of ornithine decarboxylase, and overexpressing spermidine synthase (SPE2) and spermine synthase (SPE3). The export of polyamines is facilitated by overexpressing transporters such as QDR3 and YAP1, and by deleting uptake systems such as AGP2, SAM3, and DUR3. Agmatine production is enabled by deleting CAR1 and introducing a heterologous arginine decarboxylase (speA) from E. coli, followed by expression of an agmatinase to convert agmatine to putrescine. The resulting strains are capable of producing L-ornithine at titers greater than 5 g/L in fed-batch fermentations and can be further adapted to produce putrescine, spermidine, spermine, and agmatine in single fermentation processes without external supplementation.

## EXAMPLES

### Strain Construction of Ornithine-Overproducing Strains

Plasmids were constructed using the MOPE strategy and DNA assembler technology to enable modular assembly of expression cassettes. Promoters, terminators, and coding sequences were amplified from genomic DNA of Saccharomyces cerevisiae, Escherichia coli, and Corynebacterium glutamicum using high-fidelity polymerases. Expression modules were assembled via one-step PCR fusion, and co-transformation into the CEN.PK113-11C background strain was performed using lithium acetate-mediated transformation. Successful integration of genetic elements was verified by colony PCR and restriction endonuclease digestion. The native promoter of ARG3 was replaced with either the HXT1 or KEX2 promoter to achieve fine-tuned expression, and genes were integrated into chromosomal loci using homologous recombination flanked by 50-bp homology arms. Strains were screened for L-ornithine production using ninhydrin colorimetric assays and validated by HPLC and GC-MS.

### L-Arginine Leaky Auxotroph Enables L-Ornithine Overproduction

The L-ornithine biosynthesis pathway is tightly regulated by feedback inhibition from L-arginine, which represses key biosynthetic enzymes. By replacing the native ARG3 promoter with the weaker HXT1 promoter, arginine biosynthesis was maintained at sub-inhibitory levels, allowing cell growth while preventing feedback repression of ornithine synthesis. Strain M1b, harboring the KEX2 promoter variant, produced 42 mg/L of extracellular L-ornithine, a 76% increase over the parental strain. Intracellular arginine levels remained at approximately 70% of wild-type concentrations, confirming that the engineered strain maintained viability without supplementation. The transcription factor Gcn4p was found to be upregulated in response to reduced arginine levels, leading to increased expression of genes in the ornithine biosynthetic pathway. Deletion of CAR2 in this background yielded only a marginal increase in ornithine titer, suggesting that transamination is not a major flux sink under low ornithine conditions but becomes relevant upon pathway enhancement.

### Pathway Re-Localization and Subcellular Trafficking Engineering Elevates L-Ornithine Synthesis

Overexpression of mitochondrial genes ARG5,6, ARG7, and ARG8 increased ornithine production to 59 mg/L. Further overexpression of ARG2, encoding N-acetylornithine synthase, elevated titers to 80 mg/L, indicating that the acetylated derivative cycle is a rate-limiting node. Overexpression of ORT1, the mitochondrial ornithine exporter, increased production to 115 mg/L, and co-expression of AGC1, the glutamate-aspartate exchanger, further increased titers to 149 mg/L. Re-localization of the entire pathway to the cytoplasm using a synthetic operon derived from E. coli and C. glutamicum (argA, argB, argC, argD, argE) resulted in a final titer of 192 mg/L, surpassing all mitochondrial engineering strategies. This demonstrated that cytosolic reconstitution bypasses transport bottlenecks and enables more efficient substrate channeling.

### ‘Crabtree Negative’ S. cerevisiae Construction Enables Efficient Carbon Channeling to L-Ornithine

Overexpression of wild-type PDA1, PYC2, CIT1, ACO2, and IDP1 increased ornithine production to 245 mg/L. Overexpression of the mutated PDA1[S313A] variant yielded only a modest improvement, indicating that enzyme activity, not phosphorylation status, was the limiting factor. Overexpression of HaAOX1 and NDI1 increased titers to 278 mg/L, confirming that NADH reoxidation enhances TCA cycle flux. The most significant improvement was achieved through overexpression of MTH1-ΔT, which reduced glucose uptake by 90% and eliminated ethanol production, resulting in a 778 mg/L titer. This strain exhibited a fourfold increase in yield on glucose and no detectable ethanol, demonstrating that attenuation of the Crabtree effect is a more effective strategy than TCA cycle enzyme overexpression alone. Fed-batch cultivation of the M1cM2qM3a strain under glucose-limited conditions yielded 5.1 g/L of L-ornithine with a biomass concentration of 56 g/L, confirming industrial feasibility.

### Example 5

Overexpression of CAR1 in the M1cM2qM3e strain increased L-ornithine production to 1,041 mg/L, a 34% improvement over the previous best strain. Deletion of OAZ1, the gene encoding ornithine decarboxylase antizyme, led to a 2.5-fold increase in putrescine production. Overexpression of SPE1, SPE2, and SPE3 enabled the synthesis of spermidine and spermine at titers exceeding 150 mg/L and 80 mg/L, respectively. The introduction of heterologous CANSDH and CANSDC genes allowed for the synthesis of spermidine from aspartate semialdehyde and putrescine, bypassing the classical pathway. Deletion of AGP2, SAM3, and DUR3 enhanced extracellular polyamine accumulation. Expression of speA from E. coli in a CAR1-deleted background enabled agmatine production at 120 mg/L, and subsequent expression of agmatinase converted agmatine to putrescine at 90 mg/L. The engineered strains demonstrate the capacity to produce L-ornithine and its derivatives in a single, scalable fermentation process without the need for exogenous amino acid supplementation.
