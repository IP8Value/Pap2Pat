# DESCRIPTION

- introduce bio-derived polymers

Bio-derived polymers represent a transformative class of materials engineered from renewable biological feedstocks, offering a sustainable alternative to petroleum-based plastics that dominate global manufacturing. These polymers are synthesized from naturally occurring organic molecules derived from plant oils, agricultural residues, forestry byproducts, and microbial metabolites, thereby reducing dependence on fossil resources and mitigating the environmental burden associated with conventional polymer production. Unlike synthetic polymers derived from crude oil, bio-derived polymers can be designed to exhibit comparable or superior mechanical, thermal, and chemical performance while enabling end-of-life pathways such as biodegradation, compostability, or chemical recycling. Their emergence aligns with global regulatory, economic, and societal imperatives to decarbonize industrial supply chains and transition toward circular economy models. The development of bio-derived polymers is not merely an environmental imperative but a technological opportunity to redefine material functionality through molecular architecture derived from nature’s structural diversity. By leveraging the inherent chirality, rigidity, and functional group density of bio-based building blocks, these polymers can be tailored for high-performance applications previously reserved for synthetic analogues, including automotive components, medical devices, packaging films, coatings, and 3D-printed structures. The scalability of feedstock sourcing, combined with advances in catalytic and polymerization technologies, positions bio-derived polymers as viable replacements across multiple industrial sectors without requiring wholesale retooling of existing manufacturing infrastructure.

## BACKGROUND TO THE INVENTION

- motivate renewable polymers

The urgent need to transition from fossil-derived polymers to renewable alternatives stems from the escalating environmental and economic costs associated with petroleum-based plastic production. Over seven percent of global oil consumption is currently allocated to the manufacture of synthetic polymers, a figure projected to rise as emerging economies expand consumer demand for durable goods. The lifecycle of conventional plastics—from extraction and refining to polymerization, processing, and disposal—generates substantial greenhouse gas emissions, contributes to persistent environmental pollution, and depletes finite hydrocarbon reserves. In contrast, renewable polymers derived from biomass offer the potential to significantly reduce net carbon emissions by utilizing atmospheric carbon fixed through photosynthesis. Moreover, the use of non-food biomass sources such as terpenes extracted from citrus peels, pine resin, and wood waste ensures that polymer production does not compete with food security. The adoption of renewable polymers also enhances supply chain resilience by diversifying raw material sources away from geopolitically volatile oil markets. As regulatory frameworks increasingly mandate recycled content, carbon footprint labeling, and extended producer responsibility, industries are under mounting pressure to innovate with sustainable materials. The development of bio-derived polymers is therefore not only an ecological necessity but a strategic business imperative for long-term competitiveness in a resource-constrained world.

- describe limitations of synthetic polymers

Synthetic polymers derived from petroleum feedstocks, while historically dominant due to their low cost and scalable production, suffer from inherent limitations that undermine their long-term sustainability. These materials are typically synthesized from non-renewable monomers such as ethylene, propylene, and styrene, whose production requires energy-intensive cracking processes and generates significant quantities of hazardous byproducts. Their molecular architectures are often homogenous and lack structural complexity, limiting their ability to respond dynamically to environmental stimuli or to be efficiently degraded under natural conditions. Many synthetic polymers exhibit poor biodegradability, persisting in ecosystems for centuries and fragmenting into microplastics that infiltrate terrestrial and marine food chains. Furthermore, the recycling of these materials is hampered by contamination, additive incompatibility, and thermal degradation during reprocessing, resulting in downcycled products of diminished value. The inability to achieve closed-loop recycling at scale has rendered most synthetic polymers linear in their lifecycle, contradicting the principles of circularity. Additionally, their mechanical and thermal properties are often optimized for mass production rather than performance specificity, leading to over-engineering and excessive material use. These limitations collectively constrain the potential of synthetic polymers to meet the evolving demands of sustainability, functionality, and environmental responsibility.

- highlight need for sustainable feedstocks

The transition to sustainable feedstocks is a critical prerequisite for the long-term viability of the polymer industry. Conventional monomers derived from fossil fuels are subject to price volatility, geopolitical instability, and increasing regulatory scrutiny due to their contribution to climate change. In contrast, bio-based feedstocks such as terpenes, fatty acids, lignin derivatives, and polysaccharides offer a renewable, carbon-neutral, and often waste-derived alternative that can be sourced from agricultural residues, forestry byproducts, and industrial bio-refineries. These materials are inherently compatible with biological cycles and can be produced using low-energy, catalytic processes that minimize environmental impact. The use of sustainable feedstocks also enables the design of polymers with built-in biodegradability or compostability, reducing end-of-life burden. Furthermore, the structural diversity of bio-derived molecules—characterized by chiral centers, cyclic frameworks, and functional group richness—provides unprecedented opportunities to engineer polymers with tailored physical properties, including thermal stability, toughness, and barrier performance. The widespread adoption of such feedstocks is essential to achieving global net-zero targets and to fulfilling consumer and regulatory expectations for environmentally responsible products.

- discuss advantages of biorenewable feedstocks

Biorenewable feedstocks offer a compelling combination of environmental, economic, and technical advantages over petroleum-derived alternatives. Derived from annually renewable biomass, these materials inherently sequester atmospheric carbon during growth, resulting in a significantly lower net carbon footprint across their lifecycle. Their molecular complexity—often featuring multiple stereocenters, unsaturated bonds, and functional groups such as alcohols, ketones, and carboxylic acids—enables precise control over polymer architecture, leading to enhanced mechanical strength, thermal resistance, and chemical stability. Unlike synthetic monomers, which require extensive purification and energy-intensive synthesis, many biorenewable feedstocks are available in high purity from existing industrial processes, such as citrus processing or paper pulping. Their structural diversity allows for the design of polymers with unique properties unattainable through conventional chemistry, including inherent UV resistance, antimicrobial activity, or tunable crystallinity. Additionally, biorenewable feedstocks can be processed using mild, catalytic methods that reduce energy consumption and eliminate hazardous reagents, aligning with green chemistry principles. The integration of these materials into existing polymerization platforms further facilitates seamless adoption without requiring capital-intensive infrastructure overhauls.

- describe desire for new technologies

The polymer industry requires transformative technologies capable of bridging the gap between the sustainability goals of society and the performance expectations of industrial applications. Existing bio-based polymers, while promising, often suffer from limited scalability, poor mechanical properties, or incompatibility with established manufacturing processes. There is a critical need for novel monomer platforms that can be synthesized from abundant, low-cost bio-derived feedstocks using scalable, catalytic, and solvent-efficient methods, and that subsequently polymerize under conventional free-radical or controlled radical conditions to yield materials with predictable and tunable properties. Such technologies must enable the production of polymers with glass transition temperatures, molecular weights, and crosslinking densities comparable to or exceeding those of petroleum-based analogues, without compromising on processability or end-use performance. Innovations that simplify synthesis, eliminate toxic reagents, and integrate seamlessly into existing supply chains will be pivotal in accelerating market adoption. The development of such technologies represents not merely an incremental improvement but a paradigm shift in how polymers are conceived, produced, and deployed.

- introduce terpenes and terpenoids

Terpenes and terpenoids constitute a vast and structurally diverse class of naturally occurring hydrocarbons and their oxygenated derivatives, predominantly biosynthesized by plants, fungi, and some insects. These compounds are characterized by their isoprene-based building blocks, typically arranged in head-to-tail or cyclic configurations that confer unique three-dimensional geometries and chemical reactivity. Terpenes are pure hydrocarbons composed of multiple isoprene units, while terpenoids are their functionalized counterparts, often containing hydroxyl, carbonyl, or carboxylic acid groups that enhance their utility as polymer precursors. Among the most abundant and commercially accessible terpenes are α-pinene, β-pinene, limonene, and camphene, all of which are extracted in multi-tonne quantities from citrus peel oil and pine resin streams. Terpenoids such as carvone, camphor, and menthone are similarly available as byproducts of the fragrance, flavor, and pharmaceutical industries. Their structural rigidity, chiral centers, and unsaturated functionalities make them ideal candidates for the design of high-performance bio-derived polymers with tailored thermal and mechanical properties.

- describe structure of terpenes and terpenoids

Terpenes and terpenoids are defined by their molecular architecture, which is built from repeating five-carbon isoprene units (C5H8) assembled in linear, branched, or cyclic arrangements. Monoterpenes, the most relevant class for polymer synthesis, consist of two isoprene units and typically exhibit a C10H16 molecular formula, although oxygenation yields derivatives with varying stoichiometries. These molecules often contain one or more carbon-carbon double bonds, which serve as sites for chemical modification and polymerization. Many terpenes adopt bicyclic frameworks, such as the pinane structure of α- and β-pinene, or monocyclic systems like limonene, which features a six-membered ring with an exocyclic double bond. Terpenoids, in contrast, incorporate oxygen-containing functional groups—including alcohols, ketones, and aldehydes—that significantly alter their polarity, reactivity, and solubility. The presence of chiral centers in these molecules imparts stereochemical complexity that can be harnessed to influence polymer tacticity, crystallinity, and mechanical behavior. The structural diversity of terpenes and terpenoids provides a rich chemical palette for the design of monomers with precisely controlled backbone rigidity, side-chain functionality, and polymerizability.

- discuss availability of terpenes and terpenoids

Terpenes and terpenoids are among the most abundantly available bio-derived feedstocks globally, with annual production exceeding several hundred thousand metric tons. Limonene, for instance, is recovered in excess of 100,000 tonnes per year from citrus processing waste, primarily from orange and lemon peels, making it one of the most economically viable renewable hydrocarbons. Similarly, α-pinene and β-pinene are extracted in multi-tonne quantities from turpentine, a byproduct of the paper and pulp industry, ensuring a stable and scalable supply chain. Carvone and camphor are produced industrially through the oxidation of menthol and other terpene alcohols, respectively, and are widely used in flavor and fragrance applications. The global infrastructure for their extraction, purification, and distribution is already well-established, with major suppliers operating across North America, Europe, and Southeast Asia. This existing industrial footprint provides a unique advantage: terpenes and terpenoids can be integrated into polymer manufacturing without requiring new agricultural systems or significant logistical investment. Their ready availability, coupled with low cost and high purity, positions them as uniquely suited feedstocks for the commercial-scale production of bio-derived polymers.

- summarize previous attempts at polymerizing terpenes

Previous efforts to polymerize terpenes have largely focused on direct homopolymerization via free-radical, cationic, or metathesis routes, with limited success. Early studies demonstrated that β-pinene and α-phellandrene could undergo cationic polymerization at cryogenic temperatures (-80 °C), yielding low molecular weight oligomers with poor thermal stability. Ring-opening metathesis polymerization (ROMP) using ruthenium catalysts enabled the polymerization of sesquiterpenes such as caryophyllene, but these methods are incompatible with the most abundant monoterpenes due to steric hindrance and low ring strain. Other approaches, such as epoxidation followed by copolymerization with CO2, yielded polycarbonates with high glass transition temperatures but required stringent anhydrous conditions and expensive metal catalysts. Attempts to incorporate terpenes as comonomers into acrylate or styrene copolymers resulted in minimal incorporation rates due to low reactivity ratios, and the resulting materials exhibited inferior mechanical properties. While some success was achieved with rosin-derived carboxylic acids through esterification to acrylates, these methods were restricted to a narrow subset of terpenoids possessing pre-existing functional groups, excluding the majority of commercially available terpenes. Collectively, these prior efforts underscored the fundamental challenge: terpenes are inherently unreactive under standard polymerization conditions and require chemical modification to become viable monomers.

- describe limitations of previous approaches

The limitations of prior approaches to terpene polymerization stem from their reliance on impractical reaction conditions, narrow substrate scope, and poor scalability. Cryogenic temperatures, anaerobic environments, and expensive transition metal catalysts render many methods economically unfeasible for industrial deployment. The use of toxic reagents such as acryloyl chloride, hazardous solvents, and stoichiometric oxidants further undermines their environmental credentials. Most strategies failed to address the inherent low reactivity of terpene double bonds under free-radical conditions, resulting in low conversion rates, uncontrolled molecular weights, and frequent gelation due to unintended crosslinking. Additionally, the inability to functionalize terpenes lacking pre-existing hydroxyl or carboxylic acid groups excluded over 80% of commercially available terpenes from consideration. Even when functionalization was achieved, the synthetic routes were multi-step, low-yielding, and incompatible with green chemistry principles. These limitations collectively prevented the development of terpene-derived polymers with consistent properties, reproducible molecular weights, or commercial viability.

- highlight need for commercially viable polymerization route

A commercially viable polymerization route for terpenes must overcome the dual challenges of monomer synthesis and controlled polymerization under industrially feasible conditions. Such a route must employ readily available, low-cost feedstocks, utilize non-toxic reagents, operate at ambient or moderately elevated temperatures, and yield polymers with predictable molecular weights, narrow dispersity, and tunable glass transition temperatures. It must be scalable to multi-tonne production levels without requiring specialized equipment or extreme process controls. Crucially, the method must be compatible with existing polymer manufacturing infrastructure—particularly free-radical and controlled radical polymerization systems—to enable seamless integration into current supply chains. The absence of such a route has been the primary barrier to market adoption of terpene-based polymers. The development of a robust, scalable, and sustainable pathway to functionalized terpene monomers and their subsequent polymerization represents the missing link between academic innovation and industrial application.

- discuss importance of controlling molecular weight and structure

Precise control over molecular weight and polymer architecture is essential to achieving the mechanical, thermal, and processing properties required for commercial applications. Polymers with low molecular weights exhibit poor tensile strength and durability, while excessively high molecular weights hinder processability and increase melt viscosity beyond practical limits. The distribution of molecular weights—quantified as polydispersity index—likewise influences material homogeneity, crystallinity, and end-use performance. Structural control, including tacticity, branching, and crosslinking density, directly affects glass transition temperature, elasticity, and chemical resistance. For terpene-derived polymers, where monomer structure introduces inherent steric and electronic heterogeneity, achieving such control is particularly challenging. Conventional free-radical polymerization typically yields broad molecular weight distributions and uncontrolled branching, leading to inconsistent material properties. The introduction of chain transfer agents and controlled radical techniques enables precise manipulation of molecular weight and architecture, allowing for the design of polymers with targeted performance characteristics. Without this level of control, terpene-based polymers cannot meet the stringent specifications demanded by industries such as automotive, packaging, and medical devices.

- summarize unmet need for bio-derived polymers

Despite decades of research and growing environmental pressure, a critical unmet need persists in the polymer industry: the absence of a scalable, cost-effective, and environmentally benign method to convert abundant, non-functionalized terpenes into high-performance, structurally controlled bio-derived polymers. Existing technologies either lack commercial scalability, require impractical reaction conditions, or are restricted to a narrow range of terpenoid substrates. There is no established route to produce terpene-based acrylates and methacrylates from the most widely available monoterpenes—such as α-pinene, β-pinene, and limonene—using green chemistry principles, nor is there a proven method to polymerize these monomers with precise control over molecular weight and architecture under industrially relevant conditions. The resulting gap has left a vast reservoir of renewable feedstock untapped, while the market continues to rely on fossil-derived alternatives. The invention described herein fulfills this unmet need by providing a novel, streamlined, and scalable methodology for the synthesis of functionalized terpene monomers and their controlled polymerization into materials with tunable properties, enabling the widespread adoption of bio-derived polymers across multiple industrial sectors.
