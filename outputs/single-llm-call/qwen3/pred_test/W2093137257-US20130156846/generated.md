# DESCRIPTION

## INTRODUCTION

Protein translation is a fundamental biological process through which genetic information encoded in messenger RNA is converted into functional polypeptide chains composed of amino acids. This process is executed with remarkable precision by the ribosomal machinery, supported by aminoacyl-tRNA synthetases that ensure the correct pairing of each codon with its cognate amino acid. The fidelity of translation is maintained through multiple layers of proofreading, resulting in an error rate of approximately one misincorporation per 10³ to 10⁴ amino acids added. Despite this high accuracy, rare errors can occur when non-canonical molecules structurally resemble canonical amino acids and are erroneously activated by tRNA synthetases or accepted by the ribosome. Such misincorporations can lead to the formation of aberrant proteins that lose their native conformation, gain toxic properties, or aggregate into insoluble deposits, ultimately disrupting cellular homeostasis. Among the many naturally occurring non-protein amino acids, β-N-methylamino-l-alanine (BMAA) has emerged as a compound of particular concern due to its structural similarity to l-serine and its documented presence in environmental sources such as cyanobacteria, cycad seeds, and aquatic food chains. BMAA has been detected in the brain tissues of individuals who succumbed to neurodegenerative diseases, including amyotrophic lateral sclerosis and Alzheimer’s disease, raising the hypothesis that it may act as a molecular mimic during protein synthesis. Evidence from radiolabeling studies demonstrates that BMAA is incorporated into proteins in a manner dependent on active translation, and that its association with polypeptide chains is resistant to standard extraction methods, suggesting covalent peptide bond formation. The misincorporation of BMAA into nascent polypeptides represents a previously underappreciated mechanism by which environmental toxins can directly interfere with the fundamental process of protein synthesis, thereby initiating a cascade of proteotoxic stress that may underlie the pathogenesis of chronic neurological disorders.

## SUMMARY

The discovery that l-serine effectively competes with BMAA for incorporation into proteins during translation provides a novel therapeutic strategy for preventing the initiation of protein misfolding and aggregation in neurodegenerative disease. By supplementing the cellular pool of l-serine, the likelihood of BMAA being erroneously accepted by the translational machinery is significantly reduced, thereby preserving the structural and functional integrity of newly synthesized proteins. This mechanism is particularly relevant in the context of Alzheimer’s disease, where the accumulation of amyloid-beta peptides and hyperphosphorylated tau proteins forms the pathological hallmark of the condition. The cleavage of the amyloid precursor protein (APP) by β- and γ-secretases generates amyloid-beta fragments that, when misfolded, aggregate into oligomers and plaques, triggering neuroinflammation, synaptic dysfunction, and neuronal death. The presence of BMAA in post-mortem brain tissue of Alzheimer’s patients, coupled with its ability to substitute for serine residues critical to protein folding and post-translational modification, suggests that BMAA misincorporation may accelerate or exacerbate these pathological processes. L-serine, therefore, functions not merely as a metabolic precursor but as a molecular shield against environmental proteotoxicity. The invention encompasses methods of preventing BMAA misincorporation by elevating intracellular concentrations of l-serine or its biologically active derivatives, thereby reducing the burden of misfolded proteins and mitigating downstream consequences such as endoplasmic reticulum stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and apoptosis. This approach extends beyond Alzheimer’s disease to other neurodegenerative conditions, including amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, Parkinson’s disease, Huntington’s disease, and frontotemporal dementia, all of which are characterized by protein aggregation and neuronal loss. The invention further includes the use of l-serine precursors, such as phosphorylated or esterified derivatives, as well as conjugates with polymers, lipids, or pegylated moieties, to enhance bioavailability, blood-brain barrier penetration, and sustained release. Formulations of l-serine are designed for oral, intravenous, intranasal, or intrathecal administration, with dosing regimens tailored to achieve and maintain therapeutic concentrations in the central nervous system. The invention also encompasses methods for identifying novel agents that modulate serine racemase activity, inhibit BMAA uptake, or stabilize the tRNA synthetase active site to further suppress misincorporation. The therapeutic benefit of l-serine administration is defined by the amelioration of motor, cognitive, and behavioral symptoms, the slowing of disease progression, and the preservation of neuronal viability, as measured by clinical rating scales, neuroimaging, and biomarker analysis.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

β-N-methylamino-l-alanine (BMAA) is a non-protein amino acid produced by cyanobacteria and bioaccumulated through aquatic and terrestrial food webs. Upon ingestion or environmental exposure, BMAA enters the bloodstream and crosses the blood-brain barrier, where it is recognized by aminoacyl-tRNA synthetases as a structural analog of l-serine. Once activated and loaded onto serine-specific tRNAs, BMAA is incorporated into growing polypeptide chains during ribosomal translation, leading to the production of misfolded, non-functional, or aggregation-prone proteins. This misincorporation event is not a passive consequence of chemical similarity but an active, enzymatically driven process that occurs with sufficient frequency to alter proteostasis in vulnerable neuronal populations. The resulting aberrant proteins exhibit altered hydrophobicity, disrupted folding kinetics, and resistance to degradation, ultimately forming insoluble aggregates that trigger endoplasmic reticulum stress, oxidative damage, and apoptotic signaling. L-serine, the canonical amino acid that BMAA mimics, acts as a competitive inhibitor by saturating the substrate-binding site of seryl-tRNA synthetase, thereby reducing the probability of BMAA activation and subsequent incorporation. Supplementation with l-serine elevates intracellular serine concentrations, outcompeting BMAA without interfering with normal protein synthesis. This mechanism prevents the initiation of proteotoxic cascades that lead to neuronal dysfunction and death. The invention includes methods for preventing BMAA misincorporation by administering l-serine or its derivatives in amounts sufficient to maintain a molar excess over BMAA in neural tissues. These methods extend to the prevention of protein misfolding and aggregation by preserving the native conformation of critical proteins such as tau, TDP-43, and α-synuclein, whose function is compromised when serine residues are substituted with BMAA. Neurological diseases and disorders encompass a broad spectrum of conditions characterized by progressive degeneration of neurons, including but not limited to amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, Alzheimer’s disease, Parkinson’s disease, Huntington’s disease, Pick’s disease, and frontotemporal dementia. These disorders are associated with motor and cognitive decline, speech and language impairments, visual and perceptual disturbances, mood and behavioral alterations, sleep dysregulation, and social withdrawal. The therapeutic benefit of l-serine administration is defined by the stabilization or improvement of these symptoms, as measured by standardized clinical assessments such as the Amyotrophic Lateral Sclerosis Functional Rating Scale, the Mini-Mental State Examination, and the Unified Parkinson’s Disease Rating Scale. The term “ameliorate” refers to any measurable reduction in disease severity, symptom burden, or rate of progression, whether partial or complete. Stabilization of a neurological disorder denotes the cessation or significant slowing of functional decline over time, as evidenced by longitudinal clinical evaluations and neuroimaging. The invention further encompasses the use of l-serine precursors, including hydroxyl-protected, amino-protected, or acid-protected derivatives, as well as conjugates with polyethylene glycol, lipids, or biodegradable polymers to enhance pharmacokinetic properties. Pharmaceutical formulations may include salts, esters, or alkylated variants of l-serine, formulated with carriers such as mannitol, lactose, or hydroxypropyl methylcellulose, and stabilized with antioxidants, preservatives, or tonicity agents. Administration routes include oral tablets, intravenous infusions, intranasal sprays, and direct cerebrospinal fluid delivery via implantable pumps. Kits for administering l-serine may contain multiple unit doses, calibrated dosing devices, and instructions for monitoring therapeutic response through biomarker analysis or imaging modalities such as PET scans, MRI, or CSF protein profiling. The invention also includes cell-free and cell-based screening methods for identifying agents that inhibit serine racemase, block BMAA transport, or enhance endogenous serine synthesis, thereby providing additional therapeutic avenues for preventing BMAA-induced proteotoxicity.

### EXAMPLES

Example 1 describes the in vitro incorporation of radiolabeled BMAA into human fibroblast, neuroblastoma, and endothelial cell lines, demonstrating that incorporation is abolished by the translation inhibitor cycloheximide and significantly reduced by co-incubation with l-serine. Example 2 details the time-dependent accumulation of BMAA in cellular proteins and its resistance to denaturing agents, confirming covalent peptide bonding. Example 3 presents data from Drosophila models showing that dietary BMAA induces motor deficits and premature death, which are rescued by l-serine supplementation. Example 4 reports the prevention of BMAA misincorporation in rodent neuroproteins using oral l-serine, with mass spectrometry confirmation of reduced BMAA levels in brain homogenates. Example 5 describes a vervet monkey model in which chronic BMAA exposure leads to tau hyperphosphorylation and cortical atrophy, both of which are attenuated by l-serine treatment. Example 6 confirms the reduction of protein aggregation and autofluorescence in human neuronal cultures treated with l-serine alongside BMAA. Example 7 analyzes post-mortem human brain tissue from Alzheimer’s and ALS patients using affinity purification and tandem mass spectrometry, revealing the presence of BMAA covalently bound to serine-rich domains of tau and TDP-43. Example 8 outlines a Phase I clinical trial in ALS patients, demonstrating safety, tolerability, and dose-dependent increases in plasma and CSF l-serine concentrations, with preliminary evidence of slowed functional decline over 12 months.
