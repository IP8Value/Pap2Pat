## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

Nanofluidic techniques have emerged as a powerful platform for the precise manipulation and analysis of biological samples at the single-molecule level. These systems exploit engineered micro- and nanoscale channels and chambers to isolate, contain, and process minute volumes of reagents and analytes, enabling high-throughput, parallelized biochemical reactions with minimal sample consumption. Among the most significant applications of nanofluidics is digital PCR, a method that partitions a DNA sample into thousands of discrete reaction chambers, each containing either zero or one or more target molecules. Following amplification, the presence or absence of a fluorescent signal in each chamber is used to determine the number of positive reactions, from which the absolute concentration of the target nucleic acid can be inferred through statistical modeling. Unlike conventional PCR, which relies on calibration curves and relative quantitation, digital PCR provides absolute quantification without the need for reference standards, making it particularly valuable for applications requiring high precision, such as rare mutation detection, viral load monitoring, and copy number variation analysis.

Despite its advantages, conventional digital PCR systems face critical limitations in accuracy and reproducibility, particularly when applied to samples with high target concentrations. In such cases, the probability of multiple molecules occupying a single reaction chamber increases significantly, leading to an undercounting of the true number of target molecules. This phenomenon, known as Poisson undersampling, introduces systematic bias that is difficult to correct using simple linear or empirical adjustments. Furthermore, traditional digital PCR platforms often lack the capacity for multiplexed analysis of multiple genomic targets within the same reaction, necessitating separate assays that increase pipetting error, reagent consumption, and experimental variability. These constraints severely limit the utility of digital PCR in complex genomic analyses where simultaneous quantification of multiple loci is essential.

Copy number variation (CNV) refers to structural alterations in the genome involving the deletion or amplification of DNA segments ranging from hundreds of base pairs to several megabases. CNVs are a major source of human genetic diversity and have been implicated in a wide spectrum of diseases, including cancer, neurodevelopmental disorders, and autoimmune conditions. The ability to accurately measure CNVs is therefore of profound clinical and research significance. Current whole-genome scanning technologies, such as array-based comparative genomic hybridization (array-CGH) and high-density single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) microarrays, offer broad genomic coverage but suffer from limited resolution, poor sensitivity for small or low-level CNVs, and an inability to distinguish between heterozygous and homozygous alterations with confidence. These platforms also rely on indirect hybridization signals that are susceptible to background noise, probe design artifacts, and normalization biases, making them unsuitable for precise quantitation of gene dosage.

Digital array-based CNV determination represents a significant advancement over these technologies by combining the absolute quantitation power of digital PCR with the multiplexing capability of nanofluidic partitioning. By simultaneously amplifying a target gene and a reference gene—typically a single-copy housekeeping gene such as RNase P—in the same nanofluidic chip, the ratio of their respective concentrations can be used to infer the copy number of the target relative to the reference. This approach eliminates the need for external calibration standards and minimizes technical variability by ensuring that both targets are subjected to identical amplification conditions, pipetting errors, and thermal cycling profiles. However, despite the promise of this method, existing analytical frameworks for computing CNV ratios lack rigorous statistical foundations for estimating uncertainty. Conventional approaches often assume normality of concentration estimates or rely on simplistic approximations that fail to account for the nonlinear relationship between observed positive chambers and true molecular concentration, resulting in confidence intervals that are inaccurate, overly optimistic, or inapplicable to asymmetric distributions.

There is therefore a critical and unmet need for improved methods and systems capable of accurately estimating the concentration of nucleic acid molecules in a digital array, computing the ratio of concentrations between two or more targets, and deriving statistically valid confidence intervals for these estimates—even under conditions of high molecular load, asymmetric sampling distributions, or limited panel replication. Such methods must be grounded in first-principles probability theory, be computationally robust across a wide range of input parameters, and be implementable in automated, high-throughput platforms without requiring expert statistical intervention. The invention disclosed herein fulfills this need by introducing a comprehensive mathematical and computational framework for digital array-based CNV analysis that enables precise, confidence-interval-aware quantitation of gene copy number with unprecedented accuracy and reliability.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The invention comprises a novel nanofluidic system and associated computational method for determining copy number variation in genomic DNA with statistically rigorous confidence intervals. The system partitions a biological sample containing target and reference nucleic acid molecules into a plurality of discrete reaction chambers, each with a defined volume, such that the distribution of molecules across chambers follows a Poisson process. The method involves storing the total number of reaction chambers in a memory device, distributing the biological sample uniformly among the chambers, and determining the number of chambers that contain at least one molecule of the target sequence and the reference sequence following amplification. From these counts, the proportion of positive chambers for each sequence is computed, and the concentration of molecules for each sequence is estimated using a nonlinear transformation derived from the Poisson probability model. The method further includes the computation of a standard deviation for each concentration estimate based on the binomial sampling distribution of positive chambers, and the derivation of a confidence interval for each estimated concentration using established statistical approximations valid for large chamber counts.

The invention further encompasses a method for computing the ratio of concentrations between two nucleic acid targets, such as a test gene and a reference gene, by first estimating the concentration of each target and its associated confidence interval independently, then computing the ratio of the mean concentrations and deriving a confidence interval for that ratio using a numerical algorithm that integrates over the joint probability density function of the two concentration estimates. This algorithm does not assume normality of the underlying distributions and instead constructs a probability density function for the ratio by accumulating probabilities across thin wedges of the joint distribution, thereby preserving the true shape of the sampling distribution even when it is asymmetric or skewed. An alternative embodiment employs a direct analytical approximation based on Fieller’s theorem, extended to accommodate asymmetric confidence intervals by modeling the joint distribution as a union of quadrant-wise elliptical regions, allowing for rapid estimation without numerical integration. The method further includes the generation of simulated universes of digital array chambers to validate the statistical properties of the estimators, including the accuracy of the confidence intervals and the convergence of the estimated concentration to the true population parameter.

The invention enables the determination of whether two biological samples exhibit statistically significant differences in copy number by comparing the confidence intervals of their respective concentration ratios; if the intervals do not overlap, the difference is deemed significant at the specified confidence level. The system further includes a digital array chip comprising a plurality of nanofluidic reaction chambers arranged in a panel, each chamber having a volume of approximately six nanoliters, and a network of microfluidic channels and valves for controlled loading of sample and reagent mixtures. The chip is designed to be compatible with automated thermal cycling and fluorescence imaging systems, enabling high-throughput processing of multiple panels simultaneously. The method is applicable to any nucleic acid target detectable by amplification-based assays, including DNA, RNA, and synthetic constructs, and is particularly suited for the analysis of copy number variation in cancer genomes, prenatal diagnostics, and liquid biopsy applications. The invention provides a foundational framework for absolute, confidence-aware quantitation of genomic copy number, transforming digital array technology from a qualitative counting tool into a quantitative analytical platform with statistically defensible precision.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF SPECIFIC EMBODIMENTS

Copy number variation refers to the presence of deletions or duplications of genomic segments that result in deviations from the typical diploid copy number of a gene or region. These variations are central to understanding genetic diversity, disease susceptibility, and tumor evolution, and their accurate measurement is essential for diagnostic and therapeutic decision-making. Digital PCR has long been recognized as a method capable of absolute quantitation of nucleic acids by partitioning a sample into thousands of individual reaction vessels, each acting as an independent amplification unit. In the context of copy number variation analysis, digital PCR is typically performed in a multiplex format, wherein a target gene and a reference gene—often a single-copy locus such as RNase P—are amplified simultaneously within the same reaction mixture. The ratio of their respective concentrations, derived from the proportion of positive chambers for each target, provides a direct measure of the copy number of the target relative to the reference. However, the accuracy of this ratio is contingent upon the precision with which the underlying concentrations are estimated, a task complicated by the nonlinear relationship between the observed number of positive chambers and the true molecular concentration.

The present invention introduces a mathematical framework for copy number variation analysis that explicitly accounts for the statistical nature of molecular partitioning and the resulting uncertainty in concentration estimation. The problem is decomposed into two distinct components: first, the estimation of the true concentration of each nucleic acid target from the observed number of positive chambers, and second, the computation of the ratio of these concentrations and the associated confidence interval. Unlike prior Bayesian approaches that treat the concentration as a random variable with an assumed prior distribution, the present method treats the true concentration as a fixed but unknown population parameter, consistent with classical statistical inference. The digital array chip, comprising a panel of 765 nanoliter-volume reaction chambers, serves as a finite sampling device from an effectively infinite population of potential partitions. Each chamber functions as a Bernoulli trial, with success defined as the presence of one or more target molecules. The proportion of positive chambers is therefore an unbiased estimator of the probability of a chamber containing at least one molecule, and the true concentration is derived from this probability via the inverse of the Poisson survival function.

The method begins by storing the total number of reaction chambers in a memory device, distributing the biological sample uniformly among the chambers, and performing amplification under controlled thermal cycling conditions. Following amplification, fluorescence imaging is used to identify positive chambers, and the number of positive chambers for each target is recorded. The proportion of positive chambers is computed, and the standard deviation of this proportion is derived from the binomial variance formula. The concentration estimate is then calculated using the natural logarithm transformation of one minus the proportion of negative chambers, and the confidence interval for this estimate is computed using the delta method, which propagates the uncertainty from the proportion to the concentration via the derivative of the nonlinear transformation. To determine whether two samples differ in copy number, the method computes the mean concentration ratio and its confidence interval using either a numerical integration algorithm that constructs the probability density function of the ratio from the joint distribution of the two concentration estimates, or an analytical approximation based on the extension of Fieller’s theorem to asymmetric confidence regions. Computer simulations confirm that the method achieves coverage probabilities close to the nominal 95% level, even under conditions of high molecular load and low panel replication. The invention further includes embodiments for the fabrication of the digital array chip using elastomeric materials and microfabrication techniques, the integration of thermal control systems for precise temperature regulation during amplification, and the use of optical imaging systems with synchronized excitation and emission filters to capture fluorescence signals across all chambers simultaneously. The system is compatible with existing platforms such as the BioMark system and can be adapted to other nanofluidic architectures with minor modifications to chamber geometry and fluidic routing. The method is applicable to any nucleic acid target detectable by amplification, including genomic DNA, cDNA, synthetic oligonucleotides, and circulating tumor DNA, and provides a robust, statistically grounded foundation for copy number analysis in clinical and research settings.
