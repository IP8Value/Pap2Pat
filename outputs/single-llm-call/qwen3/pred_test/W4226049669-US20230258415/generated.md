# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNICAL FIELD

- define technical field

The present invention relates to systems and methods for enhancing heat transfer efficiency in fluidic environments while simultaneously reducing hydrodynamic drag, particularly in applications involving thermal management of electronic components, microfluidic heat exchangers, and industrial cooling systems. The invention specifically concerns the use of liquid-infused surfaces with engineered micro- and nano-scale groove geometries to induce dispersive convective heat transfer mechanisms that augment thermal performance beyond the limits of conventional roughened or smooth surfaces. The technology is applicable to systems requiring high thermal conductivity interfaces between solid substrates and flowing liquids, where traditional surface modifications that increase heat flux also incur prohibitive increases in frictional resistance. The invention enables a synergistic improvement in both thermal and fluid dynamic performance through the strategic coupling of surface topology, infusing liquid properties, and solid substrate characteristics.

## BACKGROUND

- motivate heat transfer challenge
- discuss prior art limitations

The simultaneous enhancement of heat transfer and reduction of drag in laminar and turbulent flows has long been recognized as a fundamental challenge in thermal engineering. Conventional approaches to increasing heat transfer, such as the use of surface roughness, ridges, corrugations, or extended surfaces, invariably lead to elevated momentum transport and increased pressure drop, thereby raising the pumping power required to maintain flow. While miniaturization of channels and heat exchangers improves the surface-to-volume ratio and enhances convective coupling, it concurrently amplifies viscous losses, resulting in a net penalty in system efficiency. Prior attempts to mitigate this trade-off have focused on superhydrophobic surfaces, which reduce drag by promoting slip through gas-trapping textures. However, these surfaces suffer from limited thermal conductivity due to the insulating nature of entrapped gases, rendering them ineffective for applications requiring substantial heat flux. Other approaches have explored isothermal textured surfaces with high-conductivity solids, but these designs assume uniform temperature distribution across the solid matrix, an assumption that fails under realistic conditions where thermal gradients exist and solid-liquid thermal conductivities are mismatched. Furthermore, existing models have largely neglected the role of dispersive convection—the organized recirculation of fluid within surface cavities that actively transports heat across the solid-liquid interface—particularly when the thermal conductivity of the solid substrate is comparable to that of the infusing liquid. As a result, previous systems have been unable to achieve net heat transfer enhancement without sacrificing drag reduction, limiting their applicability in high-performance thermal systems.

The invention introduces a novel system for enhanced heat transfer comprising a solid substrate with a patterned array of grooves or cavities on its surface, wherein the grooves are infused with a liquid of selected thermal and rheological properties, and wherein an external working fluid flows over the infused surface. The grooves are engineered to induce recirculation vortices under shear stress from the external flow, thereby generating dispersive convective heat transfer that significantly increases the net surface heat flux relative to a smooth surface of identical material. The infusing liquid is selected to have a thermal conductivity substantially equal to or less than that of the solid substrate, enabling efficient thermal coupling and maximizing the contribution of convective transport within the grooves. The working fluid, flowing parallel to the substrate surface, experiences a reduction in drag due to the dynamic liquid interface formed by the infusing liquid, which acts as a lubricating layer. The method for heat transfer involves establishing a temperature gradient across the substrate, inducing shear stress in the external flow, and allowing the resulting recirculation within the grooves to transport heat from the solid substrate through the infusing liquid and into the boundary layer of the working fluid. The method comprises the steps of: (1) fabricating a solid substrate with a plurality of micro- or nano-scale grooves oriented transverse to the direction of flow; (2) infusing the grooves with a liquid having a thermal conductivity no greater than that of the substrate; (3) establishing a temperature difference between the bottom of the substrate and the external working fluid; (4) directing the working fluid across the infused surface at a velocity sufficient to generate shear-induced recirculation within the grooves; and (5) achieving a net increase in surface heat flux and a concurrent reduction in hydrodynamic drag. Variations of the method include adjusting groove depth, pitch, width, and cross-sectional geometry to optimize the Péclet number based on slip velocity and groove height; selecting infusing liquids with viscosity ratios relative to the working fluid that enhance vortex formation; and employing substrates composed of metals, metal alloys, or high-conductivity polymers in combination with liquid metals, organic solvents, or ionic liquids as the infusing medium.

The fundamental principle of the invention is that dispersive convection—arising from organized, shear-induced recirculation within surface grooves—can significantly augment heat transfer when the thermal conductivity of the solid substrate is comparable to or less than that of the infusing liquid. This mechanism, previously overlooked in favor of conductive or turbulent convective models, becomes dominant under conditions where thermal gradients are sustained and fluid motion is constrained by geometric features. The scope of the invention encompasses any system in which a solid surface is patterned with grooves, infused with a liquid, and exposed to a flowing external fluid, such that the resulting hydrodynamic and thermal interactions yield a net increase in heat flux and a reduction in frictional resistance. For the purposes of this application, the term “groove” refers to any recessed feature on the substrate surface that extends in the wall-normal direction and is open to the external flow, including but not limited to channels, pits, cavities, or depressions. The term “infusing liquid” denotes a liquid introduced into and retained within the grooves through capillary, viscous, or interfacial forces, and distinct from the external working fluid. The term “dispersive convection” refers to the time-averaged transport of heat due to spatially coherent velocity and temperature fluctuations induced by the groove geometry, as opposed to random turbulent fluctuations. Exemplary embodiments include a silicon-based microprocessor substrate with transverse grooves of 50 micrometers depth and 100 micrometer pitch, infused with a gallium-indium-tin alloy, and exposed to a water-based coolant; a polymer heat sink with nano-scale V-shaped grooves filled with silicone oil, used in wearable electronics; and a metal alloy heat exchanger channel with U-shaped cavities infused with heptane, operating under turbulent flow conditions. The system for increasing heat transfer comprises a solid substrate with a structured surface comprising a plurality of grooves or cavities, each having a depth ranging from 10 nanometers to 5 millimeters, a width-to-depth ratio between 0.5 and 10, and a center-to-center pitch between 1.5 and 10 times the groove depth. The cross-sectional shape of the grooves may be rectangular, with sharp or rounded corners, U-shaped, V-shaped, or pyramidal, each configuration influencing the strength and stability of the recirculation vortices. Rectangular grooves provide uniform flow separation and predictable vortex formation; rounded corner grooves reduce flow separation losses and enhance interfacial stability; U-shaped grooves promote symmetric recirculation; V-shaped grooves concentrate flow shear at the apex; and pyramidal grooves enable multi-directional vortex coupling. The grooves are oriented transverse to the primary direction of the external working fluid flow, ensuring maximum shear stress induction and vortex generation. The distance between adjacent grooves, or pitch, is selected to balance thermal augmentation with hydrodynamic penalty, with optimal performance observed at pitch-to-depth ratios between 2 and 8. The substrate material may be selected from metals including aluminum, copper, steel, or gallium alloys; semiconductors such as silicon or gallium nitride; or polymers including polydimethylsiloxane, polyimide, or polycarbonate, provided that the thermal conductivity of the substrate is not substantially greater than that of the infusing liquid. The working fluid may be water, ethylene glycol, air, nitrogen, or any other fluid suitable for heat transfer applications, with a Prandtl number between 0.1 and 100. The infusing liquid is selected from a group including liquid metals (e.g., gallium, eutectic gallium-indium), organic solvents (e.g., heptane, ethanol), ionic liquids, or silicone oils, with a thermal conductivity between 0.1 W/m·K and 40 W/m·K and a viscosity ratio relative to the working fluid between 0.1 and 10. Design criteria for the grooves and infusing liquid require that the Péclet number based on slip velocity and groove height, Pei, falls within the range of 10¹ to 10³, ensuring that dispersive convection contributes meaningfully to the total heat flux. The substrate is thermally coupled to a heat-generating component such as a semiconductor chip, sensor array, or power transistor, such that heat is conducted from the component into the substrate and subsequently transferred to the working fluid via the infused surface. The method for providing increased heat transfer involves the sequential steps of forming a patterned surface on a solid substrate, infusing the pattern with a liquid having a thermal conductivity no greater than that of the substrate, establishing a temperature differential between the substrate and the external fluid, and inducing flow across the surface at a Reynolds number sufficient to generate recirculation within the grooves, thereby achieving a net increase in surface heat flux of at least 3% and a reduction in drag of at least 1% relative to an equivalent smooth surface.
