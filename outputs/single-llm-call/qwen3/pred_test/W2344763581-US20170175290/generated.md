# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNICAL FIELD

- define technical field

The present invention relates to the field of semiconductor materials science and fabrication technologies, specifically to a method for the direct growth of single-crystalline III–V semiconductor structures on non-epitaxial, amorphous, or non-lattice-matched substrates. This technology enables the deterministic formation of high-quality, patterned single-crystal semiconductor architectures with precise geometric control, without reliance on traditional epitaxial growth techniques that require crystalline substrates or complex buffer layers. The invention is particularly applicable to the fabrication of electronic and photonic devices, including transistors, photodetectors, and integrated optoelectronic circuits, where the integration of high-mobility III–V materials onto low-cost, flexible, or unconventional substrates is essential for next-generation device platforms.

## BACKGROUND

- motivate III-V semiconductors

III–V semiconductors, such as indium phosphide, gallium arsenide, and indium antimonide, possess superior electronic and optoelectronic properties compared to conventional silicon, including high electron mobility, direct bandgaps, and strong light emission efficiency. These characteristics make them indispensable for high-speed transistors, laser diodes, photodetectors, and solar cells operating in the infrared and visible spectrum. However, their widespread adoption has been historically constrained by the necessity of growing these materials on expensive, lattice-matched crystalline substrates, such as GaAs or InP wafers, which are incompatible with large-area, low-cost manufacturing processes and limit integration with silicon-based electronics or flexible substrates. Conventional epitaxial growth methods, including metalorganic chemical vapor deposition and molecular beam epitaxy, are inherently restricted by thermodynamic and kinetic constraints that prevent nucleation and coherent growth on amorphous surfaces. As a result, the integration of III–V semiconductors into scalable, heterogeneous, or non-planar device architectures has remained a persistent challenge in materials engineering.

## SUMMARY

- outline method

The invention discloses a templated liquid-phase crystal growth process that enables the direct formation of single-crystalline III–V semiconductor structures on amorphous substrates through the controlled diffusion of a group V element into a pre-patterned group III metal template, confined by a ceramic encapsulation layer. The method involves the sequential deposition of a nucleation layer, a group III metal film, and a ceramic barrier layer on a non-crystalline substrate, followed by thermal activation in a group V element-containing atmosphere. Under controlled temperature and partial pressure conditions, the group III metal melts into a liquid phase while remaining geometrically confined by the ceramic template. The group V element diffuses through the ceramic layer, supersaturates the liquid group III metal, and induces the precipitation of a single-crystalline III–V semiconductor structure. The growth is self-limiting due to the formation of a phosphorus depletion zone around each nucleation site, which suppresses secondary nucleation and ensures that each patterned feature grows as a single crystal, preserving the original lithographic geometry.

- describe embodiment

In a preferred embodiment, the method is applied to the growth of indium phosphide on a silicon dioxide-coated silicon substrate. A thin molybdenum oxide nucleation layer is first deposited to facilitate the formation of a uniform indium film, followed by evaporation of indium to a thickness of 50 to 200 nanometers. A silicon oxide cap layer, 10 to 100 nanometers thick, is then deposited over the indium to serve as a diffusion barrier and mechanical confinement. The structure is then heated in a phosphine-hydrogen atmosphere at temperatures between 500 and 535 degrees Celsius for a duration of 10 to 20 minutes. During this process, phosphorus atoms diffuse through the silicon oxide cap, dissolve into the molten indium, and precipitate as single-crystalline indium phosphide. The resulting structure retains the exact shape of the original indium pattern, whether circular, linear, or complex geometric forms, and exhibits zincblende crystal structure with minimal defects, as confirmed by electron backscatter diffraction and transmission electron microscopy.

- describe alternative embodiment

In an alternative embodiment, the method is extended to the growth of ternary and quaternary III–V semiconductors, such as indium gallium phosphide or aluminum gallium arsenide, by depositing alloyed group III metal templates prior to encapsulation. Additionally, in situ doping is achieved by introducing a dopant precursor, such as germane, into the growth atmosphere during the crystal growth phase, enabling precise control over electron concentrations up to 1.3 × 10¹⁹ cm⁻³. The resulting doped structures exhibit tunable photoluminescence spectra, reduced Urbach tail parameters indicative of high crystalline quality, and performance metrics comparable to epitaxially grown counterparts. Furthermore, the process is adaptable to non-silicon substrates, including borosilicate glass and polymeric films, enabling the transfer of single-crystal semiconductor structures onto flexible, transparent, or unconventional platforms for wearable electronics and conformal optoelectronics.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

- introduce detailed description

The following detailed description provides a comprehensive account of the templated liquid-phase crystal growth process, its operational parameters, structural configurations, and practical implementations. This method fundamentally departs from conventional epitaxial approaches by decoupling crystal growth from substrate lattice matching, thereby enabling the direct fabrication of high-quality semiconductor structures on any surface capable of supporting thin-film deposition.

- describe patent scope

The scope of this invention encompasses all methods and structures involving the growth of single-crystalline III–V semiconductors via templated liquid-phase diffusion, including but not limited to binary, ternary, and quaternary compounds, as well as their doped variants. The invention further includes all device architectures incorporating such structures, including transistors, photodetectors, light-emitting elements, and integrated circuits fabricated on amorphous, polycrystalline, or non-traditional substrates.

- explain limitations of prior art

Prior art methods for growing III–V semiconductors on non-crystalline substrates have relied on nucleation layers, buffer stacks, or post-growth transfer techniques, all of which introduce interfacial defects, strain, or compositional inhomogeneity. These approaches are incompatible with large-scale manufacturing due to their complexity, high cost, and limited yield. Moreover, they fail to achieve true single-crystallinity over patterned features larger than a few micrometers, resulting in polycrystalline or twinned structures that degrade device performance.

- motivate single-crystal growth on amorphous substrates

The ability to grow single-crystalline III–V semiconductors directly on amorphous substrates eliminates the need for costly crystalline wafers and enables the integration of high-performance materials into low-cost, flexible, and large-area electronic systems. This capability is essential for applications in wearable sensors, transparent displays, biomedical implants, and monolithic 3D integration, where substrate flexibility, optical transparency, or compatibility with silicon CMOS processing is paramount.

- describe challenges of single-crystal growth

The primary challenge in achieving single-crystalline growth on amorphous substrates lies in controlling nucleation density and crystal orientation without the guiding influence of a crystalline lattice. Conventional vapor-phase techniques suffer from uncontrolled nucleation, leading to multi-grain structures. The present invention overcomes this by leveraging liquid-phase confinement and phosphorus depletion kinetics to ensure that only one nucleus forms per patterned feature, thereby enforcing single-crystalline growth.

- summarize prior approaches to single-crystal growth

Prior approaches have included epitaxial lateral overgrowth, seed-based growth, and nanowire assembly, all of which require intricate patterning, high temperatures, or post-growth alignment steps. None of these methods achieve deterministic, large-area, single-crystal growth on arbitrary substrates with the simplicity and scalability of the present invention.

- introduce templated liquid-phase crystal growth process

The templated liquid-phase crystal growth process is a novel technique that utilizes a pre-patterned group III metal template, encapsulated by a ceramic diffusion barrier, to guide the formation of a single-crystalline III–V semiconductor through liquid-phase reaction with a group V element. The process is initiated by depositing a thin nucleation layer to promote uniform metal wetting, followed by deposition of the group III metal and ceramic cap. Upon heating in a group V atmosphere, the metal melts, and the group V element diffuses inward, triggering crystallization exclusively within the confined volume.

- describe flow diagram of TLP crystal growth process

The process begins with the patterning of a substrate, followed by sequential deposition of a nucleation layer, group III metal, and ceramic barrier. After lithographic definition and lift-off, the exposed edges of the metal are partially coated with ceramic to ensure complete encapsulation. The structure is then heated under controlled gas flow and pressure, allowing diffusion and crystallization to occur. The final structure is a single-crystal semiconductor with the exact geometry of the original template.

- define shape of semiconductor structure

The semiconductor structure formed by this method assumes the precise geometric shape of the original group III metal template, including circles, rings, squares, letters, logos, and arbitrary micro- or nanoscale patterns. The shape is preserved with submicron fidelity, enabling the fabrication of complex, user-defined architectures.

- describe resist material and dimensions

The resist material used for patterning is typically a standard photoresist compatible with electron-beam or optical lithography, with feature sizes ranging from 150 nanometers to tens of micrometers. The thickness of the resist is selected to enable clean lift-off and is typically between 100 and 500 nanometers.

- illustrate structure at block 102

At block 102, the substrate is prepared with a thin molybdenum oxide layer, approximately 1 to 10 nanometers thick, deposited by thermal evaporation. This layer enhances the wettability of the indium film and promotes uniform nucleation.

- deposit nucleation layer

The nucleation layer is composed of a transition metal oxide, such as MoOx, WOx, or TiOx, with a thickness sufficient to reduce interfacial energy but thin enough to remain transparent to subsequent diffusion processes.

- describe nucleation layer material and thickness

The nucleation layer is preferably molybdenum oxide with a thickness of 1 to 10 nanometers. Alternative materials include tungsten oxide or titanium oxide, each selected based on their ability to promote adhesion and nucleation without impeding phosphorus diffusion.

- illustrate structure at block 104

At block 104, the indium metal layer is deposited to a thickness of 50 to 200 nanometers, forming a continuous film over the nucleation layer. The deposition is performed under cryogenic conditions to ensure smooth morphology.

- deposit boron group element

The term “boron group element” is herein used to refer to group III elements, including indium, gallium, and aluminum, which serve as the primary constituent for III–V semiconductor formation.

- describe boron group element material and thickness

The boron group element is indium, deposited to a thickness of 50 to 200 nanometers. For ternary compounds, alloys of indium and gallium may be co-deposited in controlled ratios.

- deposit ceramic material

A ceramic material, preferably silicon oxide, is deposited over the group III metal to act as a diffusion barrier and mechanical confinement.

- describe ceramic material and its properties

The ceramic material is silicon oxide, deposited via sputtering or thermal evaporation, with a thickness of 10 to 100 nanometers. It is chemically inert, thermally stable up to 600 degrees Celsius, and permits slow phosphorus diffusion while preventing metal oxidation or outgassing.

- illustrate structure at block 108

At block 108, the structure comprises the substrate, nucleation layer, group III metal, and ceramic cap, with the metal fully encapsulated and ready for thermal activation.

- remove resist

The photoresist is removed using a standard solvent lift-off process, exposing the underlying ceramic and metal stack while preserving the patterned geometry.

- illustrate structure at block 110

At block 110, the resist has been removed, revealing the patterned ceramic-metal stack with exposed lateral edges.

- deposit ceramic material to cover boron group element

An additional ceramic layer is deposited at an oblique angle to coat the sidewalls of the group III metal, ensuring complete encapsulation and preventing lateral diffusion or oxidation.

- describe ceramic material deposition process

The additional ceramic layer is deposited by angled physical vapor deposition, with the substrate rotated to ensure uniform coverage of vertical surfaces. The thickness ranges from 4 to 50 nanometers.

- illustrate structure at block 112

At block 112, the structure is fully encapsulated with ceramic on all surfaces except the top, which is exposed to the growth atmosphere.

- heat amorphous substrate and materials in nitrogen group element gas

The substrate is placed in a low-pressure furnace and heated to 500–535 °C in an atmosphere of phosphine diluted in hydrogen, with partial pressures ranging from 0.1 to 10 Torr.

- describe heating process and temperature range

The heating is conducted under controlled ramp rates of 5 to 10 degrees Celsius per minute to avoid thermal stress. The temperature is maintained for 10 to 20 minutes to ensure complete conversion of the metal to semiconductor.

- describe diffusion of nitrogen group element through ceramic material

Phosphorus atoms diffuse through the amorphous silicon oxide cap via grain boundary and defect-mediated transport, entering the molten indium and achieving supersaturation levels sufficient for nucleation.

- describe growth of single crystal semiconductor structure

Growth initiates at a single nucleation site per patterned feature, with the crystal propagating radially outward until the entire volume is filled. The phosphorus depletion zone surrounding the growing crystal suppresses secondary nucleation.

- describe growth time and factors affecting growth

Growth time is determined by the size of the feature and the phosphine partial pressure. Larger features require longer times, while lower phosphine pressures reduce nucleation rates and favor single-crystal formation.

- describe resulting single crystal semiconductor structure

The resulting structure is a single-crystalline indium phosphide with zincblende orientation, exhibiting no twins or grain boundaries within individual features, as confirmed by EBSD mapping.

- describe dimensions of single crystal semiconductor structure

The lateral dimensions range from 150 nanometers to 20 micrometers, and the thickness is equal to the original indium layer, typically 50 to 200 nanometers.

- describe importance of single crystal semiconductor structure

The single-crystalline nature of the structure ensures high electron mobility, low defect density, and excellent optoelectronic performance, enabling the fabrication of high-efficiency transistors and photodetectors.

- describe optional removal of ceramic material

After growth, the ceramic cap may be selectively removed using a buffered oxide etch, exposing the semiconductor surface for device contact fabrication.

- describe etching process for ceramic material removal

The etching is performed using a hydrofluoric acid-based solution, with etch rates calibrated to remove the silicon oxide without damaging the underlying indium phosphide.

- summarize TLP crystal growth process

The templated liquid-phase crystal growth process enables the direct, deterministic, and scalable fabrication of single-crystalline III–V semiconductors on amorphous substrates through geometric confinement and kinetic control of nucleation.

- describe advantages of TLP crystal growth process

This method offers unparalleled advantages, including compatibility with standard lithography, low growth temperatures, elimination of epitaxial constraints, and the ability to grow on flexible, transparent, or non-planar substrates.

- describe compatibility with traditional device processing technology

The process is fully compatible with CMOS fabrication workflows, including photolithography, atomic layer deposition, and metal evaporation, enabling seamless integration into existing semiconductor manufacturing lines.

- describe scalability and processing cost advantages

The use of simple cold-wall or hot-wall furnaces, low-cost substrates, and minimal precursor consumption renders the process highly scalable and economically viable for wafer-scale production.

- conclude detailed description

The templated liquid-phase crystal growth method represents a paradigm shift in semiconductor fabrication, enabling the direct integration of high-performance III–V materials onto any substrate without the constraints of lattice matching or epitaxial growth.

- describe method 100 for fabricating single crystal semiconductor structure

Method 100 comprises the steps of providing a substrate, depositing a nucleation layer, depositing a group III metal, depositing a ceramic barrier, patterning the stack, removing resist, applying an angled ceramic coating, heating in a group V atmosphere, and optionally removing the ceramic cap to expose the semiconductor.

- fabricate doped III-V semiconductor structure

Doping is achieved by introducing a dopant gas, such as germane, into the growth atmosphere, enabling in situ n-type doping with carrier concentrations up to 1.3 × 10¹⁹ cm⁻³.

- fabricate single crystal semiconductor structure comprising ternary and quaternary III-V semiconductors

Ternary and quaternary compounds are fabricated by co-depositing group III metals in controlled ratios prior to encapsulation, enabling the growth of alloys such as InGaP or AlInAs.

- fabricate single crystal structures of other binary and ternary compounds

The method is applicable to other binary compounds such as GaP, InSb, and GaAs, as well as ternary systems including AlGaAs and InGaAs, with appropriate selection of group III and group V precursors.

- describe semiconductor devices including single crystal III-V semiconductor structure

Semiconductor devices incorporating the grown structures include metal-oxide-semiconductor field-effect transistors, photodetectors, light-emitting diodes, and integrated photonic circuits.

- describe MOSFET with top gate

A top-gated MOSFET is fabricated by depositing a gate dielectric, such as zirconium oxide, followed by a metal or transparent conductive oxide gate electrode, enabling high transconductance and low leakage.

- describe single crystal indium phosphide (InP) growth

Single-crystalline indium phosphide is grown via the templated liquid-phase process with indium and phosphine, resulting in structures with zincblende orientation and minimal defects.

- describe TLP growth of InP

The TLP growth of InP occurs at 500–535 °C in a phosphine-hydrogen atmosphere, with the indium template fully converted to single-crystal InP within 10–20 minutes.

- describe InP crystal arrays growth

Arrays of InP crystals in circular, square, and linear geometries are grown across 4-inch wafers with uniform crystallinity and optical response.

- describe EBSD crystal analysis

Electron backscatter diffraction confirms that each patterned feature is a single crystal with preferential (10n) orientation and no twin boundaries.

- describe growth of InP patterns for transistor fabrication

InP microwires with dimensions of 1 × 50 μm and thickness of 125 nm are grown and used as the channel in top-gated MOSFETs.

- describe doping-dependent studies

Doping with germane increases electron concentration, blue-shifts photoluminescence, and reduces the Urbach tail, indicating improved crystal quality.

- describe phosphorous depletion zone formation

The phosphorus depletion zone forms around each growing nucleus, suppressing secondary nucleation and enabling single-crystal growth even in features up to 20 μm in diameter.

- describe SEM images of InP crystal arrays

Scanning electron microscopy reveals that the original indium pattern geometry is preserved with submicron fidelity after growth.

- describe EDS confirmation of stoichiometry

Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy confirms a 1:1 stoichiometric ratio of indium to phosphorus in the grown crystals.

- describe X-ray diffraction spectroscopy

X-ray diffraction shows only zincblende InP peaks, with no evidence of amorphous or polycrystalline phases.

- describe EBSD mapping

EBSD orientation maps confirm single-crystallinity within each feature, with distinct crystallographic orientations between adjacent features.

- describe effect of growth conditions and InP feature size on number of grains

The number of grains per feature increases quadratically with feature diameter, but is suppressed by lowering phosphine partial pressure, enabling near-uniform single-crystal growth even in large features.

- describe TEM examination of crystallinity

Transmission electron microscopy confirms a sharp interface between the single-crystal InP and the underlying amorphous SiO₂ substrate, with no epitaxial relationship.

- describe demonstration of versatility of TLP crystal growth technique

The technique is demonstrated by growing complex 3D architectures, including multilayer InP stacks and microscale text, such as the word “Lorem Ipsum” with 150 nm stroke width.

- describe scalability of TLP crystal growth

The process is scalable to full 4-inch wafers using simple cold-wall furnaces, with uniform growth across the entire substrate.

- describe growth of InP circles on borosilicate glass slide

InP circles are grown directly on borosilicate glass, with photoluminescence confirming high optical quality.

- describe transfer of grown InP onto plastic substrates

InP structures are transferred to polyethylene terephthalate using polyamic acid as an intermediate layer, preserving crystallinity and optoelectronic function.

- describe growth of complex 3D architectures

Multilayer InP structures are grown by stacking In/SiOx layers and performing a single growth cycle, enabling monolithic 3D semiconductor integration.

- describe tuning of optical and electrical properties

Optical properties are tuned via doping, with photoluminescence peak energy shifting by over 100 meV and electron concentrations reaching degenerate levels.

- describe in situ n-type Ge doping of InP

Germane is introduced during growth, enabling in situ n-type doping with carrier concentrations up to 1.3 × 10¹⁹ cm⁻³.

- describe photoluminescence spectra

Photoluminescence spectra show a clear Burstein–Moss shift with increasing doping, confirming increased electron concentration.

- describe electron concentrations

Electron concentrations are calculated from photoluminescence peak shifts and range from 10¹⁷ to 1.3 × 10¹⁹ cm⁻³.

- describe Urbach tail parameter

The Urbach tail parameter decreases with doping, indicating reduced disorder and improved band-edge sharpness.

- describe fabrication of long-channel Schottky n-type MOSFETs

Long-channel n-type MOSFETs are fabricated with InP microwires as channels, ZrO₂ as gate dielectric, and Ni as top gate, achieving ON/OFF ratios exceeding 10⁵.

- describe transfer and output characteristics of InP transistor

The transistor exhibits an ON-current of 120 μA μm⁻¹, peak transconductance of 100 S μm⁻¹, and effective electron mobility of 675 cm² V⁻¹ s⁻¹.

- describe fabrication of top-gated InP photo-MOSFETs

Photo-MOSFETs are fabricated with ITO gates, enabling optical access to the channel under illumination.

- describe photoresponse and specific detectivity

The photo-MOSFET exhibits a peak responsivity of 700 A W⁻¹ and a specific detectivity of 8.4 × 10¹¹ Jones at room temperature.

- summarize TLP crystal growth technique

The templated liquid-phase crystal growth technique enables the direct, scalable, and deterministic fabrication of single-crystalline III–V semiconductors on amorphous substrates, with tunable doping, geometric control, and device-grade performance.
