## FIELD

- relate to magnetic field patterning of nickel nanofibers

This invention relates to the field of functional printing and advanced materials fabrication, specifically to methods for patterning and aligning nickel nanofibers through the controlled application of magnetic fields during the thermal reduction of metal organic decomposition precursor inks. It encompasses the synthesis, deposition, and post-processing of ferromagnetic metal formulations that, when subjected to specific thermal and magnetic conditions, self-assemble into highly ordered, template-free nanowire structures with directional electrical and magnetic properties. The invention is particularly directed to the integration of digital printing technologies with in-situ magnetic field manipulation to achieve spatial control over the morphology and orientation of metallic nanostructures on both rigid and flexible substrates. This approach enables the scalable production of patterned metallic nanowire arrays suitable for applications in printed electronics, magnetic sensors, data storage elements, optical polarizers, and flexible transducers. Unlike conventional methods that rely on lithographic patterning, electrochemical deposition, or high-temperature vacuum processes, this invention provides a low-cost, ambient-condition-compatible pathway to fabricate anisotropic nickel nanostructures with tailored properties directly from solution-based inks, thereby bridging the gap between solution-processable materials and high-performance magnetic devices.

## BACKGROUND

- formulate Ni MOD precursor inks

A homogeneous nickel metal organic decomposition precursor ink has been developed through the controlled complexation of nickel formate with ethylenediamine in an aqueous solvent system, resulting in a stable, low-viscosity formulation suitable for digital printing techniques. This ink contains no metallic nanoparticles, eliminating the risks of particle agglomeration, solvent evaporation-induced rheological instability, and nozzle clogging commonly associated with nanoparticle-based inks. The formulation achieves a metal loading of approximately 5.7 weight percent, sufficient to yield continuous metallic structures after thermal reduction, while maintaining printability through optimized surface tension and viscosity parameters. The use of formate as a counterion facilitates a lower decomposition temperature and promotes the generation of a reducing atmosphere during heating, minimizing oxidation of the nascent nickel phase. Ethylenediamine serves as a bidentate ligand that enhances solubility, stabilizes the metal complex, and improves the homogeneity of the ink, enabling uniform deposition across diverse substrates.

- challenge of reducing nickel or other elements

The reduction of nickel from its precursor state to its elemental metallic form presents unique challenges due to its high susceptibility to oxidation under ambient conditions. Conventional reduction methods require inert atmospheres, elevated pressures, or prolonged thermal treatments to prevent re-oxidation, which complicates integration with low-temperature, roll-to-roll, or flexible substrate processes. Furthermore, the reduction kinetics of nickel complexes are less favorable than those of silver or copper, requiring precise thermal control to ensure complete conversion without residual organic contamination or incomplete sintering. These challenges have historically limited the practical use of nickel precursor inks in printed electronics and magnetic device fabrication.

- use of different digital printing techniques

Digital printing techniques such as aerosol jet, inkjet, and microextrusion offer precise, non-contact deposition of functional materials with sub-micron resolution, making them ideal candidates for patterning metallic inks. However, prior efforts to apply these methods to ferromagnetic metals like nickel have been hindered by ink instability, poor adhesion, and lack of post-deposition structural control. The present invention overcomes these limitations by combining a chemically stable precursor ink with in-situ magnetic field alignment during thermal curing, enabling the formation of oriented nanostructures regardless of the printing method employed.

- properties of nickel

Nickel possesses high magnetic permeability, significant saturation magnetization, good electrical conductivity, and excellent corrosion resistance, making it a compelling candidate for magnetic and conductive applications. Its high thermal coefficient of resistance further enables its use in sensing and thermal management systems. However, these advantageous properties are only fully realized when nickel is structured at the nanoscale with controlled orientation, which has not been previously achievable through direct printing methods.

- limitations of nanoparticle inks

Nanoparticle inks suffer from inherent instability due to particle aggregation, solvent evaporation during printing, and inconsistent rheological behavior over time. These issues lead to poor print fidelity, inconsistent film thickness, and reduced electrical performance. Additionally, nanoparticle inks require high sintering temperatures to achieve particle coalescence, limiting their compatibility with thermally sensitive substrates such as polymers and textiles.

- advantages of MOD precursor inks

Metal organic decomposition inks circumvent these limitations by being molecularly dissolved, eliminating particulate content and enabling uniform deposition. Their chemical reduction during curing allows for lower processing temperatures and the formation of continuous metallic networks without the need for post-deposition sintering aids. The absence of stabilizing ligands or surfactants in the final product results in higher purity and enhanced electrical conductivity.

- formulation of nickel MOD precursor ink

The nickel MOD ink described herein is formulated by dissolving nickel formate in a mixture of water and ethylenediamine, followed by the addition of polyvinylpyrrolidone and a surfactant to optimize printability. The resulting solution is filtered to remove any particulate impurities and exhibits surface tension and viscosity values compatible with aerosol jet and inkjet printing systems.

- prior art of fabricating nanowires

Previous methods for fabricating nickel nanowires have relied on hydrothermal synthesis, electrochemical deposition, or template-assisted growth, all of which require multiple processing steps, sacrificial templates, and post-synthesis purification. These methods are incompatible with large-area, low-cost, substrate-integrated fabrication and cannot be readily combined with digital printing technologies. No prior art discloses the direct formation of aligned nickel nanowires through the thermal reduction of a printed precursor ink under the influence of a magnetic field.

## SUMMARY

- produce aligned nickel nanowires

The invention enables the production of aligned nickel nanowires through the thermal reduction of a printed metal organic decomposition precursor ink in the presence of a static magnetic field. The magnetic field induces directional alignment of nickel nuclei and nascent nanoparticles during the reduction process, resulting in the spontaneous formation of continuous, template-free nanowires oriented along the field lines. This process occurs at ambient atmospheric pressure and at temperatures below 250°C, enabling compatibility with flexible and thermally sensitive substrates.

- produce printed, cured, and magnetically aligned geometric patterned metallic nanowires

The invention further enables the direct fabrication of geometrically patterned metallic nanowire structures by printing ink in predefined shapes and then subjecting the printed regions to magnetic field-assisted thermal curing. The resulting structures exhibit anisotropic electrical and magnetic properties that are dictated by the geometry and orientation of the printed patterns, allowing for the design of custom functional elements such as directional sensors, magnetic circuit elements, and polarizing arrays.

- sequentially print, cure, and magnetically align two or more printed geometric patterns

The invention introduces a novel method for fabricating multi-layered, interlaced nanowire architectures by sequentially printing distinct geometric patterns on a substrate, curing each layer individually, and applying a magnetic field with a different orientation during each curing step. This enables the creation of cross-grid, orthogonal, or helical nanowire networks with enhanced isotropic conductivity and multi-directional magnetic response, opening new possibilities for three-dimensional printed magnetic metamaterials.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

- print nickel or other ferromagnetic metal precursor ink

A homogeneous precursor ink containing a nickel complex dissolved in an aqueous solvent system is deposited onto a substrate using a digital printing technique such as aerosol jet printing. The ink is formulated to have a viscosity between 2.5 and 4.0 centipoise and a surface tension between 40 and 50 mN/m, ensuring stable jetting and uniform film formation. Other ferromagnetic metals, including cobalt and iron, may be similarly formulated and printed using analogous precursor chemistries.

- sintering the nickel or other ferromagnetic metal precursor ink

The printed ink is subjected to thermal treatment at a temperature between 220°C and 260°C for a duration of 10 to 30 minutes in the presence of a magnetic field. During this process, the organic ligands decompose and volatilize, while the nickel ions are chemically reduced to elemental nickel. The magnetic field, applied at intensities between 50 and 500 Oe, induces alignment of the forming nickel nuclei, resulting in the growth of elongated nanowire structures oriented parallel to the field direction.

- fabricating aligned interlayered nanofibers

By repeating the printing and curing process with a rotated magnetic field orientation between layers, interlaced nanowire networks are formed. Each layer consists of aligned nanowires, and the relative orientation between adjacent layers is controlled by the direction of the applied magnetic field during each curing step, enabling the fabrication of two-dimensional grid structures with enhanced isotropic conductivity.

- enhance isotropic electrical properties

The interlaced nanowire structures exhibit significantly reduced electrical resistivity in multiple directions compared to single-directionally aligned or unaligned films. The interconnected nanowire network minimizes resistive junctions between grains and provides multiple conduction pathways, resulting in a resistivity as low as 560 µΩ·cm, approaching the performance of bulk nickel while retaining the flexibility and patternability of printed electronics.

- illustrate technology with reference to specific examples

The invention is further illustrated through the following specific examples, which detail the materials, methods, and characterization techniques used to fabricate and evaluate the printed nanowire structures. These examples demonstrate the reproducibility, scalability, and functional versatility of the method across multiple substrate types and magnetic field configurations.

### EXAMPLE 1

- materials used

Nickel formate and ethylenediamine were obtained from commercial suppliers and used without further purification. Glass microscope slides and flexible polyimide substrates were cleaned with isopropyl alcohol prior to printing. Polyvinylpyrrolidone and a silicone-based surfactant were added to the ink to improve wetting and film uniformity.

- ink formulation

The ink was prepared by dissolving 0.8 grams of nickel formate in 4 mL of distilled water containing 0.72 mL of ethylenediamine. After 15 minutes of stirring, 1% w/w polyvinylpyrrolidone and 20 µL of a 10% v/v BYK 333 surfactant solution were added. The mixture was filtered through a 0.2 µm syringe filter to remove particulates.

- characterize surface tension and viscosity

Surface tension was measured using a pendant drop method and found to be 44.01 mN/m. Viscosity was determined using a microVISC viscometer and measured at 3.2 cP, values within the optimal range for aerosol jet printing.

- treat substrates with atmospheric plasma

Substrates were exposed to atmospheric plasma for 60 seconds to increase surface energy and improve ink adhesion, resulting in uniform film coverage without dewetting.

- perform TGA and DTA

Thermogravimetric analysis showed a two-stage decomposition: solvent evaporation below 100°C and nickel reduction at 235°C. Differential thermal analysis confirmed an endothermic peak at 235°C, establishing the optimal curing temperature at 240°C.

- study surface morphology and elemental analysis

Scanning electron microscopy revealed nanowire formation only in samples cured under a magnetic field. Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy confirmed the presence of metallic nickel with minimal oxygen or carbon contamination.

- measure thickness of printed films

Optical and stylus profilometry measured a wet film thickness of approximately 4 µm, which reduced to 1.2 µm after curing, indicating complete solvent evaporation and dense metallic structure formation.

- measure electrical conductivity

Four-point probe measurements showed a resistivity of 560 µΩ·cm in the nanowire grid structure, representing an 80-fold improvement over unaligned films and surpassing previously reported values for printed nickel.

- study magnetic properties

Vibrating sample magnetometry revealed a remanent magnetization of 20 emu/g along the alignment direction and 7 emu/g perpendicular to it, demonstrating strong shape anisotropy. The grid structure exhibited balanced remanence in both orthogonal directions.

- aerosol jet printing

Aerosol jet printing was performed using a 25-gauge nozzle at a standoff distance of 5 mm and a translation speed of 2 mm/s, producing uniform square patterns with high edge definition.

- print solid square pattern

A 1 cm × 1 cm square pattern was printed and cured under a 250 Oe magnetic field, resulting in aligned nanowires oriented perpendicular to the field gradient.

- cure nickel films

Curing was performed on a hot plate at 240°C for 15 minutes under a uniform magnetic field generated by permanent magnets.

- characterize morphology and structure

SEM imaging confirmed the formation of nanowires composed of fused nanoparticles resembling a “string of pearls,” with diameters ranging from 100 to 250 nm and lengths exceeding 10 µm.

- study isotropic properties

Electrical resistivity measurements in orthogonal directions showed a 3:1 anisotropy ratio in single-layer aligned films and near-isotropic behavior in grid structures.

- align characterization

Two-dimensional fast Fourier transform analysis of SEM images quantified alignment with peak intensities at 90° and 270°, confirming high orientation order.

- Ni MOD ink characterization

The ink exhibited thermal stability up to 200°C and remained homogeneous for over 30 days without phase separation.

- thermal decomposition

TGA confirmed complete decomposition of organic components by 300°C, leaving a residue of 98% metallic nickel.

- reduction of nickel

EDS spectra confirmed the absence of oxidized nickel species, indicating complete reduction under the applied conditions.

- EDS spectrum

Peaks at 0.851 keV and 7.471 keV corresponded to nickel Kα and Kβ lines, with negligible oxygen and carbon signals.

- print and sinter Ni samples

Multiple samples were printed and sintered under varying magnetic field strengths and orientations to establish process robustness.

- electrical characterization

Resistivity decreased with increasing layer count due to reduced void density and improved inter-wire connectivity.

- measure electrical resistivity

The lowest resistivity of 560 µΩ·cm was achieved in the cross-grid structure, demonstrating superior conductivity over unaligned and single-directional films.

- magnetic characterization

Hysteresis loops showed enhanced coercivity and remanence in aligned samples, with a 100% increase in remanent magnetization along the alignment axis.

- print on flexible substrate

The ink was successfully printed and cured on polyimide substrates, demonstrating compatibility with flexible electronics applications.

- demonstrate feasibility of printing

The method was demonstrated to be scalable, reproducible, and compatible with industrial digital printing systems, establishing its potential for commercial adoption in printed magnetic and electronic devices.
