# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

The precise three-dimensional structure of a protein is fundamental to its biological function, governing interactions with ligands, substrates, and other macromolecules. The determination of protein structure through experimental methods such as X-ray crystallography and cryo-electron microscopy remains resource-intensive, time-consuming, and often infeasible for proteins that are difficult to express, purify, or crystallize. These limitations are particularly acute for proteins lacking homologous structural templates, rendering traditional comparative modeling approaches ineffective. While computational methods have emerged to address these challenges, many rely heavily on statistical biases derived from known structures or require extensive sequence homology to generate plausible folds. Homology modeling, though widely used, fails when sequence identity falls below the threshold of reliable alignment, leaving a significant fraction of the proteome without structural annotations. Physics-based simulations, including molecular dynamics, offer a more fundamental approach by simulating atomic motions according to physical laws, yet they are computationally prohibitive for de novo folding of medium- to large-sized proteins due to the vast conformational space and the need for microsecond- to millisecond-scale sampling. Energy minimization techniques, while useful for refining known structures, are ill-suited for initiating folding from an extended chain due to their tendency to converge to local minima rather than the global free energy minimum. Prior physics-based methods have attempted to approximate folding pathways using simplified force fields, but they often neglect the nuanced interplay between electrostatic, hydrophobic, and entropic forces that govern native structure formation. Furthermore, these methods typically treat solvent effects as a static dielectric continuum, failing to capture the dynamic polarization and displacement effects arising from the movement of polar and nonpolar regions relative to one another during folding. As a result, there remains a critical unmet need for a computationally efficient, physics-driven method capable of predicting protein structure from sequence alone, without reliance on templates or statistical priors, while accurately resolving the forces that drive secondary and tertiary structure formation under physiological conditions.

## SUMMARY

A novel computational method for predicting protein structure from amino acid sequence is disclosed, grounded in the physical principles governing residue interactions during folding. The method recognizes that the formation of secondary and tertiary structure arises from the balanced interplay of electrostatic forces, electrostatic displacement forces induced by solvent polarization, and thermal energy-driven diffusion. The method first identifies regions of the polypeptide chain likely to form alpha helices or beta sheets by analyzing the spatial distribution of hydrophilic and hydrophobic residues, along with their net charge and size, using a scanning algorithm that evaluates local force balance. Alpha helices are determined when hydrophobic residues are sufficiently clustered and not opposed by strong electrostatic displacement forces from intervening charged residues. Beta sheets are identified when unstructured regions exhibit a specific combination of low net charge and moderate hydrophobicity, consistent with strand pairing under solvent-driven constraints. Once secondary structure elements are determined, the method constructs an initial three-dimensional representation by assigning hydrophobic and electrostatic properties to backbone alpha-carbon atoms and treating each secondary structure element as a rigid unit. The method then simulates the folding of the protein by applying an effective force derived from the summation of Coulombic attraction/repulsion, electrostatic displacement, and thermal diffusion to each pivot bond between alpha-carbon atoms. The direction and magnitude of motion at each time step are determined by the greatest torque generated by these forces, allowing the protein to drift and diffuse toward lower-energy configurations. The simulation incorporates a mechanism to stabilize early-formed secondary structures by freezing rotational motion once a dominant force pattern persists over multiple time steps, mimicking the emergence of autonomous folding units. The resulting coarse-grained folded structure is subsequently refined using molecular dynamics to relax local steric and energetic strain, yielding a final model with high accuracy relative to experimentally determined structures.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The disclosed method for protein structure prediction begins with the assignment of physical properties to each amino acid residue based on its chemical identity. The net charge and hydrophobicity of each side chain are mapped to the corresponding alpha-carbon atom in the protein backbone, enabling the representation of residue-level interactions at a computationally tractable scale. The method accounts for three primary forces acting on each pivot bond: the electrostatic force governed by Coulomb’s law, the electrostatic displacement force arising from solvent polarization, and the thermal force resulting from Brownian motion. The electrostatic force between two charged residues is calculated using the formula \( F_{\text{elect}} = \frac{q_1 q_2}{4\pi \epsilon_w r^2} \), where \( q_1 \) and \( q_2 \) are the partial charges, \( r \) is the interatomic distance, and \( \epsilon_w \) is the dielectric constant of water, approximated as 78 times the vacuum permittivity. The electrostatic displacement force, which arises from the movement of polar water molecules toward regions of high electric field, pushes nonpolar residues away from charged environments. This force is derived from the energy change associated with the redistribution of dielectric media and is expressed as \( F_{\text{disp}} = -\frac{4\beta |q|^2}{r^5} \), where \( \beta \) is a composite constant incorporating the volume of the nonpolar region and the electric field gradient. The thermal force, representing the stochastic motion induced by ambient temperature, is formulated as \( F_{\text{therm}} = \gamma \sqrt{kT} \), where \( k \) is Boltzmann’s constant, \( T \) is absolute temperature, and \( \gamma \) encapsulates the inverse relationship between mobility and frictional resistance. These three forces are combined into a single effective force vector that acts on each alpha-carbon pivot bond, determining the direction and magnitude of rotational motion during each simulation step. The method employs a screening algorithm to identify secondary structure elements prior to tertiary folding. For alpha helices, a scanning bracket extends from a hydrophilic residue to the next hydrophilic residue within six positions. If the intervening residues exhibit a net hydrophobic character and minimal opposing charge, an alpha helix is predicted. For beta sheets, a five-residue window is evaluated; if the sum of absolute charges minus the sum of hydrophobicity values falls below a threshold of 0.3 and the hydrophobicity sum exceeds 0.1, a beta sheet is assigned. These rules are applied iteratively across the entire sequence to map all secondary structure regions. The resulting secondary structure elements are then inserted into a three-dimensional model using graphical software, with each element tagged with its aggregate charge and hydrophobicity. The unstructured regions are initialized as linear chains. During tertiary folding, the simulation allows rotation about each pivot bond, with motion dictated by the maximum torque generated by the effective force. To prevent premature convergence, the simulation freezes rotational freedom at any bond that exhibits a dominant torque for five consecutive time steps, preserving early-formed structural motifs. The folding trajectory is simulated over thousands of time steps, with each step updating the conformation based on the net force and mobility. The method achieves high accuracy in predicting tertiary structure, as demonstrated by RMSD values below 5 Å for proteins ranging from 30 to 157 residues, and approximately 8 Å for the 320-residue human protein tyrosine phosphatome. A final refinement step employs molecular dynamics using the AMBER10 force field to relax local strain, improving accuracy by 2–3% without requiring templates. The method enables the prediction of structural changes induced by mutations, environmental perturbations such as pH or temperature, and provides insight into folding pathways and functional dynamics. Alternative physical parameters, including modified dielectric constants, solvent models, or entropy weighting factors, may be incorporated without departing from the disclosed framework. The method may be implemented on standard computing hardware, enabling rapid, template-free structure prediction for applications in drug design, protein engineering, and disease mechanism analysis.
