# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNICAL FIELD

- The present invention relates to the field of biotechnology, specifically to methods and systems for enabling efficient genetic manipulation of bacterial strains that are refractory to transformation due to the presence of complex restriction-modification systems. The invention provides a novel platform for mimicking the native DNA methylation patterns of recalcitrant bacterial species within a genetically engineered Escherichia coli host, thereby allowing exogenous plasmid DNA to evade degradation by cognate restriction enzymes in target organisms. This advancement facilitates the transfer of genetic material into previously inaccessible bacterial strains, including Gram-positive and Gram-negative species, chemoheterotrophs, and chemoautotrophs, thereby expanding the scope of microbial genetic engineering for industrial, environmental, and biomedical applications.

## BACKGROUND ART

- Restriction-modification systems are ubiquitous defense mechanisms in prokaryotes, composed of restriction endonucleases that cleave foreign DNA and DNA methyltransferases that protect endogenous DNA through site-specific methylation. These systems are classified into four major types based on subunit composition, cleavage site location, and cofactor requirements, with Type I, II, and III systems typically targeting unmethylated sequences, while Type IV systems recognize and degrade methylated DNA bearing non-native modification patterns. The prevalence of these systems is staggering, with approximately 95% of sequenced bacterial genomes encoding at least one restriction-modification system, and over one-third harboring four or more distinct systems. This diversity presents a formidable barrier to genetic manipulation, particularly in non-model organisms where transformation efficiency is severely limited or entirely absent due to the degradation of incoming plasmid DNA by host restriction enzymes.

- Conventional strategies to circumvent restriction barriers have included in vitro methylation of plasmid DNA using purified methyltransferases, heat inactivation of restriction enzymes, deletion of endogenous restriction genes, or the use of E. coli strains deficient in Dam and Dcm methylation. However, these approaches are often inadequate when dealing with strains possessing multiple, overlapping, or poorly characterized restriction-modification systems. In vitro methylation requires prior knowledge of recognition sequences and access to specific methyltransferases, which are frequently unavailable or incompatible with heterologous expression. Inactivation of restriction genes through gene knockout is labor-intensive and often incomplete, as many strains harbor cryptic or uncharacterized systems that remain functional. Furthermore, the use of Dam−/Dcm− E. coli hosts fails to address restriction mediated by Type IV systems that target methylated DNA, nor does it account for the complex methylation landscapes of organisms with multiple methyltransferases acting on diverse sequences.

- Existing techniques also suffer from low transformation efficiencies, inconsistent reproducibility, and limited scalability. For instance, even when plasmids are prepared from dam dcm mutant strains, transformation of certain Bacillus and Nitrobacter species remains inefficient or impossible, indicating the presence of additional, uncharacterized restriction barriers. Moreover, attempts to express methyltransferases from target organisms in standard E. coli hosts often lead to cytotoxicity due to unintended methylation of chromosomal DNA by orphan or promiscuous methyltransferases, which are not counteracted by the absence of protective methylation in the host. As a result, current methodologies are neither broadly applicable nor sufficiently robust to support routine genetic manipulation across diverse bacterial taxa.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

- The object of the present invention is to provide a universal and scalable method for overcoming restriction-modification barriers in bacterial strains that are refractory to genetic transformation by mimicking their native DNA methylation patterns within a genetically optimized Escherichia coli host. This is achieved by co-expressing multiple DNA methyltransferase-encoding genes derived from the target bacterium in an E. coli strain that has been engineered to lack all known restriction-modification systems and orphan methyltransferases, thereby enabling the in vivo methylation of plasmid DNA to precisely replicate the epigenetic signature of the recipient organism. The resulting methylation-modified plasmids escape recognition and cleavage by the target bacterium’s restriction enzymes, enabling high-efficiency transformation that was previously unattainable.

- The method involves the introduction of an exogenous DNA molecule, typically a plasmid vector, into a recombinant bacterium B, which is an E. coli strain lacking functional dam, dcm, hsdR, hsdM, hsdS, mrr, mcrA, and mcrBC genes, and which stably expresses one or more DNA-methyltransferase-encoding genes from a target bacterial species. The exogenous plasmid DNA is modified in vivo through the activity of these heterologous methyltransferases, resulting in a methylation pattern that mirrors that of the native host. The methylation-modified plasmid is then extracted and introduced into the target bacterium, where it is no longer recognized as foreign and can replicate, express transgenes, or integrate into the chromosome.

- The recombinant vector used in this method is an inducible expression plasmid containing multiple DNA-methyltransferase-encoding genes, each under the control of a promoter that allows for controlled expression in E. coli, such as the arabinose-inducible pBAD promoter. The genes are assembled into operons using homologous recombination in Saccharomyces cerevisiae, enabling the simultaneous expression of up to eight methyltransferases in a single construct. The target bacterium may be any bacterial species known to possess restriction-modification systems that impede transformation, including but not limited to Bacillus amyloliquefaciens, Bacillus cereus, and Nitrobacter hamburgensis.

- The DNA-methyltransferase-encoding genes are identified through genomic analysis and bioinformatic prediction based on sequence homology to known methyltransferase families, followed by functional validation through dot blot assays, liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry, and restriction enzyme protection assays. Predicted genes are cloned into an inducible expression vector, transformed into the engineered E. coli host, and tested for methylation activity. Once active methyltransferases are confirmed, they are co-expressed in the E. coli strain to generate a methylation profile that matches the target organism. The resulting plasmid DNA is then used to transform the target bacterium, with transformation efficiency quantified by colony-forming units per microgram of DNA.

- The invention further includes the engineered E. coli strain EC135, which is derived from TOP10 and contains deletions of dam, dcm, hsdR, hsdM, hsdS, mrr, mcrA, and mcrBC, with a wild-type recA allele reintroduced to restore viability. This strain serves as the universal chassis for methylation pattern mimicry. The exogenous DNA molecule may be any plasmid suitable for replication in both E. coli and the target bacterium, including shuttle vectors and integration plasmids. The method enables transformation efficiencies exceeding 10⁶ CFU/µg DNA in previously non-transformable strains, and allows for direct gene inactivation using non-replicative plasmids without the need for conjugation or transduction.

## SPECIFIC EMBODIMENTS

- The following examples illustrate the implementation of the invention in the context of three bacterial strains previously refractory to genetic manipulation: Bacillus amyloliquefaciens TA208, Bacillus cereus ATCC 10987, and Nitrobacter hamburgensis X14. Each example demonstrates the systematic identification, cloning, expression, and functional validation of methyltransferase genes, followed by the generation of methylation-modified plasmids and successful transformation of the target organism.

### Example 1

- A genetically engineered E. coli strain designated EC135 was constructed by sequentially deleting the dam, dcm, hsdR, hsdM, hsdS, mrr, mcrA, and mcrBC genes from the E. coli TOP10 background. Competent cells of TOP10 were prepared and transformed with plasmid pKD46, which encodes the lambda Red recombinase system. The chloramphenicol resistance gene, amplified by PCR from pKD3, was introduced into the dam locus using homologous recombination. Recombinants were selected on chloramphenicol-containing media and verified by PCR. Plasmid pKD46 was subsequently cured by growth at 42°C, and the recA gene was restored by transduction using P1vir phage carrying the wild-type recA allele from E. coli W3110. The dam gene was then knocked out using a similar recombination strategy, yielding strain EC135. Validation of the strain was confirmed by PCR, sequencing, and the absence of Dam and Dcm methylation activity, as demonstrated by resistance to DpnI digestion and sensitivity to MboI digestion.

### Examples 2

- The genome of Bacillus amyloliquefaciens TA208 was analyzed to predict DNA-methyltransferase-encoding genes using bioinformatic tools and the REBASE database. Four candidate genes—BAMTA208_6525, BAMTA208_6715, BAMTA208_19835, and BAMTA208_16660—were identified and cloned into the pBAD43 vector. Each gene was expressed individually in E. coli EC135, and methylation activity was confirmed by dot blot assays using antibodies against m6A, m4C, and m5C modifications. The genes were then assembled into a single operon on plasmid pWYE724 using homologous recombination in Saccharomyces cerevisiae, yielding plasmid pM.Bam. This plasmid was transformed into E. coli EC135, and methyltransferase expression was induced with arabinose. Shuttle plasmids pMK4, pAD123, pDG148StuI, and others were transformed into EC135/pM.Bam, induced for methylation, and extracted. These plasmids were then electroporated into B. amyloliquefaciens TA208, resulting in transformation efficiencies up to 3×10⁶ CFU/µg DNA, a 10⁴-fold increase over plasmids prepared from standard E. coli hosts. Integration plasmid pWYE748, carrying a chloramphenicol resistance cassette flanked by upp homology arms, was similarly transformed into EC135/pM.Bam and successfully introduced into TA208, enabling targeted gene inactivation. No transformants were obtained when plasmids were prepared from EC135 or TOP10 alone, confirming the necessity of the methylation mimicry system.

### Example 3

- For Bacillus cereus ATCC 10987, five predicted methyltransferase genes—BCE_0393, BCE_0365, BCE_4605, BCE_5606, and BCE_5607—were amplified and individually expressed in E. coli EC135. Dot blot and restriction enzyme protection assays confirmed their activity, with BCE_0393 shown to modify multiple sequences including GCWGC, GGCC, and CCGG. These genes were assembled into a single operon on plasmid pM.Bce. Plasmids pMK4, pMK3, pHCMC02, and pHCMC05 were transformed into EC135/pM.Bce, induced for methylation, and used to transform B. cereus ATCC 10987. Transformation efficiencies increased by up to 10³-fold compared to plasmids from control strains. The highest efficiency, 2×10⁷ CFU/µg DNA, was observed with pMK4. Transformants were verified by PCR and antibiotic resistance, and no growth was observed when plasmids were prepared from EC135 or TOP10 without pM.Bce, confirming the specificity of the methylation mimicry system.

### Example 4

- The genome of Nitrobacter hamburgensis X14 was screened for methyltransferase genes, and four candidates—Nham_0569, Nham_3225, Nham_0582, and Nham_0803—were identified. Nham_0569 and Nham_3225 were confirmed to modify GATC and GANTC sequences, respectively, through LC-MS and restriction enzyme assays. These genes, along with Nham_0582 and Nham_0803, were assembled into plasmid pM.Nham and introduced into E. coli EC135. The shuttle plasmid pWYE561, carrying the gfpmut3a gene under the control of the Nham_3450 promoter, was methylated in EC135/pM.Nham and transformed into N. hamburgensis X14 via electroporation. Green fluorescent protein expression was observed in cytoplasmic compartments of transformed cells under confocal microscopy, and flow cytometry revealed that 50.37% of the population was fluorescent. Plasmid rescue into E. coli TOP10 confirmed the integrity of pWYE561. No transformants were obtained using plasmids prepared from EC135 or TOP10 without pM.Nham, demonstrating the essential role of the methylation mimicry system in enabling the first successful genetic transformation of Nitrobacter.
