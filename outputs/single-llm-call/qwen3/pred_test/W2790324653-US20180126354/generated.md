# DESCRIPTION

## INTRODUCTION

- Multiple myeloma is a lethal plasma cell malignancy characterized by the clonal expansion of terminally differentiated B cells within the bone marrow microenvironment. This disease typically evolves through a stepwise progression from a clinically silent premalignant state known as monoclonal gammopathy of undetermined significance, which may advance to smoldering multiple myeloma before ultimately manifesting as overt, symptomatic disease. The transition from precursor stages to active myeloma is driven by the accumulation of somatic genomic alterations that confer proliferative advantage, evade immune surveillance, and promote resistance to apoptosis. These genetic events are heterogeneous in nature and can be broadly categorized into two major molecular subtypes: hyperdiploid and non-hyperdiploid myeloma. Hyperdiploid cases are defined by the presence of trisomies involving multiple odd-numbered chromosomes, including chromosomes 3, 5, 7, 9, 11, 15, 19, and 21, whereas non-hyperdiploid cases are frequently characterized by immunoglobulin heavy chain translocations that dysregulate oncogenic drivers through juxtaposition with potent B-cell-specific enhancer elements. The most commonly observed canonical translocations involve the IGH locus on chromosome 14q32 and partner genes such as CCND1, CCND3, FGFR3, MAF, MAFB, WHSC1, and WWOX, resulting in t(11;14), t(6;14), t(4;14), t(14;16), and t(14;20), respectively. These primary genetic lesions are often present in the premalignant phase and are considered initiating events. Secondary genetic alterations, which emerge during disease progression, include recurrent single-nucleotide variants in KRAS, NRAS, DIS3, and BRAF; focal copy number alterations such as amplification of 1q21 and deletion of 1p32, 6q21, 13q14, 14q32, and 17p13; and structural rearrangements involving the MYC locus on chromosome 8q24. The clinical behavior and therapeutic response of individual patients are strongly influenced by the combinatorial landscape of these alterations, which vary in frequency, clonality, and functional consequence. To account for this complexity, recent prognostic models have integrated traditional staging systems with molecular profiling data, enabling more precise risk stratification. These models demonstrate that the co-occurrence or mutual exclusivity of specific mutations can modulate survival outcomes, suggesting that comprehensive genomic characterization is essential for accurate prognostication. Current approaches to mutation detection, including whole-exome sequencing and fluorescence in situ hybridization, are limited by high cost, extended turnaround time, insufficient depth for subclonal variant detection, or narrow scope of genomic regions interrogated. Targeted sequencing platforms offer a promising alternative by enabling deep, cost-effective interrogation of disease-relevant loci while preserving the ability to detect diverse mutation types, including single-nucleotide variants, copy number alterations, and structural rearrangements, within a single assay.

- The premalignant phase of multiple myeloma, monoclonal gammopathy of undetermined significance, represents a critical window for early intervention and risk prediction. During this phase, the plasma cell clone exhibits minimal clinical symptoms but already harbors the initiating genetic lesions that define the future trajectory of the disease. The presence of specific translocations, such as t(11;14), or hyperdiploidy, is sufficient to distinguish high-risk MGUS from low-risk variants, indicating that the molecular architecture established early in disease evolution dictates long-term outcomes. The acquisition of secondary mutations, particularly those affecting the RAS/MAPK pathway, TP53, or DNA repair genes, correlates with progression to smoldering and then active myeloma. These events often occur in a temporally ordered fashion, with some mutations appearing as subclonal expansions that eventually dominate the tumor population. Understanding the sequence and context of these genetic events is essential for identifying patients at imminent risk of transformation and for designing preventive strategies. Furthermore, the persistence of certain mutations in the absence of overt disease suggests that some genomic alterations are permissive rather than causative, and their clinical significance must be interpreted in relation to coexisting lesions. The heterogeneity observed across patients underscores the need for comprehensive, multi-modal genomic profiling that captures the full spectrum of alterations, rather than relying on isolated assays for single mutation types.

- Genetic alterations in multiple myeloma are not randomly distributed but follow distinct patterns of recurrence, mutual exclusivity, and co-occurrence that reflect underlying biological dependencies and selective pressures. Hyperdiploid tumors rarely harbor IGH translocations, and vice versa, suggesting that these represent alternative pathways to malignant transformation. Similarly, mutations in KRAS and NRAS are almost always mutually exclusive, consistent with their redundant roles in signal transduction. In contrast, deletions of chromosome 13q frequently co-occur with amplification of 1q21, and both are associated with poor prognosis. The co-occurrence of specific translocations with deletions in DNA repair genes, such as ATM or BRCA2, suggests that genomic instability may be a driver of additional mutational burden. These patterns are not merely statistical but reflect functional interactions between pathways, such as the suppression of apoptosis by BCL2 upregulation in t(11;14) cases or the enhanced proteasome dependency in cases with MYC activation. The identification of these relationships enables the construction of molecular networks that define disease subtypes with distinct therapeutic vulnerabilities.

- The primary molecular subtypes of multiple myeloma are defined by the presence or absence of hyperdiploidy and IGH translocations, which together account for the majority of cases. Hyperdiploid myeloma is characterized by an odd number of chromosomes, with trisomies of chromosomes 3, 5, 7, 9, 11, 15, 19, and 21 occurring in a non-random pattern. These trisomies are thought to arise from a single catastrophic event during meiosis, leading to simultaneous gain of multiple chromosomes. In contrast, non-hyperdiploid myeloma is dominated by structural rearrangements involving the IGH locus, which place oncogenes under the control of powerful enhancers located in the immunoglobulin heavy chain region. These translocations are mediated by activation-induced cytidine deaminase activity during class switch recombination and are often associated with specific partner genes that dictate the clinical phenotype. For example, t(4;14) is associated with poor prognosis and sensitivity to proteasome inhibitors, whereas t(11;14) is linked to a more indolent course and responsiveness to venetoclax. These subtypes are largely mutually exclusive and represent distinct oncogenic programs that require different therapeutic approaches.

- Secondary genetic events in multiple myeloma are acquired during disease progression and are responsible for the transition from a quiescent, premalignant state to aggressive, therapy-resistant disease. These events include mutations in genes involved in the RAS/MAPK pathway, epigenetic regulation, protein homeostasis, and DNA damage repair. Amplification of 1q21, which harbors the CKS1B gene, is one of the most frequent secondary events and is associated with increased proliferation and resistance to therapy. Deletion of 17p13, which encompasses the TP53 tumor suppressor gene, is a high-risk lesion that confers poor survival and is often associated with clonal evolution following treatment. MYC translocations, which occur in approximately 15% of newly diagnosed cases, are typically late events that drive rapid proliferation and are associated with aggressive clinical behavior. These secondary alterations often arise in subclones that expand under selective pressure from therapy, leading to relapse and refractory disease.

- Prognostic models in multiple myeloma have evolved from clinical staging systems based on serum markers to integrated models that incorporate genomic data. The International Staging System, which relies on beta-2 microglobulin and albumin levels, has been superseded by models such as ISS-MUT, which combine clinical parameters with the presence of specific mutations and copy number alterations. These models demonstrate that the combination of translocations, single-nucleotide variants, and copy number changes provides superior risk stratification compared to any single class of alteration. For example, the presence of a t(4;14) translocation is associated with poor survival, but this risk is mitigated when co-occurring with trisomies of chromosomes 3 or 5. Similarly, the co-occurrence of del(13q) and amp(1q) defines a high-risk subgroup with significantly shorter progression-free survival. These findings underscore the importance of comprehensive genomic profiling in guiding clinical decision-making.

- Targeted sequencing approaches have emerged as a practical solution for the clinical implementation of genomic profiling in multiple myeloma. Unlike whole-exome sequencing, which requires extensive bioinformatic analysis and high sequencing depth to detect structural variants, targeted panels allow for deep, focused interrogation of disease-relevant genes at a fraction of the cost. These approaches enable the simultaneous detection of single-nucleotide variants, copy number alterations, and translocations in a single assay, facilitating rapid turnaround times and integration into routine clinical workflows. Commercially available panels have been developed for hematologic malignancies, but most lack comprehensive coverage of the IGH and MYC loci or fail to include key genes involved in DNA repair and B-cell biology. A tailored platform that captures the full spectrum of myeloma-relevant alterations is therefore needed to enable precision oncology in this disease.

- Existing studies have demonstrated the feasibility of targeted sequencing in multiple myeloma, but most have been limited by small sample sizes, incomplete genomic coverage, or lack of validation against gold-standard methods such as fluorescence in situ hybridization. Previous panels have targeted fewer than 100 genes and have not systematically interrogated the IGH locus beyond annotated switch regions. As a result, novel translocations, such as those involving IGLL5, have been missed. Furthermore, computational methods for detecting copy number alterations and translocations have not been optimized for the unique characteristics of myeloma genomes, including high levels of aneuploidy and somatic hypermutation. There remains a critical gap in the development of a validated, clinically applicable platform that integrates probe design, sequencing, and bioinformatic analysis to deliver comprehensive, accurate, and actionable genomic data for every patient.

## EXAMPLES

- The design and application of an oligonucleotide probe array for targeted sequence capture is illustrated through the development of a custom platform for multiple myeloma.

### Example 1

- Oligonucleotide probes are designed for a multiple myeloma-specific custom capture sequencing platform that targets 465 genes and the IGH and MYC loci. The probes are tiled across exonic, intronic, and regulatory regions to ensure comprehensive coverage. The platform is used to detect copy number alterations, single-nucleotide variants, and translocations in multiple myeloma samples with high sensitivity and specificity.

### Example 2

- Ninety-five paired tumor and normal cell DNA samples are sequenced using the custom capture platform. The samples are derived from bone marrow aspirates and peripheral blood, respectively. Sequencing achieves a mean depth of 104× for tumor samples and 107× for normal samples. The platform successfully detects known and novel mutations, including translocations involving IGLL5 and copy number alterations on chromosome 1q.

### Example 3

- Copy number alterations with prognostic significance are identified, including chromosome-level hyperdiploidy, arm-level amplifications of 1q and deletions of 1p, 13q, and 17p, and focal deletions of BRCA2 and ATM. CNV calls are filtered using a noise threshold derived from diploid regions. Somatic mutations are predicted to be deleterious using PolyPhen2 and SIFT. Multi-hit genes with bi-allelic mutations, such as TP53 and RB1, are identified as high-risk markers.

### Example 4

- IGH translocations are detected using LUMPY and filtered using a machine learning approach trained on fluorescence in situ hybridization data. Putative translocations are filtered based on thresholds for supporting split reads and discordant read pairs. Canonical translocations, including t(11;14) and t(4;14), are detected at expected frequencies. Novel translocations involving IGLL5 are validated using PCR and sequencing.

### Example 5

- Novel IGH translocations are prioritized as potential driver mutations by identifying cancer-associated genes within one megabase of the breakpoint. The t(14;22) translocation is found to juxtapose IGH with IGLL5, and the derivative chromosome 14 is found to carry the DERL3 gene. PCR amplification confirms the translocation, and RNA-seq data show overexpression of DERL3 in the same sample, suggesting a functional consequence.

### Example 6

- Intra- and inter-chromosomal MYC translocations are detected using LUMPY and filtered using a support vector machine trained on normal sample data. Five intra-chromosomal and two inter-chromosomal MYC translocations are identified, including rearrangements involving PVT1 and POU5F1B. These events are confirmed to be somatic and absent in matched normal samples.

### Example 7

- Non-silent single-nucleotide variants are identified in all 95 tumor samples, with a mean of 20 mutations per sample. Mutations are distributed across 443 genes, with frequent alterations in KRAS, NRAS, DIS3, and FAM46C. The majority of mutations are predicted to be deleterious by PolyPhen2 and SIFT.

### Example 8

- The presence of deeply subclonal variants is determined by performing additional sequencing of 15 tumor and normal samples to a mean depth of 1,259×. Comparison of allele frequencies between initial and deep sequencing reveals that the vast majority of newly discovered variants have low variant allele frequencies, suggesting they are subclonal. Only a small number of high-confidence variants are added with increased depth, indicating that 100× coverage is sufficient for most clinical applications.

### Example 9

- Integrative analysis across mutation types reveals patterns of mutual exclusivity and co-occurrence. IGLL5 mutations are mutually exclusive of RAS mutations and are enriched for a c-AID signature. Co-occurrence is detected between del(13q) and amp(1q), and between del(14q) and t(4;14). Survival analysis demonstrates that IGLL5 mutations are associated with disease progression, with a hazard ratio of 1.46 and a p-value of 0.03. These findings support the clinical utility of the platform for risk stratification and therapeutic decision-making.
