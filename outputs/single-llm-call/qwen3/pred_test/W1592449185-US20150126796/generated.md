## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

- relate to radiotherapy field

The present invention relates to the field of radiotherapy, specifically to systems and methods for ensuring accurate patient positioning during the delivery of ionizing radiation for the treatment of malignant and benign diseases. Radiotherapy involves the precise delivery of high-energy radiation beams to a defined target volume while minimizing exposure to surrounding healthy tissues. The advent of advanced techniques such as intensity-modulated radiation therapy (IMRT), volumetric-modulated arc therapy (VMAT), and stereotactic body radiotherapy (SBRT) has increased the clinical demand for submillimeter accuracy in patient setup. However, despite technological advancements in imaging and delivery systems, the risk of geometric miss due to human error in patient localization remains a persistent threat to treatment safety. This invention addresses these challenges by introducing a non-radiographic, optically-based, general-purpose patient safety system that operates independently of imaging modalities and treatment machine configurations. The system is designed to integrate seamlessly into existing radiotherapy workflows, providing real-time, continuous, and independent verification of patient position throughout all stages of treatment delivery. It is applicable across all anatomical sites, including head and neck, thoracic, abdominal, pelvic, and extremity regions, and is compatible with both coplanar and noncoplanar treatment geometries. By eliminating reliance on radiographic imaging for every setup verification, the system reduces unnecessary radiation exposure to patients and enhances operational efficiency without compromising safety. The invention is particularly suited for implementation in high-throughput clinical environments where workflow automation, reproducibility, and error detection are paramount.

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION



Radiotherapy is a complex, multistep process involving numerous personnel and technologies, and despite significant improvements in treatment planning and delivery systems, errors continue to occur with potentially catastrophic consequences. One of the most serious and frequently documented errors is geometric miss, which arises when the intended target volume receives an inadequate radiation dose due to incorrect patient positioning, resulting in either underdosing of the tumor or overdosing of critical normal structures. Such errors are particularly dangerous in hypofractionated regimens, where even minor setup inaccuracies can lead to severe morbidity or treatment failure. Studies have shown that up to 37.5% of critical radiotherapy incidents are attributable to incorrect patient setup, often stemming from misalignment during localization or failure to account for changes in patient anatomy or immobilization. While immobilization devices such as thermoplastic masks and custom body molds have improved setup reproducibility, they do not eliminate the possibility of human error during placement or indexing. Image-guided radiotherapy (IGRT) systems, including cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) and orthogonal radiographic imaging, have become standard for verifying patient position prior to treatment. However, these systems provide only a snapshot of patient geometry at the moment of imaging and cannot monitor for unintended shifts that occur after image acquisition—such as accidental couch movement, incorrect parameter overrides, or miscommunication during machine substitutions. Furthermore, CBCT is not feasible for noncoplanar treatments due to geometric constraints between the imaging source and treatment couch, and its accuracy is limited by registration errors, image artifacts, and slice thickness. Commercial non-radiographic optical tracking systems have been developed to provide continuous monitoring, but they are typically restricted to specific anatomical regions such as the head and neck or breast, requiring multiple reflective markers and manual configuration for each treatment site. These systems often demand significant therapist intervention to select the appropriate patient profile, input couch angles, or switch between devices, thereby increasing workload and introducing opportunities for error. Moreover, none of the existing systems offer a unified, automated, and universally applicable workflow that integrates with record-and-verify systems to eliminate manual data entry and ensure consistency across diverse clinical scenarios. Known methods using fiducial markers, such as metallic ball bearings placed during simulation, have been employed to establish reference points for localization, but their utility is limited to the simulation phase and cannot be carried forward into treatment without manual replacement and re-verification. The reliance on skin tattoos or temporary markers introduces additional variability and is incompatible with automated workflows. The absence of a general-purpose, fully automated, real-time patient localization system that operates independently of anatomical constraints, imaging modalities, and machine-specific parameters has created a critical gap in patient safety infrastructure. There is a compelling need for a system that can detect gross setup errors without requiring additional imaging, that can be deployed universally across all treatment sites, and that can operate seamlessly within existing clinical workflows without adding burden to staff.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION



The present invention introduces a novel method for tracking patient position during radiotherapy treatment sessions using a single infra-red reflecting marker (IRRM) whose location is predicted from a prior computed tomography (CT) simulation scan. The method begins with the placement of a computed tomography ball bearing (CT BB) on the patient’s skin or on a patient immobilization device at the time of simulation, ensuring that the marker is included within the scan volume. A treatment-simulation CT scan is then performed, and the three-dimensional coordinates of the CT BB are calculated relative to the treatment isocenter within the treatment planning system. These coordinates are transmitted to a record-and-verify system and subsequently imported into a dedicated patient safety system database, where they are associated with the patient’s treatment plan and beam-specific parameters. After the CT scan is completed, the CT BB is removed and replaced with a custom-designed infra-red reflecting marker, which is affixed to the same anatomical location—either directly on the patient's skin or permanently mounted on the immobilization device. The patient safety system employs a pair of high-resolution charge-coupled device (CCD) cameras mounted in the treatment room ceiling to continuously capture the position of the IRRM in real time. The system calculates the three-dimensional coordinates of the IRRM using triangulation and compares them against the predicted reference coordinates stored in the database, which were derived from the CT BB location and adjusted for couch rotation and beam geometry. Any deviation between the real-time position of the IRRM and the predicted reference position is displayed on a graphical user interface located both inside the treatment room and in the control area, enabling immediate visual feedback to the therapy team. If the displacement exceeds a predetermined threshold, an audible and visual alarm is triggered, and the radiation delivery system may be automatically shut off to prevent treatment delivery at an incorrect location. This method provides continuous, independent, and non-radiographic verification of patient setup accuracy throughout the entire treatment session, regardless of treatment modality, couch angle, or anatomical region, thereby eliminating reliance on repeated imaging and reducing the risk of gross setup errors.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The patient safety system comprises a fully integrated hardware and software architecture designed to ensure accurate patient localization in radiotherapy through continuous, non-radiographic, optical tracking of a single infra-red reflective marker. The system includes a pair of high-resolution CCD cameras mounted on the ceiling of the treatment room, forming the optical tracking system, which captures the spatial position of the marker from two distinct vantage points. Each camera is connected to a control box that houses the signal processing unit, which performs triangulation using the known relative positions of the cameras and the image coordinates of the marker to compute its three-dimensional location in the room’s coordinate system. The control box communicates with a dedicated computer system running proprietary software that interfaces with the treatment record and verify system via a standardized network protocol. The computer includes a central processing unit, main memory for real-time data buffering, static memory for persistent storage of patient reference coordinates, a high-speed bus for intercomponent communication, a display controller connected to a high-definition monitor for graphical visualization, an input device for operator commands, a cursor control device for navigation, a memory device for archival of daily quality assurance logs, a signal generation device for alarm triggers, a network interface device for secure file transfer, and a tangible computer-readable storage medium containing executable instructions for automated workflow execution. The operating system manages hardware resources and coordinates communication between the optical tracking system, the record and verify system, and the radiation delivery system. A communication controller and network adapter hardware facilitate seamless data exchange with the treatment planning system and the record and verify system, allowing automated import of patient-specific tracking data following treatment plan finalization. The treatment couch is equipped with indexing capabilities and is synchronized with the optical tracking system to allow for automatic couch adjustment based on real-time positional feedback. The infra-red reflective marker is a flat-surfaced, disposable device fabricated from a small round double-sided adhesive tape covered with a highly reflective coating, designed for easy application and removal without leaving residue. The marker is placed initially on the patient’s skin or immobilization device during CT simulation, where its location is identified as a point of interest and its coordinates are calculated relative to the treatment isocenter. After simulation, the marker is replaced with an identical infra-red reflective marker affixed to the exact same location, ensuring geometric continuity. The system’s calibration procedure involves the use of a five-marker jig placed at the isocenter during a CT scan to establish a transformation matrix between the camera’s native coordinate system and the room’s absolute coordinate system, ensuring submillimeter accuracy. The graphical user interface displays the three-dimensional difference between the real-time marker position and the predicted reference position, along with a vector value indicating the magnitude and direction of any deviation. An alarm system activates when deviations exceed preset thresholds, and the system is configured to automatically terminate radiation delivery if the error exceeds a safety cutoff. The workflow is fully automated: when a treatment beam is loaded in the record and verify system, a local file is generated, which the patient safety system scans and uses to retrieve the corresponding reference data. For patients treated with head and neck immobilization masks, the marker is permanently mounted on the mask during simulation, eliminating the need for daily placement. For body mold patients, the marker is transferred from the skin to the mold during the first treatment session after initial verification. For abdominal and pelvic patients, the marker may be placed on the skin initially, with the system adapting to breathing motion by averaging position over multiple respiratory cycles, and later transferred to the mold for subsequent sessions. Daily quality assurance is performed using a dedicated device with a permanently affixed marker, which is aligned weekly to the isocenter via CBCT to confirm system stability over time. Testing on anthropomorphic phantoms demonstrated accuracy within 2.3 mm under all conditions, including couch rotations up to 60 degrees. Retrospective studies on head and neck patients showed mean deviations of less than 2.5 mm compared to both the couch indexing system and CBCT, with residual errors after automatic couch motion consistently below 2.0 mm. In abdominal and pelvic patients, the system reliably detected gross setup errors exceeding 10 mm, even in the presence of breathing motion up to 16 mm, and maintained reproducibility within 1.0 mm over 12 weeks. The system’s independence from imaging modalities, treatment machines, and patient anatomy allows it to detect unusual errors such as incorrect couch parameter overrides, phantom couch indexing misalignment, or gantry-couch collisions that conventional systems fail to catch. The flat-surfaced marker design ensures visibility under optimal orientation, and reproducibility of patient positioning in body molds is ensured by aligning skin tattoos with pre-marked reference points on the mold. The system’s ability to function with a single marker eliminates complexity associated with multi-marker systems, and while it does not distinguish between translational and rotational errors, it effectively detects any deviation that compromises treatment accuracy. The autonomous operation, seamless integration with existing clinical systems, and proven capacity to prevent gross setup errors render the patient safety system an indispensable tool for modern radiotherapy.
