## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates to methods for measuring genomic DNA methylation loss through the analysis of specific CpG dinucleotide sequence contexts, particularly those defined by the Solo-WCGW motif, to determine the cumulative mitotic history of a cell or tissue sample. The invention provides a novel and precise approach for quantifying replication-associated DNA methylation loss across the genome, enabling the assessment of biological age, proliferative burden, and oncogenic risk in both normal and diseased tissues. This method is particularly useful in clinical diagnostics, cancer prognosis, and longitudinal monitoring of tissue turnover, as it captures a direct molecular signature of cell division events rather than indirect correlates of chronological aging or transcriptional activity.

## BACKGROUND

DNA methylation is a fundamental epigenetic modification that regulates gene expression, genomic stability, and cellular identity. Over time, global DNA methylation levels decline in a manner that correlates with aging and cellular proliferation, particularly within large genomic regions known as partially methylated domains (PMDs). These domains, which span hundreds of kilobases to multiple megabases, are characterized by reduced methylation compared to highly methylated domains (HMDs), and are enriched in late-replicating, lamina-associated, and heterochromatic regions. While hypomethylation in PMDs has been observed across diverse cell types and disease states, including cancer, the underlying mechanisms and molecular determinants of this phenomenon have remained incompletely understood. Previous studies have attributed PMD hypomethylation to a combination of factors, including CpG density, nucleotide flanking sequences, replication timing, and histone modifications such as H3K36me3. However, conflicting evidence has emerged regarding the relative contributions of these factors, particularly in distinguishing between methylation loss driven by passive dilution during cell division versus active demethylation processes. Moreover, prior methods for identifying PMDs relied on broad genomic segmentation algorithms that lacked the resolution to distinguish context-specific methylation dynamics, especially in tissues with low cancer cell purity or in samples with limited sequencing depth. The absence of a robust, sequence-defined molecular signature capable of accurately quantifying replication-associated methylation loss has hindered the development of reliable biomarkers for mitotic age, tissue turnover, and cancer progression. There remains a critical need for a method that isolates a minimal, universally conserved sequence motif capable of reflecting the cumulative number of cell divisions with high sensitivity, specificity, and reproducibility across species, developmental stages, and disease states.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The invention provides a method for determining the mitotic history of a cell or tissue sample by analyzing the methylation status of a defined set of CpG dinucleotides flanked by adenine or thymine residues on both sides—termed the Solo-WCGW motif—where no adjacent CpG dinucleotides are present within a 35-base-pair window. This motif, representing approximately 13% of all CpGs in the human genome, is uniquely susceptible to replication-associated methylation loss due to its low local CpG density and A/T-rich flanking context, which impair the processive activity of the maintenance methyltransferase DNMT1. By measuring the mean methylation level of all Solo-WCGW CpGs within common PMDs—defined as genomic regions exhibiting high inter-sample variability in methylation across diverse tissues—the invention enables the derivation of a quantitative measure of replication-associated DNA methylation loss that reflects the cumulative number of effective cell divisions. This measure is independent of chronological age, transcriptional activity, or environmental exposures, although such factors may accelerate the rate of methylation loss. The invention further provides for the exclusion of non-Solo-WCGW sequences, non-intergenic Solo-WCGW sequences, and Solo-WCGW sequences overlapping H3K36me3-marked chromatin, as these regions are subject to active methylation maintenance and do not reliably reflect passive dilution. The method can be applied to genomic DNA obtained from tissue biopsies, blood samples, or cell-free DNA, and requires only low-coverage whole-genome bisulfite sequencing or microarray-based methylation profiling. The invention further enables the identification of cell-type-invariant PMDs shared across normal and malignant tissues, the mapping of tissue-specific variation in PMD hypomethylation, and the determination of mitotic age for each tissue type based on the degree of Solo-WCGW hypomethylation. The method is applicable to human and mouse samples, and can be implemented using standard bioinformatics pipelines to analyze whole-genome bisulfite sequencing data, HM450 arrays, or single-cell methylomes. The invention further provides for the rescaling of methylation values based on sample-specific PMD hypomethylation levels, thereby normalizing for technical and biological variability and enabling cross-sample comparisons. The invention further enables the detection of PMD hypomethylation in fetal tissues, where it accumulates linearly during development, and in postnatal tissues, where it correlates with donor age and environmental stressors such as UV exposure. The invention further enables the identification of cancer cell or tissue samples based on the degree of PMD hypomethylation, which is nearly universal across tumor types and correlates strongly with somatic mutation density, copy number aberration burden, and LINE-1 retrotransposon insertion frequency. The invention further enables the determination of the number of effective cell divisions by comparing the Solo-WCGW methylation level of a test sample to a reference calibration curve derived from samples of known mitotic history.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

The invention is based on the discovery that a specific sequence motif—Solo-WCGW—is the most reliable genomic indicator of replication-associated DNA methylation loss. This motif consists of a single CpG dinucleotide flanked on both sides by adenine or thymine residues, with no other CpG dinucleotides within a 35-base-pair window. The presence of this motif renders the CpG highly vulnerable to incomplete re-methylation following DNA replication, due to the processive nature of DNMT1, which preferentially methylates clustered CpG sites. The absence of neighboring CpGs prevents DNMT1 from efficiently maintaining methylation at Solo-WCGW sites, resulting in progressive hypomethylation with each cell division. This effect is further amplified in late-replicating genomic regions, where the time window available for re-methylation prior to mitosis is shortest. The invention further recognizes that the presence of the H3K36me3 histone mark overrides this replication-associated loss by recruiting DNMT3B to actively maintain methylation at otherwise vulnerable CpGs. Therefore, the invention excludes all Solo-WCGW motifs overlapping H3K36me3-marked regions, as these are subject to active maintenance and do not reflect passive dilution. The invention further excludes Solo-WCGW motifs located within gene bodies, promoters, or CpG islands, as these regions are subject to regulatory methylation dynamics unrelated to mitotic history. The invention further excludes non-intergenic Solo-WCGW motifs, as these are often influenced by transcriptional activity and chromatin remodeling. The invention further excludes cell-type-invariant methylation proxies that are not sequence-dependent, ensuring that the measurement is driven solely by the intrinsic susceptibility of the Solo-WCGW motif to replication-associated loss. The invention further provides for the localization of Solo-WCGW motifs on individual chromosomes or across the entire genome, allowing for the construction of genome-wide maps of mitotic history. The invention further provides for the variation of the x value in the WCGW motif definition, where x may be any nucleotide, and for the definition of PMDs by late replication timing, nuclear lamina localization, or Hi-C-defined heterochromatic compartment B, all of which are correlated with increased hypomethylation. The invention further provides for the assessment of the standard deviation of methylation across samples within 100-kilobase bins to identify PMDs, where high SD indicates regions of variable hypomethylation characteristic of PMDs. The invention further provides for the identification of common PMDs across cell or tissue types, which are shared regardless of lineage, and for the identification of cell-type-specific PMDs, which reflect differential proliferative rates. The invention further provides for the determination of the number of effective cell divisions by comparing the mean methylation level of Solo-WCGW CpGs in a test sample to a calibrated reference set of samples with known mitotic history, such as primary cells passaged in vitro or tissues from donors of known age. The invention further provides for the determination of effective cell divisions using elastic net regression, individual probe regression, or other statistical modeling techniques trained on large-scale WGBS datasets. The invention further provides for the use of the Solo-WCGW methylation signature to distinguish between normal and cancerous tissues, as nearly all tumors exhibit profound PMD hypomethylation, even when derived from tissues with low baseline turnover. The invention further provides for the correlation of PMD hypomethylation with somatic mutation density, copy number aberrations, and LINE-1 insertion frequency, establishing a mechanistic link between replicative history and genomic instability. The invention further provides for the rescaling of methylation values within common PMD bins to normalize for sample-specific hypomethylation levels, enabling accurate cross-tissue and cross-donor comparisons. The invention further provides for the application of this method to low-coverage or single-cell WGBS data, where traditional segmentation methods fail, due to the high signal-to-noise ratio conferred by the Solo-WCGW motif. The invention further provides for the use of this method in fetal tissues, where PMD hypomethylation accumulates linearly from early gestation, and in postnatal tissues, where it increases with chronological age and environmental exposure. The invention further provides for the use of this method in hematopoietic lineages, where lymphoid cells exhibit greater hypomethylation than myeloid cells, reflecting their higher proliferative turnover. The invention further provides for the use of this method in skin, liver, colon, and other tissues with high stem cell activity, where PMD hypomethylation is most pronounced. The invention further provides for the use of this method in immortalized cell lines, where PMD hypomethylation is uniformly deep and correlates with passage number. The invention further provides for the use of this method in gametes and early embryos, where PMD structure emerges progressively during development, distinguishing passive dilution from active demethylation events. The invention further provides for the use of this method in clinical diagnostics, where the degree of PMD hypomethylation serves as a biomarker for cancer risk, tumor aggressiveness, and response to therapy.

### Example 1

The Solo-WCGW sequence motif is defined as a CpG dinucleotide flanked on both sides by adenine or thymine residues, with no other CpG dinucleotides within a 35-base-pair window. Analysis of TCGA tumors and adjacent normal samples using whole-genome bisulfite sequencing revealed that these sites are consistently and significantly hypomethylated compared to other CpG contexts. The MethPipe27 method was used to define shared PMDs across 49 core samples, and local CpG density and tetranucleotide sequence contexts were determined to be predictive of hypomethylation. Low CpG density and the WCGW context were found to act additively to promote hypomethylation, with the solo-WCGW combination showing the strongest effect. This pattern was confirmed in both human and mouse colorectal tissues, and was reproducible across all tumor and adjacent normal samples in the core set. The same sequence dependencies were observed in 390 additional human and 206 mouse WGBS samples, with the exception of germ cell samples. Solo-WCGW CpGs enabled accurate determination of PMD structure even in weakly hypomethylated tumors and in down-sampled WGBS data with coverage as low as 0.05×. The same sequence dependencies were observed in HM450 microarray data, confirming the robustness of the signature across platforms. FIG. 1A-C illustrate the hypomethylation propensity of solo-WCGW CpGs across multiple tissue types. FIGS. 10A1-A3 and B1-B2 demonstrate the reproducibility of this pattern in independent datasets. FIGS. 11A-C and 12A-B show the ability of solo-WCGW CpGs to resolve PMD structure in low-coverage and single-cell WGBS data.

### Example 2

Strong concordance was observed between the genomic locations of PMDs across all tumor and normal samples analyzed. The average methylation of solo-WCGW CpGs in core tumors was significantly lower than in core normals, yet the spatial organization of PMDs was highly conserved. PMDs ranged from 100 kilobases to 5 megabases in size and overlapped extensively between tumors and their adjacent normal tissues. The standard deviation of methylation within 100-kilobase bins was bimodally distributed in both tumor and normal samples, with high-SD bins defining PMDs and low-SD bins defining HMDs. Using the bimodal peaks as a classifier, 83% concordance was achieved between tumor and normal PMD assignments. This SD-based method identified 95% of previously reported PMDs in colorectal cancer and 93% of PMDs in the IMR90 fibroblast cell line. Rescaling of methylation values based on sample-specific PMD hypomethylation improved cross-sample comparability. FIGS. 2A-F and 13-14 illustrate the high degree of structural conservation in PMD/HMD organization across tissue types and disease states.

### Example 3

Combining the TCGA dataset with 343 published human and 206 mouse WGBS samples revealed that PMD structure is largely shared across five of six major cell type categories, including post-natal tissues, immortalized cell lines, hematopoietic cells, and solid tumors. The germline and embryo category was the only exception, showing distinct PMD boundaries. Common PMDs overlapped with lamina-associated regions and late-replicating domains. Immortalized cell lines exhibited strong PMD hypomethylation consistent with their proliferative history. Post-natal tissues showed shared PMD structure despite differences in tissue origin. Blood cell types, including both lymphoid and myeloid lineages, exhibited shared PMD architecture, with activated lymphocytes showing greater hypomethylation than naïve cells. PMD hypomethylation was already detectable in naïve B and T cells, indicating that mitotic history begins early in lineage development. Myeloid tumors showed shallower hypomethylation than lymphoid tumors, reflecting their lower proliferative rate. FIGS. 3A-E, 4, 15A-C, and 16 demonstrate the shared PMD structure across developmental lineages and disease states.

### Example 4

Analysis of human sperm, germinal vesicle oocytes, inner cell mass, blastocysts, and embryonic somatic tissues revealed that PMD structure emerges progressively during development. Human sperm was highly methylated with minimal PMD structure, whereas germinal vesicle oocytes showed deep PMD hypomethylation. Inner cell mass and blastocyst samples retained weak PMDs resembling those of oocytes, indicating that PMD boundaries are established prior to somatic differentiation. Primordial germ cells exhibited near-complete methylation erasure, precluding PMD identification. Embryonic somatic tissues rapidly reacquired methylation, but PMD structure became clearly resolvable only in later fetal stages. The progressive emergence of PMD/HMD structure along organismal development suggests that replication-associated methylation loss begins early in embryogenesis. FIG. 5a-c illustrate the developmental trajectory of PMD hypomethylation.

### Example 5

Analysis of CD4+ T cells from a newborn and a 103-year-old individual revealed a dramatic difference in solo-WCGW methylation, with the aged sample resembling a leukemia. PBMC samples from newborns exhibited significantly less PMD hypomethylation than those from elderly donors. Fetal liver samples showed a nearly linear accumulation of hypomethylation from 9 to 22 weeks of gestation. Mouse fetal tissues showed a similar association between gestational age and hypomethylation. Sun exposure accelerated PMD hypomethylation in epidermal cells, but not in dermal cells, indicating that environmental stressors increase cell turnover. Diverse hematopoietic cell types showed a significant association between donor age and hypomethylation, with lymphoid cells exhibiting a steeper rate of loss than myeloid cells. FIG. 6a-f illustrate the relationship between PMD hypomethylation and chronological age, developmental stage, and environmental exposure.

### Example 6

Analysis of 9,072 tumors from 33 cancer types using HM450 solo-WCGW probes located within common PMDs revealed that PMD hypomethylation is nearly universal in cancer. The degree of hypomethylation correlated with that of the tissue of origin and was strongly associated with somatic mutation density and copy number aberration burden. LINE-1 insertion breakpoints were significantly enriched in PMD regions, and tumors with deeper PMD hypomethylation had higher LINE-1 insertion density in eight of nine cancer types. The 60 genes most strongly associated with PMD hypomethylation were enriched for Gene Ontology terms related to proliferation and mitotic cell division. These genes were also enriched within a curated list of cell-cycle dependent genes. Proliferative tumors exhibited high expression of DNMT1, DNMT3A/B, and UHRF1, indicating that hypomethylation occurs despite active maintenance machinery. TET gene expression was not elevated in hypomethylated tumors, ruling out active demethylation as a primary driver. These findings collectively demonstrate that cumulative mitotic cell divisions are the major driving force behind PMD hypomethylation in cancer. FIG. 7a-g illustrate the association between PMD hypomethylation and genomic instability, proliferation, and retrotransposon activity.

### Example 7

Analysis of the IMR90 cell line confirmed that PMD/HMD structure coincides with nuclear architecture, as defined by replication timing, lamina association, and Hi-C compartmentalization. Solo-WCGW CpG methylation was most strongly correlated with replication timing, followed by H3K36me3. The 14% of Solo-WCGWs overlapping H3K36me3 were highly methylated regardless of replication timing, while the remaining 86% showed strong replication timing dependence. The contribution of replication timing to methylation maintenance was greater than that of H3K36me3 in IMR90 cells, but reversed in H1 embryonic stem cells. This supports a model in which H3K36me3-mediated DNMT3B recruitment overrides replication timing effects. The independent contributions of these two factors are consistent with the re-methylation window model. FIG. 8a-d illustrate the disentanglement of replication timing and H3K36me3 effects on methylation maintenance.

### Example 8

Cancer types selected for WGBS analysis included lung squamous cell carcinoma, lung adenocarcinoma, breast, colorectal, endometrial, stomach, bladder, and glioblastoma. Paired-end WGBS was performed using a standard protocol with bisulfite conversion, size selection, and Illumina sequencing. CpGs with fewer than ten reads of coverage were excluded. A 100-kilobase window size was chosen to balance resolution and statistical power. Preliminary PMD/HMD domains were defined using MethPipe27. Final PMD/HMD classification was performed using a Gaussian mixture model based on the standard deviation of solo-WCGW methylation across 40 tumor samples. The threshold for PMD classification was set at 0.125. Common PMDs and HMDs were defined using stricter thresholds of 0.15 and 0.10, respectively. Mouse PMDs were defined similarly using 32 postnatal samples with a threshold of 0.09. HM450 data were preprocessed using background subtraction and dye-bias correction. Probes targeting solo-WCGW CpGs were retained, and those overlapping CpG islands or hypomethylated in normals were excluded. IMR90 epigenome features were clustered using Spearman’s correlation. Centromeres were excluded. Histone mark signals were quantified using overlap with gapped peaks. Gene bodies were extracted from GENCODE annotation. Methylation values were rescaled within common PMD bins by trimming the top and bottom 20% and linearly scaling the remainder. Solo-WCGW CpGs were stratified by H3K36me3 overlap and replication timing quartiles. Statistics were computed using one-tailed Wilcoxon’s Rank Sum test. The SD method demonstrated high power and concordance with existing PMD definitions. The incorporation of solo-WCGW sequence features improves detection of differential PMDs. Potential applications include cancer diagnostics, aging biomarkers, and tissue turnover monitoring.

### Example 10

An elastic net modeling strategy was employed to predict mitotic age. PDL was standardized across cell lines. Multi-tissue elastic net regression was performed using 10-fold cross-validation and probe reduction. Model performance was evaluated using R-squared and mean absolute error. The elastic net strategy was compared to individual probe regression, which applied simple linear regression to each prefiltered Solo-WCGW probe. Regression coefficients were compared, and probes were filtered based on correlation with known mitotic history. Model performance was evaluated using the same metrics. The individual probe regression strategy outperformed elastic net in terms of interpretability and biological relevance. Differences in probe landscapes were observed, with individual probe regression selecting a more biologically coherent set of sites. Correlation coefficients and intercepts were compared across models. The solo-WCGW clock was compared to existing clocks, including the Hannum clock, DNAm Age, Skin & Blood Clock, DNAm PhenoAge, and epiTOC clock. The solo-WCGW clock uniquely reflects mitotic history rather than chronological age or phenotypic aging. The solo-WCGW clock is more accurate in tissues with high turnover and is less influenced by transcriptional noise. Additional exemplary methods include machine learning approaches, neural networks, and Bayesian modeling for mitotic age prediction.

The invention provides a method for studying the effects of drugs, toxins, or environmental agents on cellular proliferation by measuring changes in PMD hypomethylation. The method includes exposing cells or organisms to a test agent, obtaining samples at multiple timepoints, determining mitotic age at each timepoint, and calculating the rate of methylation loss. An accelerated rate indicates increased proliferation or genomic instability. The method further includes comparing the effect across different cell types. The method further includes identifying agents that reduce mitotic age. The method further includes screening for anti-proliferative compounds. The method further includes validating findings in animal models. The method further includes correlating mitotic age with toxicity endpoints. The method further includes generating a proliferation index for toxicology screening. The method further includes integrating with high-throughput screening platforms. The method further includes publishing standardized protocols for environmental health research. The method further includes submitting data to public repositories for reproducibility.
