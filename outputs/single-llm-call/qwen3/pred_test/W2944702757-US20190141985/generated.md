## FIELD OF THE DISCLOSED SUBJECT MATTER

Organ transplantation represents a definitive therapeutic intervention for patients with end-stage organ failure, offering the potential for prolonged survival and improved quality of life. Among the most critically needed transplants are those involving the lungs, which are frequently rendered unsuitable for transplantation due to injury sustained at the time of donor death. These injuries, including aspiration of gastric contents, pulmonary contusion, ventilator-induced lung injury, and infection, result in the rejection of up to 80% of potential donor lungs. The clinical imperative to expand the donor pool has driven innovation in organ preservation and recovery technologies, yet current methods remain insufficient to salvage severely damaged organs. The disclosed subject matter pertains to a novel extracorporeal support system that enables prolonged, physiologically regulated maintenance of donor organs outside the body, with particular application to the recovery of lungs that have sustained acute, severe injury incompatible with conventional preservation techniques. This system facilitates not only the stabilization of organ function but also the delivery of targeted therapeutic interventions, thereby transforming the paradigm of organ allocation and transplantation.

## BACKGROUND OF THE DISCLOSED SUBJECT MATTER

Organ transplantation has evolved into a standard of care for numerous end-stage diseases, yet its impact is severely limited by the persistent and growing disparity between the number of patients awaiting transplants and the availability of viable donor organs. Despite advances in surgical technique, immunosuppression, and postoperative care, the shortage of transplantable organs remains one of the most profound challenges in modern medicine. Lungs, in particular, are among the most frequently discarded organs due to their heightened susceptibility to injury during the donor’s terminal event. Common causes of lung injury include aspiration of gastric contents, which introduces acidic, enzymatic, and particulate material into the airways, triggering acute pneumonitis, epithelial denudation, capillary leak, and profound inflammation. These injuries result in hypoxemia, reduced compliance, and diffuse alveolar damage, rendering the lungs non-viable for transplantation under current clinical criteria. Ex vivo lung perfusion has emerged as a promising strategy to assess and rehabilitate marginal donor lungs, employing normothermic perfusion with blood-based or crystalloid solutions to reduce edema and improve gas exchange over a limited time window of approximately six hours. However, this approach has demonstrated limited efficacy in the context of severe aspiration injury, where persistent inflammation, surfactant dysfunction, and structural disruption overwhelm the capacity of static perfusion systems to restore function. Extracorporeal membrane oxygenation has been utilized in select cases for temporary respiratory support in critically ill patients, but its application to donor organ preservation has been constrained by the inability to sustain physiological homeostasis beyond short durations, the absence of systemic regulatory feedback, and the lack of integration with therapeutic delivery mechanisms. Initiatives to increase the donor pool have included the use of donation after circulatory death, machine perfusion, and even xenogeneic organ generation, yet none have addressed the fundamental limitation of time and physiological fidelity in extracorporeal organ maintenance. Conventional preservation techniques rely on cold ischemia, which halts metabolic activity but does not prevent the progression of ischemia-reperfusion injury upon reperfusion. Even the most advanced perfusion systems lack the capacity to replicate the dynamic, multi-system regulation provided by a living host, including autonomic control of vascular tone, immune modulation, metabolic clearance, and endocrine signaling. As a result, the window for intervention remains narrow, and the opportunity for true regeneration—beyond mere stabilization—is unattainable. There is a critical and unmet need for a system capable of sustaining organ viability for prolonged periods under normothermic, physiologic conditions while enabling continuous monitoring, real-time therapeutic intervention, and the induction of regenerative processes. Such a system would not only expand the donor pool but fundamentally alter the trajectory of organ transplantation by transforming discarded organs into viable grafts.

## SUMMARY

The disclosed subject matter introduces a novel system and method for normothermic extracorporeal support of donor organs through cross-circulation with a living host, enabling prolonged preservation, functional recovery, and regenerative repair of severely damaged organs that are otherwise deemed unsuitable for transplantation. This system permits the continuous, physiologically regulated perfusion of an extracorporeal organ for durations exceeding 36 hours, during which time the organ remains in a state of dynamic homeostasis maintained by the systemic feedback mechanisms of the host. The extracorporeal organ is contained within a sealed, temperature-controlled chamber designed to mimic the natural biomechanical and thermal environment of the thoracic cavity. Blood flow to and from the organ is regulated through a closed-loop circuit that connects the organ’s native vasculature directly to the circulatory system of a healthy host, allowing for auto-regulation of perfusion pressure, oxygen delivery, and metabolic waste clearance without reliance on external pumps or artificial control algorithms. The cross-circulation circuit incorporates an integrated heat exchanger to maintain normothermic conditions, a pressure-controlled perfusion system to preserve physiologic trans-pulmonary gradients, and a biologic cuff device that utilizes the host’s own vascular structures as a natural conduit for venous drainage, minimizing thrombogenicity and preserving endothelial integrity. The system further includes an organ-specific chamber stand with automatic height adjustability to accommodate anatomical variations, a rechargeable power source with backup capability to ensure uninterrupted operation, and a patient safety clamp with an automatic clamping mechanism that responds to hemodynamic instability by immediately isolating the extracorporeal circuit. The disclosed method enables the treatment of extracorporeal organs subjected to acute, severe injury—such as those damaged by gastric aspiration—by facilitating repeated therapeutic interventions including bronchoalveolar lavage, surfactant replacement, alveolar recruitment, and targeted delivery of cells or therapeutic agents. The system supports the recovery of structural and functional integrity of the pulmonary epithelium and microvasculature, including restoration of tight junctions, surfactant production, ciliary activity, and endothelial viability. Upon successful recovery, the extracorporeal organ may be transplanted into a recipient without the need for extended cold ischemia, thereby preserving its regenerative potential and improving post-transplant outcomes. This approach represents a paradigm shift in organ preservation, moving beyond temporary stabilization to active, sustained regeneration under physiologic conditions.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE DISCLOSED SUBJECT MATTER

The disclosed subject matter encompasses a cross-circulation support platform that enables prolonged, physiologically regulated extracorporeal maintenance of donor organs by establishing a direct, biologically integrated circulatory connection between the organ and a living host. Unlike conventional extracorporeal membrane oxygenation or ex vivo lung perfusion systems, which rely on mechanical pumps and artificial perfusates, this system leverages the host’s own cardiovascular and homeostatic regulatory mechanisms to sustain organ viability. The limitations of current organ transplant methods stem from their inability to replicate the dynamic, multi-system environment of the body, resulting in progressive deterioration of organ function during preservation, particularly in the context of severe injury. The invention overcomes these limitations by providing a platform capable of maintaining organ function for durations exceeding 36 hours under normothermic conditions, during which time the organ is continuously perfused with whole blood, exposed to physiological oxygen tensions, and subjected to natural metabolic and immune regulation. The system’s capabilities extend far beyond stabilization, enabling the delivery of multiple, sequential therapeutic interventions—including bronchoalveolar lavage, surfactant administration, alveolar recruitment maneuvers, and targeted cellular therapies—without compromising hemodynamic stability or introducing exogenous contaminants. Applications of the invention include the recovery of lungs injured by gastric aspiration, ventilator-induced lung injury, or sepsis; the regeneration of hearts, kidneys, or livers subjected to prolonged ischemia; and the preparation of xenogeneic organs for human transplantation. The mobile platform for prolonged extracorporeal support integrates a sterile, temperature-controlled organ chamber with integrated monitoring systems, allowing for transport and use in clinical or research settings. The connection between the host and the extracorporeal organ is established via a biologic cuff device that employs the host’s native vascular structures as a biological bridge, eliminating the need for synthetic grafts and reducing the risk of thrombosis. The system supports organ recovery by maintaining perfusion pressure, oxygen delivery, and metabolic clearance at physiologic levels, while simultaneously enabling the host’s immune system to modulate inflammation and promote tissue repair. Additional research applications include the study of regenerative pathways, the evaluation of novel therapeutics, and the development of biomarkers for organ viability. The system may also be employed for cross-circulation between an organ recipient and a donor organ, allowing for pre-transplant conditioning and immune tolerance induction. In alternative embodiments, a healthy animal may serve as the cross-circulation host, enabling the recovery of human donor organs in a xenogeneic bioreactor configuration. The human-xenogeneic bioreactor application offers a scalable solution for organ regeneration, particularly in cases where human hosts are unavailable or contraindicated. The benefits of this approach include the preservation of native vascular architecture, the maintenance of endogenous signaling pathways, and the avoidance of artificial perfusate toxicity. Additional uses of the human-xenogeneic bioreactor include the decellularization and recellularization of targeted lung segments, the delivery of stem cell populations, and the assessment of organ-specific immune responses. The cross-circulation platform presents unprecedented opportunities for organ recovery, enabling interventions that are currently impossible with ex vivo systems, such as regional delivery of microbeads, real-time imaging of cellular dynamics, and the reconstruction of epithelial barriers. The systems, methods, and devices for cross-circulation support of extracorporeal organs are configured to ensure hemodynamic stability, minimize hemolysis, and prevent systemic inflammatory responses in the host. Envisioned uses of cross-circulation include the rescue of severely damaged lungs, the expansion of donor pools for pediatric and adult transplantation, and the development of personalized organ repair protocols. The benefits of cross-circulation over conventional EVLP include sustained physiological regulation, prolonged support duration, enhanced regenerative capacity, and the ability to perform repeated, complex interventions without loss of organ function. ## EXAMPLES

### Methods

A pilot study was designed to evaluate the feasibility of prolonged normothermic extracorporeal support of severely injured lungs using cross-circulation. The hypothesis was that cross-circulation could sustain organ viability for 36 hours while enabling functional recovery and structural regeneration following acute gastric aspiration injury. The study was conducted using eight donor–recipient pairs of miniature swine, selected for defined major histocompatibility complex compatibility to minimize immunologic cross-reactivity. Animals were maintained in accordance with institutional guidelines for animal care and use, and all procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee. Gastric contents were standardized to a pH of 2.00 ± 0.05 and delivered bronchoscopically into a single lung of each donor animal, followed by a six-hour period of in vivo injury development. Lungs were then explanted, cannulated using a biologic cuff device constructed from the aortic arch, and connected to the extracorporeal circuit. The recipient swine were anesthetized, cannulated via the internal jugular veins, and connected to the circuit to initiate cross-circulation. Physiological parameters of both donor and recipient were monitored continuously, including heart rate, mean arterial pressure, oxygen saturation, and arterial blood gases. Extracorporeal lung function was assessed through periodic measurement of trans-pulmonary pressure gradients, dynamic compliance, PaO2/FiO2 ratios, and lung weight. Bronchoalveolar lavage was performed at six time points over 36 hours, followed by surfactant replacement and alveolar recruitment maneuvers. BAL fluid was analyzed for pepsin concentration, pH, protein content, inflammatory cytokines, and exosome levels. Histopathologic analysis was conducted at 0, 18, and 36 hours using hematoxylin and eosin, pentachrome, and immunohistochemical staining for aquaporin-5, surfactant protein C, zonula occludens-1, and CD31. Scanning and transmission electron microscopy were employed to assess ultrastructural integrity of the alveolar epithelium and microvasculature. Metabolic activity was quantified using the Alamar Blue assay, myeloperoxidase activity was measured via enzymatic assay, and surfactant uptake was confirmed using BODIPY-labeled surfactant protein B. Thermal imaging was performed at regular intervals to assess regional surface temperature changes, and hypothermic probe tests were conducted to evaluate rewarming kinetics. All analyses were performed in a blinded fashion, and statistical significance was determined using one-way analysis of variance with Tukey’s post hoc test and Student’s t-test, with a p-value of less than 0.05 considered significant.
