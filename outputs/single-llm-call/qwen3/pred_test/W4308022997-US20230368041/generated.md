## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

### 1. Field of the Invention

This invention relates to reinforcement learning methods for improving sample efficiency, learning stability, and long-term policy retention in model-free, off-policy reinforcement learning systems. Specifically, the invention provides a framework for structuring and sampling experience data through stratified partitioning of an experience replay buffer based on temporally extended state histories leading to predefined events. The method enables reinforcement learning agents to accelerate convergence toward optimal policies by prioritizing the replay of trajectories that are causally linked to meaningful behavioral milestones, while preserving the theoretical guarantees of Bellman optimality and mitigating catastrophic forgetting across extended training horizons.

### 2. Description of Prior Art and Related Information

Prior approaches to experience replay in deep reinforcement learning have relied predominantly on uniform random sampling from a monolithic buffer of state-action-reward-next-state tuples. While this method improves data efficiency over on-policy learning, it suffers from critical inefficiencies in environments where high-reward or high-impact events occur infrequently relative to the total number of state transitions. In such domains, the probability of sampling a transition directly associated with a critical behavioral milestone—such as passing another vehicle, crossing a threshold, or reaching a goal—is vanishingly small under uniform sampling, resulting in prolonged training times and unstable convergence. 

Prioritized Experience Replay (PER) was introduced to address this limitation by biasing sampling toward transitions with high temporal difference (TD) error, under the assumption that these transitions are most informative for value function updates. However, PER exhibits a fundamental flaw: it prioritizes transitions based on prediction error rather than behavioral relevance. As a result, experiences with high TD error may originate from states that are unlikely to be visited under the optimal policy, leading to misdirected learning, increased variance, and suboptimal policy degradation. Furthermore, in environments with dense shaping rewards or continuous state spaces, TD errors become volatile and poorly correlated with true policy improvement, rendering PER ineffective or even detrimental.

Other methods have attempted to improve sampling by partitioning the replay buffer based on reward magnitude, goal states, or topological structure. These approaches, while conceptually insightful, fail to capture the causal history leading to an event. Storing only the terminal state or immediate transition associated with a milestone ignores the critical sequence of prior actions and states that enabled the event, thereby preventing the propagation of value information backward from the event to the initial state. This results in fragmented learning and an inability to form coherent “fast lanes” of value backup that span the full trajectory from start to milestone.

Additionally, existing techniques for mitigating catastrophic forgetting in reinforcement learning rely on task-specific buffer partitioning, replay rehearsal, or regularization penalties. These methods are either computationally expensive, require explicit task boundaries, or are incompatible with single-task, continuous environments where skills must be maintained over time without discrete task switches. None of these prior methods provide a mechanism to dynamically preserve knowledge of rare but critical events—such as avoiding off-course driving in a racing simulator or navigating through a bottleneck state—when such events become statistically rare during training.

There remains a critical unmet need for a sampling framework that leverages domain knowledge to identify meaningful behavioral events, retains the full temporal context leading to those events, and systematically over-samples those contexts to accelerate learning without introducing bias or instability. The invention disclosed herein fulfills this need by introducing Stratified Sampling from Event Tables (SSET), a novel method that partitions the experience replay buffer into event-specific sub-buffers, each containing the complete history of transitions preceding a defined event, and employs stratified sampling to ensure consistent exposure of the agent to these high-value trajectories.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The invention introduces a novel framework for reinforcement learning known as Stratified Sampling from Event Tables (SSET), which enhances the sample efficiency, learning stability, and long-term retention of policies in model-free, off-policy reinforcement learning systems. The framework operates by defining a set of event specifications, each comprising a Boolean event condition and a history length, and dynamically partitioning the experience replay buffer into multiple event tables based on these specifications. Each event table stores a finite-length trajectory of state-action-reward sequences that immediately precede the occurrence of its corresponding event, thereby capturing the causal context necessary for value function backup from the event back to the initial state.

The SSET algorithm samples mini-batches from the replay buffer by drawing a fixed proportion of transitions from each event table and from a default table containing all other transitions, ensuring that trajectories leading to meaningful events are over-represented in training data. This stratified sampling strategy enables the agent to learn more rapidly from high-impact behavioral milestones without requiring changes to the underlying reinforcement learning algorithm. Theoretical analysis demonstrates that when event conditions are correlated with optimal policy trajectories and history lengths are sufficient to span the distance between events or from initial states, SSET reduces the sample complexity of Q-learning compared to uniform sampling and prioritized experience replay.

The invention further provides a bias correction mechanism that preserves the Bellman target under stochastic environments, ensuring convergence to the optimal policy even when event tables introduce sampling bias. Empirical results across diverse domains—including discrete grid worlds, continuous control benchmarks, and high-fidelity racing simulators—demonstrate that SSET consistently outperforms uniform experience replay and prioritized experience replay in terms of sample efficiency, policy stability, and resistance to catastrophic forgetting. The method is compatible with a wide range of base algorithms, including DDQN, SAC, and QR-SAC, and can be combined with other techniques such as TD-error prioritization and potential-based reward shaping to achieve synergistic improvements in learning performance.

The invention also enables the mitigation of catastrophic forgetting by preserving event-specific trajectories even when those events become rare during training, thereby maintaining learned behaviors that would otherwise be overwritten by dominant, frequently occurring transitions. Furthermore, the framework generalizes to multi-table partitioning schemes that can be dynamically adjusted based on domain knowledge, and it supports the use of event conditions defined over state histories rather than single states, enabling the capture of complex, temporally extended behaviors such as re-establishing course after a deviation.

The theoretical underpinnings of SSET are derived from finite-time convergence bounds for tabular Q-learning with target networks, and the invention provides formal guarantees that under appropriate conditions, the convergence rate of the value function is improved by a factor that scales with the over-sampling rate and the correlation between event conditions and optimal policy trajectories. The invention thus represents a foundational advancement in experience replay methodology, bridging the gap between domain-informed learning and theoretical robustness in deep reinforcement learning.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE PREFERRED EMBODIMENTS AND BEST MODE OF INVENTION

### Terminology

A reinforcement learning agent is defined as a system that interacts with an environment by selecting actions according to a policy, receiving observations and rewards, and updating its internal representation to maximize cumulative discounted reward. An episodic Markov Decision Process is a mathematical framework describing the agent-environment interaction as a sequence of states, actions, rewards, and transitions governed by a stationary probability distribution, with a defined initial state distribution and episode termination condition. The value function quantifies the expected cumulative reward obtainable from a given state-action pair under a specific policy, and the Q-learning update is a model-free, off-policy algorithm that iteratively adjusts the value function estimate using temporal difference errors derived from observed transitions. An event specification consists of a Boolean condition defined over the state space and a history length parameter, which together determine when and how much of the preceding trajectory is stored in an event table. An event condition is a predicate that evaluates to true when the agent enters a state satisfying a predefined behavioral criterion, such as reaching a goal, crossing a threshold, or interacting with an object. Event conditions are critical because they define the milestones around which value backups are structured, and their design directly influences the efficiency of learning. Histories refer to the sequence of state-action-reward tuples preceding an event, and their length determines the extent of the causal chain that is preserved for value propagation. Terminal goal states are states at which an episode terminates with a positive reward, high reward states are those associated with large immediate or delayed rewards, bottleneck states are those through which the optimal policy must pass, and important rare states are those that occur infrequently but are essential for task completion. Negatively rewarding states are those that incur penalties and must be avoided, and their preservation in event tables helps prevent the agent from forgetting harmful behaviors. The experience replay buffer is a data structure that stores past transitions for reuse during training, and deep reinforcement learning refers to the use of neural networks to approximate value functions or policies in high-dimensional state spaces. Model-free off-policy methods learn value functions without requiring a model of the environment and can learn from data generated by a behavior policy different from the target policy.

### MiniGrid Experiments

The SSET framework was evaluated in multiple environments from the MiniGrid domain, using a double deep Q-network (DDQN) as the base reinforcement learning algorithm. A dense neural network architecture with multiple fully connected layers was employed to represent the Q-function, and an ε-greedy behavior policy was used to collect experience during training. In a three-room grid world, two event conditions were defined: one triggered when the agent crossed a gap between rooms and another triggered at the goal state, each with a history length of 200 steps. SSET demonstrated a significant improvement in sample efficiency and learning stability compared to uniform experience replay and an off-policy reverse sweep method. The standard error across multiple random seeds was markedly lower for SSET, indicating reduced variance in policy convergence. When compared to prioritized experience replay, SSET achieved comparable or superior learning speed while exhibiting substantially lower variance, suggesting that TD-error prioritization alone introduces instability due to overestimation bias. The combination of SSET with TD-error prioritization yielded the best performance, achieving both the rapid convergence of event-based sampling and the fine-grained prioritization of high-error transitions. In a reward shaping comparison, SSET outperformed potential-based shaping in terms of both final performance and learning speed, and the combination of SSET with shaping rewards produced the highest cumulative reward, demonstrating that shaping guides exploration while SSET accelerates value backup. Experiments with poorly designed event conditions—such as triggering at states near the goal or near the initial position—showed that even suboptimal event definitions still outperformed reward shaping, and performance degraded gracefully toward uniform sampling rather than collapsing entirely. The sampling weight assigned to the default buffer was found to significantly influence learning when event conditions were poorly specified, but robust performance was maintained across a wide range of weights when event conditions were well designed. In an obstacle course environment with multiple skills—such as picking up keys, opening doors, and avoiding hazards—SSET enabled the agent to acquire and retain all skills simultaneously, whereas uniform sampling and PER failed to learn the most difficult skill due to dominance of easier trajectories. In a catastrophic forgetting test, where the agent’s starting position was shifted during evaluation, SSET maintained high performance over extended training, while uniform and prioritized replay exhibited rapid decay in performance due to overfitting to the initial state distribution.

### Lunar Lander and Mujoco Experiments

The SSET framework was evaluated on the LunarLanderContinuous-v3 environment and four continuous control tasks from the MuJoCo suite, using the Soft Actor-Critic (SAC) algorithm as the base learner. Event conditions were defined based on reward thresholds, with histories of 200 steps stored in corresponding event tables. In all environments, SSET achieved approximately half the sample complexity of uniform experience replay and significantly outperformed prioritized experience replay, which exhibited degraded performance as the priority exponent increased. This degradation was attributed to the high density of shaping rewards, which rendered TD errors noisy and uncorrelated with policy improvement. SSET demonstrated robustness to non-optimal history lengths, maintaining superior performance even when history lengths were set longer than theoretically necessary. The method consistently converged to higher average returns with lower variance across multiple random seeds, confirming its effectiveness in dense-reward, continuous-state environments where traditional prioritization methods fail.

### Simulated Car Racing Experiments

The SSET framework was tested in the Gran Turismo Sport racing simulator, using the Quantile Regression Soft Actor-Critic (QR-SAC) algorithm with a 2048×4 feed-forward neural network architecture. In a slingshot passing scenario on the Circuit de la Sarthe track, two event conditions were defined: a “slipstream” event triggered when the agent’s slipstream feature exceeded a threshold of 0.7, and a “won” event triggered when the agent finished the segment in first place. SSET achieved consistent winning rates across multiple training runs, with significantly lower variance than uniform sampling, which exhibited erratic performance due to infrequent sampling of passing trajectories. In a time-trial setting on the Lago Maggiore GP track, a “re-establish” event was defined to trigger when the agent returned to the track after leaving it, with a history length of seven seconds. SSET successfully mitigated catastrophic forgetting of on-course driving behavior, maintaining 88.9% of policies free of off-course penalties after 1000 epochs, compared to 74.7% under uniform sampling. The worst-performing SSET run still outperformed the best uniform sampling run in terms of on-course consistency, demonstrating the method’s ability to preserve rare but critical behaviors over extended training.

### Recommendations on How to Pick Helpful Events

Event conditions should be designed to correspond to states that are frequently visited by the optimal policy, such as goal states, bottleneck transitions, or rare high-reward events. In domains lacking explicit goal structure, reward-threshold events with sufficiently long histories can serve as effective proxies. Even poorly chosen event conditions typically do not degrade performance below that of uniform sampling, and the method is robust to moderate mis-specifications. Event tables require minimal additional memory, as they store only integer indices pointing to existing transitions in the main buffer. The Reverb library provides an efficient implementation of this structure. For domains with insufficient domain knowledge, starting with a goal event or reward-threshold event and a history length of 100–200 steps yields substantial benefits. The sampling weights across event tables should initially be set equal, and fine-tuning may be performed if performance plateaus. The framework is compatible with online event discovery techniques, though such extensions are beyond the scope of this disclosure.

### Summary

The Stratified Sampling from Event Tables (SSET) framework introduces a novel method for structuring and sampling experience replay in reinforcement learning by partitioning the buffer into event-specific trajectory tables. By over-sampling histories leading to meaningful events, SSET accelerates convergence, reduces variance, and prevents catastrophic forgetting without altering the underlying learning algorithm. Theoretical analysis confirms improved sample complexity under appropriate conditions, and empirical results across discrete, continuous, and high-fidelity domains validate its superiority over uniform sampling, prioritized experience replay, and reward shaping. The method is broadly applicable, computationally efficient, and compatible with existing deep reinforcement learning architectures.
