## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates to molecular recording systems for the temporal encoding of cellular physiological states within living cells, and more specifically to genetically encoded protein-based recorders that capture dynamic gene expression histories as linear assemblies of self-assembling monomers bearing distinct epitope tags. These systems enable the long-term, cell-autonomous storage of biological information in a format that is preserved upon cell fixation and readable through conventional immunofluorescence microscopy without the need for cell lysis or nucleic acid sequencing. The invention provides a novel class of intracellular recorders that transform transient molecular events—such as promoter activation, signaling pathway engagement, or environmental stimuli—into stable, spatially ordered protein polymers that retain the temporal sequence of their constituent components. This technology bridges the gap between real-time live imaging and high-throughput fixed-tissue analysis by enabling the retrospective reconstruction of cellular histories with single-cell resolution across large populations of neurons, glia, and other nondividing cell types in intact tissues, including the mammalian brain. The system leverages engineered filament-forming proteins fused to epitope tags and insulator domains to achieve stable, unidirectional, time-ordered polymerization that faithfully encodes the kinetics and timing of gene expression events over days, thereby establishing a new paradigm for molecular recording in biology and medicine.

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

The ability to read biological signals over time is fundamental to understanding cellular decision-making, neural circuit dynamics, developmental trajectories, and disease progression. While live imaging of fluorescent reporters has enabled real-time observation of intracellular processes, its utility is severely constrained by phototoxicity, limited temporal duration, high equipment costs, and the inability to scale to large tissue volumes or dense cell populations. Alternative snapshot methods such as RNA fluorescence in situ hybridization and immunohistochemistry permit high-throughput analysis of fixed tissues but provide only discrete, non-sequential data points, rendering them incapable of reconstructing continuous temporal dynamics. Nucleic acid-based recording systems, which store information in engineered DNA or RNA sequences, offer the potential for long-term molecular memory but require cell dissociation and sequencing for readout, thereby destroying tissue architecture and precluding correlation with spatial, morphological, or proteomic context. Furthermore, such systems are incompatible with intact organ imaging and cannot be readily integrated with standard histological workflows. There remains a critical unmet need for a recording technology that combines the temporal fidelity of live imaging with the scalability and compatibility of fixed-tissue analysis, enabling the retrospective interrogation of physiological histories within their native cellular and tissue environments. The present invention addresses this gap by introducing a protein-based recording platform that operates entirely within the cytoplasm, preserves cellular integrity, and yields information readable through routine immunofluorescence techniques.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The invention discloses an expression-recording island (XRI) composition comprising a genetically encoded, self-assembling filament-forming monomer fused to a detectable epitope tag and a protein spacer that sterically inhibits lateral aggregation while permitting longitudinal polymerization. The XRI composition is designed to form a linear, unidirectionally elongating protein assembly within the cytoplasm of a living cell, wherein the temporal sequence of gene expression events is recorded as a spatial gradient of epitope tag density along the growing filament. Each monomer unit of the XRI contains a self-assembling core domain derived from a human-engineered filament-forming protein, a detectable tag such as HA, FLAG, or V5, and a monomeric insulator domain such as mEGFP or MBP that prevents aberrant lateral binding and promotes stable, linear growth. The XRI composition is encoded by a nucleic acid sequence operably linked to a promoter of interest, such that expression of the XRI monomer is induced in response to specific cellular stimuli. Upon expression, monomers polymerize into a filament that grows bidirectionally from a nucleation site, incorporating new monomers in the order of their availability, thereby encoding the temporal history of promoter activity as a linear sequence of epitope tag densities. The resulting XRI filament can be fixed and stained with fluorescently labeled antibodies against the epitope tags, enabling high-resolution imaging and quantitative analysis of tag distribution along the filament to reconstruct the timing and duration of gene expression events. The invention further encompasses vectors containing the XRI composition, including adeno-associated virus vectors for in vivo delivery, and cells—particularly postmitotic neurons—stably expressing the XRI construct. The method of identifying expression history involves expressing the XRI in a target cell, fixing the cell at a defined timepoint, detecting the XRI filament via immunofluorescence, and determining the spatial pattern of epitope tags to infer the temporal sequence of expression events. Comparison of expression records across multiple cells enables population-level analysis of stimulus responses, while fixation prior to detection ensures preservation of the temporal record. The amount of expressed XRI is quantified by measuring the intensity profile of epitope tags along the filament, and the pattern of epitope tags is determined by splitting the filament into symmetric halves and integrating signal intensity as a function of fractional cumulative expression. The identity of epitope tags is determined using orthogonal antibody staining, enabling multiplexed recording of multiple biological signals. The temporal history of expression is calibrated by correlating the fractional line integral of a constitutively expressed tag with the onset of an inducible tag, thereby establishing a quantitative, time-resolved readout of gene expression dynamics. The invention supports the use of multiple detectable labels and protein spacers, with at least two distinct epitope tags and at least one insulator domain per monomer, enabling multiplexed, high-fidelity recording of complex physiological events.

## BRIEF DESCRIPTION OF CERTAIN OF THE SEQUENCES

The XRI compositions incorporate engineered protein motifs derived from human-designed filament-forming proteins, including 1POK(E239Y), a variant of the bacterial isoaspartyl dipeptidase with enhanced filament-forming properties in mammalian cells. The self-assembling core domain contains a conserved β-sheet interface that mediates longitudinal monomer-monomer interactions with a fixed spacing of approximately 4 nm. The epitope tags include HA (YPYDVPDYA), FLAG (DYKDDDDK), V5 (GKPIPNPLLGLDST), and other immunogenic peptides with minimal cross-reactivity. The protein spacers include monomeric enhanced green fluorescent protein (mEGFP), maltose binding protein (MBP), and other non-oligomerizing domains that prevent lateral aggregation without disrupting filament elongation. The nucleic acid sequences encoding these components are mammalian codon-optimized for efficient expression in neurons and other eukaryotic cells, and are flanked by regulatory elements such as the human ubiquitin (UBC) promoter for constitutive expression, the c-fos promoter for activity-dependent expression, and synthetic promoters responsive to calcium, cAMP, or other signaling molecules. The sequences further include P2A self-cleaving peptides for bicistronic expression, trafficking sequences such as nuclear export signals, and fusion tags for purification or detection. The engineered variants of 1POK(E239Y) incorporate point mutations and domain fusions that reduce cytosolic aggregation and enhance filament stability, with the final XRI design comprising 1POK(E239Y)-MBP-HA as the constitutive backbone and 1POK(E239Y)-MBP-FLAG as the inducible component.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

### Protein-based recording of cellular physiological information

The invention provides a novel method for recording cellular physiological information through the continuous, covalent incorporation of genetically encoded protein monomers into a linear polymer that grows within the cytoplasm of a living cell. Unlike nucleic acid-based recorders that rely on enzymatic synthesis and editing, this system exploits the intrinsic self-assembly properties of engineered proteins to create a physical, spatial record of gene expression dynamics. As the cell experiences transient stimuli—such as neuronal depolarization, hormonal signaling, or circadian entrainment—the expression of specific promoters leads to the production of monomers bearing distinct epitope tags. These monomers are incorporated into a growing filament at a rate proportional to their abundance, resulting in a spatial gradient of tag density that mirrors the temporal profile of gene expression. The filament serves as a molecular tape recorder, preserving the order and timing of events with single-cell precision, and remains stable after fixation, enabling retrospective analysis without perturbing cellular architecture.

### Expression-recording island (XRI) strategy

The XRI strategy is based on a modular protein design in which a filament-forming core is fused to a detectable epitope tag and a monomeric insulator domain. The insulator domain, such as MBP or mEGFP, sterically blocks lateral interactions between adjacent filaments, ensuring that polymerization proceeds exclusively in the longitudinal direction. This design prevents the formation of unstructured aggregates and promotes the formation of long, linear filaments that can span the entire cytoplasm. The constitutive expression of one XRI variant (e.g., HA-tagged) provides a temporal scaffold, while the inducible expression of a second variant (e.g., FLAG-tagged) creates a detectable signal whose position along the filament corresponds to the timing of the stimulus. The resulting filament exhibits a polarized distribution of epitope tags, with the constitutive tag dominating the central region and the inducible tag concentrated at the distal ends, allowing the temporal onset of expression to be precisely localized.

### Linear protein assembly growth

The XRI filament grows bidirectionally from a nucleation site, with monomers added at both ends at a rate determined by their intracellular concentration. The elongation rate is governed by the expression level of the XRI construct, which can be modulated by promoter strength, transduction efficiency, or external stimuli. The filament exhibits a characteristic growth curve, with initial lag phase followed by exponential elongation and eventual stabilization, consistent with the kinetics of protein synthesis and polymerization. The width of the filament increases during the early stages of growth but plateaus after approximately three days, indicating stabilization of the polymer structure. The growth rate is independent of the identity of the monomer being incorporated, ensuring that the temporal record is not distorted by competition between different epitope-tagged variants.

### Recording of gene expression time course

The XRI system enables the continuous recording of gene expression time courses over multi-day periods. For example, when the c-fos promoter drives expression of a V5-tagged XRI variant, neuronal depolarization with potassium chloride induces a transient burst of V5 monomer production. This burst is recorded as a peak in V5 signal located at a specific position along the HA scaffold, corresponding to the time of stimulation. The position of the peak can be calibrated to absolute time using the fractional cumulative HA expression, which increases linearly with time and serves as an internal clock. This allows the reconstruction of the c-fos expression profile with a temporal resolution of approximately one day, matching the kinetics observed in live imaging experiments.

### Calibratable measure of time

The fractional cumulative expression of the constitutive epitope tag (e.g., HA) provides a calibratable, quantitative measure of time. By normalizing the line integral of HA signal from the center of the filament to its end as a value ranging from 0 to 1, the position of an inducible signal (e.g., FLAG or V5) can be mapped to a specific timepoint within the recording window. This calibration is validated by timed induction experiments in which the onset of inducible expression is triggered at known intervals, and the resulting position of the signal consistently correlates with the elapsed time. The calibration is robust across cell types, expression levels, and filament geometries, making it a reliable internal timer for temporal reconstruction.

### Application of XRI to record c-fos promoter-driven gene expression

The XRI system was successfully applied to record the dynamics of c-fos promoter activation in response to neuronal stimulation. By co-expressing a constitutive HA-XRI and a c-fos-driven V5-XRI, the system captured the temporal profile of c-fos expression following potassium chloride depolarization. The V5 signal appeared as a symmetric peak centered at the position corresponding to the time of stimulation, with the amplitude and width of the peak scaling with the duration and intensity of the stimulus. This demonstrated the system’s sensitivity to both the magnitude and timing of transcriptional events.

### Evidence for XRI preserving temporal order of protein monomers

Immunofluorescence analysis of XRIs following timed 4-OHT induction revealed that the FLAG signal was consistently localized to the distal ends of the filament, while the HA signal was enriched toward the center. This polarized pattern was absent in constructs lacking the insulator domain, confirming that the insulator is essential for preserving temporal order. The positional fidelity of the signal was maintained across hundreds of filaments, demonstrating that the XRI system reliably encodes the sequence of expression events without scrambling or diffusion.

### Comparison of XRI to nucleic acid-based systems

Unlike nucleic acid-based recorders that require cell lysis and sequencing, the XRI system preserves tissue architecture and enables multimodal analysis. The XRI filament can be imaged alongside other markers such as NeuN, GFAP, or synaptic proteins, allowing direct correlation between gene expression history and cellular phenotype. The readout requires only standard fluorescence microscopy, making the system accessible to laboratories without sequencing infrastructure. Furthermore, the XRI system does not rely on enzymatic fidelity or error-prone DNA synthesis, resulting in higher signal-to-noise and greater reproducibility.

### Use of XRI for investigating circadian genes and transcription factor activities

The XRI system is adaptable to any promoter of interest, enabling the recording of circadian gene rhythms, transcription factor activation, or signaling pathway dynamics. By coupling XRI expression to promoters regulated by CLOCK, BMAL1, CREB, or NF-κB, the system can capture the temporal oscillations of these factors over multiple cycles. The ability to record multiple signals simultaneously using orthogonal epitope tags allows for the reconstruction of complex regulatory networks.

### Information storage platform for cell identification

The XRI system can serve as a molecular barcode for cell identification. By expressing unique combinations of epitope tags in different cell populations, each cell can be assigned a distinct temporal signature that is preserved after fixation. This enables lineage tracing, clonal analysis, and identification of rare cell types within heterogeneous tissues.

### Multimodal data analysis

The XRI record can be integrated with transcriptomic, proteomic, and morphological data. For example, after imaging the XRI filament, cells can be subjected to single-cell RNA sequencing, with the XRI position serving as a proxy for the timing of the stimulus. This allows for the direct correlation of gene expression history with downstream transcriptional responses.

### Non-limiting examples of self-assembling proteins and detectable tags

Self-assembling proteins include 1POK(E239Y), DHF40, and other engineered β-sheet-forming domains. Detectable tags include HA, FLAG, V5, Myc, and biotin ligase acceptor peptides. The system supports at least three distinct epitope tags for multiplexed recording.

### Advantages of XRI compositions and methods

The XRI system offers unparalleled advantages: it is genetically encoded, scalable, compatible with fixed-tissue imaging, non-invasive to cell physiology, and capable of multi-day recording. It requires no specialized equipment for readout, functions in vivo, and preserves spatial context. It enables the reconstruction of temporal dynamics in complex tissues such as the brain, opening new avenues for neuroscience, developmental biology, and drug discovery.

### Compositions and Components

The XRI composition comprises a polypeptide chain comprising a self-assembling filament-forming domain, a detectable epitope tag, and a monomeric protein spacer. The filament-forming domain is derived from 1POK(E239Y) and contains a β-sheet interface that mediates longitudinal polymerization. The epitope tag is selected from HA, FLAG, V5, or Myc, and is fused to the C-terminus of the filament-forming domain. The protein spacer is a monomeric domain such as MBP or mEGFP, fused to the N-terminus of the filament-forming domain to prevent lateral aggregation. The nucleic acid sequence encoding the XRI is operably linked to a promoter, such as UBC or c-fos, and includes a polyadenylation signal and intron for enhanced expression. The XRI may be expressed as a fusion with a nuclear export signal to ensure cytoplasmic localization. The composition may further include a P2A peptide for bicistronic expression with a fluorescent reporter.

### Expression and Use of XRI Components

The XRI system is used to record the temporal history of gene expression in response to physiological stimuli. Baseline expression characteristics are determined in control cells lacking inducible components. Test cells are exposed to candidate stimuli such as depolarization, neurotransmitters, or pharmacological agents. Changes in expression characteristics are quantified by measuring the position and intensity of epitope tags along the XRI filament. The system is used to assess internal and external environments, screen therapeutic agents, and study normal cell development. The XRI recorder system enables high-throughput screening of compounds that modulate gene expression dynamics by comparing the temporal profiles of XRI records across treated and untreated populations.

### Molecules and Compounds

The invention encompasses polynucleotides encoding the XRI composition, including plasmids, viral vectors, and synthetic genes. Functional variants include point mutations in the filament-forming domain that enhance stability, substitutions in the epitope tag that reduce cross-reactivity, and modifications to the spacer domain that optimize solubility. The compositions include isolated nucleic acids, recombinant proteins, and pharmaceutical formulations for delivery to cells. Codon-optimized sequences are provided for expression in human, mouse, rat, and other mammalian cells.

### Delivery of XRI-Based Recorder Components

The XRI components are delivered to cells using viral vectors, electroporation, lipid nanoparticles, or microinjection. Adeno-associated virus serotype 9 is preferred for neuronal delivery due to its tropism and low immunogenicity. Fusion proteins include XRI fused to trafficking sequences such as nuclear export signals or mitochondrial targeting sequences. Genetic targeting is achieved using cell-type-specific promoters, such as Synapsin for neurons or GFAP for astrocytes.

### Vectors, Plasmids, and Molecules

A vector is a nucleic acid molecule capable of replicating in a host cell and expressing the XRI composition. Episomes include plasmids and viral genomes that replicate independently of chromosomal DNA. Expression vectors contain promoters, enhancers, and polyadenylation signals. Examples include pAAV-UBC, pAAV-cFos, and pAAV-FLEX. Lentivirus is used for stable integration in dividing cells. Promoters include constitutive (UBC, CMV), inducible (Tet-On, ERT2-iCre), and cell-specific (Synapsin, CamKIIα). Inducible promoters are activated by 4-OHT, doxycycline, or light. Trafficking sequences include NES, NLS, and mitochondrial targeting peptides. Detectable label polypeptides include HA, FLAG, V5, and Myc.

### Cells and Subjects

Cells used include primary neurons, glia, cardiomyocytes, and other postmitotic cells. Prokaryotic and eukaryotic cells are suitable, with preference for mammalian cells. Excitable cells include neurons, muscle cells, and pancreatic beta cells. Abnormal cells include cancer cells, neurodegenerative cells, and inflamed tissues. Control cells are untransduced or express non-recording constructs. The system is applicable to in vivo models including mice, rats, and non-human primates.

### Controls

Control values are established using cells expressing constitutive XRI without inducible components. Comparative groups include cells exposed to different stimuli, timepoints, or drug concentrations. Controls are used to establish baseline signal, background fluorescence, and nonspecific binding.

### Administration Means

The XRI components are administered to cells or subjects via intracranial injection, intravenous delivery, or systemic administration. Pharmaceutical compositions include the XRI vector in a pharmaceutically acceptable carrier such as saline, PBS, or liposomes. Modes of administration include injection, infusion, or inhalation. Protocols are optimized for dose, timing, and route to achieve efficient transduction and minimal toxicity.

### Assessment Methods

The XRI system is used to assess disorders including epilepsy, neurodegeneration, psychiatric disease, and cancer. Monitoring of disorders involves recording gene expression dynamics in response to treatment. Uses include screening therapeutic agents, studying developmental trajectories, and mapping neural circuit activity. Nucleic acid sequences are prepared by chemical synthesis or recombinant cloning and expressed in host cells using standard transfection or transduction protocols.

## EXAMPLES

### Example 1

Animal procedures were conducted in accordance with institutional guidelines. Primary mouse hippocampal neurons were cultured from postnatal day 0–1 pups and plated on Matrigel-coated coverslips. Molecular cloning was performed using synthetic genes encoding 1POK(E239Y)-MBP-HA and 1POK(E239Y)-MBP-FLAG under the control of the UBC or c-fos promoter. DNA transfection was performed using calcium phosphate, and AAV transduction was performed using serotype 9 vectors at a titer of 10^10–10^13 GC/mL. Chemical treatments included 1 µM 4-OHT for 15 minutes to induce Cre-mediated recombination, and 55 mM KCl for 1–3 hours to stimulate neuronal activity. Electrophysiology was performed using whole-cell patch clamp recordings on DIV 14–16 neurons. Mouse surgery involved stereotaxic injection of AAV into the CA1 region of the hippocampus at coordinates −2.0 AP, ±1.5 ML, −1.5 DV. 4-OHT was administered intraperitoneally at 40 mg/kg. Histology included transcardial perfusion with 4% paraformaldehyde, cryosectioning at 50 µm, and immunofluorescence staining with anti-HA, anti-FLAG, and anti-V5 antibodies. Expansion microscopy was performed using acryloyl-X anchoring and gelation with acrylamide/sodium acrylate. Fluorescence microscopy was performed on a spinning disk confocal microscope with 40× water immersion objective. RNA-Seq was performed using SMART-Seq v4 and Illumina sequencing. Image analysis involved centerline tracing, line integral calculation, and signal normalization. The fraction of HA line integral was defined as the cumulative HA intensity normalized to total filament length. FLAG signal was calculated as the derivative of FLAG line integral with respect to HA fraction. The point at which FLAG signal began to rise was determined by linear extrapolation of the initial rising phase. Statistical analysis was performed using Kruskal-Wallis and Dunn’s tests. The XRI system successfully recorded the timing of 4-OHT induction and KCl stimulation with a temporal resolution of one day, and preserved temporal information in vivo. The FLAG signal onset occurred at a fractional HA line integral of 0.3 when induced at 5/7 of the recording window, confirming calibration across systems. Sequential stimulations were resolved as two distinct peaks in the V5 signal. The system did not alter neuronal electrophysiology or gene expression profiles, as confirmed by patch clamp and RNA-Seq.
