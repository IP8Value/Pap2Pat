# DESCRIPTION

- introduce retro-inverso peptides for cancer treatment

The present invention relates to a novel class of retro-inverso peptides designed for the therapeutic intervention of cancer, particularly in the context of inhibiting metastatic dissemination. These peptides are engineered to disrupt the pathological interaction between the urokinase plasminogen activator receptor (uPAR) and the formyl peptide receptor type 1 (FPR1), a key molecular axis that drives tumor cell motility, invasion, and angiogenesis. Unlike conventional therapeutic peptides, which are rapidly degraded in biological fluids due to proteolytic cleavage of their L-amino acid backbone, these retro-inverso analogs are composed entirely of D-amino acids in reverse sequence order, thereby conferring exceptional resistance to enzymatic degradation while preserving the spatial orientation of critical side-chain pharmacophores. This structural inversion maintains the topological complementarity required for high-affinity binding to FPR1 without triggering receptor activation, effectively acting as silent antagonists. The resulting molecules exhibit potent, selective, and sustained inhibition of metastatic processes across multiple tumor types, including sarcomas, melanomas, and epithelial carcinomas, without inducing cytotoxicity or interfering with normal physiological functions such as cell proliferation or immune surveillance. Their stability, specificity, and efficacy position them as first-in-class agents capable of targeting the metastatic cascade at its molecular origin, offering a transformative approach to cancer therapy that moves beyond conventional cytotoxic or growth-inhibitory strategies.

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

- describe cancer treatment limitations

Despite advances in surgical resection, radiation, and systemic chemotherapy, the majority of cancer-related deaths continue to arise not from the primary tumor but from the development of distant metastases. Current therapeutic modalities are largely ineffective at preventing or eradicating disseminated disease, as they primarily target proliferating cells while leaving behind dormant, invasive, and migratory cell populations that drive metastatic recurrence. The failure to address the fundamental biological processes underlying tumor cell dissemination—particularly cell migration, tissue invasion, and intravasation—has resulted in a persistent clinical gap in metastasis-specific interventions. Even targeted therapies and immunotherapies, while improving survival in select cohorts, rarely prevent the systemic spread of cancer cells, and their benefits are often transient due to the emergence of resistance mechanisms. There remains an urgent and unmet need for agents that specifically interfere with the molecular machinery enabling tumor cells to detach, migrate, invade, and colonize distant organs, without compromising host tissue integrity or inducing systemic toxicity.

- explain cell motility in metastasis

Cell motility is a highly orchestrated, multi-step process essential for the metastatic cascade, involving the coordinated remodeling of the cytoskeleton, adhesion dynamics, and proteolytic degradation of the extracellular matrix. Tumor cells acquire migratory capabilities through the dysregulation of signaling pathways normally reserved for developmental and wound-healing processes. Once activated, these cells transition from an epithelial, adherent phenotype to a mesenchymal, motile state, enabling them to navigate through dense stromal environments and breach vascular barriers. This motility is not random but is guided by chemotactic gradients established by endogenous signaling molecules, including those derived from the uPAR-FPR1 axis. The ability of cancer cells to respond to these signals with directed movement is a critical determinant of their metastatic potential, making the suppression of this behavior a compelling therapeutic objective.

- discuss cell migration process

The process of cell migration involves the extension of actin-rich protrusions at the leading edge, the formation of transient focal adhesions with the extracellular matrix, and the contraction of the cell body via myosin-mediated force generation, followed by rear retraction. This cyclical sequence is tightly regulated by intracellular signaling networks, including Rho GTPases, integrins, and G-protein-coupled receptors. In cancer, aberrant activation of these pathways leads to sustained, uncontrolled motility. The urokinase plasminogen activator receptor (uPAR), though lacking intrinsic enzymatic activity, serves as a central scaffold that integrates extracellular cues with intracellular signaling by binding to vitronectin and forming functional complexes with integrins and FPR1. The interaction between uPAR and FPR1 initiates downstream signaling cascades that promote actin polymerization, membrane ruffling, and directional migration, effectively transforming uPAR from a proteolytic regulator into a potent motility inducer.

- introduce urokinase receptor (uPAR) as a prognostic marker

Elevated expression of uPAR on tumor cells and increased levels of soluble uPAR fragments in serum have been consistently correlated with advanced disease stage, poor prognosis, and reduced survival across a broad spectrum of malignancies, including breast, lung, colorectal, and sarcomas. uPAR is not merely a biomarker of tumor burden but an active driver of metastatic progression. Its presence on circulating tumor cells and within the tumor microenvironment facilitates the degradation of basement membranes, enhances cell adhesion to endothelial surfaces, and promotes intravasation into the bloodstream. Clinical studies have demonstrated that serum uPAR levels are predictive of metastatic relapse even in early-stage disease, underscoring its utility as a prognostic indicator and a rational target for therapeutic intervention.

- describe uPAR's role in cell migration

uPAR mediates cell migration through its ability to assemble multimolecular signaling complexes that link extracellular matrix recognition to intracellular motility pathways. Upon binding to its ligand uPA, uPAR undergoes conformational changes that expose a cryptic signaling domain within its linker region between domains I and II. This region, encompassing residues 84–95, contains a minimal chemotactic sequence (SRSRY) that, when released or presented on the cell surface, activates FPR1. This interaction triggers G-protein-dependent signaling that leads to the inside-out activation of αvβ3 and αvβ5 integrins, enhancing cell adhesion and traction. The uPAR-FPR1 axis thus functions as a molecular switch that converts proteolytic activity into directed motility, making it a pivotal node in the metastatic cascade.

- discuss Formyl Peptide Receptors (FPRs) and their functions

Formyl peptide receptors (FPRs) are a family of G-protein-coupled receptors originally identified for their role in detecting bacterial formylated peptides during innate immune responses. In humans, FPR1, FPR2, and FPR3 are expressed not only on myeloid cells but also on a variety of non-immune cell types, including epithelial, endothelial, and tumor cells. FPR1, in particular, responds to a diverse array of ligands, including mitochondrial-derived peptides and cleaved uPAR fragments, and upon activation, induces chemotaxis, oxidative burst, cytokine release, and cytoskeletal rearrangement. In the context of cancer, FPR1 is aberrantly overexpressed in numerous tumor types and has been shown to directly promote cell migration, invasion, and angiogenesis, independent of its immune functions.

- explain FPR1's involvement in tumor progression

FPR1 contributes to tumor progression by serving as a downstream effector of uPAR-mediated signaling. Its activation by uPAR-derived peptides stimulates the PI3K/AKT and MAPK pathways, leading to enhanced survival, motility, and matrix degradation. In vivo models have demonstrated that genetic knockdown or pharmacological inhibition of FPR1 significantly reduces metastatic burden without affecting primary tumor growth, confirming its specific role in dissemination. Furthermore, FPR1 expression correlates with aggressive histological features and poor clinical outcomes, positioning it as a critical mediator of metastatic competence and a viable target for anti-metastatic therapy.

- introduce retro-inverso (RI) concept for peptide stability

The retro-inverso (RI) modification represents a strategic approach to enhance the metabolic stability of bioactive peptides by inverting both the sequence direction and the chirality of all constituent amino acids. In this configuration, the side chains retain their original spatial orientation relative to the peptide backbone, preserving the pharmacophore required for receptor binding, while the amide bonds are reversed from L- to D-configured residues. This structural alteration renders the peptide resistant to proteolytic degradation by endogenous peptidases, which are highly specific for L-amino acid substrates and N-to-C backbone orientation. The RI approach has been successfully applied to a limited number of peptide therapeutics, offering prolonged half-life and enhanced bioavailability without compromising biological activity.

- discuss limitations of RI concept

Despite its promise, the retro-inverso strategy is not universally applicable. The inversion of the peptide backbone can disrupt critical hydrogen-bonding networks essential for maintaining the bioactive conformation, particularly in peptides whose activity depends on backbone-mediated interactions rather than side-chain recognition. Additionally, the increased hydrophobicity and altered conformational flexibility of RI peptides may lead to non-specific binding, reduced solubility, or unintended interactions with off-target receptors. Previous attempts to apply the RI concept to uPAR-derived peptides have yielded compounds with diminished potency or unpredictable pharmacokinetic profiles, highlighting the need for rational design based on structural insights into the native ligand-receptor interface.

- summarize prior art peptides and their limitations

Prior art has described several linear peptides derived from the uPAR84–95 region, such as RERF and UPARANT, which function as antagonists of the uPAR-FPR1 interaction. While these peptides effectively inhibit migration and angiogenesis in vitro, they suffer from rapid degradation in human serum, limiting their in vivo efficacy. Some analogs exhibit off-target binding to the αvβ3 integrin, leading to cytotoxic effects, while others lack sufficient potency or fail to inhibit invasion in animal models. These limitations have precluded their clinical translation, underscoring the necessity for a more stable, selective, and potent alternative.

- highlight the need for new peptides

There exists a critical and unmet need for novel peptide-based therapeutics that can selectively and durably disrupt the uPAR-FPR1 axis without inducing systemic toxicity or losing activity in physiological environments. Such agents must combine high binding affinity, resistance to proteolysis, favorable pharmacokinetics, and the ability to inhibit multiple steps of the metastatic cascade, including trans-endothelial migration and angiogenesis. The development of such molecules would represent a paradigm shift in cancer therapy, shifting the focus from tumor shrinkage to metastasis prevention.

- describe the importance of preventing uPAR-FPRs interaction

The uPAR-FPR1 interaction is a linchpin in the metastatic process, integrating proteolytic activity with directed cell motility. Disrupting this interaction not only inhibits tumor cell migration and invasion but also suppresses endothelial tube formation and intravasation, thereby targeting multiple stages of metastasis simultaneously. Unlike agents that target single components of the cascade, such as VEGF or MMPs, inhibition of uPAR-FPR1 signaling offers a broader, more upstream intervention that is less likely to be bypassed by compensatory mechanisms. Preventing this interaction thus represents a high-value therapeutic strategy with the potential to significantly improve survival outcomes in patients with aggressive, metastatic cancers.

- discuss the role of RI peptides in cancer treatment

Retro-inverso peptides designed to antagonize the uPAR-FPR1 interaction offer a unique combination of structural stability, target specificity, and functional efficacy. By mimicking the side-chain topology of native uPAR-derived agonists while resisting enzymatic degradation, these peptides act as long-lasting, non-activating blockers of FPR1 signaling. Their ability to inhibit migration, invasion, and angiogenesis in multiple tumor types, coupled with their lack of cytotoxicity and favorable in vivo tolerability, positions them as ideal candidates for systemic administration in both therapeutic and prophylactic settings. Their mechanism of action is distinct from conventional cytotoxics, offering the potential for combination with existing therapies to achieve synergistic anti-metastatic effects.

- conclude the need for new RI peptides

The limitations of prior peptide-based inhibitors and the challenges inherent in the retro-inverso approach necessitate the development of novel, rationally designed RI peptides with optimized conformational stability and binding affinity. The invention described herein fulfills this need by introducing a series of retro-inverso tetrapeptides and tripeptides that exhibit superior stability, potency, and specificity in disrupting the uPAR-FPR1 axis, thereby providing a new class of anti-metastatic agents with transformative clinical potential.

## DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

- introduce retro-inverso peptides of the invention

The present invention discloses a novel class of retro-inverso peptides that function as potent and selective antagonists of the urokinase plasminogen activator receptor (uPAR) and formyl peptide receptor type 1 (FPR1) interaction. These peptides are designed to inhibit the metastatic cascade by blocking the signaling axis that drives tumor cell migration, invasion, and angiogenesis without inducing cytotoxicity or interfering with normal physiological functions. Unlike prior peptide inhibitors, these retro-inverso analogs are composed entirely of D-amino acids arranged in reverse sequence order, ensuring resistance to proteolytic degradation while maintaining the spatial configuration of key side chains necessary for high-affinity binding to FPR1. The resulting molecules act as silent antagonists, preventing receptor activation without eliciting downstream signaling, thereby offering a uniquely safe and effective mechanism for metastasis suppression.

- describe the general formula of the peptides

The retro-inverso peptides of the invention are characterized by the general formula Ac-(D)-X1-(D)-X2-(D)-X3-(D)-X4-NH2, where X1 is Tyr or Phe, X2 is Arg or Aib, X3 is Arg, and X4 is Glu or Ser, or alternatively, the tripeptide formula R-CO-(D)-X2-(D)-X3-NH2, where R is an aromatic acyl group and X2 and X3 are as defined above. The N-terminal acetyl group and C-terminal amide group are essential for stabilizing the peptide termini and enhancing membrane permeability. The presence of α,α-disubstituted amino acid residues, particularly Aib (α-aminoisobutyric acid), at position X2 confers a stable β-turn conformation critical for receptor binding, while the D-configuration of all amino acids ensures resistance to enzymatic cleavage. The peptides are designed to mimic the side-chain topology of the native uPAR84–95 sequence (SRSRY) but in an inverted backbone orientation, thereby achieving competitive inhibition without agonist activity.

- list preferred compounds of the invention

Preferred compounds of the invention include RI-1: Ac-(D)-Tyr-(D)-Arg-(D)-Glu-(D)-Arg-NH2; RI-2: Ac-(D)-Tyr-(D)-Arg-(D)-Ser-(D)-Arg-NH2; RI-3: Ac-(D)-Tyr-(D)-Arg-Aib-(D)-Arg-NH2; RI-4: Phenyl-Acetyl-D-Arg-Aib-D-Arg-NH2; and RI-5: 3-Phenyl-Propionyl-D-Arg-Aib-D-Arg-NH2. Among these, RI-3 is the most preferred compound due to its superior stability, potency, and conformational rigidity conferred by the Aib residue at position 2, which stabilizes a β-turn structure essential for FPR1 binding. RI-3 exhibits nanomolar inhibitory activity against cell migration and invasion across multiple tumor types and demonstrates exceptional stability in human serum, making it the lead candidate for clinical development.

- describe the synthesis of the peptides

The retro-inverso peptides of the invention are synthesized using solid-phase peptide synthesis employing Fmoc/t-Bu chemistry on Rink amide resin. All amino acid derivatives are used in their D-configured form, and coupling reactions are performed using HBTU and DIPEA in DMF under inert atmosphere. After chain assembly, the peptides are cleaved from the resin using a TFA-based cocktail, precipitated in cold diethyl ether, and purified by preparative reversed-phase high-performance liquid chromatography (RP-HPLC) using a water/acetonitrile gradient with 0.1% trifluoroacetic acid. Final products are characterized by ultra-performance liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (UPLC-MS) to confirm molecular weight and purity, with all compounds exhibiting purity greater than 95% and identity confirmed by high-resolution mass spectrometry.

- discuss the biological activity of the peptides

The retro-inverso peptides of the invention exhibit potent and selective biological activity as antagonists of the uPAR-FPR1 interaction. They do not induce cell migration or activate intracellular signaling pathways in the absence of agonists, but effectively inhibit FPR1-dependent responses triggered by formyl peptides or uPAR-derived ligands. These peptides block FPR1 internalization, prevent agonist-induced phosphorylation of AKT, ERK, and p38 MAPK, and suppress downstream motility signals without affecting calcium mobilization or cell viability. Their activity is specific to FPR1, as demonstrated by lack of effect in FPR1-deficient cells and no cross-reactivity with other formyl peptide receptors. The peptides also inhibit endothelial tube formation and trans-endothelial migration, indicating broad anti-metastatic potential.

- explain the peptides' ability to prevent cell migration

The retro-inverso peptides inhibit cell migration by competitively binding to FPR1 and preventing its activation by endogenous ligands such as the uPAR84–95 sequence or formyl-methionyl-leucyl-phenylalanine (fMLF). This blockade disrupts the G-protein-mediated signaling cascade that drives actin polymerization, lamellipodia formation, and directional motility. In real-time migration assays using xCELLigence technology, all peptides significantly reduce the cell index increase associated with chemotactic stimuli, with RI-3 demonstrating the highest potency. The inhibition is dose-dependent, occurring at sub-nanomolar concentrations, and persists over extended time periods, indicating sustained receptor occupancy and functional antagonism.

- describe the peptides' ability to prevent cell invasion

The peptides inhibit cell invasion through extracellular matrix barriers such as Matrigel by suppressing the proteolytic and motile machinery required for tissue penetration. In Boyden chamber and real-time invasion assays, the peptides reduce the number of cells crossing the matrix by up to 64% in fibrosarcoma and 47% in chondrosarcoma cells. This inhibition correlates with reduced expression of matrix metalloproteinases and decreased integrin activation, downstream consequences of FPR1 blockade. The effect is most pronounced in cells co-expressing uPAR and FPR1, confirming the specificity of the mechanism. The peptides do not alter cell adhesion to the matrix itself but prevent the coordinated movement necessary for invasion.

- discuss the peptides' ability to prevent angiogenesis

The retro-inverso peptides inhibit angiogenesis by blocking FPR1-mediated signaling in endothelial cells, thereby preventing the formation of capillary-like structures in response to pro-angiogenic factors such as VEGF and FBS. In tube formation assays, RI-3 reduces endothelial network formation by up to 75% in the presence of VEGF and by 61% in the presence of FBS. This effect is not due to cytotoxicity, as endothelial cell viability remains unaffected. The inhibition of angiogenesis occurs through suppression of ERK and AKT phosphorylation, key mediators of endothelial proliferation and morphogenesis. This dual action—blocking both tumor cell invasion and the neovascularization required to support metastatic growth—confers a unique therapeutic advantage.

- explain the peptides' potential in cancer treatment

The retro-inverso peptides hold significant potential as systemic anti-metastatic agents in the treatment of solid tumors, particularly those with high metastatic propensity such as sarcomas, melanomas, and ovarian carcinomas. Their ability to inhibit multiple steps of the metastatic cascade—migration, invasion, intravasation, and angiogenesis—without inducing cytotoxicity or impairing immune function makes them ideal for long-term administration. Their stability in human serum allows for infrequent dosing, and their lack of off-target effects suggests a favorable safety profile. These peptides may be used as monotherapy in high-risk patients or in combination with conventional therapies to prevent recurrence and metastatic progression.

- discuss the peptides' potential in treating disorders related to neo-angiogenesis

Beyond cancer, the retro-inverso peptides have therapeutic potential in other pathologies driven by pathological angiogenesis, including age-related macular degeneration, diabetic retinopathy, rheumatoid arthritis, and atherosclerotic plaque instability. In these conditions, FPR1 is expressed on endothelial and inflammatory cells and contributes to neovascularization and tissue destruction. By inhibiting FPR1-mediated signaling, these peptides can suppress aberrant vessel growth and reduce inflammatory infiltration, offering a novel approach to treating chronic inflammatory and vascular diseases where current anti-angiogenic therapies are limited by toxicity or incomplete efficacy.

- describe the peptides' potential as theranostic agents

The retro-inverso peptides, particularly RI-3, possess intrinsic properties that render them suitable for use as theranostic agents. Their high affinity for FPR1, which is overexpressed in metastatic lesions, allows for radiolabeling with isotopes such as technetium-99m or fluorine-18 for non-invasive imaging of metastatic burden. Simultaneously, their therapeutic activity enables targeted delivery of anti-metastatic effects to FPR1-positive sites. This dual functionality permits real-time monitoring of treatment response, early detection of metastatic spread, and personalized adjustment of therapeutic regimens, transforming these peptides from simple inhibitors into integrated diagnostic and therapeutic platforms.

## EXAMPLE 1. SYNTHESIS OF THE RETRO-INVERSO PEPTIDES RI-1, RI-2, RI-3, RI-4, RI-5

- describe the synthesis of RI-1

RI-1 was synthesized on Rink amide resin using Fmoc-(D)-Arg(Pbf)-OH, Fmoc-(D)-Glu(OtBu)-OH, Fmoc-(D)-Arg(Pbf)-OH, and Fmoc-(D)-Tyr(tBu)-OH as building blocks. Sequential coupling was performed using HBTU (0.2 M) and DIPEA (0.4 M) in DMF for 45 minutes at room temperature. After completion of chain assembly, the peptide was cleaved from the resin using a cocktail of TFA, water, and triisopropylsilane (95:2.5:2.5) for 2 hours at room temperature. The crude peptide was precipitated in cold diethyl ether, dissolved in water/acetonitrile (1:1), and purified by preparative RP-HPLC using a C18 column with a gradient of 5–60% acetonitrile in 0.1% TFA over 40 minutes. Fractions were collected, lyophilized, and analyzed by UPLC-MS, confirming a molecular weight of 663.7 Da and a purity of 97.2%. The N-terminal acetyl group was introduced by acetic anhydride treatment prior to cleavage.

- describe the synthesis of RI-2

RI-2 was synthesized using Fmoc-(D)-Arg(Pbf)-OH, Fmoc-(D)-Ser(tBu)-OH, Fmoc-(D)-Arg(Pbf)-OH, and Fmoc-(D)-Tyr(tBu)-OH on Rink amide resin. Coupling conditions were identical to those for RI-1. After cleavage and purification, the product was analyzed by UPLC-MS, yielding a molecular ion at m/z 620.4, corresponding to the theoretical mass of 619.7 Da. The purity was determined to be 96.8% by analytical HPLC. The peptide was lyophilized and stored at -80°C until use. The acetyl group was incorporated during the final acetylation step prior to resin cleavage.

- describe the synthesis of RI-3

RI-3 was synthesized using Fmoc-Aib-OH, Fmoc-(D)-Arg(Pbf)-OH, Fmoc-(D)-Arg(Pbf)-OH, and Fmoc-(D)-Tyr(tBu)-OH on Rink amide resin. The Aib residue was coupled using HATU and DIPEA due to its steric hindrance. After chain assembly, cleavage was performed using the same TFA-based cocktail as for RI-1. Purification by RP-HPLC yielded a single peak at 21.3 minutes under a 5–60% acetonitrile gradient. UPLC-MS analysis confirmed the molecular weight of 621.3 Da and a purity of 98.5%. The peptide was lyophilized and characterized by NMR to confirm the β-turn conformation stabilized by the Aib residue.

- describe the synthesis of RI-4

RI-4 was synthesized by coupling Fmoc-(D)-Arg(Pbf)-OH and Aib-OH sequentially on Rink amide resin, followed by N-terminal acylation with phenylacetic acid using HBTU and DIPEA prior to resin cleavage. The cleavage cocktail contained 90% TFA, 5% water, and 5% triisopropylsilane. After purification, the product exhibited a molecular ion at m/z 533.5, matching the theoretical mass of 533.1 Da. Purity was confirmed at 95.7% by analytical HPLC. The absence of an N-terminal acetyl group and the presence of the phenylacetyl moiety were verified by mass spectrometry and amino acid analysis.

- describe the synthesis of RI-5

RI-5 was synthesized by coupling Fmoc-(D)-Arg(Pbf)-OH and Aib-OH on Rink amide resin, followed by N-terminal acylation with 3-phenylpropionic acid using HATU and DIPEA. Cleavage was performed using the standard TFA cocktail. Purification by RP-HPLC yielded a single peak with retention time of 22.1 minutes. UPLC-MS analysis confirmed the molecular weight of 547.1 Da and a purity of 96.3%. The structure was further validated by tandem mass spectrometry, which confirmed the sequence and the presence of the 3-phenylpropionyl cap.

## EXAMPLE 2. RETRO-INVERSO PEPTIDES DO NOT PROMOTE CELL MIGRATION

- describe the experiment to test cell migration

Human fibrosarcoma HT1080 cells were seeded at a density of 2 × 10⁴ cells per well in CIM-16 plates of the xCELLigence RTCA system, suspended in serum-free medium. The lower chamber was filled with medium containing 10 nM of each retro-inverso peptide (RI-1 through RI-5), 10 nM fMLF as a positive control, or vehicle (PBS). Cell migration was monitored continuously for 18 hours by measuring impedance changes corresponding to cell adhesion and movement across the microelectrode-coated membrane. The cell index, a dimensionless parameter proportional to cell number and motility, was recorded every 15 minutes. The experiment was performed in quadruplicate and repeated three times independently.

## EXAMPLE 3. RETRO-INVERSO PEPTIDE RI-3 INTERFERES WITH FPR1 BIOLOGICAL ACTIVITY BY BLOCKING AGONIST/FPR1 INTERACTION AND BY PREVENTING AGONIST-INDUCED FPR1 INTERNALIZATION

- describe the experiment to test FPR1 activation

RBL-2H3 cells stably expressing FPR1 (RBL-2H3/ETFR) were pre-incubated for 60 minutes at 4°C with 100 nM RI-3, 100 nM fMLF, 100 nM RERF, or 100 nM ERFR (scrambled control). Cells were then exposed to 10 nM FITC-labeled fMLF for an additional 60 minutes at 4°C to prevent internalization. Cells were washed with PBS, harvested, and analyzed for cell-associated fluorescence using a fluorescence plate reader with excitation at 485 nm and emission at 535 nm. Parallel experiments were conducted at 37°C to assess internalization. Cells were fixed, permeabilized, and stained with rhodamine-phalloidin, and FPR1 localization was visualized using a Zeiss 510 Meta LSM confocal microscope with z-stack acquisition at 0.20 µm intervals.

- describe the results of the experiment

Pre-incubation with RI-3 reduced FITC-fMLF binding to RBL-2H3/ETFR cells by 82% compared to vehicle-treated controls, with efficacy comparable to that of unlabeled fMLF and RERF. No inhibition was observed with the scrambled peptide ERFR. At 37°C, RI-3 prevented the internalization of FITC-fMLF into cytoplasmic vesicles, maintaining FPR1 localization at the plasma membrane, whereas vehicle-treated cells exhibited extensive intracellular fluorescence. Three-dimensional reconstructions confirmed that RI-3-treated cells retained FPR1 on the cell surface, while control cells showed complete internalization. These results demonstrate that RI-3 acts as a competitive antagonist that blocks both ligand binding and receptor internalization.

## EXAMPLE 4. RETRO-INVERSO PEPTIDES INHIBIT CELL MIGRATION IN A DOSE DEPENDENT MANNER

- describe the experiment to test cell migration inhibition

HT1080 cells were seeded in Boyden chambers at a density of 1 × 10⁵ cells per well in serum-free medium, with 10% FBS as the chemoattractant in the lower chamber. Increasing concentrations of RI-3 ranging from 10⁻¹⁴ M to 10⁻⁷ M were added to the upper chamber. After 4 hours of incubation at 37°C, cells that migrated to the lower surface of the membrane were fixed, stained with haematoxylin, and counted in ten random fields per filter. The experiment was repeated three times in triplicate. Additional real-time migration assays were performed using the xCELLigence system with the same concentration range over 18 hours.

- discuss the results of the experiment

RI-3 inhibited cell migration in a dose-dependent manner, with a detectable effect starting at 10⁻¹³ M and achieving 50% inhibition at 10⁻¹³ M. Maximal inhibition of 78% was observed at 10⁻¹¹ M, with no further reduction at higher concentrations. The IC₅₀ for migration inhibition was determined to be 3.2 × 10⁻¹⁴ M. Real-time monitoring confirmed sustained inhibition throughout the 18-hour period, with no rebound effect. The dose-response curve was sigmoidal, indicating specific receptor-mediated antagonism. No inhibition was observed in FPR1-knockdown cells, confirming target specificity.

## EXAMPLE 5. RETRO-INVERSO PEPTIDES INHIBIT CELL INVASION IN A DOSE DEPENDENT MANNER

- describe the experiment to test cell invasion inhibition

HT1080 cells were seeded at 3 × 10⁴ cells per well in Matrigel-coated Boyden chambers with 10% FBS as the chemoattractant. Increasing concentrations of RI-3 from 10⁻¹⁴ M to 10⁻⁷ M were added to the upper chamber. After 6 hours of incubation, cells that invaded through the Matrigel were fixed, stained, and counted. Real-time invasion was also monitored using E-well plates and the xCELLigence system over 20 hours. The experiment was performed in triplicate and repeated three times.

- discuss the results of the experiment

RI-3 inhibited cell invasion in a dose-dependent manner, with an IC₅₀ of 1.1 × 10⁻¹¹ M. At 10⁻¹¹ M, invasion was reduced by 52%, and at 10⁻¹⁰ M, inhibition reached 74%. The real-time invasion curves showed a progressive reduction in the rate of cell index increase, indicating suppression of invasive motility rather than cytotoxicity. No effect was observed in the absence of Matrigel, confirming that inhibition was specific to the invasive process. The potency of RI-3 in invasion assays was significantly higher than that of prior antagonists, correlating with its enhanced stability and binding affinity.

## EXAMPLE 6. RETRO-INVERSO PEPTIDE RI-3 DOES NOT AFFECT CELL PROLIFERATION

- describe the experiment to test cell proliferation

HT1080 cells were seeded at 2 × 10³ cells per well in E-well plates and cultured in growth medium containing 10 µM RI-3 or vehicle. Cell proliferation was monitored continuously for 96 hours using the xCELLigence RTCA system. Parallel MTS assays were performed in 96-well plates, with absorbance measured at 490 nm every 24 hours for 96 hours. The medium was replaced daily to maintain peptide concentration. Cell doubling times were calculated from the exponential growth phase.

- discuss the results of the experiment

No significant difference in cell index or absorbance was observed between RI-3-treated and vehicle-treated cells over the 96-hour period. The doubling time remained constant at approximately 22 hours in both groups. Viability, as assessed by trypan blue exclusion, was unchanged. These results demonstrate that RI-3 does not inhibit cell proliferation or induce cytotoxicity, confirming that its anti-migratory and anti-invasive effects are not secondary to growth suppression.

## EXAMPLE 7. RETRO-INVERSO PEPTIDE RI-3 IS STABLE IN HUMAN SERUM

- investigate stability of RI-3 in human serum

RI-3 was incubated at a concentration of 10⁻³ M in human serum at 37°C for 18 hours. The mixture was then centrifuged to remove proteins, and the supernatant was diluted to 10 nM and tested in a Matrigel invasion assay using HT1080 cells. A parallel sample of RI-3 not exposed to serum served as control. The cell index was monitored over 20 hours using the xCELLigence system. The experiment was repeated three times.

- report results of cell index values

The invasion inhibition by RI-3 pre-incubated in human serum was 81% of the inhibition observed with fresh RI-3, indicating minimal degradation. The cell index curves for serum-treated and untreated RI-3 overlapped almost completely, with no shift in IC₅₀ or time-dependent inhibition profile. UPLC-MS analysis of the serum supernatant confirmed the presence of intact RI-3 with no detectable cleavage products, demonstrating exceptional stability in human serum.

## EXAMPLE 8. RETRO-INVERSO PEPTIDE RI-3 PREVENTS MATRIGEL INVASION BY HUMAN SARCOMA, MELANOMA AND EPITHELIAL OVARIAN CARCINOMA CELLS

- investigate ability of RI-3 to prevent matrigel invasion

Human chondrosarcoma Sarc cells, melanoma A375 cells, and epithelial ovarian carcinoma SKOV-3 cells were seeded in Matrigel-coated Boyden chambers with 10% FBS as chemoattractant. RI-3 at 10 nM was added to the upper chamber. After 20 hours, invading cells were counted. uPAR and FPR1 expression was confirmed by Western blot. The experiment was performed in quadruplicate and repeated three times.

- report results of matrigel invasion

RI-3 reduced invasion by 47% in Sarc cells, 51% in A375 cells, and 44% in SKOV-3 cells. Inhibition correlated with high co-expression of uPAR and FPR1. Cells with low FPR1 expression showed minimal response. No effect was observed in FPR1-knockdown variants, confirming target specificity. The consistency of inhibition across tumor types with distinct origins underscores the broad applicability of RI-3 as a metastasis inhibitor.

## EXAMPLE 9. RETRO-INVERSO PEPTIDE RI-3 PREVENTS IN VITRO TRANS-ENDOTHELIAL MIGRATION BY SARCOMA, MELANOMA AND EPITHELIAL OVARIAN CARCINOMA CELLS AS WELL AS BY HUMAN MONOCYTES

- monitor trans-endothelial migration of various cell types

HUVEC monolayers were grown on E-plates until confluent. Sarc, A375, SKOV-3, and human monocytes (THP-1) were seeded on top in the presence of 10 nM RI-3 or vehicle. Trans-endothelial migration was monitored in real time for 10 hours using the xCELLigence system. The drop in cell index, reflecting monolayer disruption, was recorded. The experiment was performed in quadruplicate and repeated three times.

- report results of cell index changes

RI-3 reduced trans-endothelial migration by 24% in Sarc cells, 21% in A375 cells, 20% in SKOV-3 cells, and 27% in monocytes. The rate of impedance decline was significantly slower in RI-3-treated wells compared to controls. No effect was observed on HUVEC barrier integrity in the absence of migrating cells, confirming that RI-3 specifically inhibits transmigration without damaging endothelial junctions.

## EXAMPLE 10. RETRO-INVERSO PEPTIDE RI-3 INHIBITS BOTH FBS AND VEGF-TRIGGERED ENDOTHELIAL TUBE FORMATION

- investigate effect of RI-3 on endothelial tube formation

HUVECs were seeded on growth factor-reduced Matrigel in the presence of 10% FBS, 40 ng/mL VEGF, or vehicle, with or without 10 nM RI-3. After 6 hours, tube formation was imaged and quantified by counting cord-like structures exceeding 100 µm in length. The experiment was performed in duplicate and repeated three times.

- report results of tube formation quantification

RI-3 reduced FBS-induced tube formation by 61% and VEGF-induced tube formation by 75%. The number of branching points and total tube length were significantly reduced. No effect was observed on endothelial cell viability. The inhibition was comparable to that of the positive control bevacizumab, but without inducing endothelial cell apoptosis, indicating a more favorable safety profile.

## EXAMPLE 11. RETRO-INVERSO PEPTIDE RI-3 EXERTS ANTI-METASTATIC EFFECT REDUCING IN VIVO TUMOR GROWTH AND VASCULAR INFILTRATION BY SARCOMA CELLS

- inject Sarc cells into mice and treat with RI-3

Sixteen Foxn1nu/nu female nude mice received subcutaneous injections of 1 × 10⁶ Sarc cells in the right flank. Eight mice received daily intraperitoneal injections of 6 mg/kg RI-3; eight received vehicle (PBS). Tumor volume was measured every two days using calipers. After 10 days, mice were sacrificed, blood was collected for circulating tumor cell analysis, and tumors were excised for histological analysis.

- monitor tumor volume and weight

Tumor volume in the RI-3-treated group was 185 ± 82 mm³ compared to 330 ± 163 mm³ in the vehicle group (p < 0.05). Body weight remained stable in both groups, indicating no systemic toxicity.

- analyze tumor vascularization and CTCs

Tumors were fixed, sectioned, and stained with CD31. Microvessel density was reduced by 58% in RI-3-treated tumors. Circulating tumor cells were quantified by Alu-sequence PCR and found to be 2.6 ± 2.7 CTCs/mL in the RI-3 group versus 10.04 ± 3.7 CTCs/mL in the vehicle group (p < 0.01).

- report results of tumor growth and vascular infiltration

RI-3 significantly reduced primary tumor growth, intratumoral vascularization, and circulating tumor cell burden, demonstrating its ability to inhibit multiple steps of the metastatic cascade in vivo. The absence of weight loss and normal organ histology confirmed the safety of chronic administration. These findings establish RI-3 as a potent, well-tolerated anti-metastatic agent with translational potential.
