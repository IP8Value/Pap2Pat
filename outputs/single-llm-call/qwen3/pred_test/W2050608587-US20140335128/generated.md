# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

- introduce malaria  
Malaria is a life-threatening infectious disease caused by protozoan parasites of the genus Plasmodium, transmitted to humans through the bite of infected female Anopheles mosquitoes. The most virulent species, Plasmodium falciparum, is responsible for the majority of severe cases and fatalities worldwide, particularly in tropical and subtropical regions. The parasite undergoes a complex life cycle involving both human and mosquito hosts, with distinct developmental stages in the liver and bloodstream that present unique immunological targets. Despite decades of global control efforts, malaria remains a persistent public health crisis, with high morbidity and mortality rates concentrated in resource-limited settings.

- describe malaria statistics  
According to global surveillance data, malaria continues to affect hundreds of millions of individuals annually, with an estimated 247 million clinical cases and over 600,000 deaths reported in recent years. The burden is disproportionately borne by children under five years of age and pregnant women in sub-Saharan Africa, where transmission is perennial and healthcare infrastructure is often inadequate. The economic impact extends beyond direct medical costs, encompassing lost productivity, reduced educational attainment, and diminished agricultural output in endemic regions. These statistics underscore the urgent need for scalable, durable, and cost-effective interventions capable of interrupting transmission and preventing disease progression.

- discuss malaria impact on military  
Military personnel deployed to malaria-endemic regions face elevated risks of infection due to prolonged exposure during field operations, limited access to preventive measures, and operational constraints that hinder consistent use of insecticide-treated nets or chemoprophylaxis. Outbreaks among troops have historically disrupted mission readiness, increased evacuation rates, and strained medical logistics. The U.S. Department of Defense and allied forces have long prioritized malaria prevention as a critical force protection issue, investing in research to develop vaccines that can provide robust, long-lasting immunity without reliance on daily medication or environmental controls.

- describe limitations of current prevention methods  
Current malaria prevention strategies rely heavily on vector control, such as insecticide-treated bed nets and indoor residual spraying, and chemoprophylactic regimens like atovaquone-proguanil or doxycycline. While effective in reducing transmission, these methods are subject to logistical challenges, including inconsistent distribution, insecticide resistance in mosquito populations, and poor adherence among users. Chemoprophylaxis is further limited by drug resistance, side effects, and the impracticality of lifelong administration in endemic areas. These limitations have created a compelling need for a vaccine that can confer durable, sterilizing immunity without requiring continuous intervention.

- motivate vaccine development  
The development of a malaria vaccine represents one of the most significant challenges in modern infectious disease research. Evidence from natural exposure and experimental models demonstrates that sterile immunity can be achieved through prior exposure to irradiated or genetically attenuated sporozoites, suggesting that the human immune system is capable of recognizing and eliminating the parasite during its earliest hepatic stages. This biological proof-of-concept has galvanized efforts to replicate this protection using recombinant technologies that safely present key parasite antigens to the immune system without the risks associated with live parasite administration.

- review current malaria vaccine development  
The most advanced malaria vaccine to date, RTS,S/AS01, targets the circumsporozoite protein (CSP) and has demonstrated partial efficacy in reducing clinical episodes in young children, primarily through the induction of antibody-mediated neutralization of sporozoites. However, its protective effect is modest, wanes over time, and does not reliably prevent infection or induce robust T cell responses. This limitation has prompted the exploration of alternative platforms capable of eliciting cell-mediated immunity, particularly cytotoxic CD8+ T cells, which are essential for eliminating infected hepatocytes and achieving sterile protection.

- discuss Phase 1/2 vaccine trials  
Numerous Phase 1 and 2 clinical trials have evaluated DNA-based, viral vector, and protein-adjuvant platforms for malaria vaccination. While some candidates have shown immunogenicity, few have achieved consistent levels of protection across diverse populations. Heterologous prime-boost strategies—combining different vaccine platforms to enhance immune breadth and magnitude—have emerged as a promising approach, with adenoviral vectors demonstrating particular potency in stimulating CD8+ T cell responses. Nevertheless, challenges remain in overcoming pre-existing immunity to common adenovirus serotypes, optimizing antigen expression, and ensuring sustained immune memory.

- describe limitations of current vaccine approaches  
Current vaccine approaches suffer from several key shortcomings. DNA vaccines, while safe and stable, often induce weak immunogenicity in humans due to inefficient cellular uptake and limited antigen presentation. Viral vectors derived from common human serotypes, such as adenovirus serotype 5, are frequently neutralized by pre-existing antibodies, diminishing their ability to deliver antigens effectively. Additionally, many subunit vaccines focus exclusively on humoral immunity, neglecting the critical role of T cell responses in controlling intracellular parasite stages. The failure to induce strong, multifunctional CD8+ T cell responses has been a major barrier to achieving high levels of sterile protection.

- discuss development of immune-stimulatory conjugates  
Recent advances in immunology have led to the design of immune-stimulatory conjugates that enhance antigen presentation by targeting dendritic cells or modulating co-stimulatory pathways. These include fusion proteins, nanoparticle carriers, and molecular adjuvants designed to amplify innate immune signaling. While promising, these approaches have yet to be successfully integrated into a single, scalable vaccine platform capable of inducing both potent cellular and humoral responses against multiple parasite stages.

- describe DNA and viral based vaccines  
DNA vaccines involve the intramuscular or intradermal delivery of plasmids encoding parasite antigens under the control of mammalian promoters, leading to endogenous protein expression and MHC class I presentation. Viral vector vaccines utilize replication-deficient viruses, such as adenoviruses, to deliver antigen-encoding genes directly into host cells, resulting in high levels of intracellular antigen expression and robust T cell activation. Both platforms have been extensively studied in preclinical models and early-phase human trials, with viral vectors showing superior immunogenicity, particularly in inducing CD8+ T cell responses.

- discuss immunogenicity of DNA vaccines  
Despite their safety profile and ease of manufacturing, DNA vaccines have consistently demonstrated suboptimal immunogenicity in human trials. This limitation is attributed to inefficient transfection of muscle cells, low levels of antigen expression, and inadequate activation of dendritic cells necessary for priming adaptive immunity. Strategies to enhance DNA vaccine potency, such as electroporation, molecular adjuvants, and heterologous boosting, have been explored, but none have yet achieved consistent, high-level protection in large-scale human studies.

- state need for improved methods  
There remains a critical and unmet need for a malaria vaccine that can reliably induce high-magnitude, durable, and multifunctional CD8+ T cell responses against multiple pre-erythrocytic and blood-stage antigens, while overcoming the limitations of pre-existing immunity to common viral vectors. A platform capable of delivering multiple antigens in a single construct, with optimized expression cassettes, enhanced cellular targeting, and evasion of neutralizing antibodies, would represent a transformative advancement in the field.

## BRIEF SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

- introduce method of inducing immune response  
The invention provides a novel method for inducing a protective immune response against Plasmodium falciparum by administering a multivalent adenoviral vector formulation that expresses multiple parasite antigens under the control of optimized transcriptional elements. This method leverages a replication-deficient adenoviral vector engineered to enhance transduction of antigen-presenting cells, promote robust endogenous antigen expression, and evade pre-existing humoral immunity, thereby eliciting potent CD8+ and CD4+ T cell responses critical for sterile protection.

- describe composition and administration  
The composition comprises two or more replication-deficient adenoviral vectors, each encoding a codon-optimized Plasmodium falciparum antigen, including circumsporozoite protein (CSP) and apical membrane antigen-1 (AMA1), under the transcriptional control of a human cytomegalovirus immediate-early promoter and a bovine growth hormone polyadenylation signal. The vectors are administered intramuscularly as a single dose or as part of a prime-boost regimen following priming with a DNA plasmid encoding the same antigens. The adenoviral vectors are derived from serotype 5 but are modified to delete immunodominant epitopes and incorporate chimeric fiber proteins that enhance binding to dendritic cell receptors, thereby improving antigen presentation and reducing neutralization by pre-existing antibodies.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

- introduce multivalent vaccine  
The invention discloses a multivalent adenoviral vaccine platform designed to simultaneously express multiple Plasmodium falciparum antigens within a single host cell, thereby promoting coordinated immune recognition of key parasite targets across distinct life cycle stages. This approach overcomes the limitations of monovalent vaccines by engaging a broader repertoire of T cell clones and reducing the likelihood of immune escape through antigenic variation.

- advantage over current vaccine methodologies  
Unlike conventional subunit vaccines that rely on exogenous antigen delivery and primarily induce antibody responses, the inventive platform enables endogenous antigen synthesis within the cytosol of infected cells, facilitating MHC class I presentation and the activation of cytotoxic CD8+ T cells. The use of chimeric adenoviral vectors with modified coat proteins further enhances targeting of dendritic cells, minimizes neutralization by pre-existing antibodies, and increases the magnitude and durability of T cell responses compared to prior adenoviral constructs.

- describe method of inducing immune response against malaria  
The method involves the intramuscular administration of a replication-deficient adenoviral vector encoding codon-optimized sequences of Plasmodium falciparum antigens, including CSP and AMA1, under the control of a strong viral promoter and a stabilizing polyadenylation signal. Upon delivery, the vector transduces local muscle cells and antigen-presenting cells, leading to intracellular expression of full-length or modified parasite proteins. These antigens are processed and presented via MHC class I and class II pathways, resulting in the activation of antigen-specific CD8+ and CD4+ T cells, respectively, which migrate to the liver and bloodstream to eliminate infected hepatocytes and erythrocytes.

- describe adenoviral vector composition  
The adenoviral vector is derived from human adenovirus serotype 5 and is rendered replication-deficient through deletion of the E1 and E4 regions, which are essential for viral DNA replication and late gene expression. The E3 region is also deleted to accommodate insertion of heterologous antigen-encoding sequences. The vector genome is maintained in a complementing cell line that provides E1 and E4 functions in trans, enabling propagation of the vector without risk of replication in the vaccinated host.

- describe adenovirus characteristics  
Human adenovirus serotype 5 is a non-enveloped, double-stranded DNA virus with a capsid composed of hexon, penton base, and fiber proteins that mediate cell attachment and internalization. The virus naturally infects respiratory and gastrointestinal epithelial cells but can be retargeted through genetic modification of its fiber protein to enhance uptake by dendritic cells and other antigen-presenting cells.

- describe deletion of E3 region  
The E3 region of the adenoviral genome, which encodes proteins involved in immune evasion, is deleted to increase the cloning capacity of the vector and to reduce potential immunomodulatory effects that could interfere with antigen presentation. Deletion of E3 does not impair vector replication in complementing cell lines and enhances the immunogenicity of the expressed antigens by removing viral proteins that suppress host immune responses.

- describe AAV-Ad chimeric vector  
The invention further encompasses chimeric vectors composed of adenoviral backbone elements fused with adeno-associated virus (AAV) terminal repeat sequences, which improve vector stability and persistence in vivo. These chimeric constructs exhibit enhanced transduction efficiency and prolonged antigen expression, contributing to sustained T cell priming and memory formation.

- describe adenovirus sources  
The adenoviral backbone is derived from human adenovirus serotype 5 isolates obtained from clinical isolates or reference strains maintained in public repositories. Alternative serotypes, including serotypes 26, 35, and 48, are also employed to circumvent pre-existing immunity in populations with high seroprevalence of adenovirus 5.

- describe human adenovirus serotypes  
Human adenoviruses are classified into seven species (A–G), with serotype 5 belonging to species C. Serotypes vary in tissue tropism, receptor usage, and seroprevalence across global populations. Serotypes with low seroprevalence, such as those from species B and F, are preferred for use in vaccine formulations to minimize neutralization by pre-existing antibodies.

- describe adenoviral vector construction  
The adenoviral vector is constructed by homologous recombination in bacteria or by direct ligation of antigen-expression cassettes into a shuttle plasmid containing the left and right inverted terminal repeats and packaging signal of adenovirus. The resulting plasmid is transfected into a complementing cell line, where the recombinant vector is rescued and amplified.

- describe chimeric adenoviral vector  
The chimeric adenoviral vector incorporates a fiber protein derived from a non-human adenovirus serotype or a synthetic fiber containing a targeting ligand, such as an RGD motif or a CD40-binding peptide, fused to the adenoviral fiber shaft via a flexible spacer sequence. This modification redirects vector entry toward dendritic cells expressing integrins or CD40, enhancing antigen uptake and cross-presentation.

- describe adenoviral vector genome composition  
The adenoviral genome is approximately 36 kb in length and includes the inverted terminal repeats, packaging signal, and genes encoding structural and non-structural proteins. In the inventive vector, the E1 and E4 regions are deleted, and the antigen-expression cassette is inserted in place of the E1 region, under the control of a cytomegalovirus promoter and a bovine growth hormone polyadenylation signal.

- define replication-deficient  
A replication-deficient adenoviral vector is one that lacks essential genes required for autonomous viral replication in normal human cells. Such vectors can only propagate in specialized cell lines that provide the missing gene functions in trans, ensuring safety in vaccinated individuals.

- describe replication-essential gene functions  
The E1 region encodes transcriptional regulators necessary for activation of other viral genes, while the E4 region encodes proteins involved in viral DNA replication, mRNA export, and inhibition of host cell apoptosis. Deletion of either region renders the virus incapable of completing its replication cycle in non-complementing cells.

- describe E1-deficient adenoviral vector  
An E1-deficient adenoviral vector lacks the E1A and E1B genes and is propagated in 293 cells, which stably express the adenoviral E1 region. These vectors are widely used in gene therapy and vaccine development due to their safety profile and high transduction efficiency.

- describe E4-deficient adenoviral vector  
An E4-deficient vector lacks genes encoding E4orf1–E4orf6, which are required for efficient viral DNA replication and late gene expression. These vectors exhibit reduced cytotoxicity and enhanced persistence of transgene expression, making them suitable for prolonged immune stimulation.

- describe E1/E3-deficient adenoviral vector  
This vector lacks both E1 and E3 regions, allowing for larger transgene inserts and reduced immunomodulation. The absence of E3 enhances the visibility of the expressed antigen to the immune system by removing viral proteins that inhibit antigen presentation.

- describe E1/E4-deficient adenoviral vector  
The E1/E4-deficient vector combines the benefits of both deletions, resulting in a highly attenuated vector with minimal cytotoxicity and maximal transgene expression duration. These vectors are particularly suited for prime-boost regimens requiring repeated administration.

- describe multiply replication-deficient adenoviral vector  
A multiply replication-deficient vector contains deletions in three or more essential regions, such as E1, E3, and E4, further reducing the risk of recombination or replication in vivo. Such vectors are ideal for use in immunocompromised individuals and for applications requiring high safety margins.

- describe deletion endpoints  
Deletions are precisely engineered to remove the entire coding regions of E1, E3, and E4 while preserving the inverted terminal repeats and packaging signal. The deletion boundaries are defined by restriction enzyme sites or homology arms to ensure accurate excision and genomic integrity.

- describe singly replication-deficient adenoviral vector  
A singly replication-deficient vector contains a deletion in only one essential region, such as E1, and is propagated in a complementing cell line. While simpler to construct, these vectors may be less safe than multiply deficient constructs due to residual replication potential.

- describe E1/E3/E4-deficient adenoviral vector  
This vector contains deletions in all three regions and is propagated in a dual-complementing cell line expressing both E1 and E4. It offers the highest level of attenuation, minimal cytotoxicity, and maximal antigen expression, making it the preferred construct for clinical use.

- describe adenoviral vector genome modifications  
The genome is further modified by insertion of a spacer sequence between the promoter and the antigen coding region to optimize translational efficiency, and by substitution of native polyadenylation signals with stronger, more stable alternatives such as the SV40 or bovine growth hormone polyA sequences.

- describe immune response alteration  
The vector is engineered to alter the immune response by incorporating a chimeric fiber protein that binds to CD40 on dendritic cells, thereby enhancing co-stimulatory signaling and promoting Th1-polarized T cell responses. This modification shifts the immune response away from neutralizing antibody dominance toward robust cellular immunity.

- describe coat protein modification  
The fiber protein, penton base, and hexon proteins are modified to remove native receptor-binding domains and replace them with non-native sequences that target dendritic cell-specific receptors. These modifications reduce binding to the coxsackievirus and adenovirus receptor (CAR) and enhance binding to integrins or CD40.

- describe spacer sequence  
A spacer sequence of 15 to 45 amino acids, composed of glycine and serine residues, is inserted between the targeting ligand and the adenoviral fiber shaft to ensure proper folding and accessibility of the ligand for receptor binding.

- describe spacer sequence length  
The optimal spacer length is determined empirically to balance ligand exposure and structural stability. A spacer of 20–30 amino acids has been shown to maximize dendritic cell transduction without compromising vector integrity.

- describe spacer sequence composition  
The spacer sequence consists of flexible, unstructured amino acids such as glycine, serine, and threonine, which allow for conformational freedom and prevent steric hindrance between the targeting domain and the viral capsid.

- describe polyadenylation sequence  
The polyadenylation sequence is derived from the bovine growth hormone gene and is positioned downstream of the antigen coding region to ensure efficient termination of transcription and stabilization of mRNA transcripts.

- describe promoter-variable expression cassette  
The expression cassette includes a human cytomegalovirus immediate-early promoter, which drives high-level constitutive expression in a broad range of cell types, and a synthetic intron to enhance mRNA processing and nuclear export.

- describe passenger gene  
The passenger gene refers to the heterologous nucleic acid sequence encoding the Plasmodium falciparum antigen, which is inserted into the adenoviral genome in place of the deleted E1 region.

- describe E4 polyadenylation sequence  
The E4 polyadenylation signal is replaced with a stronger, non-viral polyA sequence to prevent interference with transgene expression and to ensure consistent mRNA stability.

- describe SV40 polyadenylation sequence  
The SV40 polyadenylation sequence is used as an alternative to the bovine growth hormone sequence in certain constructs to provide additional transcriptional termination efficiency and mRNA stability.

- describe glucuronidase gene  
The glucuronidase gene is included in certain control vectors as a reporter gene to monitor transduction efficiency in preclinical models, though it is excluded from clinical-grade vaccine formulations.

- describe trans-acting factor modulation  
The vector is engineered to modulate trans-acting factors such as NF-κB and IRF3 through inclusion of synthetic enhancer elements that amplify innate immune signaling pathways, thereby enhancing dendritic cell maturation and antigen presentation.

- describe transgene expression modulation  
Transgene expression is further modulated by inclusion of a WPRE (woodchuck hepatitis virus posttranscriptional regulatory element) downstream of the coding sequence to increase mRNA stability and translation efficiency.

- describe adenoviral vector propagation  
The vector is propagated in a complementing cell line, such as HEK293 or PER.C6, which stably express the E1 and E4 genes, allowing for high-titer production without risk of replication in the vaccinated host.

- describe replication-deficient adenoviral vector  
The replication-deficient adenoviral vector is incapable of replicating in normal human cells due to the absence of essential viral genes and is only capable of producing progeny in specialized cell lines that provide the missing functions in trans.

- describe adenoviral vector modification  
The vector is modified to contain a chimeric fiber protein with a targeting ligand, a spacer sequence, and a modified penton base to reduce CAR binding and enhance dendritic cell uptake.

- define replication-deficient adenoviral vectors  
Replication-deficient adenoviral vectors are viral constructs engineered to lack genes essential for autonomous replication, rendering them incapable of producing infectious progeny in normal human cells, thus ensuring safety in clinical applications.

- describe complementing cell lines  
Complementing cell lines are engineered human cell lines that stably express the adenoviral E1 and E4 genes, enabling the propagation of replication-deficient vectors. Examples include HEK293, PER.C6, and 293T cells.

- motivate E1 region complementation  
E1 region complementation is essential for the rescue and amplification of replication-deficient vectors, as E1 proteins are required to initiate viral gene expression and drive the cell cycle into S-phase, facilitating viral DNA replication.

- describe E4 region complementation  
E4 region complementation supports efficient viral DNA replication, mRNA export, and inhibition of host cell apoptosis, thereby increasing vector yield and transgene expression levels during production.

- explain construction of complementing cell lines  
Complementing cell lines are constructed by stable transfection of human embryonic kidney cells with plasmids encoding the adenoviral E1 and E4 open reading frames under the control of constitutive promoters, followed by selection and clonal expansion.

- list examples of complementing cell lines  
Examples of complementing cell lines include HEK293, PER.C6, 293T, and 293SG cells, each engineered to express varying combinations of E1 and E4 genes.

- describe helper virus  
A helper virus is not required for the production of the inventive vector, as the complementing cell lines provide all necessary functions in trans, eliminating the risk of contamination with replication-competent adenovirus.

- motivate coat protein manipulation  
Coat protein manipulation is motivated by the need to evade pre-existing neutralizing antibodies and to redirect vector tropism toward professional antigen-presenting cells, thereby enhancing immunogenicity and reducing dose requirements.

- describe fiber protein manipulation  
The fiber protein is modified by replacing the native knob domain with a ligand that binds to CD40, integrin αvβ3, or DC-SIGN, thereby redirecting vector entry away from CAR-expressing cells and toward dendritic cells.

- describe penton base manipulation  
The penton base is modified by substitution of arginine residues with alanine to reduce binding to integrins and minimize inflammatory responses, while preserving internalization efficiency.

- describe short-shafted fiber  
A short-shafted fiber variant is employed to reduce steric hindrance and improve accessibility of the targeting ligand, enhancing binding affinity to dendritic cell receptors.

- describe mutated nucleic acid residues  
Specific nucleic acid residues in the fiber gene are mutated to encode non-native amino acids that confer binding specificity to dendritic cell receptors, while preserving the structural integrity of the viral capsid.

- describe removal of native binding sites  
Native binding sites for CAR and integrins are removed by deletion or point mutation to prevent non-specific uptake by hepatocytes and other non-target cells.

- describe addition of binding sequences  
Binding sequences such as RGD, CD40L, or DC-SIGN ligands are genetically fused to the N-terminus of the fiber protein to confer targeted transduction of dendritic cells.

- describe targeting of adenovirus  
Targeting is achieved through genetic fusion of ligands to the fiber protein, enabling selective binding to receptors expressed on dendritic cells, macrophages, and B cells, thereby enhancing antigen presentation and immune activation.

- describe chimeric coat protein  
The chimeric coat protein consists of adenoviral structural domains fused to non-native ligand sequences, enabling dual functionality: capsid integrity and targeted cell entry.

- motivate binding to immune cells  
Binding to immune cells, particularly dendritic cells, is critical for initiating robust T cell responses, as these cells are the primary activators of naïve T cells and are essential for the induction of sterile immunity.

- describe chimeric fiber protein  
The chimeric fiber protein comprises the adenoviral shaft and tail domains fused to a ligand domain derived from CD40 ligand, RGD motif, or a synthetic peptide that binds to dendritic cell-specific receptors.

- describe RGD motif  
The RGD motif (arginine-glycine-aspartic acid) is a tripeptide sequence that binds to integrin receptors αvβ3 and αvβ5, which are highly expressed on dendritic cells and macrophages, thereby enhancing vector uptake.

- describe insertion of RGD motif  
The RGD motif is inserted into the HI loop of the fiber knob domain via recombinant DNA techniques, preserving the overall structure of the fiber while conferring new binding specificity.

- describe spacer sequence  
A flexible spacer sequence of 20 amino acids composed of glycine and serine residues is inserted between the RGD motif and the fiber shaft to ensure optimal orientation and accessibility of the ligand.

- describe target proteins on dendritic cells  
Target proteins on dendritic cells include CD40, DC-SIGN, integrin αvβ3, and CLEC9A, all of which are involved in antigen uptake, processing, and T cell priming.

- describe CD40 recognition  
CD40 recognition by the chimeric fiber protein triggers dendritic cell maturation, upregulation of co-stimulatory molecules, and secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines, thereby enhancing the magnitude and quality of the T cell response.

- describe target proteins on macrophages  
Target proteins on macrophages include CD14, CD163, and mannose receptor, which are exploited to enhance vector uptake and antigen presentation in tissue-resident macrophages.

- describe examples of macrophage surface target proteins  
Examples include CD14, CD163, CD206, and DC-SIGN, all of which are endocytic receptors that facilitate internalization of the chimeric vector.

- conclude adenoviral vector design  
The adenoviral vector is designed as a highly targeted, replication-deficient platform capable of delivering multiple antigens to antigen-presenting cells with high efficiency, while evading pre-existing immunity and promoting durable T cell memory.

- list B-cell surface proteins  
B-cell surface proteins targeted for potential future modifications include CD19, CD20, and CD21, which may be exploited in next-generation constructs to enhance humoral responses.

- describe chimeric virus coat protein  
The chimeric virus coat protein is a genetically engineered fusion molecule comprising adenoviral structural domains and non-native targeting ligands, enabling selective transduction of immune cells and enhanced immunogenicity.

- explain binding of chimeric coat protein  
The chimeric coat protein binds specifically to receptors on dendritic cells and macrophages, triggering receptor-mediated endocytosis and promoting efficient antigen processing and presentation via MHC class I and class II pathways.

- discuss use of bi-specific molecule  
A bi-specific molecule may be incorporated into the vector surface to simultaneously engage two different immune cell receptors, such as CD40 and DC-SIGN, to synergistically enhance dendritic cell activation.

- describe conjugation of non-native amino acid sequence  
Non-native amino acid sequences are conjugated to the fiber protein via genetic fusion, ensuring stable incorporation into the viral capsid and consistent receptor binding across vector batches.

- list adenoviral coat proteins  
Adenoviral coat proteins include hexon, penton base, and fiber, each of which can be modified to alter tropism, immunogenicity, or stability.

- explain generation of chimeric adenoviral coat protein  
Chimeric coat proteins are generated by PCR-based cloning of ligand sequences into adenoviral fiber genes, followed by transfection into complementing cell lines for vector rescue and amplification.

- describe modification of coat protein portion  
The fiber knob domain is replaced with a ligand sequence, while the shaft and tail domains are retained to preserve capsid assembly and stability.

- discuss incorporation of non-native amino acid sequence  
Incorporation of non-native amino acid sequences is achieved through recombinant DNA technology, ensuring precise control over ligand identity, orientation, and density on the viral surface.

- explain use of spacer sequence  
The spacer sequence ensures that the targeting ligand is displayed at an optimal distance from the viral surface, preventing steric interference and maximizing binding affinity to target receptors.

- describe determination of binding affinity  
Binding affinity is determined by surface plasmon resonance and flow cytometry using purified vector particles and recombinant receptor proteins, with Kd values optimized to be in the nanomolar range.

- discuss transduction levels of host cells  
Transduction levels are quantified by measuring transgene expression via flow cytometry or luciferase reporter assays, with chimeric vectors demonstrating 5- to 10-fold higher transduction of dendritic cells compared to unmodified vectors.

- explain binding of non-native amino acid sequence to compound  
The non-native amino acid sequence is designed to bind specifically to a receptor on dendritic cells, such as CD40, and does not bind to unrelated serum proteins or non-target cells.

- list examples of compounds bound by non-native amino acid sequence  
Examples include CD40, integrin αvβ3, DC-SIGN, and CLEC9A, all of which are expressed on dendritic cells and macrophages.

Disruption of native binding is achieved by point mutations in the fiber knob domain that abolish CAR binding, thereby reducing hepatocyte transduction and minimizing liver toxicity.

- explain limitation of repeated administration of adenoviral vector  
Repeated administration of adenoviral vectors is limited by the development of neutralizing antibodies that block subsequent doses, necessitating the use of rare serotypes or chimeric vectors to circumvent this issue.

- describe stimulation of humoral immunity  
While the primary focus is on cellular immunity, the vector also stimulates humoral immunity through MHC class II presentation and B cell activation, particularly when antigens contain B cell epitopes.

- discuss cell-mediated immune functions  
Cell-mediated immune functions include cytotoxic T lymphocyte-mediated lysis of infected hepatocytes, secretion of IFN-γ to induce nitric oxide production, and recruitment of macrophages to clear infected erythrocytes.

- explain rapid clearance of virus  
The adenoviral vector is rapidly cleared from circulation by the reticuloendothelial system, but transduction of local antigen-presenting cells occurs within hours, ensuring efficient immune priming before clearance.

- describe modification of adenoviral coat protein to evade host immunity  
Modification of the coat protein to remove immunodominant epitopes and replace them with non-native sequences reduces recognition by pre-existing antibodies, enabling effective boosting even in seropositive individuals.

- explain generation of diversity in coat protein  
Diversity in coat protein is generated through directed evolution, wherein libraries of fiber variants are screened for enhanced dendritic cell binding and resistance to neutralization.

- discuss use of directed evolution techniques  
Directed evolution involves iterative rounds of mutagenesis, selection, and screening to identify fiber variants with improved binding affinity, reduced immunogenicity, and enhanced transduction efficiency.

- outline steps of directed evolution process  
The process involves random mutagenesis of the fiber gene, packaging of mutant libraries into viral particles, selection on dendritic cell lines, amplification of selected variants, and reiteration until high-affinity, neutralization-resistant variants are obtained.

- introduce coat protein diversity generation  
Coat protein diversity is generated by error-prone PCR, DNA shuffling, or site-saturation mutagenesis of the fiber gene, followed by functional screening for enhanced dendritic cell transduction.

- describe mutated coat protein genes incorporation into adenoviral vectors  
Mutated fiber genes are cloned into adenoviral shuttle plasmids and recombined into the viral genome using homologous recombination in complementing cell lines.

Vectors are propagated under competitive conditions in the presence of pooled human sera to select for variants that retain transduction efficiency despite neutralizing antibodies.

Biological activity is assessed by measuring transduction efficiency, cytokine secretion, and T cell activation in human dendritic cells, with top candidates selected for clinical evaluation.

Immunogenicity is evaluated in mice and non-human primates by measuring antigen-specific CD8+ T cell responses via ELISpot and intracellular cytokine staining, with variant vectors demonstrating 2- to 5-fold higher responses than wild-type.

- list suitable modifications to adenoviral vectors  
Suitable modifications include fiber chimerization, E1/E3/E4 deletion, polyA sequence substitution, promoter optimization, and inclusion of WPRE elements.

- define heterologous nucleic acid sequence  
A heterologous nucleic acid sequence is a DNA sequence not naturally found in the adenoviral genome, encoding a foreign antigen such as Plasmodium falciparum CSP or AMA1.

- describe antigen and immune response  
The antigen is expressed intracellularly following vector transduction, leading to proteasomal degradation, MHC class I presentation, and activation of CD8+ T cells, while secreted or membrane-bound forms engage MHC class II and CD4+ T cells.

- specify Plasmodium antigen and its origin  
The antigens are derived from the 3D7 strain of Plasmodium falciparum and include the circumsporozoite protein (CSP) and apical membrane antigen-1 (AMA1), both of which are expressed during the pre-erythrocytic and blood stages of the parasite life cycle.

- outline Plasmodium life cycle  
The Plasmodium life cycle involves transmission via mosquito bite, invasion of hepatocytes, replication in the liver, release of merozoites into the bloodstream, invasion of red blood cells, and gametocyte formation for mosquito transmission.

- describe blood-stage and pre-erythrocytic stage antigens  
Pre-erythrocytic antigens such as CSP are expressed on sporozoites and infected hepatocytes, while blood-stage antigens such as AMA1 are expressed on merozoites and infected erythrocytes, providing distinct targets for immune attack.

- specify preferred P. falciparum antigens  
Preferred antigens include CSP, AMA1, TRAP, and EXP1, each of which has been shown to elicit protective immune responses in animal models and human trials.

- describe signal sequence and glycosylation effects  
Signal sequences direct antigen secretion or membrane localization, while glycosylation can modulate antigen stability and immunogenicity; in this invention, native signal sequences are replaced with TPA signal sequences to enhance expression.

The CSP gene is modified by deletion of central repeats and fusion to a TPA signal sequence, while AMA1 is expressed as a full-length protein with native signal sequence to preserve conformational epitopes.

- describe codon optimization for mammalian expression  
Codon optimization replaces rare codons in the Plasmodium genome with codons frequently used in human cells, thereby increasing translational efficiency and antigen yield.

- specify codon-optimized nucleic acid sequences  
The nucleic acid sequences encoding CSP and AMA1 are codon-optimized for human expression using algorithms that maximize GC content, eliminate cryptic splice sites, and enhance mRNA stability.

The expression cassette is inserted into the E1 region of the adenoviral genome, replacing the deleted viral genes, and is flanked by the CMV promoter and BGH polyA signal.

- outline E1 and E4 region modifications  
The E1 and E4 regions are completely deleted to prevent replication and to accommodate large antigen inserts, while preserving the inverted terminal repeats and packaging signal.

- describe promoter and enhancer elements  
The human cytomegalovirus immediate-early promoter is used for its strong, constitutive activity in a wide range of cell types, and is enhanced by inclusion of intronic sequences and enhancer elements.

- specify viral and cellular promoters  
Viral promoters such as CMV and SV40 are preferred for high-level expression, while cellular promoters such as ubiquitin and elongation factor-1α are used for sustained, tissue-specific expression.

- describe human CMV immediate-early promoter  
The human CMV immediate-early promoter is a potent transcriptional activator that drives high-level expression of transgenes in muscle cells, dendritic cells, and hepatocytes.

- outline ubiquitin promoter  
The ubiquitin promoter provides constitutive, moderate-level expression and is used in constructs requiring prolonged antigen presentation without excessive inflammation.

- describe other cellular promoters
