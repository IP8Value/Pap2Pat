## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

Phase sensing devices have long been recognized as essential tools for extracting structural and compositional information from transparent or weakly absorbing samples, particularly in biological imaging where conventional intensity-based detection fails to reveal critical details. These devices operate by detecting subtle variations in the optical phase of light transmitted through or reflected from a sample, which correlate with refractive index gradients and thickness variations invisible to standard cameras. Despite their utility, commercial optical sensors remain fundamentally limited to measuring only the amplitude or intensity of light, effectively discarding the phase component that carries up to 80% of the information in unstained biological specimens. This limitation has constrained the widespread adoption of quantitative phase imaging in clinical, diagnostic, and field-based applications where minimal sample preparation and rapid results are paramount. The phase of a light wave, unlike its intensity, is not directly detectable by conventional photodetectors, necessitating indirect methods that convert phase modulations into measurable intensity variations through interference, diffraction, or polarization manipulation. Phase microscopes, including phase contrast, differential interference contrast (DIC), and Hoffman modulation contrast systems, have dominated the field for decades due to their ability to render contrast in unstained tissues, enabling real-time observation of living cells, embryos, and pathogens without chemical fixation or staining. Among these, DIC microscopy has been the most widely used in research and clinical settings due to its pseudo-three-dimensional rendering and enhanced edge contrast. However, DIC systems rely on polarized light and biprism interference to generate intensity modulations that are intrinsically coupled with phase gradients, resulting in ambiguous image interpretations where dark or bright features cannot be reliably distinguished as arising from absorption, refractive index changes, or birefringence. Conventional DIC microscopes employ a complex optical train consisting of polarizers, Wollaston prisms, and analyzers that must be precisely aligned and mechanically inserted or removed during operation, increasing system cost, size, and susceptibility to misalignment. The reliance on polarization also renders DIC systems highly sensitive to birefringent materials such as collagen fibers, muscle striations, and starch granules, where intrinsic anisotropy disrupts the polarization state and generates severe artifacts that obscure true morphological features. Alternative phase microscopy techniques have emerged in recent years, including phase shifting interferometry, digital holography, Hilbert phase microscopy, swept-source phase microscopy, polarization quadrature microscopy, and harmonically matched grating-based methods. While these approaches offer quantitative phase retrieval, they typically require coherent laser sources, multiple image acquisitions at different phases or wavelengths, mechanical scanning of the detector or sample, or intricate optical components that are incompatible with standard microscope platforms. These constraints limit their practicality in routine laboratory or point-of-care environments due to high cost, complexity, slow acquisition speed, and lack of robustness. Furthermore, many of these techniques rely on bulky interferometric setups that are not amenable to miniaturization or integration into compact imaging systems. A particularly promising approach involves calculating optical phase from a sequence of defocused images captured at different axial positions. While this method avoids polarization and interferometry, it requires precise mechanical translation of the camera or objective, which introduces motion artifacts, limits temporal resolution, and is incompatible with live or dynamic samples. Additionally, these systems often require sophisticated computational algorithms and high-performance computing resources to reconstruct phase maps, further increasing their operational burden. The fundamental challenge across all existing methods is the entanglement of amplitude and phase information in the detected signal, which prevents true quantitative separation of these two independent physical properties. This entanglement leads to misinterpretations in stained or heterogeneous samples, where absorption and phase gradients produce visually similar contrasts. As a result, despite decades of innovation, no practical, low-cost, and universally compatible solution has emerged that can seamlessly replace conventional cameras in standard microscopes while providing direct, quantitative, artifact-free phase information without requiring specialized optics, lasers, or mechanical actuation.

## BRIEF SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention introduces a novel wavefront imaging device (WIS) capable of simultaneously measuring both the amplitude and phase gradient of an incident light field using a compact, integrated sensor architecture that requires no moving parts, polarized light, or external optical components beyond those found in a standard microscope. The WIS comprises three essential components: an aperture layer containing a two-dimensional array of precisely defined light-transmissive openings, a light detector layer composed of an array of photosensitive elements, and a transparent layer positioned between and physically separating these two layers. The aperture layer is fabricated directly atop the light detector, with the transparent layer serving as a fixed spacer that maintains a precise, sub-millimeter separation distance optimized for high Fresnel number operation. Upon illumination, light transmitted through each aperture forms a localized projection on the underlying detector, where the position of the projection’s center is directly proportional to the local phase gradient of the incident wavefront over the corresponding aperture, while the total intensity of the projection provides a direct measurement of the local light amplitude. The WIS operates by capturing the spatial displacement of these projection spots relative to their expected centroid positions under uniform illumination, enabling the direct computation of normalized phase gradients without the need for interferometry, polarization modulation, or multiple image acquisitions. The device generates high-resolution intensity and phase gradient images from a single exposure, producing output that is inherently quantitative, artifact-free, and decoupled from sample birefringence or absorption. Numerical processing of the detector signals allows the reconstruction of full wavefront information, including phase maps, amplitude distributions, and vector field representations of phase gradients, all derived from a single, static sensor configuration. The WIS is designed to be seamlessly integrated into existing optical systems as a direct replacement for conventional image sensors, enabling any standard bright-field microscope to function as a quantitative wavefront microscope without modification to its illumination or objective optics. The simplicity of the design, its compatibility with standard semiconductor fabrication processes, and its independence from polarized light or mechanical scanning make it uniquely suited for mass production, low-cost deployment, and integration into clinical, field-deployable, and point-of-care diagnostic platforms.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

The wavefront imaging sensor (WIS) is a fully integrated solid-state device comprising three core components: an aperture layer, a transparent spacer layer, and a light detector layer, all arranged in a stacked configuration with no intervening lenses or moving parts. The aperture layer is formed from an opaque or semi-opaque material, such as aluminum or chromium, deposited onto a rigid substrate and patterned using photolithography or focused ion beam milling to create a dense two-dimensional array of circular, square, or hexagonal apertures. Each aperture functions as a light-transmissive region, either as a physical hole through the opaque layer or as a region filled with a transparent material such as fused silica or polymer, allowing incident light to pass through while blocking all other regions. The apertures are arranged in a regular grid with spacing between adjacent apertures ranging from 5 to 20 micrometers, enabling high spatial sampling density and sub-wavelength resolution when paired with appropriate optical systems. The transparent layer, typically composed of a dielectric material such as SU8 photoresist, silicon dioxide, or a polymer with a refractive index between 1.4 and 1.8, is deposited or bonded between the aperture layer and the light detector layer to maintain a fixed separation distance H, which is critical to the device’s operation. This distance is engineered to place the light detector at the self-focusing plane of the aperture array under high Fresnel number conditions, where diffraction effects cause the transmitted light to converge into a tightly confined spot before diverging again. The light detector layer consists of a complementary metal-oxide-semiconductor (CMOS) or charge-coupled device (CCD) sensor array, with each photosensitive element corresponding to a dedicated subregion of pixels assigned to a single aperture. The size and arrangement of these pixel groups are optimized to enable sub-pixel resolution in determining the lateral displacement of each projection spot, with typical configurations employing 5×5 or 7×7 pixel grids per aperture. The WIS operates by illuminating the aperture layer with an unpolarized broadband light source, such as a halogen lamp, commonly used in standard microscopes. Light passing through each aperture propagates through the transparent layer and forms a projection on the underlying detector. In the absence of phase gradients, the projection is centered on the assigned pixel grid. When a sample modulates the wavefront of the incident light, the phase gradient across each aperture induces a lateral shift in the projection’s center, proportional to the local phase derivative along the direction of propagation. The magnitude of this shift is mathematically related to the normalized phase gradient θ, defined as θ = (λ/nH) × (∂ϕ/∂x), where λ is the wavelength of light, n is the refractive index of the spacer, H is the separation distance, and ∂ϕ/∂x is the spatial derivative of the optical phase. By measuring the centroid of each projection spot using algorithms such as Fourier-demodulation or cyclic weighting, the lateral displacement is determined with sub-nanometer precision, enabling phase gradient sensitivity below 0.1 milliradians. Simultaneously, the total integrated signal from the assigned pixel group provides a direct measurement of the local light intensity. The combination of these two measurements allows for the independent reconstruction of amplitude and phase information from a single exposure. The high Fresnel number regime, defined as F = a² / (λH), where a is the aperture radius, is essential to the device’s performance. Operating at F > 0.5 ensures that the projection spot is intrinsically focused near the detector plane, minimizing spot size and maximizing sensitivity to phase gradients. This self-focusing effect eliminates the need for relay lenses or micro-lens arrays, reducing system complexity and cost. The WIS can be implemented as a standalone imaging module, integrated into digital cameras, or embedded within microscope systems to transform them into wavefront microscopes. The device is compatible with standard illumination schemes, including Köhler illumination, and requires no polarizers, prisms, or phase plates. The resulting images include intensity maps equivalent to bright-field microscopy and phase gradient maps in orthogonal directions, which can be combined to form vector magnitude images or integrated to reconstruct full phase profiles. The WIS is fabricated using standard semiconductor processes, enabling mass production at low cost and scalability to large sensor formats. Its design is inherently robust, immune to mechanical vibration, and suitable for integration into portable, handheld, or point-of-care diagnostic devices. Applications extend beyond microscopy to include LASIK surgery guidance, retinal imaging, machine vision, object ranging, texture analysis, and adaptive optics systems, where direct wavefront sensing is required without the bulk or complexity of traditional Shack-Hartmann sensors.
