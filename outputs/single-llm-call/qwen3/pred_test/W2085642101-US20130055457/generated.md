## FIELD OF THE DISCLOSURE

The present disclosure relates to compositions and methods for increasing crop yield in plants, particularly in monocotyledonous species such as maize, through the genetic engineering of heterozygous allelic combinations of the ZAR1 gene. Specifically, the invention encompasses polynucleotides, recombinant expression cassettes, transgenic plants, and methods for modulating plant growth, organ development, and seed yield by introducing and expressing allelic variants of the ZAR1 gene in a heterozygous configuration within the plant genome. The disclosed methods are particularly useful for improving yield stability across variable environmental conditions, including drought stress and high planting density, and for enhancing agronomic performance in elite breeding germplasm without reliance on conventional hybridization alone. The invention further provides for the use of native allelic variants derived from distinct heterotic groups to achieve synergistic effects in transgenic plants, thereby bridging the gap between traditional breeding strategies and modern biotechnological approaches.

## BACKGROUND OF THE DISCLOSURE

The domestication of plants over millennia has resulted in the selection of traits that enhance reproductive output, vegetative biomass, and resilience to environmental stressors. Through centuries of selective breeding, crop species such as maize have undergone dramatic phenotypic transformation, with modern hybrids exhibiting substantially greater yield potential than their wild ancestors. This improvement has been driven largely by the accumulation of favorable alleles within genetically structured populations, particularly in heterotic groups that complement each other in hybrid crosses. Despite the success of conventional breeding, the underlying genetic mechanisms governing yield enhancement remain incompletely understood, especially for complex traits influenced by multiple genes and environmental interactions. While transgenic technologies have enabled the introduction of single-gene traits—such as insect resistance and herbicide tolerance—with remarkable consistency, their application to quantitative traits like grain yield has been limited by unpredictable performance across environments and a lack of alignment with naturally selected allelic diversity. The identification of genes that regulate organ size and developmental timing, such as the ARGOS family in Arabidopsis and its maize orthologues, has provided new targets for yield improvement. However, prior efforts to over-express these genes in transgenic systems have yielded inconsistent results, often failing to replicate the beneficial effects observed in elite germplasm. Furthermore, the partitioning of allelic variation between heterotic groups in maize has not been systematically leveraged in transgenic contexts, despite evidence that heterozygosity at key loci underlies much of the hybrid vigor observed in commercial varieties. The present disclosure addresses these limitations by demonstrating that the functional allelic variants of ZAR1, naturally selected and maintained in distinct heterotic groups, exhibit enhanced transgene efficacy when co-expressed in a heterozygous configuration, thereby recapitulating and amplifying the genetic principles of heterosis through biotechnological means.

## BRIEF SUMMARY OF THE DISCLOSURE

The present invention provides a novel approach to optimizing transgene efficacy in crop plants by engineering heterozygous allelic combinations of the ZAR1 gene, thereby achieving synergistic improvements in yield and environmental resilience. The invention is based on the discovery that two naturally occurring allelic variants of ZAR1, derived from distinct maize heterotic groups, exhibit differential expression patterns and protein structures that, when combined in a single transgenic plant, result in enhanced performance compared to either allele alone. The invention encompasses the creation of recombinant expression cassettes that co-express both allelic forms under native or heterologous promoters, the generation of transgenic plants stably harboring these heterozygous constructs, and the use of such plants to improve agronomic traits including seed size, kernel number, plant vigor, and drought tolerance. The invention further extends to the application of this strategy to genes from other species, enabling the transfer of heterotic allele combinations across crop systems. Compositions of the invention include polynucleotides encoding the ZAR1 allelic variants, DNA constructs comprising promoter and coding sequences from both alleles, expression cassettes operably linked to regulatory elements, and plant parts such as seeds, leaves, and meristems containing the recombinant constructs. The invention also provides methods for modulating yield improvement sequences by altering expression levels, introducing silent mutations to optimize codon usage, or combining the ZAR1 constructs with other yield-enhancing genes to achieve stacked trait performance. The disclosed methods enable the precise engineering of heterosis-like effects without the need for traditional crossing, thereby accelerating the development of high-yielding, environment-stable crop varieties.

### Describe standard methodologies

Standard methodologies for the construction of recombinant DNA constructs, transformation of plant cells, regeneration of transgenic plants, and phenotypic evaluation are well established in the art and include techniques such as restriction enzyme digestion and ligation, Gateway cloning, Agrobacterium-mediated transformation, biolistic particle bombardment, polymerase chain reaction, quantitative reverse transcription PCR, Western blot analysis, and field trial design under randomized complete block or incomplete block arrangements. Nucleic acid extraction is typically performed using commercial kits or phenol-chloroform protocols, followed by DNase treatment to remove genomic DNA contamination. RNA is reverse transcribed into cDNA using oligo(dT) or random hexamer primers and reverse transcriptase enzymes. For allele-specific expression analysis, primers are designed to flank polymorphic regions such as indels or SNPs to enable differential amplification of allelic transcripts. Protein expression is confirmed by immunoblotting using antibodies raised against the target polypeptide or by detection of epitope tags such as FLAG or HA. Transgene copy number is determined by quantitative PCR or Southern blot analysis. Transformation efficiency is assessed by the number of resistant calli or regenerated plants per number of explants treated. Phenotypic data are analyzed using statistical software packages such as SAS or R, employing ANOVA, mixed linear models, or generalized linear models to account for environmental and genetic variation. Environmental classification is performed using climatic data collected from on-site weather stations and processed through crop modeling systems such as EnClass® to assign each trial location to a defined environmental class. Sequence alignment and identity calculations are performed using programs such as BLAST, CLUSTAL, or PileUp, with parameters adjusted for nucleotide or amino acid comparisons. Hybridization conditions for nucleic acid probes are defined by salt concentration, temperature, and wash stringency, with high stringency defined as 0.1×SSC at 65°C. For protein sequence analysis, secondary structure prediction is performed using algorithms such as PSIPRED or Jpred, and tertiary structure modeling is conducted using homology modeling tools such as SWISS-MODEL or AlphaFold. Codon usage optimization is performed using tools such as CodonW or GeneArt, which adjust codon frequencies to match those of the host organism. All methods are performed in accordance with standard laboratory protocols and regulatory guidelines for genetically modified organisms.

### Introduce materials and methods

Materials used in the practice of this invention include maize inbred lines SS1 and NS1, obtained from DuPont Pioneer, and the Hi-II and PHWWZ transformation-competent lines. Plasmid vectors such as pENTR/D-TOPO, pCAMBIA, and pBI121 were obtained from Invitrogen and Clontech. Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain LBA4404 was used for plant transformation. Tungsten microprojectiles coated with DNA were prepared using standard protocols. Selection agents such as hygromycin, kanamycin, and phosphinothricin were used for selection of transformed tissues. Growth media for callus induction, shoot regeneration, and root development were formulated in accordance with established protocols for maize tissue culture. Environmental chambers and greenhouses were maintained at controlled temperature, humidity, and photoperiod conditions. Field trials were conducted at multiple locations across the U.S. Corn Belt, including Johnston, Iowa, and other sites representative of Temperate Dry, Temperate, Temperate Humid, and High Latitude environments. Weather data were collected using automated weather stations and integrated into crop modeling software to generate environmental classification indices. Statistical analysis was performed using SAS version 9.4, employing mixed linear models with genotype, environment, and their interaction as fixed effects, and replication and block as random effects. All experiments were conducted in compliance with institutional biosafety guidelines and applicable federal regulations governing genetically modified organisms.

The invention encompasses multiple embodiments, including transgenic plants expressing a heterozygous combination of ZAR1 alleles derived from distinct heterotic groups, wherein the alleles are co-expressed under native or heterologous promoters. In a further embodiment, the ZAR1 polynucleotide is optimized for codon usage in maize, with silent mutations introduced to enhance translational efficiency. In a still further embodiment, the ZAR1 polypeptide includes conservative amino acid substitutions that maintain function while altering sequence identity for patentability. In yet another embodiment, the ZAR1 construct is integrated into a single chromosomal locus using site-specific recombination systems such as Cre/lox or FLP/FRT. In a preferred embodiment, the transgenic plant is grown under conditions of high plant density or drought stress, wherein the heterozygous ZAR1 combination confers a yield advantage of at least 5% over non-transgenic controls. ### Describe conventional techniques

Conventional techniques for crop improvement include phenotypic selection, pedigree breeding, recurrent selection, and hybridization between inbred lines. These methods rely on the natural genetic variation present in germplasm collections and are limited by the time required for multiple generations of selection and the inability to precisely control the inheritance of complex traits. Conventional breeding does not permit the direct introduction of specific alleles from non-compatible species or the precise stacking of multiple favorable variants into a single genetic background. While marker-assisted selection has improved the efficiency of conventional breeding, it remains constrained by linkage drag, limited allelic diversity within elite populations, and the inability to create novel allelic combinations not present in nature. The present invention overcomes these limitations by enabling the artificial creation of heterozygous allelic combinations that mimic and enhance the effects of natural heterosis, thereby providing a powerful tool for accelerating yield improvement beyond the constraints of traditional breeding.

### Calculate Tm

The melting temperature (Tm) of a nucleic acid duplex is calculated using the formula Tm = 64.9 + 41 × (G + C − 16.4)/N, where G and C are the number of guanine and cytosine residues, and N is the total number of nucleotides. Adjustments are made for salt concentration and formamide content.

### Describe adjustment of Tm

Tm may be adjusted by altering salt concentration, adding formamide or DMSO, or changing the length of the hybridizing sequence. Higher salt concentrations increase Tm, while formamide decreases Tm by destabilizing hydrogen bonding.

### Describe hybridization and wash conditions

Hybridization is typically performed at a temperature 5–10°C below the calculated Tm for the probe-target pair. Washes are performed at temperatures ranging from 10°C below Tm for low stringency to 5°C above Tm for high stringency, using buffers containing sodium chloride and sodium citrate.

### Describe PileUp program

### Introduce BLAST family of programs

### Describe GAP program

The GAP program is a global alignment tool that uses dynamic programming to align two sequences with penalties for gaps and mismatches, and is used to calculate percent identity and similarity.

### Explain gap creation penalty

The gap creation penalty is a scoring parameter that assigns a cost to the initiation of a gap in an alignment, discouraging the introduction of multiple small gaps.

### Explain gap extension penalty

The gap extension penalty is a scoring parameter that assigns a cost to each additional residue in a gap, discouraging long gaps and promoting more contiguous alignments.

### Examples

#### Example 1

Favorable alleles of the ZAR1 gene were identified through genetic association analysis of 424 elite maize inbred lines. Haplotype 1 was found to be predominant in Non Stiff Stalk lines, while haplotype 2 was predominant in Stiff Stalk lines. Both haplotypes were associated with improved yield under drought stress. Vectors were constructed containing the coding sequences of each allele under the control of the maize ubiquitin promoter. Transgenic maize plants expressing either allele showed increased biomass and kernel number compared to non-transgenic controls.

#### Example 2

Molecular stacking of the SS1 and NS1 alleles was achieved by co-introducing both coding sequences into a single expression cassette under the control of the ubiquitin promoter. Transgenic plants expressing the heterozygous stack exhibited a 12% increase in yield across five environments compared to plants expressing either allele alone. The effect was statistically significant (P < 0.01) and consistent across years. Phenotypic attributes such as ear size, secondary ear growth, and plant vigor were significantly enhanced.

#### Example 3

Sequence variation in the ZAR1 promoter region revealed 1724 bp in NS1 and 1376 bp in SS1, with multiple indels in the upstream region. Allele-specific expression analysis showed that the NS1 allele was expressed at higher levels in immature ear tissue, while the SS1 allele was more highly expressed in seedling tissue. The differential expression was maintained under drought stress, indicating cis-regulatory divergence.

#### Example 4

Immature maize embryos were bombarded with tungsten particles coated with DNA encoding the ZAR1 SS1 allele under the ubiquitin promoter. Embryos were cultured on selection medium containing hygromycin. Transformed calli were regenerated into whole plants, which were monitored for increased drought tolerance. Kernel-earing capacity under drought conditions increased by 15% compared to controls.

#### Example 5

Immature embryos were infected with Agrobacterium strain LBA4404 carrying the ZAR1 NS1 expression cassette. Co-cultivation was performed for 72 hours, followed by rest on non-selective medium to eliminate bacteria. Transformed calli were selected on hygromycin-containing medium and regenerated into plants. Transgene expression was confirmed by RT-PCR.

#### Example 6

Transgenic maize plants expressing the ZAR1 SS1 allele were evaluated in the field. RT-PCR confirmed transgene expression. Plants exhibited increased plant height, stem diameter, and biomass accumulation. Flowering date was unchanged, but grain yield increased by 10% in three of four environments. Results were confirmed in advanced generations.

#### Example 7

Somatic embryos were induced from soybean cotyledons and maintained in suspension culture. Embryogenic tissue was bombarded with DNA encoding the ZAR1 SS1 allele. Transformed tissue was selected on hygromycin and regenerated into whole plants. Transgenic plants exhibited increased seed weight and improved drought tolerance.

#### Example 8

Sunflower meristem explants were surface sterilized, bisected, and cultured on GBA medium. Explants were bombarded with tungsten particles coated with the ZAR1 NS1 expression cassette. Transformed shoots were grafted onto rootstock and maintained in vitro. Transgene expression was confirmed by NPTII assay. Progeny were recovered without chemical selection, demonstrating stable inheritance.

#### Example 9

Variant nucleotide sequences of ZAR1 were generated by introducing conservative amino acid substitutions based on a substitution table. Artificial sequences with 80%, 85%, 90%, and 95% identity to the native ZAR1 polypeptide were synthesized. All variants retained the ability to enhance yield in transgenic maize, demonstrating that sequence diversity can be expanded while preserving function.
