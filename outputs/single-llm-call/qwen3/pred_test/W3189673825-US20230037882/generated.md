# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

- describe limitations of custom optical systems

Traditional optical systems for precision atomic physics experiments, particularly those involving trapped ion quantum computing, have long relied on manually assembled arrangements of discrete optical components mounted on breadboards using adjustable posts and clamps. While this approach offers flexibility in initial configuration, it suffers from inherent instability under environmental perturbations such as thermal drift, acoustic noise, and mechanical vibration. These systems are highly sensitive to minute changes in ambient temperature, which induce differential thermal expansion in metal mounts, optical elements, and supporting structures, leading to gradual misalignment of laser beams over time. Even sub-micron displacements in beam path geometry can degrade the fidelity of quantum logic operations that depend on phase-stable Raman transitions or precise state detection. Furthermore, the cumulative effect of multiple free-space beam paths, each requiring independent alignment, introduces exponential complexity in system maintenance and reproducibility. The reliance on manual alignment renders such setups unsuitable for scalable quantum architectures where consistency, reliability, and minimal human intervention are paramount. In cryogenic environments, these challenges are exacerbated by thermal contraction of materials, condensation of residual gases on optical surfaces, and the introduction of mechanical stress from differential coefficients of thermal expansion between dissimilar components. Conventional optical tables, even when vibration-isolated, cannot eliminate the intrinsic coupling between the laboratory frame and the cryogenic sample stage, resulting in residual motion that directly perturbs the interaction between laser fields and trapped ions. The absence of a monolithic, integrated mechanical reference frame prevents the realization of long-term optical stability necessary for multi-hour quantum gate sequences. Additionally, the use of multiple optical interfaces—lenses, mirrors, beam splitters—each introducing potential scattering, absorption, and polarization distortion—further degrades signal-to-noise ratios in fluorescence detection and state readout. These limitations collectively impede the transition from proof-of-principle atomic physics experiments to robust, factory-reproducible quantum computing platforms requiring turnkey operation and high uptime.

## SUMMARY

- introduce optical block

The invention introduces a novel optical block designed as a monolithic, precision-machined structural element that integrates multiple optical components into a single, rigid, thermally stable module. Each optical block serves as a self-contained functional unit, eliminating the need for individual component mounting and alignment outside the system. Constructed from a low-stress, isotropic cast aluminum alloy, the optical block provides a stable mechanical reference plane with embedded alignment features that ensure consistent positioning of optical elements relative to a common datum. The block’s geometry is engineered to minimize thermal expansion gradients and to maintain optical alignment across a wide operational temperature range, including cryogenic conditions below 10 K. By consolidating all optical functions—such as beam delivery, focusing, modulation, and detection—into a single, pre-aligned module, the optical block enables rapid deployment, replacement, and calibration without requiring realignment of the entire optical train. This modular architecture transforms the traditionally chaotic optomechanical layout into a standardized, repeatable assembly process, significantly enhancing system reliability and reducing downtime.

- describe plate with tapped holes and dowel pin holes

The optical block is fabricated from a flat, rigid plate that features a precisely machined grid of tapped holes and dowel pin holes arranged in a uniform two-dimensional lattice. The tapped holes, sized to accept standard machine screws, provide secure attachment points for optical mounts, lenses, mirrors, and modulators. Adjacent to each tapped hole is a corresponding dowel pin hole, dimensioned to accept precision ground dowel pins with tight tolerance fit. This dual-feature arrangement ensures both positional accuracy and rotational constraint, preventing any angular drift or lateral shift of mounted components. The spacing between adjacent tapped and dowel pin holes is standardized to enable compatibility across multiple optical blocks and mounting platforms. The dowel pin holes are drilled with sub-micron concentricity and perpendicularity to the plate surface, ensuring that when dowel pins are inserted, the optical block aligns with the mounting surface to within a few microns of positional error. This design eliminates the need for iterative alignment procedures and allows for plug-and-play integration of optical modules into larger systems.

- specify dowel pin hole arrangement

The dowel pin holes are arranged in a square grid pattern with a pitch of 25.4 mm (1 inch), and each hole is located such that its center is offset by exactly 1.27 mm (0.05 inch) from the center of the adjacent tapped hole, forming a staggered, non-overlapping configuration. This arrangement ensures that each optical component mounted via a tapped hole has two independent, non-collinear dowel pin references, thereby fully constraining all six degrees of freedom—three translational and three rotational—of the component relative to the optical block. The dowel pin holes are machined to a diameter of 2.000 ± 0.002 mm, with surface finishes better than 0.1 μm Ra, to ensure repeatable interference fit with hardened steel dowel pins of identical specification. The positioning of the dowel pin holes is determined using a coordinate measuring machine calibrated to NIST traceable standards, ensuring that the relative placement of all optical elements within a single block is reproducible across manufacturing batches.

- describe optical element coupling

Optical elements are coupled to the optical block through direct mechanical mounting using precision dowel pins and threaded fasteners. Each optical mount is machined with matching dowel pin receptacles and tapped holes that correspond exactly to the geometry of the optical block. Upon assembly, dowel pins are inserted into the block’s dowel pin holes and engage with the corresponding holes in the optical mount, establishing a rigid, non-deformable connection. Screws are then tightened into the tapped holes to apply axial clamping force, securing the optical element without inducing stress or distortion. This coupling method eliminates the use of adhesives, spring-loaded clamps, or flexible mounts that introduce hysteresis, creep, or thermal drift. The resulting interface provides sub-micron positional stability over time and temperature, enabling long-term optical coherence without recalibration.

- list optical element types

The optical block is compatible with a wide range of optical elements including but not limited to: high-numerical-aperture lenses for ion imaging, anti-reflection coated mirrors for beam steering, acousto-optic modulators for frequency shifting and shuttering, electro-optic modulators for phase control, beam splitters for signal routing, collimators for fiber coupling, waveplates for polarization manipulation, and spatial filters for mode cleaning. Each element is mounted in a custom-designed holder that conforms to the dowel pin and tapped hole grid, ensuring that the optical axis of each element is aligned to within 5 microradians of the block’s reference plane. The holders are themselves machined from the same low-stress aluminum alloy as the optical block to minimize differential thermal expansion.

- introduce optomechanical system

The invention further encompasses a complete optomechanical system comprising a base plate, one or more optical blocks, an intermediate plate, and a cryostat, all mechanically integrated through a unified alignment architecture. This system provides a thermally and mechanically stable platform for delivering laser light to trapped ions within a cryogenic ultra-high vacuum environment. The base plate serves as the foundational structural element, rigidly mounted to the cryostat’s cold finger. The intermediate plate, positioned above the base plate, provides a secondary mounting surface for optical blocks and is thermally isolated to reduce heat transfer. Optical blocks are mounted onto the intermediate plate using the same dowel pin and tapped hole interface, ensuring alignment consistency across the entire optical chain. The system is enclosed within a metallic housing to suppress air turbulence and acoustic noise, and all components are temperature stabilized using active water cooling or cryogenic thermal anchoring.

- describe intermediate plate

The intermediate plate is a flat, rigid structure fabricated from the same low-stress cast aluminum alloy as the optical blocks, with a thickness sufficient to resist bending under its own weight and external loads. It is machined with a grid of tapped holes and dowel pin holes identical in pitch and geometry to those on the optical blocks, allowing direct, repeatable mounting of multiple optical blocks without the need for additional alignment fixtures. The intermediate plate is thermally anchored to the 90-K stage of the cryostat, ensuring that its temperature remains stable during cooldown cycles. Its surface is coated with a low-outgassing, non-reflective black anodization to minimize stray light. The plate includes threaded inserts for securing it to the base plate via dowel pins and screws, forming a rigid, kinematic interface that isolates the optical blocks from vibrations originating in the cryocooler.

- describe optical block coupling

Each optical block is coupled to the intermediate plate using two precision dowel pins inserted into matching holes on the block and plate, followed by the tightening of two or more screws into the tapped holes. This coupling method ensures that the optical block is positioned with sub-micron repeatability and zero angular drift. The dowel pins are made of hardened stainless steel with a surface finish of 0.05 μm Ra, and the holes are reamed to size after final assembly to ensure perfect coaxiality. The screws are torqued to a calibrated specification to avoid over-compression of the aluminum substrate. The interface between the optical block and the intermediate plate is designed to be thermally conductive, allowing heat generated by laser absorption to be efficiently conducted away through the intermediate plate to the 90-K stage.

- specify dowel pin hole arrangement

The dowel pin hole arrangement on the optical block is identical to that on the intermediate plate and base plate, maintaining a 25.4 mm square grid with dowel pin holes offset by 1.27 mm from each tapped hole. This uniformity across all structural elements ensures that any optical block, regardless of its function or manufacturing batch, can be interchangeably mounted on any compatible plate without requiring recalibration. The dowel pin holes are located such that no two pins on a single block are collinear with the center of any tapped hole, guaranteeing full six-degree-of-freedom constraint. The positional tolerance of each dowel pin hole is ±1 μm relative to the reference datum established during the initial machining of the plate.

- describe base plate and cryostat coupling

The base plate is a thick, rigid platform fabricated from MIC-6 aluminum, measuring 890 mm on each side and 35 mm in thickness, providing a massive, low-resonance foundation for the entire optomechanical system. It is mounted directly to the 5-K sample mount of the closed-cycle cryostat using a combination of dowel pins and screws, ensuring that the base plate shares the same thermal expansion profile as the cryostat’s cold finger. The base plate includes a grid of tapped holes and dowel pin holes matching the intermediate plate, allowing the intermediate plate to be mounted with the same precision interface. This design ensures that all optical blocks, regardless of their position on the intermediate plate, are mechanically and thermally referenced to the same inertial frame as the trapped ions, minimizing relative motion between laser beams and the ion trap.

- describe optical block optical coupling

The optical coupling within each optical block is achieved by mounting optical elements such that their optical axes are aligned perpendicular to the reference plane of the block and intersect at a common focal point defined by the block’s internal geometry. Lenses are mounted in kinematic holders that constrain axial and angular movement while allowing for fine axial adjustment via micrometer screws. Mirrors are mounted with flexure-based mounts that eliminate stress-induced birefringence. All optical surfaces are coated with anti-reflection layers optimized for the operating wavelengths (355 nm, 370 nm, 399 nm, 935 nm), and their positions are verified using interferometric metrology prior to sealing. The optical block is designed so that input and output beams enter and exit through fiber-coupled ports, eliminating free-space propagation between modules and reducing sensitivity to environmental perturbations.

- introduce cryo-package assembly

The invention further includes a cryo-package assembly designed to maintain ultra-high vacuum conditions around the ion trap at cryogenic temperatures. This assembly consists of a copper lid sealed to a ceramic pin grid array package containing the ion trap, with integrated getter material, meandering vacuum passageways, and imaging windows. The cryo-package is cooled to below 10 K, enabling cryopumping of residual gases by activated carbon, achieving pressures below 10⁻¹⁰ Torr. The assembly is designed for mechanical and thermal compatibility with the optomechanical system, allowing direct mounting to the cryostat’s sample mount.

- describe package and lid

The package is a 100-pin ceramic pin grid array (CPGA) fabricated from high-purity alumina, modified with a brazed ringframe that provides a hermetic sealing surface for the copper lid. The lid is machined from oxygen-free high-conductivity copper and features a machined trench forming a meandering passageway for initial evacuation. The interior of the lid contains a getter cavity filled with activated charcoal encapsulated in a fine copper mesh, which adsorbs residual gases upon cooldown. The lid is sealed to the ringframe using indium wire, providing a thermally conductive, mechanically compliant bond that withstands thermal cycling without cracking. The lid is secured to the sample mount via eight tapped holes, ensuring thermal contact and mechanical stability.

- describe meandering passageway

The meandering passageway is a narrow, serpentine channel machined into the inner surface of the copper lid, extending from the perimeter to the central region of the package. This geometry maximizes surface area for cryopumping while minimizing the volume accessible to residual gases during room-temperature evacuation. The passageway is designed with a width of 0.5 mm and a depth of 0.3 mm, ensuring that gas molecules collide repeatedly with the cold surfaces before reaching the getter material. This significantly enhances the efficiency of cryopumping and reduces the time required to achieve ultra-high vacuum conditions after initial pump-down.

- describe getter cavity

The getter cavity is a recessed chamber located on the interior surface of the copper lid, positioned directly beneath the imaging window to maximize thermal contact with the coldest region of the assembly. It is sized to accommodate a cylindrical pellet of activated charcoal, encapsulated in a fine copper mesh to prevent particulate release. The cavity walls are electroplated with a thin layer of titanium to enhance gettering efficiency and reduce outgassing. Upon cooldown to below 10 K, the getter material adsorbs water vapor, hydrogen, and hydrocarbons, reducing the pressure within the sealed volume to below 10⁻¹⁰ Torr. The getter is not intended to be regenerated, but its capacity is sufficient to maintain UHV for the operational lifetime of the trap.

- describe ring frame and imaging window

The ringframe is a 1-mm-wide, 2.5-mm-tall annular structure brazed to the perimeter of the CPGA package, providing a mechanically robust sealing interface for the copper lid. The ringframe is composed of a copper-nickel alloy matched to the CPGA’s coefficient of thermal expansion, ensuring minimal stress during thermal cycling. The imaging window is a 15-mm-diameter sapphire disk, 2-mm thick, mounted flush with the lid’s interior surface. Sapphire is selected for its low hydrogen permeability, excellent thermal expansion match with copper, and high mechanical strength. The window is anti-reflection coated for the 370-nm fluorescence detection wavelength and bonded using cryogenic epoxy to prevent outgassing.

- describe ground shield wall

Beneath the imaging window, a machined, oblong conical ground shield wall extends inward from the lid’s interior surface, forming a Faraday cage that shields the ion trap from electric fields induced by charge accumulation on the dielectric window. The shield is electrically connected to the trap electrodes and extends to within 1 mm of the window’s edge, ensuring that the full numerical aperture of the imaging lens is preserved while minimizing field distortion. The shield is coated with a thin layer of gold to enhance conductivity and reduce secondary electron emission.
