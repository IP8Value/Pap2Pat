## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

- The present invention pertains to the field of ophthalmology and genetic medicine, specifically to the treatment of inherited retinal degenerative diseases, including autosomal dominant retinitis pigmentosa. The invention relates to methods and compositions for the in vivo correction of dominant-negative mutations in photoreceptor cells using genome editing technologies, particularly those involving CRISPR-Cas systems. It further encompasses therapeutic strategies for preserving retinal structure and function by selectively ablating mutant alleles while sparing wild-type gene expression, thereby enabling endogenous restoration of photoreceptor integrity and visual signaling pathways.

## BACKGROUND

- Retinitis pigmentosa is a group of inherited retinal disorders characterized by progressive degeneration of photoreceptor cells, leading to night blindness, peripheral vision loss, and eventual central visual impairment. The disease manifests through the gradual loss of rod photoreceptors followed by cone degeneration, resulting in a narrowing of the visual field and, in advanced stages, legal blindness. Diagnosis typically relies on clinical examination, electroretinography, visual field testing, and genetic screening to identify causative mutations. Autosomal dominant retinitis pigmentosa accounts for approximately 30 to 40 percent of all RP cases and is frequently associated with mutations in the rhodopsin gene, particularly the P23H substitution and the S334ter truncation. These mutations confer a toxic gain-of-function phenotype, wherein the mutant protein misfolds, accumulates in the endoplasmic reticulum, and triggers apoptotic pathways in photoreceptor cells. Conventional therapeutic approaches, such as gene replacement therapy, are ineffective in dominant conditions because they cannot distinguish between the mutant and wild-type alleles, and supplementation of wild-type transcripts alone is insufficient to counteract the dominant-negative effect. The advent of CRISPR-Cas9 technology has enabled precise, programmable cleavage of genomic DNA at specific loci, offering a novel paradigm for allele-specific disruption of disease-causing mutations. This approach holds particular promise for autosomal dominant retinitis pigmentosa, where the selective elimination of the mutant allele permits the remaining wild-type allele to restore normal photoreceptor function without the need for exogenous gene delivery or protein supplementation.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

- The invention provides a method for treating autosomal dominant retinitis pigmentosa by administering a therapeutic composition comprising a CRISPR-associated protein and a guide RNA designed to selectively cleave a mutant allele of the rhodopsin gene while leaving the wild-type allele intact. The guide RNA is configured to hybridize to a target sequence within the mutant rhodopsin allele that contains a protospacer adjacent motif uniquely present in the mutant sequence and absent in the wild-type sequence, thereby enabling allele-specific genomic disruption. The target polynucleotide sequence corresponds to a region in exon 1 of the rhodopsin gene containing a single nucleotide variant that generates a non-canonical PAM sequence in the mutant allele but not in the wild-type allele. The disease treated is autosomal dominant retinitis pigmentosa caused by the S334ter mutation in the rhodopsin gene, which results in a truncated protein that mislocalizes and induces photoreceptor apoptosis. The therapeutic vector is an adeno-associated viral vector or a plasmid encoding the CRISPR-associated protein and the guide RNA, optionally fused to a reporter gene such as mCherry for tracking transfection efficiency. The method of administration involves subretinal injection into the eye of a subject, followed by electroporation to enhance cellular uptake by photoreceptor cells. The genomic editing method entails the introduction of a double-strand break at the mutant rhodopsin locus, which is repaired by non-homologous end joining, resulting in frameshift mutations and premature stop codons that abolish expression of the toxic mutant protein, while the wild-type allele remains unaltered and functional.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

- The present invention is supported by prior scientific literature describing the molecular pathology of retinitis pigmentosa, the structure and function of rhodopsin, and the mechanism of CRISPR-Cas9-mediated genome editing. For the purposes of this disclosure, technical terms such as “CRISPR-associated protein,” “guide RNA,” “protospacer adjacent motif,” and “allele-specific ablation” are defined in accordance with established usage in the field of molecular genetics and gene therapy. The term “administering” refers to the delivery of a therapeutic composition into a subject via a route suitable for ocular targeting, including subretinal, intravitreal, or suprachoroidal injection, with subretinal delivery being preferred for photoreceptor-specific transduction. “Modulation” in this context refers to the alteration of gene expression or protein function through genomic disruption, without requiring homology-directed repair or exogenous template delivery. “Pharmaceutically acceptable carriers” include aqueous suspensions, lipid nanoparticles, viral vectors, and polymer-based delivery systems compatible with ocular tissues and non-toxic upon administration. The term “promote” refers to the facilitation of a biological process, such as photoreceptor survival or synaptic preservation, through the removal of a pathological agent. A “subject” is a mammal, preferably a human, suffering from or at risk of developing autosomal dominant retinitis pigmentosa. A “therapeutically effective amount” is the quantity of the CRISPR system sufficient to reduce mutant rhodopsin expression by at least 50 percent and to preserve photoreceptor cell density and visual function for a clinically meaningful duration. “Treat” encompasses the prevention, amelioration, or reversal of disease symptoms, including the halt of photoreceptor degeneration and the restoration of visual acuity. Retinitis pigmentosa is a progressive retinal dystrophy characterized by the loss of rod photoreceptors followed by cone degeneration, with autosomal dominant forms often caused by missense or truncating mutations in the rhodopsin gene. Current mutation-focused therapies, such as gene replacement or RNA interference, are limited by their inability to discriminate between mutant and wild-type alleles, leading to suppression of essential wild-type function. The use of CRISPR-based gene editing tools offers a superior alternative by exploiting single-nucleotide differences between mutant and wild-type sequences to achieve allele-specific targeting. In autosomal dominant retinitis pigmentosa, the S334ter mutation generates a truncated rhodopsin protein lacking the C-terminal 15 amino acids, including critical residues for proper trafficking and deactivation. This mutant protein accumulates in the cell body, disrupts cellular homeostasis, and induces apoptosis. The wild-type rhodopsin allele, when expressed at levels between 10 and 200 percent of normal, is sufficient to maintain photoreceptor viability and function. The invention hypothesizes that selective ablation of the mutant allele will eliminate the source of toxicity while preserving the function of the wild-type allele. In transgenic S334ter rats, the mutant allele contains a single nucleotide substitution that creates a protospacer adjacent motif of 5′-TGG-3′, whereas the wild-type allele contains 5′-TGC-3′, rendering the mutant allele susceptible to cleavage by Cas9 when paired with a guide RNA complementary to the adjacent sequence. CRISPR/Cas9 disruption of the mutant allele results in insertion or deletion mutations that disrupt the open reading frame, preventing expression of the toxic protein. The wild-type allele remains intact and functional, allowing for proper trafficking of rhodopsin to the outer segments and restoration of phototransduction. Gene replacement strategies are inadequate for dominant mutations because they require co-expression of the wild-type gene, which is unnecessary when the endogenous wild-type allele is preserved. The invention introduces allele-specific genomic ablation as a novel therapeutic modality for dominant retinal diseases. The S334ter-line-3 rat model exhibits rapid photoreceptor degeneration beginning at postnatal day 11, with complete loss by postnatal day 28, making it an ideal model for evaluating therapeutic efficacy. The truncated rhodopsin protein in this model lacks three serine residues required for phosphorylation-dependent deactivation and a signal sequence necessary for outer segment localization. The invention demonstrates that CRISPR/Cas9 can be delivered via plasmid or viral vector to the subretinal space, where it selectively cleaves the mutant allele, resulting in indel mutations that abolish expression of the toxic protein. The wild-type allele remains uncut due to the absence of the requisite PAM sequence, allowing it to produce functional rhodopsin. This leads to the preservation of photoreceptor nuclei, restoration of outer segment morphology, and rescue of synaptic connectivity between photoreceptors and bipolar cells. Immunohistochemical analysis confirms the absence of mutant rhodopsin in transfected regions and the proper localization of wild-type rhodopsin to the outer segments. Visual function, as measured by optokinetic response, is significantly improved in treated eyes compared to controls. The invention further describes the design of guide RNAs that target the unique PAM sequence in the mutant allele, with optimal specificity achieved by positioning the guide RNA upstream of the mutation to avoid potential restoration of the open reading frame. The use of Staphylococcus aureus Cas9, which is smaller than Streptococcus pyogenes Cas9, enables efficient packaging into adeno-associated viral vectors. Alternative delivery methods, including lipid nanoparticles, peptide-conjugated Cas9-gRNA complexes, and engineered exosomes, are also contemplated to enhance transfection efficiency and reduce off-target effects. The invention provides a method for altering the target polynucleotide sequence of the rhodopsin gene in vivo by introducing a double-strand break at a site unique to the mutant allele, followed by error-prone repair that results in permanent inactivation of the mutant transcript. This approach is applicable to other dominant retinal diseases caused by mutations that generate novel PAM sequences, and may be extended to other tissues through similar allele-specific targeting strategies.

### Genomic Editing.

- Genomic editing refers to the deliberate modification of a nucleotide sequence within a chromosome to correct, disrupt, or replace a genetic element. In the context of this invention, site-specific chromosomal integration is not required; instead, the goal is to induce targeted disruption of a mutant allele through the introduction of double-strand breaks. Therapeutic gene cassettes are not employed for replacement, but rather to deliver the components necessary for cleavage: a CRISPR-associated protein and a guide RNA. The method specifically disrupts the mutant allele of the rhodopsin gene while leaving the wild-type allele untouched. Zinc finger nucleases and transcription activator-like effector nucleases were previously used for genome editing but suffer from limitations in design complexity, delivery efficiency, and off-target activity. The CRISPR-Cas system, particularly Cas9 from Streptococcus pyogenes or Staphylococcus aureus, offers superior specificity, ease of design, and multiplexing capability. The mechanism involves the formation of a ribonucleoprotein complex between the Cas9 protein and a guide RNA, which directs cleavage to a genomic locus complementary to the guide RNA sequence and adjacent to a protospacer adjacent motif. The PAM sequence is a short DNA motif required for Cas9 recognition and activation, and its sequence varies between Cas9 orthologs. In this invention, the mutant rhodopsin allele contains a PAM sequence of 5′-TGG-3′, while the wild-type allele contains 5′-TGC-3′, allowing for discrimination between the two alleles. The CRISPR-Cas system offers advantages over other editing tools due to its programmability, high efficiency, and ability to function in post-mitotic cells such as photoreceptors. Cas9-induced double-strand breaks are repaired predominantly by non-homologous end joining, which introduces small insertions or deletions that disrupt the open reading frame. Homology-directed repair with donor templates is not required for this invention, as the objective is ablation, not correction. Multiple guide RNAs may be employed simultaneously to target additional loci or to increase editing efficiency. Simultaneous genetic editing of multiple alleles is feasible using polycistronic constructs or multiple viral vectors. Adeno-associated viral vectors are preferred for delivery due to their low immunogenicity, long-term transgene expression, and tropism for retinal cells. The AAV-CRISPR system described herein includes a single vector encoding both the Cas9 protein and the guide RNA, optionally fused to a fluorescent reporter for tracking transfection. The use of S. aureus Cas9, which is approximately 3.3 kilobases in size, allows for efficient packaging into AAV serotypes with limited cargo capacity. The RhoS334ter transgene in the S334ter-3 rat model expresses a truncated rhodopsin protein that is toxic to photoreceptors. CRISPR/Cas9 gene correction is achieved not by repairing the mutation, but by disrupting the mutant allele to prevent its expression. The invention enables prophylactic prevention of retinitis pigmentosa by intervening early in disease progression, before extensive photoreceptor loss occurs. In vivo gene modification is performed by direct injection of the CRISPR components into the subretinal space, where they are taken up by photoreceptor cells. The method is applicable to dominant retinal diseases caused by point mutations that generate novel PAM sequences, including missense mutations in RHO or RDS. A single gene mutation is sufficient to cause disease in autosomal dominant inheritance, and the invention targets such mutations with high specificity. The dominant mutation is distinguished from a recessive mutation by its ability to cause disease in the heterozygous state. Retinitis pigmentosa is caused by mutations in over two dozen genes, with RHO mutations accounting for the largest proportion of autosomal dominant cases. Therapeutic vector delivery is achieved via subretinal injection, which allows direct access to photoreceptor cells. In vivo generation of double-strand breaks is mediated by the Cas9-gRNA complex, which cleaves the mutant allele at a site adjacent to the unique PAM. Homologous recombination of double-strand breaks is not required, as non-homologous end joining is sufficient to inactivate the mutant transcript. The invention distinguishes between non-homologous end joining and homology-directed repair, with the former being the primary repair pathway utilized. An in vitro method of genomic editing is also encompassed, wherein cells derived from a subject are edited ex vivo and reintroduced. The composition includes a CRISPR-associated protein and a guide RNA, optionally formulated in a viral vector or lipid nanoparticle. A vector encoding the CRISPR protein and guide RNA may be delivered as a plasmid, AAV, or lentivirus. Guide RNA design is optimized for specificity, with the target sequence selected to maximize complementarity to the mutant allele while minimizing homology to the wild-type allele. The method for altering a target polynucleotide sequence involves introducing a double-strand break at a site unique to the mutant allele, followed by endogenous repair that results in frameshift mutations and premature termination.

### Animal Model

- The S334ter-line-3 rat model is a transgenic strain expressing a mouse-derived rhodopsin transgene containing the S334ter mutation, which results in a truncated protein lacking the C-terminal 15 amino acids. This model recapitulates the key pathological features of human autosomal dominant retinitis pigmentosa, including rapid photoreceptor degeneration beginning at postnatal day 11, mislocalization of rhodopsin to the cell body, absence of outer segment formation, and complete loss of photoreceptor nuclei by postnatal day 28. The model is characterized by a single-copy integration of the mutant transgene, ensuring that all photoreceptors express the toxic protein. The retinal phenotype is highly reproducible, making it ideal for evaluating therapeutic interventions. The model allows for direct comparison between treated and untreated retinas within the same animal, serving as an internal control. Histological and functional assessments confirm that photoreceptor degeneration is cell-autonomous and directly linked to the expression of the mutant rhodopsin protein. The model has been validated for use in gene therapy studies and provides a robust platform for demonstrating the efficacy of allele-specific ablation.

### CRISPR/Cas9 Constructs, Generally

- The CRISPR/Cas9 system is motivated for use in treating RHO-linked autosomal dominant retinitis pigmentosa due to its ability to distinguish between mutant and wild-type alleles based on single-nucleotide differences. The mechanism involves the formation of a ribonucleoprotein complex between the Cas9 endonuclease and a guide RNA, which directs cleavage to a specific genomic locus adjacent to a protospacer adjacent motif. The system is highly specific, programmable, and capable of functioning in post-mitotic cells such as photoreceptors. The invention utilizes this mechanism to selectively disrupt the mutant allele while preserving the wild-type allele, thereby eliminating the source of toxicity without compromising essential gene function. The approach is distinct from gene replacement or RNA interference, as it permanently alters the genome to inactivate the disease-causing allele.

### CRISPR/Cas9 Constructs, Specifically

- The guide RNA is designed to target a 20-nucleotide sequence immediately upstream of the protospacer adjacent motif unique to the RHO S334 mutant allele. The sequence is selected to maximize complementarity to the mutant allele while introducing at least one mismatch with the wild-type allele at position 10 or 11 of the guide RNA to reduce off-target cleavage. The guide RNA is cloned into a plasmid vector under the control of a U6 promoter, with the Cas9 gene driven by a constitutive promoter such as CAG or chicken β-actin. The vector also includes a fluorescent reporter, such as mCherry, under the control of a cytomegalovirus promoter to enable tracking of transfected cells. The guide RNA sequence is optimized to avoid homology with other genomic loci, and potential off-target sites are computationally screened and experimentally validated. The resulting construct enables efficient and specific cleavage of the mutant RHO allele in vivo.

### Methods

- The CRISPR/Cas9/guide-RNA constructs are delivered via subretinal injection into the eyes of neonatal S334ter-3 rats at postnatal day 0, followed by electroporation to enhance cellular uptake. The injected constructs include a plasmid encoding SaCas9 and a guide RNA targeting the RHO S334 allele, along with an mCherry reporter for transfection visualization. Retinal tissue is harvested at postnatal day 4, and mCherry-positive photoreceptor cells are isolated by fluorescence-activated cell sorting. Genomic DNA is extracted and amplified by PCR using primers flanking the target site, followed by Sanger sequencing to detect indel mutations. Clonal expansion of PCR products in NEB-α cells allows quantification of indel frequency, with cleavage efficiencies of 33 to 36 percent observed in treated cells. No cleavage is detected at the wild-type RHO locus or at eight predicted off-target sites. Immunohistochemical evaluation at postnatal day 33 reveals preservation of up to eight layers of photoreceptor nuclei in treated regions, compared to a single layer in controls. N-terminal rhodopsin labeling confirms the absence of mutant protein in transfected areas, while C-terminal labeling demonstrates proper localization of wild-type rhodopsin to the outer segments. Synaptic preservation is assessed by immunostaining for protein kinase C-alpha and synaptic markers such as ribeye, revealing intact dendritic arborization of rod-bipolar cells and preserved synaptic density. Visual function is evaluated using the optokinetic response, with treated animals showing a 53 percent improvement in visual acuity compared to controls. Statistical significance is determined using two-tailed Student’s t-tests with unequal variance, with p-values less than 0.01 considered significant. The distribution of mCherry expression correlates with regions of photoreceptor rescue, confirming that functional improvement is directly linked to CRISPR activity. The method confirms that RHO S334 ablation prevents retinal degeneration and preserves visual function, establishing a proof-of-principle for allele-specific genome editing in autosomal dominant retinitis pigmentosa. Future investigations will explore long-term stability of editing, the use of dual AAV vectors for enhanced delivery, and the application of peptide-mediated ocular delivery systems such as POD. The invention demonstrates the first in vivo functional correction of a dominant retinal mutation using CRISPR/Cas9, providing a scalable therapeutic strategy for patients with similar mutations.
