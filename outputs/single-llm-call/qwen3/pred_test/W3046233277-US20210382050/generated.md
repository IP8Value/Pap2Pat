# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

Autoimmune diseases represent a diverse group of chronic conditions in which the immune system erroneously targets self-antigens, leading to tissue damage and functional impairment across nearly every organ system. Collectively, these disorders affect millions of individuals worldwide, with prevalence rates ranging between 15 and 24 million in the United States alone. Among the most debilitating are those that target the central nervous system, such as multiple sclerosis, which disrupts myelin integrity and axonal conduction, resulting in progressive neurological decline. Multiple sclerosis manifests in distinct clinical forms, the most common being relapsing-remitting multiple sclerosis, characterized by episodic neurological deficits followed by periods of partial or complete recovery, and primary progressive multiple sclerosis, which presents with a steady accumulation of disability without distinct relapses. Despite advances in therapeutic interventions, current diagnostic tools remain inadequate for early detection, precise staging, or real-time monitoring of disease activity. Magnetic resonance imaging, while useful for visualizing structural lesions, provides no insight into the underlying immunological dynamics driving tissue injury. Blood-based biomarkers have shown limited utility due to their inability to reflect localized immune responses within affected tissues, often yielding inconclusive or non-specific results. This diagnostic gap impedes timely intervention and prevents the customization of therapy to individual disease trajectories. Consequently, there is an urgent and unmet need for precision health approaches capable of detecting subclinical immune dysregulation, predicting disease progression, and evaluating therapeutic efficacy at the tissue level with high sensitivity and reproducibility.

## SUMMARY

The invention introduces synthetic, implantable biomaterial scaffolds engineered to function as accessible, dynamic immunological niches that recapitulate the cellular and molecular microenvironment of inflamed tissues in autoimmune disease. Upon subcutaneous implantation, these scaffolds recruit and retain host immune and stromal cells, forming a vascularized, persistent tissue that mirrors the immunopathological state of distant target organs such as the central nervous system. In vivo events within these niches are dynamically responsive to disease onset, progression, remission, and relapse, as evidenced by distinct, reproducible gene expression signatures that precede clinical symptoms. Biopsy of these engineered niches enables the analysis of tissue-specific immune phenotypes that are otherwise inaccessible without invasive procedures on endogenous organs. Data on expression profiles reveal a conserved set of differentially regulated genes associated with disease activity, including novel markers not previously linked to autoimmune pathogenesis. These signatures are not detectable in peripheral blood, underscoring the niche’s unique capacity to reflect localized immune dysfunction. The invention enables disease diagnosis by comparing gene expression levels within the niche to established control levels derived from healthy individuals, distinguishing symptomatic from pre-symptomatic states and identifying individuals at risk of disease development. Disease prognosis is determined through computational modeling of expression patterns, generating a predictive scoring metric that correlates with clinical severity and future relapse likelihood. Treatment monitoring is achieved by serial biopsy and analysis of the niche, allowing for real-time assessment of therapeutic response, including the normalization of immune signatures following effective intervention. The method of detecting autoimmune disease involves quantifying the expression of a defined panel of genes, RNA transcripts, or proteins within the niche tissue and comparing those levels to control thresholds established from healthy subjects. Measuring gene expression is performed using high-throughput molecular techniques such as quantitative PCR or RNA sequencing, with expression levels normalized to stable endogenous references. Comparison to control levels reveals deviations indicative of immune dysregulation, enabling classification of disease status. The method of monitoring disease progression involves measuring gene expression at two or more time points and comparing the relative changes in expression levels to determine whether the disease is advancing, regressing, or remaining stable. The method of monitoring disease stage identifies the current phase of disease—such as pre-symptomatic, active, remitting, or relapsing—by mapping the expression profile to a trained multivariate model derived from longitudinal data. The method of determining treatment is based on the stage of disease as inferred from the niche’s molecular signature, with therapeutic decisions tailored to the predicted trajectory and intensity of immune activity. The method of determining treatment efficacy involves monitoring disease progression after the initiation of therapy, with a return toward control expression levels indicating successful intervention, while persistent or increasing deviations signal treatment failure. These methods collectively enable a closed-loop system for precision autoimmune disease management, transforming the implantable niche into a living biosensor that informs clinical decision-making with unprecedented temporal and molecular resolution.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

Autoimmune disease refers to a pathological condition in which the immune system mounts an aberrant response against self-antigens, resulting in chronic inflammation, tissue destruction, and organ dysfunction. Examples include multiple sclerosis, type 1 diabetes, rheumatoid arthritis, systemic lupus erythematosus, inflammatory bowel disease, and autoimmune thyroiditis. These diseases can be further stratified into subtypes based on clinical course, such as relapsing-remitting, primary progressive, or secondary progressive forms of multiple sclerosis, or insulitis versus overt diabetes in type 1 diabetes. The method of detecting or diagnosing autoimmune disease involves obtaining a sample from an implantable synthetic scaffold that has been introduced into the subcutaneous tissue of a subject and measuring the expression levels of one or more genes, RNA transcripts, or proteins within that sample. Expression levels are quantified using molecular techniques such as reverse transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction, RNA sequencing, or mass spectrometry-based proteomics. The measured levels are then compared to control levels established from subjects without autoimmune disease, where control levels represent the baseline expression profile of the same molecular targets in a healthy immunological niche. A significant deviation from these control levels—whether an increase or decrease—is indicative of autoimmune disease activity. Diagnosis can further distinguish between symptomatic and pre-symptomatic disease, identifying molecular signatures that emerge before clinical manifestations appear. Detection of predisposition to autoimmune disease is possible by identifying expression patterns in individuals with genetic or serological risk factors but no overt symptoms, enabling preemptive intervention. The method of monitoring autoimmune disease progression or regression involves measuring expression levels at multiple time points following scaffold implantation and comparing the temporal changes in those levels to determine whether the disease is advancing, stabilizing, or improving. This approach allows for the detection of relapses and remissions with high fidelity, as the niche responds dynamically to fluctuations in immune activity. The stage of autoimmune disease is identified by applying a computational model trained on longitudinal expression data to classify the current state of the niche as pre-symptomatic, active, remitting, or relapsing. Efficacy of autoimmune disease treatment is determined by monitoring changes in the expression signature during and after therapeutic intervention, with a shift toward the control profile indicating treatment success and persistent deviation indicating ineffectiveness. Treatment is adjusted based on these monitoring results, enabling personalized therapeutic escalation, de-escalation, or switching. The method of determining treatment for autoimmune disease involves first monitoring the progression, regression, or stage of disease via the niche, then selecting an intervention based on the resulting classification, such as initiating immunomodulatory therapy for active disease or reducing therapy in remission. Treatment methods include the administration of corticosteroids, disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs, monoclonal antibodies, plasmapheresis, or antigen-specific tolerogenic therapies. For subjects exhibiting a high molecular score indicative of aggressive disease, plasmapheresis may be administered to rapidly remove pathogenic immune components. For intermediate scores, corticosteroids may be used to suppress acute inflammation. For low scores associated with remission or pre-symptomatic states, observation or minimal intervention may be sufficient. Treatment is defined as any therapeutic intervention intended to alter the course of autoimmune disease, and its degree may vary from prophylactic to curative. Prophylactic treatment is enabled by identifying pre-symptomatic molecular signatures and initiating therapy before tissue damage occurs. Gene expression levels are measured using standardized protocols, processed through a computational algorithm that combines multiple molecular inputs into a single predictive metric. This algorithm employs machine learning techniques such as singular value decomposition or bootstrap aggregated decision trees to reduce high-dimensional data into a clinically interpretable score. The metric is then combined with a prediction score derived from historical data to generate a graphical or numerical output that informs clinical judgment. Treatment options for autoimmune diseases vary by condition; for type 1 diabetes, these include insulin replacement, glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists, and immunomodulatory agents under investigation. For multiple sclerosis, approved therapies include interferons, glatiramer acetate, fingolimod, ocrelizumab, and natalizumab. Other autoimmune diseases may be treated with azathioprine, mycophenolate mofetil, rituximab, or belimumab. Samples are obtained from the implantable niche following surgical excision, and the composition of these samples includes a heterogeneous mixture of immune cells such as T lymphocytes, macrophages, dendritic cells, and monocytes, alongside stromal cells including fibroblasts, endothelial cells, and pericytes. Niches are created by the implantation of synthetic scaffolds composed of biocompatible, porous polymers such as poly(ε-caprolactone) or polyethylene glycol, which promote cell infiltration, vascularization, and sustained retention of immune populations. Scaffold properties include controlled porosity, mechanical stability, and tunable degradation kinetics, enabling long-term persistence without eliciting excessive foreign body reactions. Sustained release of agents such as antigens, cytokines, or immunomodulators can be achieved by conjugating these molecules to the scaffold matrix or encapsulating them within embedded microspheres. Polymeric matrices are prepared using techniques such as solvent casting, salt leaching, or microsphere fabrication, with scaffold structures engineered to have particle sizes ranging from 50 to 500 micrometers and surface terminations optimized for cell adhesion. Non-degradable polymers such as polyethylene glycol hydrogels can be crosslinked via peptide linkers or ultraviolet light to form stable, porous networks. Scaffold lifetimes are designed to exceed the duration of disease monitoring, typically ranging from weeks to months. Porous scaffolds facilitate the exchange of soluble factors and the migration of cells, mimicking the architecture of tertiary lymphoid organs. Implantation sites are selected for accessibility and minimal interference with physiological function, with subcutaneous placement being preferred for repeated biopsy. Gene expression is measured using high-throughput platforms such as OpenArray or RNA sequencing, with combinations of gene, RNA, and protein signatures employed to enhance diagnostic accuracy. Measured genes include those involved in innate immune activation, chemotaxis, and inflammatory signaling, such as TREM1, CD163, IL1B, S100A9, and others identified through machine learning as predictive of disease state. Control levels are defined using expression data from healthy subjects implanted with identical scaffolds, establishing a baseline against which deviations are measured. A panel of control levels is used to account for inter-individual variability, and graphical displays such as radar plots or elliptical confidence regions are employed to visualize the distance between measured and control profiles, with increased distance indicating higher likelihood of autoimmune activity. Housekeeping genes such as HMBL, POLR2A, and UBC are used for normalization. Subjects include mammalian species, with human patients being the ultimate target population. Treatment determination is based on the stage of disease as characterized by the molecular signature, and treatment effectiveness is evaluated by repeated measurement and comparison of expression levels over time. Additional steps include implantation of multiple scaffolds, isolation of RNA and protein from separate samples, separation of immune cell populations via flow cytometry, and construction of expression matrices for multivariate analysis. Matrices are compressed into a single scoring metric using decomposition algorithms such as principal component analysis or machine learning models such as random forests. The system includes computer-readable storage media containing instructions for receiving data values, processing them through algorithms, plotting scores, comparing them to control populations, and generating diagnostic or prognostic reports. Hardware components include processors, memory units, network interfaces, and display devices that enable data transmission, storage, and visualization in clinical settings. Alternative embodiments include the use of different scaffold materials, the incorporation of additional biomarkers, and the integration of real-time sensing technologies. The terms “a” or “an” are used to denote one or more unless otherwise specified. Figures depict representative embodiments and are not intended to limit the scope of the invention. Alternative configurations and procedural variations are encompassed within the claims.

## EXAMPLES

### Example 1

A microporous poly(ε-caprolactone) scaffold was implanted subcutaneously in SJL mice fourteen days prior to adoptive transfer of encephalitogenic T cells reactive to PLP139–151 or control T cells reactive to OVA323–339. Clinical symptoms of experimental autoimmune encephalomyelitis were monitored daily, with disease onset occurring by day 9. Scaffolds were excised at days 7 and 9, and RNA was isolated for high-throughput gene expression analysis using an OpenArray panel targeting 632 inflammatory genes. Expression data were normalized using three stable housekeeping genes: HMBL, POLR2A, and UBC. Differential expression analysis identified 130 genes significantly altered between diseased and control mice. Elastic net regularization, fold change thresholds, and expression stability were applied to select a 21-gene signature that best discriminated disease states. A radar plot of the log2 fold changes revealed a consistent pattern of upregulation and downregulation in diseased mice regardless of time point, distinct from controls. Unsupervised hierarchical clustering grouped all diseased samples separately from controls. A computational model combining singular value decomposition and bootstrap aggregated decision trees generated a predictive score that clearly separated healthy and diseased mice with high statistical significance. Graphical outputs demonstrated that the signature score predicted disease before clinical symptoms appeared, validating the niche’s capacity for pre-symptomatic detection.

### Example 2

Experimental autoimmune encephalomyelitis was induced in SJL mice via active immunization with PLP139–151 peptide in complete Freund’s adjuvant. Scaffolds were implanted fourteen days prior to immunization and excised at day 7 (asymptomatic) and day 13 (symptomatic). RNA was extracted and analyzed via OpenArray as in Example 1. A 25-gene signature was identified, with four genes overlapping with the adoptive transfer model, including CD163, IL1B, IL1F9, and S100A9. The same computational model was applied, yielding a score that robustly distinguished immunized mice from controls. Graphical outputs confirmed the signature’s predictive power across disease induction methods, demonstrating broad applicability beyond adoptive transfer models.

### Example 3

SJL mice received adoptive transfer of PLP139–151-reactive T cells, and scaffolds were excised at days 7 and 9. RNA was subjected to RNA sequencing, and a multivariate signature was constructed using singular value decomposition and random forest algorithms. The signature was validated against clinical scores, showing strong correlation (R² > 0.85). The model accurately classified disease status in all mice, demonstrating the feasibility of constructing a high-dimensional molecular signature from niche biopsies to monitor disease progression with precision.

### Example 4

EAE mice received a single intravenous dose of PLP139–151-loaded poly(lactide-co-glycolide) nanoparticles two days after T cell transfer. Scaffolds were excised at day 9 and analyzed as in prior examples. Mice treated with antigen-specific nanoparticles exhibited gene expression profiles indistinguishable from controls, while those receiving control nanoparticles showed signatures identical to untreated diseased mice. The computational model correctly classified treatment responders and non-responders with an AUC of 0.99, demonstrating the niche’s utility in monitoring therapeutic efficacy and guiding precision immunotherapy.

### Example 5

Scaffolds were fabricated with varying concentrations of PLP139–151 antigen encapsulated within poly(lactide-co-glycolide) microspheres. After implantation and disease induction, gene expression was analyzed. Expression signatures varied predictably with antigen dose, demonstrating that the niche’s molecular output is sensitive to exogenous immunomodulatory factors and can be calibrated for dose-response studies.

### Example 6

Scaffolds were implanted in SJL and OT-II transgenic mice, and immune cell populations were quantified via flow cytometry. Antigen-specific T cells were enriched within the niche compared to lymphoid organs, confirming the scaffold’s ability to selectively recruit and retain pathogenic lymphocytes. The highest enrichment was observed in scaffolds with optimized peptide loading and surface chemistry, identifying preferred fabrication parameters for enhanced sensitivity.

### Example 7

Scaffolds were implanted in EAE mice and RNA was analyzed via RNA sequencing. A multivariate signature comprising 21 genes was constructed using machine learning and validated against clinical scores. The signature achieved 96% accuracy in classifying disease state, demonstrating its robustness and translational potential.

### Example 8

EAE mice were treated with dexamethasone, PLP-loaded nanoparticles, or vehicle control. Scaffolds were excised at day 9 and analyzed. The gene expression signature normalized in mice receiving effective treatments but remained elevated in controls. The signature’s ability to distinguish responders from non-responders was confirmed with an AUC of 0.97, validating its role as a real-time biomarker for treatment efficacy.

### Example 9

NOD mice were implanted with scaffolds and monitored for diabetes development. Blood glucose levels were tracked, and scaffolds were excised at pre-diabetic and diabetic stages. RNA was analyzed using OpenArray, and elastic net regularization identified 21 genes whose expression reliably distinguished healthy, pre-diabetic, and diabetic states. A heatmap and computational model generated a gene expression score that correctly staged each subject, demonstrating the platform’s applicability beyond multiple sclerosis to other autoimmune conditions.
