## TECHNICAL FIELD

- define technical field  
The present invention relates to the field of environmental microbiology and molecular biotechnology, specifically to methods for the selective isolation, immobilization, and retrieval of living microorganisms from complex solid matrices such as soil and sediment. More particularly, the invention concerns a non-invasive, electrically mediated technique for the controlled attachment of viable microbial cells to conductive electrode surfaces under defined electrochemical conditions, followed by their gentle, high-efficiency detachment using high-frequency alternating potentials. This approach enables the extraction of intact, metabolically active microbial populations from environmental samples without the need for chemical lysis, mechanical disruption, or cultivation, thereby preserving the native physiological state of the organisms for downstream genomic, proteomic, or functional analyses. The invention finds particular utility in metagenomic studies, microbial ecology, bioprospecting for novel enzymes and bioactive compounds, and the development of biosensing platforms for environmental monitoring.

## BACKGROUND ART

- motivate meta-genomic analysis  
The vast majority of microorganisms inhabiting natural environments such as terrestrial soils and deep-sea sediments remain uncultivable under standard laboratory conditions, rendering traditional microbiological techniques inadequate for comprehensive characterization of microbial diversity. Metagenomic analysis has emerged as a powerful alternative strategy for accessing the genetic potential of these uncultured communities by directly extracting and sequencing environmental DNA. This approach bypasses the limitations of cultivation-dependent methods and provides unprecedented insights into the taxonomic composition, functional capabilities, and ecological interactions of microbial ecosystems. The growing demand for novel biomolecules—including antibiotics, extremozymes, and bioactive metabolites—has further accelerated the adoption of metagenomics in industrial biotechnology, pharmaceutical discovery, and environmental remediation. However, the success of metagenomic studies is critically dependent on the quality and representativeness of the DNA extracted, which in turn is influenced by the efficiency of microbial cell recovery from complex environmental matrices.

- limitations of prior art  
Prior methods for microbial extraction from soil and sediment rely predominantly on direct lysis protocols or indirect physical separation techniques such as centrifugation, filtration, or density gradient separation. These approaches suffer from several critical limitations. First, direct extraction methods co-isolate inhibitory substances such as humic acids, polyphenols, and clay particles, which severely interfere with downstream molecular processes including polymerase chain reaction, restriction enzyme digestion, and cloning. Second, indirect methods, while yielding purer DNA, typically recover only a fraction—often as low as 25 to 35 percent—of the total microbial biomass, due to the strong adhesion of many bacterial taxa to soil aggregates and mineral surfaces. This selective loss introduces significant bias into microbial community profiles, obscuring the true diversity and abundance of key functional groups. Furthermore, existing single-cell isolation techniques—including flow cytometry, micromanipulation, microfluidics, and dilution-to-extinction—are ill-suited for direct application to environmental samples due to the physical complexity and heterogeneity of soil and sediment matrices. None of these prior methods enable the simultaneous capture of viable, metabolically active microorganisms in a spatially controlled manner, nor do they permit their non-destructive retrieval for subsequent analysis or cultivation. Consequently, there remains a critical unmet need for a method that enables efficient, selective, and non-destructive isolation of living microorganisms from environmental samples with minimal bias and maximal fidelity to their native state.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

### Object to be Achieved by the Invention

- state object  
The primary object of the present invention is to provide a novel, reproducible, and scalable method for the selective immobilization and retrieval of living microorganisms from complex environmental matrices such as soil and sediment, without compromising cell viability or introducing taxonomic bias. A further object is to enable the precise spatial patterning of microbial cells on conductive surfaces for downstream applications including single-cell analysis, biosensing, and microbial cultivation. Another object is to facilitate the extraction of high-quality, inhibitor-free nucleic acids from environmentally derived microbial populations by isolating intact cells prior to lysis. A further object is to overcome the limitations of conventional microbial recovery techniques by employing a non-chemical, non-mechanical, and non-invasive approach based on electrostatic and electrochemical interactions between microbial cells and electrode surfaces under controlled potential conditions.

### Means for Achieving the Objects

- discover attachment method  
It has been discovered that a weak negative constant potential applied to an indium tin oxide electrode surface, in the range of −0.2 to −0.4 volts relative to an Ag/AgCl reference electrode, induces the selective and reversible attachment of a broad spectrum of living microorganisms suspended in non-nutritive aqueous media such as phosphate-buffered saline, MOPS buffer, tricine buffer, or artificial seawater. This attachment occurs without cell lysis, metabolic inhibition, or significant alteration of cellular physiology, and is dependent on the viability of the microorganism, as dead cells do not exhibit comparable adhesion under identical conditions. The phenomenon is not attributable to electrophoretic forces alone, as demonstrated by the absence of attachment in nutrient-rich media such as Luria-Bertani broth, suggesting a more complex interaction involving microbial surface structures and electrochemical signaling.

- discover detachment method  
It has further been discovered that the attachment of microorganisms to the electrode surface can be reversed by the application of a high-frequency triangular wave potential with an amplitude of ±4 to ±10 millivolts and a frequency of approximately 9 megahertz. This alternating potential induces rapid and efficient detachment of the immobilized cells without causing significant cytotoxicity, enabling the recovery of viable microorganisms with survival rates exceeding 80 percent in most tested taxa. The detachment mechanism is believed to involve oscillatory disruption of electrostatic and adhesive interactions between microbial surface appendages and the electrode surface, coupled with localized hydration changes at the interface.

- separate microorganisms from soil  
The method enables the effective separation of microorganisms from soil and sediment particles by suspending the sample in a non-nutritive electrolyte and applying the negative constant potential to the electrode, thereby attracting and immobilizing microbial cells while leaving particulate matter and inorganic components unbound. This physical separation occurs without the need for centrifugation, filtration, or sonication, and minimizes the co-extraction of inhibitory substances.

- extract nucleic acids  
Following detachment, the recovered microbial cells can be lysed under controlled conditions to yield high-purity, high-molecular-weight nucleic acids that are free from humic acid contamination and suitable for direct use in PCR amplification, cloning, and sequencing workflows.

- perform PCR analysis  
The purified nucleic acids obtained through this method support robust amplification of target genes such as the 16S ribosomal RNA gene, enabling accurate phylogenetic profiling of microbial communities with minimal amplification bias or inhibition.

- describe invention  
The invention comprises a method for the electrical immobilization and retrieval of living microorganisms from environmental samples using a conductive electrode, a controlled potential source, and a defined electrolyte medium. The method involves the sequential application of a constant negative potential to induce selective attachment, followed by a high-frequency alternating potential to induce non-destructive detachment, thereby enabling the recovery of viable, metabolically active microbial populations.

- outline method steps  
The method comprises the following steps: (1) preparing a suspension of the environmental sample in a non-nutritive electrolyte; (2) disposing the suspension onto a conductive electrode surface; (3) applying a constant negative potential between −0.2 and −0.4 volts relative to an Ag/AgCl reference electrode for a period of 1 to 24 hours to facilitate microbial attachment; (4) washing the electrode to remove unbound particles; (5) applying a high-frequency triangular wave potential of ±4 to ±10 millivolts at a frequency of 8 to 10 megahertz for 30 to 60 minutes to detach the microorganisms; (6) collecting the detached microorganisms for downstream analysis or cultivation.

- specify potential ranges  
The constant potential is applied in the range of −0.2 to −0.4 volts relative to an Ag/AgCl reference electrode, with optimal attachment occurring at −0.3 to −0.4 volts. The high-frequency potential is applied in the range of ±4 to ±10 millivolts, with a frequency of 8 to 10 megahertz, and a duration of 30 to 60 minutes. For seawater matrices, the optimal constant potential is shifted to −0.2 to −0.3 volts due to ionic interference.

## MODES FOR CARRYING OUT THE INVENTION

### <Method for Immobilizing Living Microorganisms>

- define method for immobilizing living microorganisms  
The method for immobilizing living microorganisms involves the controlled application of a constant negative electrical potential to a conductive electrode surface in the presence of a microbial suspension derived from an environmental sample. The microorganisms are attracted to and adhere to the electrode surface in a viable, metabolically active state, without the use of chemical adhesives, surface coatings, or biological ligands.

- dispose suspension containing microorganisms on electrode surface  
A suspension containing microorganisms is prepared by homogenizing a soil or sediment sample in a non-nutritive electrolyte solution such as phosphate-buffered saline, MOPS buffer, tricine buffer, or artificial seawater. The suspension is then carefully dispensed onto the surface of a patterned indium tin oxide electrode, ensuring uniform coverage and minimal air bubble formation.

- apply constant potential to electrode  
A constant negative potential is applied to the electrode using a potentiostat, with the potential maintained between −0.2 and −0.4 volts relative to an Ag/AgCl reference electrode. The potential is applied for a duration of 1 to 24 hours, during which time microorganisms selectively adhere to the electrode surface while non-adherent particles remain suspended.

- attach microorganisms to substrate surface  
The microorganisms become firmly attached to the surface of the electrode through a combination of electrostatic and electrochemical interactions, which are dependent on the metabolic state of the cells. The attachment is specific to viable cells, as heat-killed or chemically fixed cells show negligible adhesion under identical conditions.

- specify constant potential range  
The optimal constant potential range for microbial attachment is between −0.3 and −0.4 volts relative to an Ag/AgCl reference electrode. In seawater matrices, the optimal range is shifted to −0.2 to −0.3 volts due to the higher ionic strength and the formation of an ion adsorption layer at the electrode interface.

- describe electrolyte composition  
The electrolyte used for immobilization is a non-nutritive aqueous solution, including but not limited to phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), MOPS buffer, tricine buffer, or artificial seawater. The solution must lack organic carbon sources such as peptones, yeast extract, or glucose, as these inhibit the attachment process.

- specify electrode material  
The electrode is composed of indium tin oxide (ITO) deposited on a glass substrate, with a surface resistivity of approximately 10 ohms per square. Alternative conductive materials such as gold, platinum, or carbon-based electrodes may be used, provided they are chemically inert under the applied potential conditions.

- describe electrode layer formation  
The ITO layer is formed by vacuum evaporation onto a clean glass slide, followed by patterning using photolithography to define discrete electrode regions. An insulating layer of silicon dioxide is deposited around the ITO regions to prevent non-specific adhesion and to enable spatial control of microbial attachment.

- specify electrode layer shape  
The electrode surface is patterned into reticulated or arrayed square regions, each measuring between 5 micrometers and 1 millimeter in diameter, allowing for the spatial organization of microbial colonies. The shape may be modified to suit specific applications, including linear arrays, circular wells, or multi-electrode grids.

- describe electrode layer size  
The total surface area of the electrode may range from 1 square centimeter to 100 square centimeters, depending on the sample volume. For high-throughput applications, arrays of multiple microelectrodes may be fabricated on a single substrate.

- specify application period of constant potential  
The constant potential is applied for a period of 1 to 24 hours, with optimal attachment occurring after 16 to 24 hours. Shorter durations may be used for rapid screening, while longer durations enhance recovery from low-biomass samples.

- describe electrolyte preparation  
The electrolyte is prepared by dissolving the appropriate buffer salts in ultrapure water, adjusting the pH to between 6.8 and 7.4, and filtering through a 0.22-micrometer membrane to ensure sterility and remove particulate contaminants.

- specify salt concentration in electrolyte  
The ionic strength of the electrolyte is maintained at a concentration of 10 to 150 millimolar, with phosphate-buffered saline containing 137 millimolar sodium chloride and 2.7 millimolar potassium chloride being preferred for most applications.

- describe buffer solution composition  
The buffer solution may be composed of HEPES, MOPS, or tricine, each at a concentration of 10 grams per liter, with the pH adjusted to 7.0 using hydrochloric acid or sodium hydroxide. The buffer must be free of reducing agents or chelating agents that interfere with electrode function.

- specify pH range of buffer solution  
The pH of the electrolyte is maintained between 6.8 and 7.4 to ensure optimal microbial viability and consistent electrochemical behavior of the electrode surface.

- describe microorganism attachment to electrode  
Microorganisms attach to the electrode surface in a non-random, spatially defined manner, with cells congregating exclusively over the conductive ITO regions and avoiding the insulating SiO₂ regions. The attachment is reversible and does not require cell wall modification or surface labeling.

- specify microorganism density in electrolyte  
The microbial suspension is adjusted to a density of 1 × 10⁵ to 1 × 10⁸ cells per milliliter, with optimal attachment occurring at densities between 1 × 10⁶ and 5 × 10⁷ cells per milliliter.

- describe microorganism attachment in viable state  
The microorganisms remain metabolically active during attachment, as confirmed by the production of fluorescent formazan upon incubation with the respiratory dye CTC. Viability is preserved throughout the immobilization process, with no significant loss of colony-forming units observed.

- specify microorganism types  
The method is effective for a wide range of microorganisms, including gram-negative bacteria such as Escherichia coli and Shewanella oneidensis, gram-positive bacteria such as Bacillus subtilis and Bacillus halodurans, actinomycetes such as Kocuria rosea, and marine extremophiles such as Shewanella violacea.

- describe electrolyte composition for seawater  
For marine samples, artificial seawater is prepared by dissolving sodium chloride, magnesium chloride, magnesium sulfate, calcium chloride, and potassium chloride in ultrapure water to achieve a salinity of 3.5 percent, with a pH of 8.0 to 8.2.

- specify constant potential range for seawater  
In artificial seawater, the optimal constant potential for microbial attachment is between −0.2 and −0.3 volts relative to an Ag/AgCl reference electrode, due to the influence of divalent cations on the double-layer structure at the electrode interface.

- describe microorganism attachment in seawater  
Microorganisms suspended in artificial seawater exhibit similar attachment behavior to those in freshwater buffers, with the exception of a slight shift in the optimal potential due to ionic screening effects. The attachment efficiency remains high, with survival rates exceeding 85 percent.

- specify microorganism survival rate  
The survival rate of microorganisms following immobilization is consistently greater than 80 percent, as determined by live/dead staining and colony-forming unit assays.

- describe microorganism attachment conditions  
Attachment occurs under ambient or controlled temperature conditions, ranging from 4 to 37 degrees Celsius, depending on the origin of the microorganisms. The process is effective under both aerobic and anaerobic conditions.

- specify microorganism detachment conditions  
Detachment is achieved by applying a high-frequency triangular wave potential of ±4 to ±10 millivolts at 8 to 10 megahertz for 30 to 60 minutes in the same electrolyte used for attachment.

- describe microorganism cultivation  
Following detachment, the recovered microorganisms may be directly transferred to liquid or solid culture media for propagation, or used immediately for single-cell analysis, enzymatic assays, or genomic extraction.

### <Method for Preparing Living Microorganisms>

- define method for preparing living microorganisms  
The method for preparing living microorganisms combines the steps of electrical immobilization and non-destructive detachment to yield a purified, viable microbial population suitable for downstream applications.

- dispose suspension containing microorganisms on electrode surface  
A suspension of environmental microorganisms is prepared and dispensed onto the electrode surface as described above.

- apply constant potential to electrode  
A constant negative potential of −0.3 to −0.4 volts is applied for 16 to 24 hours to immobilize the microorganisms.

- attach microorganisms to substrate surface  
The microorganisms adhere selectively to the conductive regions of the electrode, forming a spatially organized layer.

- apply high-frequency wave potential to electrode  
After washing to remove unbound material, a high-frequency triangular wave potential of ±10 millivolts at 9 megahertz is applied for 60 minutes to detach the microorganisms.

- specify high-frequency wave potential frequency  
The frequency of the triangular wave potential is maintained at 8 to 10 megahertz, with 9 megahertz being optimal for most microbial taxa.

- specify high-frequency wave potential range  
The amplitude of the potential is maintained between ±4 and ±10 millivolts, with ±10 millivolts providing the highest detachment efficiency without compromising viability.

- describe electrolyte composition for detachment  
The electrolyte used for detachment is identical to that used for attachment, ensuring consistency in ionic strength and pH.

- specify salt concentration in electrolyte for detachment  
The salt concentration is maintained at 10 to 150 millimolar, with 137 millimolar sodium chloride being preferred.

- describe microorganism detachment conditions  
Detachment occurs within 30 to 60 minutes, with over 95 percent of attached microorganisms being released into the supernatant.

- specify microorganism survival rate  
The survival rate of detached microorganisms exceeds 85 percent, as measured by live/dead staining and colony-forming unit counts.

- describe microorganism cultivation  
The detached microorganisms may be resuspended in growth media and incubated under conditions appropriate for their physiology, enabling the cultivation of previously uncultivable strains.

- specify microorganism types  
The method is effective for gram-positive and gram-negative bacteria, archaea, actinomycetes, and marine extremophiles.

- describe microorganism attachment and detachment  
The process of attachment and detachment is fully reversible and repeatable, allowing for iterative purification cycles to enhance microbial purity and reduce contamination from particulate matter.

- specify distance of microorganism detachment  
The detached microorganisms are collected within a radius of less than 5 millimeters from the electrode surface, ensuring minimal dilution and high recovery efficiency.

- describe electrode shape and disposition  
The electrode is patterned into discrete regions to allow for spatial control of microbial localization, and may be arranged in multi-well formats for parallel processing.

- specify microorganism attachment and detachment conditions  
Both attachment and detachment occur under sterile conditions, with the electrode surface sterilized by UV irradiation and the electrolyte filtered to prevent contamination.

- describe microorganism retrieval  
The detached microorganisms are retrieved by aspiration or pipetting of the supernatant, followed by centrifugation or filtration for concentration and downstream processing.

## EXAMPLES

- introduce examples  
The following examples illustrate the practical implementation of the invention under controlled laboratory conditions, demonstrating the efficacy, reproducibility, and versatility of the method across diverse environmental samples and microbial taxa.

### Example 1

- prepare soil sample  
A garden soil sample was collected from the grounds of the Japan Agency for Marine-Earth Science and Technology and sieved to remove large particulates.

- attach microorganisms to electrode  
The soil was suspended in phosphate-buffered saline at a concentration of 0.1 grams per milliliter and dispensed onto a patterned ITO electrode.

- apply potential and detach microorganisms  
A constant potential of −0.4 volts was applied for 24 hours, followed by detachment using a 9-megahertz triangular wave potential of ±10 millivolts for 60 minutes.

- stain and observe microorganisms  
The microorganisms were stained with SYTO9 and propidium iodide and visualized using confocal laser scanning microscopy.

- determine live/dead microorganisms  
Live cells were identified by green fluorescence, while dead cells exhibited red fluorescence.

- retrieve and count microorganisms  
The detached cells were collected, counted using a hemocytometer, and quantified as 1.5 × 10⁵ cells per 50 micrograms of soil.

- present results  
The method recovered 3.0 × 10⁹ viable cells per gram of soil, representing approximately 71 percent of the total microbial biomass estimated by direct counting.

### Example 2

- culture microorganisms  
Escherichia coli and Bacillus subtilis were grown to mid-log phase in Luria-Bertani broth and washed three times in PBS.

- attach microorganisms to electrode  
The washed cells were suspended in PBS at a density of 1 × 10⁶ cells per milliliter and applied to the electrode.

- apply potential and detach microorganisms  
A constant potential of −0.4 volts was applied for 24 hours, followed by detachment using ±10 millivolts at 9 megahertz for 60 minutes.

- stain and observe microorganisms  
The cells were stained with SYTO9 and imaged using confocal microscopy.

- present results  
Over 95 percent of E. coli and 90 percent of B. subtilis cells were successfully detached, with survival rates of 83 percent and 47 percent, respectively.

### Example 3

- prepare soil sample  
Garden soil was suspended in PBS and applied to the electrode.

- attach microorganisms to electrode with varied potentials  
Constant potentials of −0.6, −0.4, −0.2, 0.0, +0.2, and +0.6 volts were applied for 24 hours.

- present results  
Maximum attachment occurred at −0.4 volts, with negligible attachment at positive potentials or at open circuit.

### Example 4

- prepare soil sample  
Soil was suspended in PBS and attached using −0.4 volts for 24 hours.

- detach microorganisms with varied potentials  
Triangular wave potentials of ±4, ±6, ±8, and ±10 millivolts at 9 megahertz were applied for 60 minutes.

- present results  
Detachment efficiency increased with amplitude, with ±10 millivolts achieving 99.8 percent detachment and ±8 millivolts resulting in 65.5 percent detachment.

### Example 5

- prepare soil sample with varied electrolytes  
Soil was suspended in PBS, MOPS buffer, tricine buffer, LB medium, and artificial seawater.

- attach microorganisms to electrode  
A constant potential of −0.4 volts was applied for 24 hours.

- present results  
Strong attachment occurred in PBS, MOPS, and tricine buffer, but not in LB medium or artificial seawater at −0.4 volts; optimal attachment in seawater occurred at −0.3 volts.

#### (1) Various Buffers

- present results  
No significant difference in attachment efficiency was observed between PBS, MOPS, and tricine buffer, confirming the robustness of the method across buffer systems.

#### (2) Artificial Seawater

- present results  
Attachment in artificial seawater was optimal at −0.3 volts, consistent with the influence of ionic strength on electrode double-layer formation.

### Example 6

- prepare deep sea bottom sediment  
Deep-sea sediment was collected from Sagami Bay at a depth of 1,176 meters.

- attach and detach microorganisms  
A constant potential of −0.3 volts was applied for 2 hours in artificial seawater, followed by detachment using ±10 millivolts at 9 megahertz for 30 minutes.

- extract DNA  
DNA was extracted using the CTAB method.

- perform PCR amplification  
The 16S rRNA gene was amplified using universal primers 27F and 1492R.

- perform electrophoresis and purification  
PCR products were purified using a gel extraction kit.

- perform subcloning and transformation  
Clones were inserted into a pT7Blue-2 vector and transformed into DH5α competent cells.

- perform sequencing and analysis  
Sequencing of 130 clones revealed 19 bacterial phyla and 23 classes, with Proteobacteria dominating the community.

### Example 7

- perform phylogenetic analysis  
Sequences were aligned and analyzed using BLAST and SILVA databases.

- obtain bacterial flora composition  
The microbial composition was compared to that obtained from direct DNA extraction from sediment.

- show results in FIGS. 10 and 11  
The electrical retrieval method recovered 95 percent of the phyla and 87 percent of the classes detected by direct extraction.
