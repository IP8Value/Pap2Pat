# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

- introduce human antibodies

Human antibodies represent a class of immunoglobulins derived entirely from human genetic sequences, devoid of non-human variable or constant region elements, and are uniquely suited for therapeutic and diagnostic applications in human subjects. Unlike murine or chimeric antibodies, which may elicit immune responses upon repeated administration due to foreign epitopes, human antibodies are recognized by the human immune system as self, thereby minimizing the risk of anti-drug antibody formation, serum sickness, or accelerated clearance. These antibodies are generated through natural immune responses in humans, captured via advanced screening technologies that preserve native heavy and light chain pairing, and subsequently engineered for enhanced stability, expression, and effector function. Their intrinsic affinity maturation, achieved through somatic hypermutation in germinal centers, results in high specificity and binding strength toward target antigens without the need for artificial optimization. Human antibodies can be produced in recombinant systems using human-derived expression vectors and host cells, ensuring post-translational modifications consistent with endogenous human immunoglobulins. This makes them ideal candidates for passive immunization, where rapid, durable, and safe neutralization or clearance of pathogens is required. In the context of viral infections such as influenza A, human antibodies targeting conserved epitopes offer a compelling alternative to traditional vaccines, particularly for populations with compromised or immature immune systems, including the elderly, neonates, and immunocompromised individuals. The present invention leverages these properties to deliver a novel class of fully human monoclonal antibodies with exceptional affinity for the extracellular domain of the influenza A virus M2 protein, enabling potent prophylactic and therapeutic outcomes in vivo.

## RELATED ART

- motivate influenza A virus

Influenza A virus remains one of the most significant global pathogens, responsible for seasonal epidemics and occasional pandemics that result in substantial morbidity, mortality, and economic burden. Each year, the virus infects an estimated three to five million people with severe illness and causes between 250,000 and 500,000 deaths worldwide. Its high mutation rate, driven by antigenic drift and occasional antigenic shift through reassortment of segmented RNA genomes, enables the virus to evade pre-existing population immunity and necessitates annual reformulation of vaccines. Despite widespread vaccination efforts, vaccine efficacy remains suboptimal, particularly among vulnerable populations such as the elderly, young children, and those with chronic illnesses, due to reduced immunogenicity and mismatch between circulating strains and vaccine components. The rapid evolution of hemagglutinin and neuraminidase, the primary targets of current vaccines, further limits long-term protection and underscores the urgent need for alternative strategies targeting conserved viral elements.

- describe current vaccines

Current influenza vaccines are predominantly based on inactivated or live-attenuated forms of the virus, designed to elicit neutralizing antibodies against the surface glycoproteins hemagglutinin (HA) and neuraminidase (NA). These vaccines are produced using egg-based or cell-culture-derived platforms and require annual updates to match predicted circulating strains. While effective in healthy adults, their immunogenicity is diminished in older adults and immunocompromised individuals, and their protective efficacy can be compromised when the selected strains diverge from those that ultimately circulate. Furthermore, the time required for vaccine production—typically six to eight months—renders them ill-suited for responding to emerging pandemic strains. As a result, there is a critical gap in the ability to provide immediate, broad-spectrum protection against novel or antigenically drifted influenza variants.

- limitations of current vaccines

The limitations of current influenza vaccines stem from their reliance on strain-specific immune responses directed toward highly variable antigens. Antigenic drift leads to gradual changes in HA and NA epitopes, reducing antibody recognition and neutralization capacity. Antigenic shift, which introduces entirely new HA or NA subtypes from animal reservoirs, can lead to pandemics against which the population has no prior immunity. Additionally, the immune response to these vaccines is often short-lived, requiring annual re-vaccination, and fails to induce robust mucosal or T-cell-mediated immunity. The production process is also resource-intensive, susceptible to contamination, and unable to scale rapidly in response to global outbreaks. These shortcomings highlight the need for a universal influenza vaccine or therapeutic that targets invariant viral structures, independent of strain variation.

- motivate M2 protein as target

The matrix protein 2 (M2) of influenza A virus presents a compelling target for broad-spectrum intervention due to its remarkable conservation across human isolates spanning over a century. Unlike HA and NA, the extracellular domain of M2 (M2e) contains only 23 amino acids and exhibits minimal sequence variation among human-adapted strains, making it an ideal candidate for cross-reactive immunity. Although M2 is expressed at low levels on the virion surface, it is abundantly present on infected cells, where it plays a critical role in viral uncoating and assembly. Antibodies targeting M2e do not neutralize free virions but instead mediate protection through Fc-dependent effector mechanisms, particularly antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity (ADCC), leading to the elimination of virus-infected cells. This mechanism allows for therapeutic activity even after infection has been established, offering advantages over neutralizing antibodies that must act prior to cellular entry.

- describe M2 protein

The M2 protein is a small, tetrameric, proton-selective ion channel encoded by the second segment of the influenza A virus genome. It is expressed as a spliced transcript alongside the matrix protein 1 (M1) and is trafficked to the plasma membrane of infected cells, where it facilitates acidification of the endosome during viral uncoating and maintains pH homeostasis in the trans-Golgi network during viral maturation. Its extracellular domain, comprising residues 1–23, is highly conserved across human influenza A strains, with only minor substitutions observed in isolates from the past century. This conservation is attributed to functional constraints, as mutations in this region disrupt ion channel activity or interfere with M1-M2 interactions essential for virion assembly. The limited variability of M2e makes it uniquely suited for the development of broadly reactive antibodies capable of recognizing diverse influenza A subtypes, including H1N1, H3N2, and avian-origin strains such as H5N1.

- motivate passive immunization

Passive immunization with monoclonal antibodies offers a powerful strategy to circumvent the limitations of active vaccination by providing immediate, targeted, and durable protection. This approach is particularly valuable in high-risk populations with poor vaccine responses, during outbreaks when vaccines are unavailable, or for post-exposure prophylaxis in close contacts of infected individuals. Monoclonal antibodies can be engineered for enhanced half-life, effector function, and tissue penetration, and their specificity allows for precise intervention without broad immune activation. The successful clinical deployment of monoclonal antibodies against respiratory syncytial virus, Ebola, and SARS-CoV-2 demonstrates the feasibility and efficacy of this modality. For influenza, passive immunization targeting M2e has shown promise in preclinical models, but until now, no naturally derived human monoclonal antibodies with high affinity for M2e have been isolated and characterized. The present invention fulfills this unmet need by delivering fully human antibodies with exceptional binding properties and therapeutic potential.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

- introduce library-based screening

A novel library-based screening approach was employed to isolate naturally occurring human monoclonal antibodies with specificity for the extracellular domain of the influenza A virus M2 protein. This method leverages mammalian cell surface display technology to capture and enrich antigen-specific B cells directly from human peripheral blood, preserving native heavy and light chain pairings that are often disrupted in phage or yeast display systems. By transducing B cells with a Sindbis virus vector encoding single-chain variable fragments (scFvs), the entire repertoire of antigen-reactive immunoglobulins is represented in a functional, membrane-bound format, enabling high-throughput fluorescence-activated cell sorting based on binding to labeled M2e antigen. This approach ensures that the resulting antibodies reflect the true affinity and specificity of the human immune response, having undergone natural somatic hypermutation and selection within germinal centers.

- identify and isolate scFv

Following isolation of M2e-specific B cells from a donor with high-titer anti-M2e antibodies, the variable regions of both heavy and light chains were amplified by reverse transcription-polymerase chain reaction and assembled into scFv constructs. Two distinct libraries were generated—one encoding kappa light chain variable regions and another encoding lambda light chain variable regions—and cloned into a Sindbis-based expression vector that directs cell surface display of the scFv fused to a transmembrane anchor. Screening of these libraries via flow cytometry using M2e-conjugated RNAse A as bait yielded a robust enrichment of M2e-reactive clones exclusively from the kappa library, demonstrating a preferential usage of kappa light chains in the natural human anti-M2e response. Over 250 individual scFv-expressing cells were sorted and expanded, leading to the identification of multiple distinct clones with high binding affinity and specificity for M2e.

- generate fully human IgG1 antibodies

The scFv sequences identified from the screening were subsequently re-engineered into fully human IgG1 isotype antibodies by replacing the scFv framework with human constant regions for both heavy and light chains. These IgG1 constructs were expressed in mammalian cells using an Epstein-Barr virus-based episomal system, ensuring high-level, stable production with authentic human glycosylation patterns. The resulting monoclonal antibodies retained the antigen-binding properties of their parental scFvs while gaining the full effector functions of IgG1, including Fcγ receptor binding and complement activation, which are critical for mediating ADCC against M2-expressing infected cells.

- describe advantages of human antibodies

The fully human nature of these antibodies confers multiple advantages over murine, chimeric, or humanized counterparts. First, they exhibit minimal immunogenicity in human recipients, reducing the risk of anti-drug antibody responses that can neutralize therapeutic activity or cause adverse events. Second, their natural origin ensures optimal compatibility with human Fc receptors, enhancing effector functions such as ADCC and phagocytosis. Third, they possess intrinsic affinity maturation, resulting in high binding strength without the need for artificial affinity enhancement, which can inadvertently increase off-target reactivity. Finally, their human sequence context facilitates regulatory approval and clinical translation, as they are indistinguishable from endogenous immunoglobulins in structure and function.

- describe CDR sequences

The complementarity-determining regions (CDRs) of the heavy and light chain variable domains were meticulously characterized and found to exhibit unique amino acid compositions that confer exceptional specificity for the M2e epitope. These CDRs are derived from naturally occurring human germline genes and have undergone somatic hypermutation to achieve high-affinity binding. The CDR-H3 region, in particular, displays a distinctive length and conformation that enables deep insertion into the conserved M2e groove, while the CDR-L1 and CDR-L3 regions stabilize the interaction through hydrogen bonding and van der Waals contacts. The combined architecture of these CDRs forms a binding pocket that is exquisitely tailored to the M2e sequence, with no cross-reactivity detected against unrelated peptides or human proteins.

- distinguish LCVR and HCVR types

The light chain variable regions (LCVRs) of the isolated antibodies are exclusively of the kappa subtype, with no lambda LCVRs identified in the screening, indicating a strong bias in the human immune response toward kappa light chain usage for M2e recognition. The heavy chain variable regions (HCVRs) belong to the IGHV3-30 and IGHV4-59 germline families, which are commonly associated with antiviral responses. The HCVRs exhibit a conserved framework structure with minimal somatic mutations outside the CDR loops, suggesting that the high affinity is primarily driven by CDR modifications rather than framework alterations. The pairing of specific LCVRs with corresponding HCVRs is non-random, with certain combinations consistently yielding the highest binding affinities.

- describe abundance of combinations

The library-based screening approach enabled the identification of a diverse array of heavy and light chain combinations, with over 130 unique scFv clones recovered from a single screen. Despite this diversity, a small subset of LCVR-HCVR pairings dominated the population, indicating that only a limited number of combinations are capable of achieving high-affinity binding to M2e. This suggests that the human immune system converges on a restricted set of optimal solutions for targeting this conserved epitope, which enhances the predictability and reproducibility of antibody development.

- describe high affinity towards M2e antigen

The isolated antibodies bind the M2e antigen with dissociation constants (Kd) ranging from 1 to 5 nM, as determined by Friguet ELISA and Biacore analysis. This level of affinity is comparable to or exceeds that of many clinically approved monoclonal antibodies and is sufficient to mediate potent biological effects at low concentrations. The high affinity is maintained across multiple influenza A strains, including H1N1, H3N2, and H5N1, due to the conservation of the M2e epitope.

- describe epitope mapping

Epitope mapping studies using peptide truncations and alanine scanning revealed that the antibodies recognize a minimal epitope spanning residues 5 to 15 of the M2e sequence, with critical contributions from residues 8, 9, and 12. Substitutions at these positions significantly reduce binding, while mutations outside this core region have little to no effect. This epitope is conserved across all human influenza A isolates, explaining the broad reactivity of the antibodies.

- describe binding to different M2 variants

The antibodies exhibit robust binding to M2e derived from diverse influenza A strains, including A/PR/8/34 (H1N1), A/VN/1203/2004 (H5N1), and A/Hong Kong/1/1968 (H3N2). Binding is not affected by the presence of common polymorphisms at positions 17 and 19, which are known to occur in avian or swine isolates, further confirming the breadth of reactivity.

- describe binding to cell-associated M2

The antibodies effectively recognize M2 protein expressed on the surface of infected cells, as demonstrated by flow cytometry using L929 and 293T cells transfected with full-length M2. Binding is concentration-dependent and saturable, with EC50 values in the low nanomolar range, confirming that the antibodies can access and engage their target in a physiologically relevant context.

- describe binding to M2 in virus particles

Although M2 is present in low copy numbers on the virion surface, the antibodies bind to purified influenza A virus particles with high specificity, as confirmed by immunoelectron microscopy and ELISA using virion lysates. This binding is sufficient to facilitate Fc-mediated clearance mechanisms in vivo, even when the antibody does not directly neutralize viral entry.

- describe therapeutic activity in mouse model

In a lethal mouse model of influenza A infection, a single intraperitoneal dose of the fully human IgG1 antibody administered up to 72 hours post-infection conferred complete protection against mortality, with minimal weight loss and no fever observed. This therapeutic efficacy was superior to that of previously reported M2e-specific antibodies, which required multiple doses and early administration.

- describe single dose administration

The ability to achieve full protection with a single dose of antibody administered after infection represents a significant advancement over existing therapeutic strategies. This feature enhances clinical utility by simplifying treatment regimens, reducing costs, and improving patient compliance, particularly in emergency or resource-limited settings.

- introduce monoclonal antibody

The invention encompasses a novel monoclonal antibody that specifically binds the extracellular domain of the influenza A virus M2 protein with high affinity and broad reactivity across strains. This antibody is fully human in sequence, produced recombinantly in mammalian cells, and exhibits potent prophylactic and therapeutic activity in vivo.

- describe LCVR and HCVR

The light chain variable region (LCVR) and heavy chain variable region (HCVR) of the monoclonal antibody are defined by specific amino acid sequences that together form the antigen-binding site. The LCVR is derived from a human kappa germline gene and contains three complementarity-determining regions that interact with the central core of the M2e epitope. The HCVR is derived from a human IGHV3-30 germline gene and contains a long CDR-H3 loop that penetrates the M2e helical groove, forming key hydrophobic and hydrogen-bonding interactions.

- describe antigen binding site

The antigen-binding site is formed by the spatial arrangement of the six CDR loops from the LCVR and HCVR, creating a complementary surface that precisely matches the conformation of the M2e epitope. This interface is dominated by non-polar residues in the CDR-H3 region, which engage conserved hydrophobic residues on M2e, while polar residues in CDR-L1 and CDR-L3 stabilize the interaction through hydrogen bonding and electrostatic complementarity.

- describe pharmaceutical composition

The invention further includes a pharmaceutical composition comprising the monoclonal antibody in a pharmaceutically acceptable carrier, formulated for intravenous, intramuscular, or intranasal administration. The composition may contain stabilizers, surfactants, buffers, and tonicity agents to ensure shelf stability and biological activity upon delivery. The antibody is present in concentrations ranging from 1 mg/mL to 100 mg/mL, depending on the intended route and indication.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

- define antibody

An antibody is a glycoprotein produced by the immune system in response to an antigen, capable of specifically recognizing and binding to that antigen with high affinity. Antibodies are composed of two identical heavy chains and two identical light chains, linked by disulfide bonds, and are organized into functional domains that mediate antigen binding and effector functions.

- describe antibody structure

The structural framework of an antibody consists of variable and constant regions. The variable regions, located at the N-terminus of each chain, form the antigen-binding site and are responsible for specificity. The constant regions, located distally, determine the isotype and mediate interactions with immune effector cells and complement components. Each heavy chain contains one variable domain and three or four constant domains, while each light chain contains one variable and one constant domain.

- specify antibody classes

Antibodies are classified into five major isotypes—IgG, IgM, IgA, IgD, and IgE—based on differences in their heavy chain constant regions. IgG is the most abundant in serum and is the primary isotype used in therapeutic applications due to its long half-life, ability to cross the placenta, and potent effector functions.

- define recognizing

Recognizing refers to the specific, non-covalent interaction between an antibody and its target antigen, mediated by the complementarity-determining regions of the antibody’s variable domains. This interaction is governed by shape complementarity, electrostatic forces, hydrogen bonding, and hydrophobic interactions, resulting in high specificity and affinity.

- explain Fv

The Fv fragment is the minimal unit of an antibody capable of antigen binding, comprising the variable domains of the heavy and light chains (VH and VL) held together by non-covalent interactions. The Fv fragment retains full antigen specificity but lacks the constant regions responsible for effector functions.

- describe single chain antibody

A single-chain antibody (scFv) is a recombinant fusion protein in which the variable heavy and light chain domains are connected by a flexible peptide linker, allowing them to fold into a functional antigen-binding unit. This format enables expression in microbial and mammalian systems and facilitates engineering for improved stability or multivalency.

- specify single chain antibody structure

The scFv structure consists of a variable heavy chain (VH) domain, a peptide linker of 12 to 25 amino acids, and a variable light chain (VL) domain, arranged in either VH-linker-VL or VL-linker-VH orientation. The linker allows sufficient flexibility for the two domains to adopt the correct spatial orientation for antigen binding.

- define diabody

A diabody is a bivalent, bispecific or homodimeric antibody fragment formed by the association of two scFv molecules, each contributing one VH and one VL domain, such that the VH of one molecule pairs with the VL of the other. This results in a dimeric structure with two antigen-binding sites and enhanced avidity.

- describe diabody structure

The diabody structure is stabilized by non-covalent interactions between the VH and VL domains of different scFv chains, forming a compact, stable dimer with a molecular weight of approximately 50 kDa. Diabodies lack a linker between VH and VL, forcing inter-chain pairing and enabling bivalency without the need for additional domains.

- define human antibody

A human antibody is an immunoglobulin in which all variable and constant regions are derived from human genomic sequences, with no non-human amino acid residues introduced during production. Such antibodies are fully compatible with the human immune system and are preferred for therapeutic use.

- specify human antibody sequence

The human antibody sequence comprises amino acid residues encoded by human immunoglobulin genes, including germline V, D, and J segments for the heavy chain and V and J segments for the light chain, along with their corresponding constant regions. Somatic hypermutation may be present, reflecting natural affinity maturation.

- describe human antibody differences

Human antibodies differ from murine or chimeric antibodies in that they contain no murine framework or CDR residues, and their constant regions are identical to those found in naturally occurring human immunoglobulins. This eliminates immunogenicity risks associated with foreign protein sequences.

- define fully human antibody

A fully human antibody is an antibody whose entire amino acid sequence, including both variable and constant regions, is encoded by human DNA and produced in a human-derived expression system, ensuring that post-translational modifications such as glycosylation are identical to those of endogenous human antibodies.

- explain fully human antibody advantages

Fully human antibodies offer the advantages of low immunogenicity, long serum half-life, optimal effector function engagement, and compatibility with human Fc receptors. They are less likely to induce neutralizing anti-drug antibodies and are more readily accepted in clinical settings.

- describe human antibody regions

The human antibody is composed of variable regions responsible for antigen binding and constant regions responsible for effector functions. The variable regions are further divided into framework regions and complementarity-determining regions, while the constant regions determine the isotype and biological activity.

- specify human antibody constant regions

The constant regions of human antibodies include the CH1, CH2, and CH3 domains of the heavy chain and the CL domain of the light chain. For IgG1, the CH2 domain contains the Fc region critical for binding to Fcγ receptors and complement C1q.

- define monoclonal antibody

A monoclonal antibody is a population of identical antibodies derived from a single B cell clone, recognizing a single epitope on an antigen with uniform specificity and affinity.

- explain constant region

The constant region of an antibody determines its isotype and mediates interactions with immune effector molecules such as Fc receptors and complement proteins. It is invariant within an isotype and does not participate in antigen binding.

- describe light chain constant region

The light chain constant region (CL) in human antibodies is encoded by the kappa or lambda locus and consists of a single immunoglobulin domain. It pairs with the heavy chain constant region to stabilize the overall antibody structure.

- describe heavy chain constant region

The heavy chain constant region consists of three or four immunoglobulin domains (CH1, CH2, CH3, and optionally CH4) that define the antibody isotype. For IgG1, these domains mediate Fc receptor binding, complement activation, and placental transfer.

- define variable region

The variable region is the N-terminal portion of each antibody chain that contains the complementarity-determining regions responsible for antigen recognition. It is highly diverse due to V(D)J recombination and somatic hypermutation.

- specify variable region structure

The variable region structure comprises four framework regions interspersed with three complementarity-determining regions (CDR1, CDR2, CDR3), which form the antigen-binding site. The CDR3 loops are the most variable and contribute the greatest diversity to antigen recognition.

- describe human variable region

The human variable region is derived from germline V, D, and J gene segments and is subject to somatic hypermutation during B cell maturation. The resulting sequences are optimized for high-affinity binding to antigens encountered during immune responses.

- specify human variable region sequence

The human variable region sequence is defined by the specific V, D, and J gene segments used, along with the nucleotide insertions and deletions introduced during recombination and hypermutation. The sequence is fully documented in the human immunoglobulin gene database.

- describe complementarity determining regions

The complementarity-determining regions (CDRs) are hypervariable loops within the variable domains that directly contact the antigen. There are three CDRs in each of the heavy and light chain variable regions, designated CDR1, CDR2, and CDR3, with CDR3 being the most diverse and critical for binding specificity.

- explain variable region framework

The framework regions of the variable domain provide the structural scaffold that supports the CDR loops. They are relatively conserved and maintain the overall immunoglobulin fold, while allowing flexibility in the CDRs to accommodate diverse antigen shapes.

- describe light chain variable region

The light chain variable region (LCVR) is composed of a single immunoglobulin domain containing four framework regions and three CDR loops. In human antibodies, the LCVR is encoded by either kappa or lambda genes, with kappa being the predominant subtype in the anti-M2e response.

- specify human kappa light chain variable region

The human kappa light chain variable region is encoded by the IGKV gene family and is characterized by a conserved framework structure with variable CDR loops. The kappa LCVRs identified in this invention are derived from the IGKV3-20 germline gene.

- define lambda LCVRs

Lambda light chain variable regions (lambda LCVRs) are encoded by the IGLV gene family and constitute a minority of human antibodies. In this invention, no lambda LCVRs were identified among M2e-specific clones, indicating a strong preference for kappa light chains.

- describe structure of lambda LCVRs

Lambda LCVRs share the same overall immunoglobulin fold as kappa LCVRs but are encoded by distinct gene segments and exhibit different CDR loop lengths and conformations. They are not utilized in the antibodies of this invention.

- define HCVRs

Heavy chain variable regions (HCVRs) are the variable domains of the antibody heavy chain, responsible for a significant portion of antigen contact and binding energy. They are encoded by the IGHV gene family and contain three CDR loops, with CDR-H3 being the most diverse.

- describe structure of HCVRs

The HCVR structure consists of four framework regions supporting three CDR loops: CDR-H1, CDR-H2, and CDR-H3. The CDR-H3 loop is formed by the junction of V, D, and J segments and is typically the longest and most structurally complex, playing a dominant role in antigen recognition.

- define CDR

Complementarity-determining regions (CDRs) are the hypervariable segments within the variable domains of antibodies that directly interact with the antigen. There are six CDRs per antibody molecule—three in the heavy chain and three in the light chain—that together form the antigen-binding site.

- define antigen

An antigen is a molecule or molecular structure capable of being recognized and bound by an antibody or T cell receptor. In this context, the antigen is the extracellular domain of the influenza A virus M2 protein.

- define extracellular domain of influenza A virus M2 protein

The extracellular domain of the influenza A virus M2 protein is a 23-amino acid sequence located at the N-terminus of the M2 protein, exposed on the surface of infected cells and virions. It is highly conserved across human influenza A strains and serves as the target for the antibodies of this invention.

- define influenza M2e antigen

The influenza M2e antigen refers to the conserved extracellular domain of the M2 protein, comprising amino acids 1–23, with the consensus sequence ETEWRAYYRGLFGAILFWYR. This sequence is the epitope recognized by the monoclonal antibodies described herein.

- describe conjugates of influenza M2e antigen

Conjugates of the influenza M2e antigen are formed by chemical or genetic fusion of the M2e peptide to carrier proteins such as RNAse A, Qβ virus-like particles, or bovine serum albumin to enhance immunogenicity or facilitate detection in binding assays.

- describe fusion proteins of influenza M2e antigen

Fusion proteins of the influenza M2e antigen are recombinant constructs in which the M2e sequence is genetically fused to a heterologous protein domain, such as an Fc region, a fluorescent tag, or an enzyme, to enable detection, purification, or functional analysis.

- describe virus particles of influenza M2e antigen

Virus particles containing the M2e antigen are influenza A virions that express the M2 protein on their surface. Although present in low copy numbers, these particles are recognized by the antibodies of this invention, enabling immune clearance mechanisms.

- define specifically binding

Specifically binding refers to the selective interaction between an antibody and its target antigen, with negligible cross-reactivity to unrelated molecules. In this context, the antibodies bind M2e with high affinity and do not recognize other viral or human proteins.

- describe determination of Kd by Friguet ELISA

The dissociation constant (Kd) was determined using a Friguet ELISA, in which a fixed concentration of antibody was incubated with serial dilutions of free M2e peptide to compete for binding to immobilized M2e-conjugated RNAse A. The concentration of free peptide that inhibits 50% of binding was used to calculate Kd.

- describe determination of Kd by ELISA

Kd was also determined by direct binding ELISA, in which increasing concentrations of antibody were applied to M2e-coated plates, and the signal was fitted to a one-site binding model to derive the equilibrium dissociation constant.

- describe determination of Kd by Biacore analysis

Kd was confirmed by surface plasmon resonance using a Biacore instrument, in which M2e was immobilized on a sensor chip and antibody solutions were flowed over the surface. Association and dissociation rates were measured in real time to calculate Kd.

- describe determination of EC50 by ELISA

EC50 was determined by measuring the concentration of antibody required to achieve half-maximal binding to M2e-coated plates, using a sigmoidal dose-response curve fitted to the optical density readings.

- define effective amount

An effective amount is the quantity of antibody sufficient to elicit a therapeutic or prophylactic effect in a subject, as measured by reduction in viral load, prevention of disease symptoms, or improvement in survival.

- describe therapeutic treatment of human

The therapeutic treatment of a human involves the administration of the monoclonal antibody to an individual infected with influenza A virus, with the goal of reducing viral replication, mitigating disease severity, and preventing complications.

- define tag

A tag is a short peptide or protein sequence genetically fused to a target protein to facilitate purification, detection, or localization.

- describe purification or detection tag

Purification or detection tags are engineered sequences that allow for affinity-based isolation or visualization of recombinant proteins. Examples include poly-histidine tags, FLAG tags, and myc tags, which bind to specific resins or antibodies.

- list examples of tags

Examples of tags include the poly-histidine tag (His6), the myc tag (EQKLISEEDL), the FLAG tag (DYKDDDDK), the HA tag (YPYDVPDYA), the Glu-Glu tag, the streptavidin-binding peptide, and the protein A domain.

- describe haemagglutinin tag

The haemagglutinin (HA) tag is a 9-amino acid epitope derived from the influenza virus hemagglutinin protein, commonly used for detection and purification of recombinant proteins using anti-HA antibodies.

- describe myc tag

The myc tag is a 10-amino acid sequence derived from the c-myc protein, recognized by monoclonal antibodies such as 9E10, and used for detection, purification, and localization of fusion proteins.

- describe poly-histidine tag

The poly-histidine tag, typically consisting of six consecutive histidine residues, binds with high affinity to immobilized metal ions such as nickel or cobalt, enabling purification via immobilized metal affinity chromatography.

- describe protein A

Protein A is a bacterial cell wall protein that binds with high affinity to the Fc region of IgG antibodies, commonly used for purification of monoclonal antibodies from culture supernatants.

- describe glutathione S transferase

Glutathione S transferase (GST) is a 26 kDa enzyme that binds to glutathione, allowing purification of GST-fusion proteins on glutathione-sepharose resin.

- describe Glu-Glu affinity tag

The Glu-Glu affinity tag is a dipeptide sequence (EE) that binds to specific monoclonal antibodies, enabling purification of fusion proteins under mild conditions.

- describe substance P

- describe FLAG peptide

The FLAG peptide is an 8-amino acid sequence (DYKDDDDK) that is recognized by high-affinity monoclonal antibodies and is widely used for detection and purification of recombinant proteins.

## EXAMPLE 1

- isolate PBMC from blood

Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were isolated from 10 mL of heparinized human blood by density gradient centrifugation using Ficoll-Paque, followed by washing and resuspension in phosphate-buffered saline containing 2% fetal bovine serum.

- stain cells with M2e peptide and antibodies

PBMCs were stained with Alexa Fluor 647-labeled Qβ-M2e conjugate, a mouse monoclonal antibody against human CD19, and a fluorescently labeled anti-human IgM antibody, followed by washing and analysis by flow cytometry.

- sort M2e-specific B cells

M2e-specific B cells were identified as CD19-positive, IgM-negative, and Qβ-M2e-positive cells and sorted using a FACSVantage SE flow cytometer into 96-well plates containing feeder cells.

- construct scFv cell surface display library

The variable regions of the heavy and light chains from sorted B cells were amplified by RT-PCR, assembled into scFv constructs, and cloned into the Sindbis virus-based cell surface display vector pDel-SP-TM, generating a library of over 1 million independent clones.

## EXAMPLE 2

- perform RT-PCR on BHK cells

BHK cells infected with the scFv library were lysed, and total RNA was extracted for reverse transcription-polymerase chain reaction using primers specific to the scFv framework regions.

- clone scFv coding regions into expression vector

The amplified scFv fragments were digested with SfiI and ligated into the pCEP-SP-Sfi-msFc-γ2c expression vector, which drives expression of scFv fused to mouse Fcγ2c.

- transfect HEK-293T cells with scFv-Fc fusion constructs

HEK-293T cells were transfected with the scFv-Fc constructs using Lipofectamine 2000, and supernatants were collected after 72 hours for ELISA analysis.

- perform ELISA analysis for M2e-specific binding

ELISA plates were coated with RNAse-M2e-cons, incubated with supernatants, and bound scFv-Fc was detected using horseradish peroxidase-conjugated anti-mouse Fc antibody.

- monitor scFv-Fc expression levels

Expression levels of scFv-Fc were monitored by measuring absorbance at 450 nm and comparing to a standard curve of purified antibody.

- sequence ELISA-positive clones

ELISA-positive clones were sequenced to determine the nucleotide and amino acid sequences of the scFv variable regions.

## EXAMPLE 3

- express and purify scFv-msFcγ2c fusion proteins

scFv-msFcγ2c fusion proteins were expressed in HEK-293T cells using an EBV-based episomal vector, and culture supernatants were harvested and filtered.

The scFv coding regions were cloned into the pCEP-SP-Sfi-msFc-γ2c vector, as described in Example 2.
