# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

- relate to remediation methods of heavy metal contaminated soil

The present invention relates to systems and methods for the remediation of soil contaminated with heavy metal cations, particularly in agricultural and urban environments where long-term soil fertility and ecological safety are paramount. The invention provides a novel, scalable, and sustainable approach to the removal of toxic heavy metals such as copper, lead, and cadmium from contaminated soil matrices without compromising the essential nutrient content or structural integrity of the soil. The method integrates a recirculating chelating agent washing system with an asymmetrical alternating current electrochemical filtration device, enabling the selective extraction, reduction, and immobilization of heavy metal species into zero-valent metallic forms while preserving nutrient cations such as calcium and magnesium in the soil. This innovation addresses critical limitations of existing remediation technologies by eliminating the need for continuous chemical replenishment, preventing secondary pollution, and enabling the recovery of valuable metals for potential reuse.

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

- introduce soil contamination as a global environmental crisis

Soil contamination by heavy metals has emerged as a pervasive and escalating global environmental crisis, driven by decades of industrial activity, mining operations, agricultural chemical use, and improper waste disposal. Contaminated soils pose significant risks to human health through the food chain, particularly in regions where crops are grown on polluted land, and impair ecosystem functions by disrupting microbial communities and reducing biodiversity. The persistence of heavy metals in the environment, coupled with their bioaccumulative nature, renders conventional remediation strategies inadequate for large-scale, long-term applications. The increasing demand for arable land to support a growing global population further intensifies the urgency for effective, economically viable, and environmentally benign soil restoration techniques.

- describe heavy metal contamination in soil

Heavy metals in contaminated soils predominantly exist as cationic species—such as Cu²⁺, Pb²⁺, Cd²⁺, Zn²⁺, and Ni²⁺—that are bound to soil particles via electrostatic interactions, ligand exchange, or incorporation into mineral structures. These cations are not biodegradable and remain in the environment indefinitely unless physically removed or chemically transformed into inert forms. Their mobility and bioavailability are influenced by soil pH, organic matter content, and the presence of chelating agents, which can either enhance or suppress their uptake by plants and groundwater systems. The challenge lies not only in extracting these metals but in doing so without inducing collateral damage to the soil’s natural physicochemical properties.

- discuss limitations of soil washing with strong chelating agents

Soil washing using strong chelating agents such as ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) has been widely explored as a means to mobilize heavy metal cations from soil matrices by forming stable, water-soluble complexes. However, this approach suffers from several critical drawbacks. First, it requires large volumes of chelating agents, leading to high operational costs and significant chemical waste. Second, the effluent generated contains concentrated metal-chelate complexes that are difficult to treat and often require additional downstream processing to prevent leaching into water systems. Third, the chelating agents indiscriminately extract both toxic heavy metals and essential nutrient cations such as Ca²⁺ and Mg²⁺, resulting in long-term soil degradation and reduced agricultural productivity. The inability to regenerate or recycle the chelating solution renders this method unsustainable for repeated or large-scale applications.

- discuss limitations of using high-surface-area sorbent

Alternative approaches employing high-surface-area sorbents, such as activated carbon, biochar, or clay minerals, aim to immobilize heavy metals through physical adsorption or weak chemical binding. While these materials can reduce metal mobility and bioavailability, their capacity is inherently limited by equilibrium-driven processes, resulting in slow kinetics and low saturation thresholds. Moreover, the bound metals remain in a potentially labile state, susceptible to re-mobilization under changing environmental conditions such as pH fluctuations or redox shifts. Long-term stability is therefore uncertain, necessitating continuous monitoring and often leading to the need for secondary containment measures that increase overall cost and complexity.

- discuss limitations of phytoremediation

Phytoremediation, which utilizes plants to uptake and accumulate heavy metals from soil, offers a low-energy, aesthetically pleasing solution. However, its applicability is severely constrained by the extremely slow rate of metal extraction, often requiring multiple growing seasons to achieve meaningful remediation. Additionally, the harvested biomass becomes a hazardous waste stream requiring safe disposal or metal recovery infrastructure, which is rarely available in affected regions. The method is also ineffective for deep contamination and is highly sensitive to soil type, climate, and plant species suitability, limiting its utility in diverse or densely populated environments.

- introduce electrochemistry as a method for heavy metal reduction

Electrochemical methods present a compelling alternative by enabling the direct reduction of heavy metal cations to their zero-valent metallic states, thereby achieving permanent immobilization and facilitating potential metal recovery. The principle of applying an electric field to drive ion migration and electrodeposition has been investigated in situ, particularly through direct current (DC) field application. This approach exploits the difference in reduction potentials between toxic metals and nutrient ions to selectively deposit heavy metals onto electrodes. However, practical implementation has been hindered by fundamental inefficiencies that limit scalability and energy economy.

- discuss limitations of current electrochemical remediation methods

Current electrochemical remediation techniques rely on high-voltage DC fields to induce electroosmotic flow and ion migration through the soil matrix. These systems require voltages on the order of 100 V/m to overcome the low ionic conductivity of soil, resulting in excessive energy consumption and parasitic water electrolysis at the electrodes. Furthermore, the formation of hydroxide precipitates at the cathode due to local pH elevation leads to electrode passivation, reduced efficiency, and co-deposition of nutrient cations such as calcium. The resulting non-conductive layers impede further metal deposition and cause inconsistent performance over time. The inability to effectively manage the charge state of metal-chelate complexes in solution further diminishes the efficacy of DC-based systems, rendering them unsuitable for field-scale deployment in complex, heterogeneous soil environments.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

- introduce AACE method and system for heavy metal contaminated soil remediation

The present invention introduces a novel asymmetrical alternating current electrochemistry (AACE) method and integrated system for the efficient, sustainable, and scalable remediation of heavy metal-contaminated soil. The AACE system combines a recirculating chelating agent washing unit with a specialized electrochemical filtration device that employs asymmetrically pulsed voltage waveforms to selectively extract, bind, and reduce heavy metal cations to their zero-valent metallic states. This approach enables the continuous reuse of chelating agents, eliminates secondary pollution, and preserves soil nutrient content, offering a transformative solution to the limitations of prior technologies.

- describe recirculating soil washing system and AACE filtration device

The system comprises a recirculating soil washing apparatus in which contaminated soil is placed in a column and washed with a chelating agent solution, such as EDTA, to mobilize heavy metal cations as anionic metal-chelate complexes. The effluent is then directed through an AACE filtration device consisting of two parallel amidoxime-functionalized porous carbon (Ami-PC) electrodes separated by a porous membrane. The electrodes are subjected to an asymmetrical alternating current waveform, alternating between a mild positive bias to facilitate chelate binding and a strong negative bias to drive electrochemical reduction, thereby achieving high-efficiency metal capture and immobilization without electrode passivation.

- explain heavy metal cation binding and electrochemical reduction

The amidoxime functional groups on the Ami-PC electrodes serve dual roles: they enhance the hydrophilicity of the electrode surface to maximize contact with the aqueous effluent and provide high-affinity chelation sites that compete with EDTA to bind heavy metal cations. During the positive bias phase, the metal-chelate complexes are adsorbed onto the electrode surface, with the amidoxime groups displacing EDTA and stabilizing the metal ions in close proximity to the electrode. Upon switching to the negative bias, the metal ions are reduced to their zero-valent metallic states, forming discrete, conductive particles that remain firmly anchored to the electrode surface. The EDTA anions, now devoid of bound metal, are repelled by the negatively charged electrode and return to the washing solution, enabling its continuous reuse.

- describe advantages of AACE method, including fast remediation speed and limited chemical cost

The AACE method achieves rapid remediation by combining the high extraction efficiency of chelation with the high selectivity and speed of electrochemical reduction. Unlike conventional methods requiring multiple treatment cycles or extended timeframes, the AACE system achieves compliance with regulatory thresholds within hours, even for highly contaminated soils. The closed-loop design minimizes chelating agent consumption, reducing chemical costs by over 90% compared to single-use washing systems. The system’s modular design allows for scalability from small field applications to industrial-scale treatment facilities.

- discuss high sustainability of the method and its feasibility for agricultural use

The AACE method is uniquely sustainable due to its zero-waste effluent profile, minimal energy consumption relative to performance, and preservation of soil nutrients. The absence of nutrient leaching ensures that treated soils retain their fertility, making the method directly applicable to agricultural land rehabilitation. The recovered heavy metals exist as solid, non-leachable metallic particles that can be harvested and recycled, transforming remediation from a cost center into a potential resource recovery operation.

- provide examples of chelating agents and heavy metal cations

The chelating agents suitable for use in the AACE method include ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA), diethylenetriaminepentaacetic acid (DTPA), and citric acid derivatives, with EDTA being the preferred embodiment due to its high stability constants with target heavy metals. The heavy metal cations effectively treated by the AACE method include copper (Cu²⁺), lead (Pb²⁺), cadmium (Cd²⁺), zinc (Zn²⁺), and nickel (Ni²⁺), with demonstrated efficacy across a broad concentration range from 100 ppm to 10,000 ppm.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

- introduce AACE remediation method

The AACE remediation method is a two-stage process integrating soil washing with electrochemical filtration, designed to achieve complete removal of heavy metal cations from contaminated soil while preserving soil structure and nutrient content. The method operates under ambient conditions without the need for high temperatures, extreme pH, or hazardous reagents. It is applicable to a wide range of soil types, including sandy, loamy, and clay-rich matrices, and is particularly suited for sites where agricultural reuse is the intended post-remediation outcome.

- describe recirculating chelating agent washing system and AACE filtration device

The recirculating washing system consists of a soil column enclosed within a sealed chamber, connected via tubing to two reservoirs containing the chelating agent solution. A peristaltic pump circulates the solution through the soil column, mobilizing heavy metal cations into the effluent. The effluent is then directed into the AACE filtration device, which contains two parallel Ami-PC electrodes separated by a microporous separator. The electrodes are electrically connected to a programmable power supply capable of delivering asymmetrical alternating current pulses with precisely controlled amplitude and duration.

- explain fabrication of Ami-PC electrodes

The Ami-PC electrodes are fabricated by dip-coating a carbon felt substrate with a slurry composed of polyacrylonitrile (PAN), activated carbon, and dimethylformamide. After drying, the coated felt undergoes a hydrothermal reaction in an aqueous solution of hydroxylamine hydrochloride and sodium carbonate at 70°C for 90 minutes, converting nitrile groups in PAN to amidoxime functionalities. The resulting material exhibits a high density of chelating sites, enhanced hydrophilicity, and improved electrical conductivity due to the embedded activated carbon particles.

- describe morphology of Ami-PC electrodes

The Ami-PC electrodes possess a fibrous, porous morphology with fiber diameters of approximately 20 micrometers and pore sizes ranging from tens to hundreds of micrometers. Scanning electron microscopy reveals a uniform coating of amidoxime polymer across the carbon fibers, ensuring consistent chelation capacity and electrical contact throughout the electrode volume. The porous structure facilitates high fluid throughput while maintaining intimate contact between the solution and functional groups.

- explain AACE method process

The AACE method process consists of three sequential phases: ion dispersion, chelate binding, and electrochemical reduction. In the first phase, metal-chelate complexes are uniformly distributed in the effluent. In the second phase, a mild positive voltage (5 V) is applied, inducing ion migration and establishing an electrical double layer at the electrode surface, where amidoxime groups selectively bind heavy metal cations. In the third phase, the voltage is reversed to a strong negative bias (−10 V), triggering the reduction of bound metal ions to zero-valent states, while EDTA anions are repelled back into solution.

- describe Step I of AACE method

In Step I, the washing effluent, containing anionic metal-chelate complexes such as [Cu(EDTA)]²⁻, [Pb(EDTA)]²⁻, and [Cd(EDTA)]²⁻, flows freely through the filtration device with no applied bias. The ions remain dispersed in solution, with no preferential interaction with the electrode surfaces. This phase ensures complete mixing and prepares the system for controlled electrochemical activation.

- describe Step II of AACE method

In Step II, a 5 V bias is applied for 0.5 milliseconds, generating an electric field that drives anions toward the anode and cations toward the cathode. The amidoxime functional groups on the Ami-PC electrode surface competitively bind heavy metal cations from the chelate complexes, displacing EDTA and forming a stabilized metal-amidoxime intermediate. The anionic EDTA remains in solution, preserving its capacity for reuse in subsequent washing cycles.

- describe Step III of AACE method

In Step III, the bias is reversed to −10 V for 2 milliseconds, inducing a strong reducing potential at the electrode surface. The bound metal cations are reduced to their zero-valent metallic states, forming discrete nanoparticles that adhere to the electrode. The now-neutral metal particles no longer interact with EDTA, which is electrostatically repelled and returns to the washing solution. Nutrient cations such as Ca²⁺ and Mg²⁺, which form weaker complexes with EDTA and possess lower reduction potentials, remain in solution and are not deposited.

- discuss remediation performance of AACE method

The AACE method achieves complete removal of heavy metals from contaminated soil within six hours, reducing concentrations of copper, lead, and cadmium below regulatory thresholds established for residential and agricultural land use. The method demonstrates consistent performance across a wide range of initial contamination levels, from 100 ppm to 10,000 ppm, with filtration efficiencies exceeding 90% at optimized flow rates.

- describe experimental setup for remediation performance evaluation

Experimental evaluation was conducted using synthetic soil samples prepared by spiking clean agricultural soil with copper, lead, and cadmium nitrates, followed by aging at 80°C for one month to simulate field conditions. Soil columns were treated using the AACE system at controlled flow rates, with effluent analyzed by inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS) to quantify metal removal and nutrient retention.

- discuss results of remediation performance evaluation

Results demonstrated that the AACE method reduced copper concentrations from 10,000 ppm to 12 ppm, lead from 1,000 ppm to 47 ppm, and cadmium from 100 ppm to 1.2 ppm—all below California Human Health Screening Levels. In contrast, systems without applied bias or using DC voltage failed to achieve comparable removal, with filtration efficiencies below 70%.

- compare AACE method with DC method

Compared to direct current electrochemical methods, the AACE method exhibits superior filtration efficiency, achieving over 90% metal recovery versus 30–70% for DC systems. The DC method suffers from electrode passivation due to hydroxide precipitation and co-deposition of nutrient cations, whereas the AACE method avoids these issues through its asymmetric waveform and selective chelation mechanism.

- discuss filtration efficiency of AACE method

Filtration efficiency is directly influenced by the flow rate of the washing effluent. At flow rates below 0.1 mL/min, efficiency exceeds 95%, while higher rates reduce contact time and lower efficiency. The optimal flow rate is determined by the contamination level and soil permeability, allowing for tailored system design.

- discuss effect of flow rate on filtration efficiency

Increasing flow rate reduces the residence time of metal-chelate complexes at the electrode surface, limiting the extent of binding and reduction. A flow rate of 0.1 mL/min was identified as the upper threshold for maintaining filtration efficiency above 90% across all tested metals, balancing remediation speed with treatment efficacy.

- discuss nutrient metal ion concentration during remediation

Concentrations of nutrient cations such as magnesium and calcium remained stable throughout the remediation process, demonstrating that the AACE method selectively targets heavy metals without depleting soil nutrients. In contrast, fresh EDTA washing caused a 40–60% decline in soil nutrient content.

- discuss treatment of mixed contamination

The AACE method effectively treats soils contaminated with multiple heavy metals simultaneously. In mixed-contamination trials, copper, lead, and cadmium were all reduced below regulatory thresholds within six hours, with no interference between metal species during binding or reduction.

- compare AACE method with DC method for mixed contamination

Under identical conditions, the DC method exhibited inconsistent performance in mixed-contamination scenarios, with preferential deposition of calcium and incomplete removal of cadmium due to hydroxide interference. The AACE method maintained uniform efficiency across all metals.

- discuss remediation mechanism of AACE method

The mechanism is based on the synergistic interaction between chelation-driven mobilization and voltage-controlled reduction. The amidoxime groups act as molecular switches, binding metal cations during the positive phase and releasing them for reduction during the negative phase, preventing electrode fouling and maximizing metal recovery.

- describe characterization of heavy metal species

Metal species deposited on the electrodes were characterized using scanning electron microscopy, energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy, X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy, and transmission electron microscopy. Results confirmed the presence of zero-valent copper, lead, and cadmium as polycrystalline particles with lattice spacings corresponding to metallic phases.

- discuss XPS analysis of heavy metal species

XPS spectra revealed Cu 2p peaks at 932.8 eV and 952.6 eV, indicative of metallic copper, with negligible satellite features confirming the absence of Cu(II). Similar analysis confirmed Pb and Cd in their zero-valent states, with minimal hydroxide or oxide signatures.

- discuss TEM analysis of heavy metal particles

TEM images showed polycrystalline particles with lattice fringes matching the (110) plane of copper (2.6 Å), the (110) plane of lead (3.5 Å), and the (002) plane of cadmium (2.8 Å), confirming the formation of pure metallic deposits.

- discuss long-term performance of AACE filter

After 25 consecutive treatment cycles, the AACE filter retained 90% of its initial filtration efficiency, with only a 20% decline in EDTA extraction capacity. The electrode surface remained structurally intact, with no signs of corrosion or polymer degradation.

- discuss recyclability of EDTA solution

The EDTA solution was reused for 25 cycles without significant loss of chelating capacity, demonstrating that the AACE method prevents EDTA degradation or accumulation in soil. Residual EDTA concentration in treated soil was below 12 mmol/kg, well below phytotoxic thresholds.

- discuss regeneration of AACE filter

Regeneration was achieved by applying a 1 V reverse DC bias in 0.1 M HCl, which dissolved deposited metals without damaging the electrode. Filtration efficiency was fully restored to 100%, confirming the system’s durability and reusability.

- discuss plant assay results

Pea sprouts grown in AACE-treated soil exhibited no accumulation of cadmium, with concentrations in all plant tissues below 0.1 ppm, meeting international food safety standards. Shoot height and leaf biomass were statistically indistinguishable from control plants grown in uncontaminated soil.

- discuss feasibility of AACE method for agricultural land remediation

The AACE method is uniquely suited for agricultural land remediation due to its preservation of soil nutrients, absence of chemical residues, and compatibility with post-treatment crop cultivation. The method enables rapid return of contaminated land to productive use without long-term monitoring requirements.

- discuss Cd accumulation in pea sprouts

No detectable cadmium accumulation was observed in roots, stems, or leaves of pea sprouts grown in AACE-treated soil, confirming the immobilization of residual cadmium in a non-bioavailable form.

- discuss Cd bioavailability in treated soil

Bioavailability assays confirmed that cadmium in AACE-treated soil was effectively immobilized, with extraction using weak chelators yielding negligible concentrations, indicating low environmental mobility.

- discuss pea growth in treated soil

Pea plants grown in AACE-treated soil exhibited growth metrics equivalent to those in uncontaminated soil, demonstrating no phytotoxic effects from residual treatment components or metal residues.

- discuss limitations of using fresh EDTA solution

Use of fresh EDTA solution resulted in significant nutrient loss, stunted plant growth, and generation of hazardous effluent requiring costly disposal. The method is economically and environmentally unsustainable for repeated applications.
