## DESCRIPTION

The presence of metal pollutants in wood, introduced through the application of metal-based preservatives for the protection of timber against fungal decay, insect infestation, and microbial degradation, represents a significant environmental and economic challenge in the sustainable utilization of lignocellulosic biomass. These preservatives, historically dominated by chromated copper arsenate (CCA), copper azole (CA), and ammoniacal copper quaternary (ACQ), embed copper, arsenic, and chromium ions into the cellular matrix of wood, rendering the material toxic and unsuitable for conventional disposal methods such as landfilling or incineration without stringent regulatory controls. The persistence of these heavy metals in the environment poses long-term risks to soil and groundwater systems, and their accumulation in waste streams from construction, demolition, and forestry operations has led to increasing restrictions on the use and disposal of treated wood. Conventional methods for managing metal-contaminated wood often involve costly containment, specialized hazardous waste facilities, or incomplete removal techniques that fail to recover the embedded metals for reuse. The economic burden of disposal, coupled with the loss of valuable lignocellulosic feedstock, necessitates the development of integrated processes that simultaneously deconstruct the biomass and recover the embedded metal pollutants in a manner that is both environmentally benign and economically viable.

The application of metal-based preservatives to wood results in the formation of stable, insoluble complexes between the metal cations and hydroxyl, carboxyl, and phenolic functional groups within the lignin and hemicellulose components of the cell wall. These interactions are reinforced by the dense, hierarchical structure of the lignocellulosic matrix, which impedes the accessibility of conventional solvents and chemical reagents to the bound metal ions. As a result, traditional leaching methods using water, dilute acids, or chelating agents often achieve incomplete extraction, require excessive reagent volumes, generate large volumes of contaminated effluent, and fail to preserve the structural integrity of the cellulose fraction for downstream valorization. The limitations of these approaches underscore the need for a solvent system capable of penetrating the recalcitrant architecture of treated wood, solubilizing the lignin and hemicellulose components to expose the metal-binding sites, and selectively complexing or mobilizing the metal pollutants without inducing excessive degradation of the cellulose. The introduction of a controlled landfill method for metal-enriched ash, while mitigating immediate environmental exposure, does not address the root issue of resource loss and fails to recover the economic value inherent in both the biomass and the metal content. Thermal treatment of metal-enriched ash, though effective in volume reduction, concentrates the pollutants into a hazardous residue that still requires secure disposal, and does not enable the recovery of the metals for industrial reuse.

The present invention introduces a novel, integrated process for the deconstruction of metal-polluted lignocellulosic biomass through the use of protic ionic liquids, followed by the selective extraction, recovery, and recycling of both the biomass components and the embedded metal pollutants. This method enables the simultaneous solubilization of lignin and hemicellulose, the release of metal ions from their binding sites within the biomass matrix, and the subsequent recovery of these metals via electrochemical or chemical precipitation techniques, while preserving the cellulose fraction in a highly digestible form suitable for biofuel production. The process begins with the contact of metal-enriched wood chips or sawdust with a protic ionic liquid composed of an ammonium-based cation and a hydrogen sulfate anion, under controlled temperature and agitation conditions. The ionic liquid penetrates the cell wall structure, disrupts the hydrogen-bonding network of lignin and hemicellulose, and facilitates the dissociation of metal ions from their coordination sites. The resulting mixture is then subjected to solid-liquid separation, yielding a cellulose-rich solid residue and a metal-containing ionic liquid solution. The cellulose residue is washed to remove residual ionic liquid and lignin fragments, then subjected to enzymatic saccharification to produce fermentable sugars. The metal-laden ionic liquid solution is processed to recover the metal pollutants through electrodeposition, acid leaching, or chemical precipitation, enabling the regeneration of the ionic liquid for reuse and the recovery of high-purity metal salts or elemental metals for industrial applications.

The method for determining the presence of ODAM or anti-ODAM antibodies is not applicable to the present invention, as the invention relates exclusively to the deconstruction of metal-contaminated lignocellulosic biomass and the recovery of embedded metal pollutants. The invention does not involve biological proteins, antibodies, or diagnostic assays. The term “extraction” as used herein refers to the physical and chemical mobilization of metal ions from the lignocellulosic matrix into the ionic liquid phase, while “cellulosic” or “lignocellulosic biomass” refers to plant-derived materials composed primarily of cellulose, hemicellulose, and lignin, including but not limited to wood, agricultural residues, and energy crops such as Miscanthus, switchgrass, and sugarcane bagasse. The term “metal pollutant” encompasses copper, chromium, arsenic, zinc, and other heavy metals introduced into wood through commercial preservative treatments, and their presence inhibits downstream biological and chemical processes by poisoning enzymes, deactivating catalysts, and contaminating biofuel streams. Preferred metal pollutants include copper(II) and chromium(VI), which are most commonly associated with CCA and ACQ treatments and exhibit the highest environmental and economic impact due to their toxicity and persistence.

The ionic liquid composition comprises a protic cation derived from the protonation of an amine, preferably an alkylammonium ion such as triethylammonium, diethylammonium, or monoethylammonium, paired with the hydrogen sulfate anion. The cation may be substituted with aliphatic, alkyl, alkenyl, or carbocyclic groups containing two to six carbon atoms, and may include C2-6 alkoxy or C2-6 alkoxyalkyl substituents to modulate solvent polarity and viscosity. The preferred cation is triethylammonium, which, when combined with hydrogen sulfate, forms a low-cost, thermally stable, and highly effective solvent for lignin solubilization and metal extraction. The ionic liquid may contain excess acid or excess base to modulate its acidity, which influences the rate of hemicellulose hydrolysis, lignin depolymerization, and metal ion release. Water may be present in the ionic liquid composition in concentrations ranging from 10 to 40 weight percent to facilitate hydrolytic cleavage of ether and ester linkages in lignin and hemicellulose, while maintaining sufficient solvent power to dissolve the released components. The ionic liquid is prepared by the stoichiometric or non-stoichiometric mixing of a tertiary amine with sulfuric acid under controlled temperature and stirring, followed by removal of volatile byproducts under vacuum. The resulting mixture is a viscous, hygroscopic liquid with low melting point, high thermal stability, and negligible vapor pressure, making it suitable for closed-loop recycling.

The process of contacting biomass with the ionic liquid involves mixing the metal-contaminated wood with the ionic liquid at a solid-to-liquid ratio of 1:5 to 1:20, followed by heating to a temperature between 100 and 180°C for a duration of 1 to 24 hours under mechanical agitation. During this step, the ionic liquid solubilizes lignin and hemicellulose, releases metal ions into solution, and preserves the cellulose as a filterable solid residue. The lubrication properties of the ionic liquid reduce friction and abrasion during mixing, minimizing mechanical degradation of the biomass particles. Following the reaction, the mixture is filtered or centrifuged to separate the cellulose-rich pulp from the metal-laden ionic liquid solution. The pulp is washed with water and ethanol to remove residual ionic liquid and lignin fragments, then dried for subsequent saccharification. The ionic liquid solution is concentrated by evaporation under reduced pressure, and the metal pollutants are recovered by electrodeposition onto a cathode, precipitation as hydroxides or sulfides, or acid leaching followed by solvent extraction. The regenerated ionic liquid is reconstituted with water and reused in subsequent cycles, achieving recovery rates exceeding 98%. The recovered metals are purified and sold as commercial-grade salts or elemental forms, while the cellulose is converted into glucose via enzymatic hydrolysis, enabling the production of bioethanol or other bioproducts. The entire process is designed to minimize waste, maximize resource recovery, and eliminate the need for hazardous disposal of treated wood.

The invention further encompasses the use of the recovered cellulose as a feedstock for biorefinery applications, where the absence of inhibitory metal contaminants enables high-efficiency enzymatic hydrolysis and fermentation. The lignin recovered from the ionic liquid solution is precipitated by dilution with water, filtered, dried, and characterized for potential use as a renewable aromatic feedstock for polymers, adhesives, or carbon materials. The process eliminates the need for external chemical additives, avoids the generation of toxic effluents, and enables the circular economy of both biomass and metal resources. The techno-economic model demonstrates that the operating cost of metal extraction and ionic liquid recycling is significantly lower than the cost of virgin wood feedstock for biofuel production, particularly when compared to conventional pretreatment methods such as dilute acid or steam explosion, which do not recover metals and require extensive wastewater treatment. The integration of electrochemical metal recovery further enhances process efficiency by enabling direct conversion of dissolved metal ions into pure metallic deposits, reducing downstream purification steps and energy consumption. The invention thus provides a comprehensive, scalable, and economically viable solution to the global challenge of managing metal-contaminated wood waste.
