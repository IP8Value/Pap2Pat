## BACKGROUND

- motivate synthetic lethality analysis

Synthetic lethality represents a fundamental biological phenomenon in which the concurrent disruption of two non-essential genes results in cellular death, whereas the loss of either gene alone permits viable cell survival. This concept, first observed in model organisms such as Saccharomyces cerevisiae, has emerged as a powerful framework for understanding genetic interactions and their implications in human disease, particularly in oncology. The therapeutic potential of synthetic lethality lies in its capacity to selectively target cancer cells harboring specific genetic deficiencies while sparing normal tissues, thereby enabling the design of combination therapies with high specificity and reduced systemic toxicity. For instance, the clinical success of PARP inhibitors in BRCA1- or BRCA2-mutated ovarian and breast cancers exemplifies how synthetic lethal interactions can be exploited to achieve precision medicine outcomes. However, despite its promise, the systematic identification of synthetic lethal pairs in humans remains a formidable challenge due to the prohibitive scale of experimental screening. A comprehensive interrogation of all possible gene pairs in a single human cell type would require over 200 million pairwise genetic perturbation assays, a scale that is neither ethically nor logistically feasible. Moreover, the context-dependent nature of synthetic lethality—where a gene pair may be lethal in one tissue or disease state but not another—further complicates the identification of robust, broadly applicable interactions. Previous computational approaches have attempted to circumvent this experimental burden by leveraging evolutionary conservation, primarily through the transfer of known synthetic lethal relationships from yeast to human via orthologous gene mapping. Yet these methods are inherently limited by the sparse overlap of genetically conserved genes between distantly related species; fewer than 2,000 human genes have clear orthologs in Saccharomyces cerevisiae, representing less than one percent of all possible human gene pairs. Furthermore, evolutionary divergence has introduced substantial functional redundancy, gene duplication, and network rewiring that render direct homology-based predictions unreliable. The failure of such approaches to account for the topological and systemic properties of biological networks has left a critical gap in our ability to predict synthetic lethality across species with high accuracy and generalizability. Consequently, there exists an urgent need for a novel, systems-level methodology that transcends sequence or functional homology and instead captures the conserved architectural principles of genetic interaction networks across species, enabling the reliable prediction of synthetic lethal pairs in human cells without reliance on prior experimental validation in humans.

## SUMMARY

- introduce synthetic lethality identification method

A novel method for identifying synthetic lethal gene pairs across species has been developed, leveraging the topological architecture of protein-protein interaction networks rather than genetic, structural, or functional homology. This method enables the prediction of synthetic lethality in any species for which a protein-protein interaction network is available, even in the absence of prior experimental evidence of synthetic lethal interactions within that species. The approach is grounded in the discovery that synthetic lethal gene pairs exhibit conserved patterns of connectivity within biological networks, independent of sequence similarity or shared biochemical function. By training a machine learning model on well-characterized synthetic lethal interactions in a source species—specifically Saccharomyces cerevisiae—the method learns to recognize the multivariate network signatures that distinguish synthetic lethal pairs from non-synthetic lethal pairs. Once trained, this model can be applied to any target species by translating its network parameters into a standardized, species-independent representation, thereby enabling cross-species prediction without direct ortholog mapping. The method does not require prior knowledge of gene function, protein structure, or evolutionary conservation, and it is capable of generating probabilistic scores for every possible gene pair in the target organism, indicating the likelihood of a synthetic lethal relationship. This innovation fundamentally shifts the paradigm of synthetic lethality prediction from homology-dependent extrapolation to topology-driven inference, opening new avenues for discovering therapeutic targets in genetically complex diseases such as cancer.

- describe biological network model framework

The biological network model framework underpinning this invention is constructed from protein-protein interaction data represented as undirected graphs, wherein each node corresponds to a gene product and each edge represents a documented physical interaction between two proteins. The framework incorporates a comprehensive set of graph-theoretic parameters that quantify the position and relational context of each gene within the network, including degree centrality, betweenness centrality, closeness centrality, clustering coefficient, eigenvector centrality, shortest path length, communicability, shared neighbors, shared non-neighbors, and shared second-degree neighbors. These parameters are computed for each individual gene as well as for every unordered pair of genes, resulting in a 20-dimensional vector that fully describes the connectivity profile of each gene pair. The model operates under the hypothesis that synthetic lethal gene pairs share distinctive topological signatures—such as high mutual connectivity, shared neighborhood structure, or complementary centrality patterns—that are evolutionarily conserved across species despite differences in gene sequence or function. These signatures are learned through supervised machine learning using experimentally validated synthetic lethal and non-synthetic lethal pairs from a source organism, and the resulting model encodes the statistical relationship between network topology and synthetic lethality. The framework is designed to be agnostic to species-specific biological features, relying solely on network structure to infer functional interaction outcomes, thereby enabling its application to any organism with a sufficiently complete protein-protein interaction network.

- outline method for predicting synthetic lethality

The method for predicting synthetic lethality comprises a three-phase workflow: network construction, parameter translation, and model application. First, a protein-protein interaction network is constructed for the target species using publicly available interaction data, which is then pruned to retain only the largest connected component to ensure topological coherence. Second, the network parameters for each gene and gene pair are calculated and subsequently rank-normalized to eliminate species-specific scaling biases, transforming raw values into a uniform scale between zero and one. This normalization process ensures that connectivity profiles from disparate organisms can be meaningfully compared, effectively translating the network topology into a common mathematical language. Third, the trained machine learning model, previously optimized on synthetic lethal data from Saccharomyces cerevisiae, is applied to the normalized connectivity vectors of all gene pairs in the target species. The model outputs a continuous SINaTRA score between zero and one for each pair, where higher scores indicate a greater probability of synthetic lethality. This score is not derived from gene identity or homology but from the statistical likelihood that the pair’s network topology matches the learned signature of synthetic lethality. The method thus enables the prediction of synthetic lethal interactions in any species without requiring prior knowledge of such interactions within that species, making it uniquely suited for applications in human biology where experimental validation is sparse.

- detail normalizing network parameters

The normalization of network parameters is a critical step that enables the cross-species generalization of the synthetic lethality prediction model. Raw network parameters, such as degree centrality or betweenness centrality, exhibit significant variation in distribution and magnitude across species due to differences in genome size, network density, and experimental data completeness. To overcome this limitation, a rank-based normalization procedure is applied to each parameter individually. For each parameter, all values across the entire network are ranked from lowest to highest, with tied values assigned the median rank. These ranks are then divided by the total number of genes (for single-node parameters) or the total number of gene pairs (for node-pair parameters), resulting in a continuous value between zero and one for each gene or gene pair. This rank-normalization technique preserves the relative ordering of connectivity profiles while eliminating absolute scale differences, ensuring that a high-degree node in a small network is comparable to a high-degree node in a large network. Importantly, this transformation is non-parametric and does not assume any underlying distribution, making it robust to incomplete or noisy data. The resulting normalized parameters are termed “translated” and serve as the standardized input features for the machine learning model, allowing the model trained on yeast to be directly applied to human, mouse, or any other species with a protein-protein interaction network.

- describe training synthetic lethality model

The synthetic lethality model is trained using a supervised machine learning approach based on random forest classification, which is selected for its ability to handle high-dimensional feature spaces, mitigate overfitting, and provide robust performance on imbalanced datasets. Training data is derived from experimentally validated synthetic lethal and non-synthetic lethal gene pairs obtained from the BioGRID database for Saccharomyces cerevisiae. Positive training examples consist of 13,196 gene pairs confirmed to exhibit synthetic lethality through high-throughput genetic screens, while negative examples are randomly sampled from the pool of all possible gene pairs not annotated as synthetic lethal, ensuring a balanced dataset for training. Each gene pair is represented by its 20-dimensional translated connectivity profile, generated from rank-normalized network parameters. The random forest model is trained using 100 decision trees, with five-fold cross-validation employed to optimize hyperparameters and evaluate performance. The model learns to distinguish synthetic lethal from non-synthetic lethal pairs by identifying the most informative combinations of network parameters that collectively define the topological signature of synthetic lethality. Feature importance analysis reveals that node-pair parameters—including shared neighbors, communicability, and inverse shortest path—are the most predictive, underscoring the centrality of local network structure in determining synthetic lethal outcomes. The resulting model achieves an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) of 0.92 in cross-validation, demonstrating high accuracy and reliability in predicting synthetic lethality within the source species.

- outline method for selecting cancer drug treatment

The method for selecting cancer drug treatment involves the application of the trained synthetic lethality model to human gene pairs to identify high-confidence synthetic lethal interactions that can be therapeutically exploited. First, the SINaTRA score is computed for all 109 million possible human gene pairs using the human protein-protein interaction network and the model trained on Saccharomyces cerevisiae. Gene pairs with SINaTRA scores exceeding a stringent threshold of 0.85 are prioritized as putative synthetic lethal candidates. These candidates are then filtered using population genomic data from the 1000 Genomes Project and the Sweden-Schizophrenia Exome Sequencing Study to exclude gene pairs in which both genes are simultaneously homozygous for deleterious mutations in healthy individuals, as such co-occurrence indicates functional redundancy and thus a low likelihood of synthetic lethality. The remaining high-scoring gene pairs are annotated with known drug targets from the Drug Combination Database, and those pairs in which both genes are targeted by existing or investigational cancer therapeutics are identified as high-priority candidates for combination therapy development. The SINaTRA scores of these drug-targeted pairs are significantly elevated compared to non-targeted pairs, suggesting that many current combination therapies already exploit synthetic lethal mechanisms. The method further enables the discovery of novel drug combinations by identifying “hotspots” of high SINaTRA scores that correspond to clusters of genes not yet targeted by approved therapies but predicted to exhibit synthetic lethality, thereby guiding the rational design of next-generation polypharmacological regimens.

- describe filtering synthetic lethality pairs

Filtering of synthetic lethality predictions is performed to reduce false positives and enhance biological relevance by integrating orthogonal genomic and functional constraints. First, gene pairs exhibiting concurrent homozygous deleterious mutations in human populations are excluded, as such co-occurrence implies that the simultaneous loss of both genes is tolerated in vivo and therefore unlikely to be synthetically lethal. From two large-scale human exome sequencing datasets, 450,010 gene pairs were identified in which both genes harbored homozygous loss-of-function mutations in at least one individual, and these pairs were removed from the candidate list. Second, gene pairs are filtered based on tissue-specific gene expression profiles derived from the Human Protein Atlas, such that only pairs expressed in the same tissue or cell type are retained, as synthetic lethality requires co-expression for therapeutic relevance. Third, gene pairs are evaluated for enrichment within known protein complexes and biological pathways, with those showing significant co-localization in the same functional module prioritized due to their higher likelihood of mechanistic coherence. Finally, predictions are cross-referenced against existing databases of experimentally validated human synthetic lethal pairs and computational predictions from other methods such as DAISY and Syn-Lethality to validate consistency and exclude outliers. This multi-layered filtering strategy reduces the false discovery rate to less than 0.4% at a SINaTRA threshold of 0.85, yielding a high-confidence set of 10,936 putative human synthetic lethal pairs suitable for therapeutic development.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

- introduce method for identifying SL

The method for identifying synthetic lethal gene pairs is based on the discovery that synthetic lethality is encoded not in the sequence or function of individual genes, but in the topological architecture of their interactions within biological networks. This invention introduces a species-independent computational framework that predicts synthetic lethality by analyzing the connectivity patterns of gene pairs across protein-protein interaction networks, without reliance on evolutionary homology, structural similarity, or functional annotation. The method operates by first constructing a network representation of protein interactions for a target species, then computing a comprehensive set of graph-theoretic parameters that describe the positional and relational context of each gene and gene pair within the network. These parameters are normalized to eliminate species-specific scaling biases, enabling direct comparison of connectivity profiles across organisms. A machine learning model, trained on experimentally validated synthetic lethal interactions in Saccharomyces cerevisiae, is then applied to these normalized profiles to generate a continuous probability score for each gene pair in the target species. This score, termed the SINaTRA score, reflects the likelihood that the pair exhibits synthetic lethality based solely on the similarity of its network topology to known synthetic lethal pairs in the source organism. The method is entirely data-driven, requiring only a protein-protein interaction network and no prior knowledge of synthetic lethality in the target species, thereby enabling the discovery of novel synthetic lethal interactions in human cells and other poorly characterized organisms.

- use biological network connectivity profiles

Biological network connectivity profiles serve as the foundational representation for identifying synthetic lethal gene pairs in this invention. Each gene is characterized by a vector of eight single-node parameters that quantify its topological role within the protein-protein interaction network, including degree centrality, betweenness centrality, closeness centrality, clustering coefficient, eigenvector centrality, information centrality, page rank, and eccentricity. Each gene pair is further described by four node-pair parameters—shared neighbors, shared non-neighbors, communicability, and inverse shortest path—as well as the concatenated single-node profiles of both genes, resulting in a 20-dimensional connectivity profile for each pair. These parameters collectively capture the local and global structural context of the interaction, such as the number of common interaction partners, the efficiency of information flow between the two genes, and their relative positions within the network hierarchy. The connectivity profile is not derived from gene sequence, protein structure, or functional ontology, but from the empirically observed patterns of physical interactions, making it a purely topological descriptor. Synthetic lethal pairs are found to consistently exhibit distinctive connectivity profiles, such as high shared neighbor counts, elevated communicability, and complementary centrality distributions, which are learned by the machine learning model during training. This approach enables the identification of synthetic lethality based on network architecture alone, revealing that the functional consequence of gene pair disruption is predictable from the pattern of their interactions rather than their molecular identity.

- translate parameters for comparison

The translation of network parameters across species is achieved through a rank-based normalization procedure that renders connectivity profiles comparable regardless of differences in network size, density, or experimental coverage. For each of the 20 network parameters, all values across the entire network are ranked in ascending order, with tied values assigned the median rank. Each rank is then divided by the total number of genes (for single-node parameters) or the total number of gene pairs (for node-pair parameters), resulting in a dimensionless value between zero and one. This transformation ensures that a gene with the highest degree in a small network receives the same normalized value as a gene with the highest degree in a large network, thereby eliminating scale-dependent biases. The translated parameters are no longer tied to the absolute values of connectivity but instead reflect the relative position of a gene or gene pair within its network’s connectivity distribution. This normalization is essential for applying a model trained on Saccharomyces cerevisiae to human, mouse, or any other species, as it allows the model to recognize topological patterns irrespective of the underlying biological context. The translated parameters form the input feature space for the synthetic lethality prediction model, enabling cross-species inference without requiring ortholog mapping or sequence alignment.

- construct model on source species

The synthetic lethality model is constructed using a supervised machine learning approach trained on experimentally validated data from Saccharomyces cerevisiae, a species with the most comprehensive and well-curated synthetic lethal interaction database. A set of 13,196 gene pairs with confirmed synthetic lethal phenotypes is obtained from the BioGRID database, and an equal number of non-synthetic lethal pairs are randomly sampled from all possible gene pairs not annotated as synthetic lethal. Each pair is represented by its 20-dimensional translated connectivity profile, generated from the rank-normalized network parameters of the yeast protein-protein interaction network. A random forest classifier with 100 decision trees is trained on this dataset, with five-fold cross-validation used to optimize model parameters and assess performance. The model learns to distinguish synthetic lethal from non-synthetic lethal pairs by identifying the most discriminative combinations of network parameters, with shared neighbors, communicability, and inverse shortest path emerging as the most influential features. The resulting model achieves an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.92 in cross-validation, demonstrating high accuracy and robustness. The model is validated by its ability to correctly classify known synthetic lethal pairs not included in the training set and by its superior performance compared to homology-, structure-, and function-based prediction methods. This trained model serves as the core predictive engine for all subsequent cross-species applications.

- apply model to target species

The model trained on Saccharomyces cerevisiae is applied to any target species by first constructing a protein-protein interaction network for that species using available high-throughput interaction data, such as from BioGRID or STRING. The network is pruned to retain only the largest connected component to ensure topological integrity. For each gene and gene pair in the target network, the same 20 network parameters are computed and then rank-normalized using the identical procedure applied to the source species. The resulting translated connectivity profiles are then input into the pre-trained random forest model, which outputs a SINaTRA score between zero and one for each gene pair. This score quantifies the probability that the pair exhibits synthetic lethality based on the similarity of its network topology to the learned signature of synthetic lethality in yeast. The model does not require any knowledge of gene orthology, protein structure, or functional annotation in the target species, making it applicable to organisms with minimal genetic characterization. The output is a comprehensive, genome-wide map of synthetic lethal potential, enabling the prioritization of gene pairs for experimental validation or therapeutic targeting without prior biological assumptions.

- predict SL pairs in target species

Prediction of synthetic lethal pairs in the target species is accomplished by thresholding the SINaTRA scores generated by the model. Gene pairs with scores above a predefined cutoff, such as 0.85, are classified as high-confidence synthetic lethal candidates. These predictions are not based on gene identity or evolutionary conservation but on the statistical likelihood that the pair’s network topology matches the signature learned from yeast. The method generates predictions for all possible gene pairs in the target species, producing a ranked list of candidates that can be further refined using additional biological filters such as tissue expression, co-mutation frequency, and pathway enrichment. The predictions are validated by their enrichment in known synthetic lethal interactions from independent databases and by their biological coherence, such as co-localization within protein complexes or shared functional pathways. The method’s ability to predict synthetic lethal pairs in species with no prior experimental data—such as Mus musculus or Homo sapiens—demonstrates its generalizability and utility in translational research.

- generate biological networks

Biological networks are generated from high-throughput protein-protein interaction data obtained from public repositories such as BioGRID, STRING, or IntAct. The data are filtered to include only physical, direct interactions with high confidence scores, excluding predicted or indirect associations. Each gene is mapped to its corresponding protein product, and the network is represented as an undirected graph with nodes as proteins and edges as interactions. To ensure topological consistency and eliminate noise from disconnected components, the network is pruned to retain only the largest connected component, which contains the majority of functionally relevant interactions. This process results in a clean, coherent network structure suitable for parameter computation and machine learning analysis. The network is then used as the foundation for calculating all 20 network parameters for every gene and gene pair, forming the basis for synthetic lethality prediction.

- describe network parameters

The network parameters used in this invention encompass a suite of graph-theoretic metrics that quantify the structural and relational properties of genes within a protein-protein interaction network. Single-node parameters include degree centrality, betweenness centrality, closeness centrality, clustering coefficient, eigenvector centrality, information centrality, page rank, and eccentricity, each capturing a distinct aspect of a gene’s topological role. Node-pair parameters include shared neighbors, shared non-neighbors, communicability, and inverse shortest path, which describe the local interaction context between two genes. Shared neighbors reflect the number of common interaction partners, indicating functional proximity. Shared non-neighbors quantify the number of genes connected to one gene but not the other, revealing structural asymmetry. Communicability measures the efficiency of information flow between two nodes through all possible paths, while inverse shortest path reflects the directness of their connection. These parameters are selected for their proven utility in network biology and their ability to capture both local and global network properties that correlate with functional interaction outcomes. Together, they form a comprehensive, multidimensional descriptor of gene pair topology that enables the model to distinguish synthetic lethal from non-synthetic lethal interactions with high fidelity.

- normalize network parameters

Network parameters are normalized using a rank-based transformation to ensure cross-species comparability. For each parameter, all values across the network are sorted in ascending order, and each value is assigned a rank. Tied values receive the median rank of their group. The rank of each value is then divided by the total number of genes (for single-node parameters) or the total number of gene pairs (for node-pair parameters), yielding a normalized value between zero and one. This process eliminates differences in scale and distribution between species, such as those arising from variations in network size or data completeness. The resulting normalized parameters are termed “translated” and form the standardized input for the synthetic lethality model. This normalization is non-parametric, robust to outliers, and preserves the relative ordering of connectivity profiles, enabling the model trained on yeast to be applied to human, mouse, or any other species without modification.

- train species-independent model

The species-independent model is trained using a random forest classifier on the translated connectivity profiles of synthetic lethal and non-synthetic lethal gene pairs from Saccharomyces cerevisiae. The model learns to associate specific combinations of network parameters with synthetic lethality, regardless of the biological context of the genes involved. The training process is performed using five-fold cross-validation to ensure generalizability, and feature importance analysis identifies the most predictive parameters. The model is optimized to maximize the area under the ROC curve and is validated on held-out test sets and independent datasets. Once trained, the model is frozen and applied to any target species using only its translated network parameters, without retraining or parameter adjustment. This design ensures that the model is truly species-independent, relying solely on topological similarity rather than biological homology to predict synthetic lethality.

- apply model to normalized target networks

The trained model is applied to the normalized connectivity profiles of gene pairs in the target species by inputting each 20-dimensional vector into the random forest classifier. The model outputs a SINaTRA score between zero and one for each pair, representing the probability that the pair exhibits synthetic lethality based on its network topology. This score is computed independently for each pair and is not influenced by gene identity, sequence, or function. The output is a genome-wide map of synthetic lethal potential, with higher scores indicating greater likelihood of synthetic lethality. The model’s application to human, mouse, and other species demonstrates its ability to generalize across evolutionary distance, providing a universal framework for synthetic lethality prediction.

- predict SL pairs

Synthetic lethal pairs are predicted by selecting gene pairs with SINaTRA scores above a predefined threshold, such as 0.85, which corresponds to a high positive predictive value and low false discovery rate. These predictions are not constrained by prior knowledge of gene function or orthology and are derived solely from network topology. The predicted pairs are enriched for known synthetic lethal interactions, co-localization in protein complexes, and shared biological pathways, validating their biological relevance. The method enables the discovery of novel synthetic lethal pairs in species with no prior experimental data, providing a powerful tool for therapeutic target identification.

- choose source species based on known SL information

The source species for model training is selected based on the availability of comprehensive, high-confidence experimental data on synthetic lethal interactions. Saccharomyces cerevisiae is chosen as the optimal source species due to its extensive, manually curated synthetic lethal dataset, which includes over 13,000 validated pairs from genome-wide screens. This dataset provides the statistical power and biological fidelity necessary to train a robust model. Other species, such as Schizosaccharomyces pombe or Caenorhabditis elegans, are not used as primary sources due to their smaller, less complete datasets. The choice of yeast ensures that the model is trained on the most reliable and diverse set of synthetic lethal interactions available, maximizing its predictive accuracy when applied to other species.

- define connectivity homologous relationship

A connectivity homologous relationship is defined as the similarity in network connectivity profiles between two genes or gene pairs across different species, irrespective of genetic, structural, or functional homology. Two genes are considered connectivity homologous if their respective connectivity vectors—comprising the 20 network parameters—are statistically similar in their topological positioning and interaction patterns within their respective networks. This concept extends to gene pairs: two gene pairs are connectivity homologous if their combined connectivity profiles exhibit comparable structural signatures, even if the individual genes are not orthologous. This definition underpins the method’s ability to predict synthetic lethality across species, as it posits that the topological signature of synthetic lethality is conserved even when gene sequences and functions diverge.

- represent connectivity profiles as vectors

Each gene is represented as a vector of eight single-node network parameters, and each gene pair is represented as a vector of 20 parameters, comprising the eight single-node parameters for each gene plus four node-pair parameters. These vectors are computed from the rank-normalized network data and serve as the fundamental input to the machine learning model. The vector representation captures the multidimensional topological context of each gene and gene pair, enabling the model to detect complex, non-linear relationships between network structure and synthetic lethality. This representation is agnostic to biological identity and is purely structural, allowing for cross-species comparison and prediction.

- determine network parameters

Network parameters are determined using established graph-theoretic algorithms applied to the protein-protein interaction network. For each gene, the eight single-node parameters are computed using standard network analysis tools such as NetworkX. For each gene pair, the four node-pair parameters are calculated based on adjacency matrices and path enumeration. All computations are performed on the pruned, largest connected component of the network to ensure biological relevance. The parameters are computed in a reproducible, automated manner, ensuring consistency across species and analyses.

- prune networks to contain one connected component

To ensure topological coherence and eliminate noise from disconnected or spurious interactions, the protein-protein interaction network is pruned to retain only the largest connected component. This step removes genes that are isolated or belong to small, potentially artifactual subnetworks, ensuring that all remaining genes are functionally interconnected. The resulting network is dense enough to support meaningful parameter computation while minimizing false positives from incomplete data. This pruning step is critical for the accuracy and reliability of the synthetic lethality predictions.

- normalize network parameters

Network parameters are normalized using a rank-based transformation to eliminate species-specific scaling biases. Each parameter value is ranked across the entire network, tied values are assigned the median rank, and each rank is divided by the total number of genes or gene pairs, yielding a normalized value between zero and one. This process ensures that connectivity profiles from different species can be directly compared, enabling the application of a model trained on yeast to human or mouse networks without retraining.

- perform entropy analysis

Entropy analysis is performed to assess the information content and distributional properties of the network parameters across species. The Shannon entropy of each parameter is computed to quantify its variability and predictive power. Parameters with low entropy are found to be less informative, while those with moderate to high entropy—such as shared neighbors and communicability—are identified as the most discriminative features for synthetic lethality prediction. This analysis informs the selection of optimal parameters for model training and confirms that the chosen features are biologically informative rather than arbitrary.

- train models of synthetic lethality

Models of synthetic lethality are trained using a random forest classifier on the translated connectivity profiles of synthetic lethal and non-synthetic lethal gene pairs from Saccharomyces cerevisiae. The model is trained using five-fold cross-validation to ensure robustness, and hyperparameters are optimized to maximize the area under the ROC curve. The resulting model is validated on independent datasets and shown to outperform homology-, structure-, and function-based methods. The model is then frozen and applied to target species without modification, enabling species-independent prediction.

- select SL and NSL pairs

Synthetic lethal pairs are selected from experimentally validated datasets in Saccharomyces cerevisiae, consisting of 13,196 pairs with confirmed synthetic lethal phenotypes. Non-synthetic lethal pairs are selected by randomly sampling an equal number of gene pairs from the full set of possible pairs that are not annotated as synthetic lethal. This balanced dataset ensures that the model is not biased toward the majority class and can accurately distinguish synthetic lethal from non-synthetic lethal interactions. The selection process is performed independently of gene function or orthology, ensuring that the model learns purely from network topology.

- evaluate model performance

Model performance is evaluated using standard metrics including area under the ROC curve (AUC), precision-recall curve, positive predictive value, and false discovery rate. Cross-validation is employed to assess generalizability, and performance is compared against alternative methods such as genetic homology, functional similarity, and information centrality. The model achieves an AUC of 0.92 in yeast and maintains high performance when applied to other species, demonstrating superior accuracy and robustness. The method’s ability to predict known synthetic lethal interactions in human and mouse validates its translational utility.
