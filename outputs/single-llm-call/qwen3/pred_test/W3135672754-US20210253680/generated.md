## BACKGROUND

### 1. Field of the Invention

- The present invention relates generally to the field of therapeutic interventions for neurological injuries, neurodegenerative diseases, inflammatory conditions, and cancer-related pathologies, and more specifically to methods and compositions for modulating connexin 43 hemichannel activity using targeted monoclonal antibodies. The invention encompasses the use of antibodies that selectively inhibit the opening of connexin 43 hemichannels without affecting gap junction function, thereby reducing secondary damage, inflammation, and tissue degeneration in a variety of pathological contexts. The invention is particularly directed to the treatment of spinal cord injury, osteoporosis, neurodegenerative disorders, cancer bone metastasis, osteoarthritis, and other conditions in which aberrant connexin 43 hemichannel activity contributes to disease progression.

### 2. Description of Related Art

- Traumatic spinal injury is a debilitating condition characterized by immediate mechanical disruption of spinal cord tissue followed by a cascade of secondary pathological events, including edema, ischemia, excitotoxicity, and sustained inflammatory responses. These processes lead to widespread neuronal death, demyelination, and the formation of a glial scar that impedes functional recovery. Despite decades of research, no clinically effective therapy exists to halt or reverse the secondary injury phase, leaving patients with permanent neurological deficits. Current standard-of-care interventions, such as corticosteroid administration and surgical decompression, provide only marginal benefit and are associated with significant side effects. More recently, pharmacological agents targeting glutamate receptors or inflammatory cytokines have been explored, but their efficacy remains limited due to poor specificity, short half-lives, or systemic toxicity. Connexin 43, a major component of astrocytic gap junctions and hemichannels, has been implicated in the propagation of secondary injury through the uncontrolled release of ATP, glutamate, and other neurotoxic molecules. However, prior approaches to inhibit connexin 43 have lacked selectivity, often blocking both gap junction communication and hemichannel activity, thereby disrupting essential intercellular signaling required for tissue homeostasis. This lack of specificity has hindered the development of safe and effective therapeutics. There remains a critical and unmet need for a targeted therapeutic modality that selectively inhibits connexin 43 hemichannel opening while preserving gap junction function, thereby mitigating secondary damage without compromising physiological communication between cells.

## DESCRIPTION

- Connexin proteins are a family of transmembrane proteins that assemble into hexameric structures known as connexons, which can form gap junctions between adjacent cells or remain as hemichannels on the cell surface. Connexin 43, the most widely expressed connexin isoform in the central nervous system and bone tissue, plays a dual role in intercellular communication and extracellular signaling. Under physiological conditions, connexin 43 gap junctions facilitate the exchange of ions, metabolites, and second messengers between coupled cells, contributing to tissue homeostasis. However, under pathological conditions such as trauma, inflammation, or malignancy, connexin 43 hemichannels become pathologically activated, allowing the uncontrolled efflux of ATP, glutamate, NAD+, and other signaling molecules that promote inflammation, excitotoxicity, and cell death. In the spinal cord, connexin 43 is predominantly expressed in astrocytes, where its hemichannel activity is upregulated following traumatic injury, contributing to secondary damage. In bone, connexin 43 is highly expressed in osteocytes, where hemichannel opening stimulates the release of RANKL and prostaglandins, promoting osteoclast activation and bone resorption. In cancer, connexin 43 is overexpressed in tumor cells that metastasize to bone, where it facilitates communication with the bone microenvironment and enhances tumor survival and proliferation. The bone remodeling process is a tightly regulated balance between osteoblast-mediated bone formation and osteoclast-mediated bone resorption, which is disrupted in conditions such as osteoporosis, metastatic bone disease, and osteoarthritis. Bisphosphonates, such as alendronate, are widely used to inhibit osteoclast activity and reduce bone loss, but they are associated with significant side effects, including osteonecrosis of the jaw, atypical femoral fractures, and gastrointestinal toxicity. Moreover, bisphosphonates do not address the underlying inflammatory and signaling dysregulation driven by connexin 43 hemichannels. There is a clear need for a targeted therapeutic strategy that directly inhibits the pathological activity of connexin 43 hemichannels without disrupting essential gap junction function or inducing systemic toxicity.

### I. ANTIBODIES

- The complementarity-determining regions, or CDRs, are the hypervariable segments within the variable domains of immunoglobulins that directly contact and bind to an antigen. The CDRs are classified into three regions per chain: CDR1, CDR2, and CDR3, with CDR3 typically exhibiting the greatest diversity and contribution to antigen specificity. The variable light chain and variable heavy chain each contain three CDRs, and together they form the antigen-binding site. Variant CDRs may be generated by site-directed mutagenesis to enhance binding affinity, reduce immunogenicity, or improve stability. Amino acid substitutions may be introduced at positions within the CDRs or framework regions to modulate antibody properties without compromising antigen recognition. Insertions and deletions may be introduced in the CDR loops to fine-tune binding geometry or to confer resistance to proteolytic degradation. The Fab region refers to the antigen-binding fragment of an antibody, comprising the light chain and the first constant domain of the heavy chain (CH1), linked together by a disulfide bond. The Fv fragment is the minimal unit of antigen binding, consisting of the variable domains of the heavy and light chains (VH and VL). The framework refers to the conserved regions of the variable domains that support the CDR loops and maintain the structural integrity of the antigen-binding site. The antigen-binding portion of an antibody encompasses any structural unit capable of binding the target antigen, including full-length antibodies, Fab fragments, F(ab′)2 fragments, Fd fragments, Fv fragments, dAb fragments, single-chain variable fragments (scFv), diabodies, minibodies, and engineered binding peptides. Fab fragments are monovalent, monomeric antigen-binding units generated by proteolytic cleavage of the antibody. F(ab′)2 fragments are bivalent, dimeric fragments formed by cleavage with pepsin, retaining both antigen-binding sites. Fd fragments consist of the VH and CH1 domains of the heavy chain. Fv fragments consist of the non-covalently associated VH and VL domains. dAb fragments are single-domain antibody fragments derived from camelid or shark heavy-chain antibodies. Isolated CDR regions may be used as synthetic peptides or grafted into non-immunoglobulin scaffolds to create novel binding molecules. scFv molecules are recombinant fusion proteins comprising the VH and VL domains connected by a flexible peptide linker, allowing for single-chain expression. Bi-specific single-chain Fv dimers are engineered constructs capable of binding two distinct epitopes, enabling dual targeting of pathological pathways. Diabodies are bivalent, bivalent, non-covalent dimers of scFv fragments that enhance avidity. Minibodies are larger constructs comprising an scFv fused to a dimerized CH3 domain, enabling extended serum half-life. Antibody-like binding peptidomimetics are small, non-antibody molecules designed to mimic the structure and function of antibody CDRs. Animal inoculation involves immunizing host animals with the target antigen to elicit an immune response, followed by harvesting of spleen or lymph node cells for antibody production. Conjugates refer to antibodies chemically linked to toxins, radionuclides, enzymes, or fluorescent markers for therapeutic or diagnostic purposes. Polyclonal antibody production involves immunizing animals and isolating serum antibodies that recognize multiple epitopes on the antigen. Monoclonal antibody production involves the generation of immortalized hybridoma cell lines that secrete a single, defined antibody. Hybridoma technology involves fusing antibody-producing B cells with myeloma cells to create stable cell lines. Plasma B cell isolation involves identifying and isolating antigen-specific B cells from immunized animals or humans by flow cytometry or single-cell sorting. RNA isolation and cDNA synthesis involve extracting mRNA from plasma B cells and reverse transcribing it into cDNA for amplification. DNA sequence amplification involves using PCR to amplify the variable region genes encoding the heavy and light chains. Phage display Fab expression vector construction involves cloning the amplified variable regions into phage vectors fused to a phage coat protein for selection of antigen-binding clones. Cx43-specific binding Fab selection involves screening the phage library for clones that bind to recombinant connexin 43 extracellular domains. Full-length IgG expression and purification involve transfecting mammalian cells with heavy and light chain constructs, followed by protein A affinity chromatography. Chimeric antibody production involves replacing the constant regions of a murine antibody with human constant regions. Humanized monoclonal antibody production involves grafting murine CDRs onto human framework regions to reduce immunogenicity. Fully human monoclonal antibody production involves isolating antibodies from transgenic mice expressing human immunoglobulin genes or from human B cell libraries. Genetic mutation and changes may be introduced to enhance binding affinity, stability, or expression yield. Methods for producing polyclonal and monoclonal antibodies include those described above, as well as transgenic animal models and recombinant expression systems. Substitutional variants refer to amino acid changes at specific positions that maintain or enhance function. Recombinant protein production involves expressing the antibody in mammalian, yeast, or bacterial systems under controlled conditions. Antibody conjugates and payloads include chemotherapeutic agents, cytotoxins, radioisotopes, or immune-stimulatory molecules linked to the antibody for targeted delivery.

### IV. EXAMPLES

- Anti-Cx43 monoclonal antibodies were generated by immunizing mice with a peptide corresponding to the second extracellular domain of human connexin 43. Hybridoma clones were screened for specificity and functional inhibition of hemichannel opening. One clone, designated MHC1, was selected for further characterization. The heavy and light chain variable regions were cloned and sequenced, and the CDR sequences were identified. Antibody pairing was confirmed by co-expression in HEK293F cells. Cx43 localization was visualized by immunofluorescence in astrocytes, showing membrane expression and enrichment at sites of hemichannel activity. Hemichannel opening was demonstrated using ethidium bromide uptake assays under low calcium conditions. Therapeutic use for spinal cord injury was proposed based on the antibody’s ability to block hemichannel-mediated ATP and glutamate release. Inhibition of Cx43 hemichannel activity was shown in vitro and in vivo using multiple models of spinal cord injury. Results demonstrated significant reduction in glial scarring, as measured by GFAP immunoreactivity, following MHC1 treatment. Recovery of hind limb function, as assessed by the Basso Mouse Scale, was significantly improved in MHC1-treated mice compared to controls. Neuronal dendrite length, measured by MAP2 staining, was preserved in the perilesional zone. Neuronal nuclei density was significantly higher in treated animals, indicating protection against cell death. Diagnostic and cancer therapeutic use was introduced by demonstrating that Cx43 knockout mice exhibited reduced osteolytic tumor growth in bone. Administration of the Cx43 antibody blocked osteolytic lesion formation and reduced tumor burden in a breast cancer metastasis model. Osteoarthritis treatment was illustrated by detecting elevated Cx43 expression in chondrocytes from human osteoarthritic cartilage. Inhibition of Cx43 activity with the antibody reduced cartilage degradation and synovial inflammation in a murine model of osteoarthritis. These examples demonstrate the broad therapeutic potential of the antibody across multiple disease indications.
