## FIELD

- The present invention relates to methods of making proteins with non-standard amino acids through post-translational proofreading systems that selectively degrade polypeptides containing incorrect or undesired amino acid residues at their N-termini. More specifically, the invention pertains to engineered cellular systems that utilize the N-end rule pathway of protein degradation to distinguish between standard amino acids and non-standard amino acids incorporated at the N-terminus of a target polypeptide, thereby enabling the enrichment of correctly synthesized proteins and the elimination of misincorporated variants. The methods described herein are particularly useful in synthetic biology, industrial bioproduction, biocontainment, and the development of therapeutic proteins requiring high chemical fidelity.

## BACKGROUND

- The twenty standard amino acids constitute the fundamental building blocks of all naturally occurring proteins in living organisms, each encoded by specific codons within the genetic code and recognized by cognate aminoacyl-tRNA synthetases and transfer RNAs. Over the past two decades, advances in genetic code expansion have enabled the site-specific incorporation of non-standard amino acids into proteins through the use of orthogonal aminoacyl-tRNA synthetase/tRNA pairs that recognize reassigned stop codons, such as the UAG codon. These non-standard amino acids, which include chemically modified analogs of phenylalanine, tyrosine, and lysine, offer unique physical, chemical, and biological properties that are inaccessible to the canonical amino acid repertoire. However, a persistent limitation in this field is the promiscuity of many orthogonal translation systems, which frequently misincorporate structurally similar standard amino acids—such as tyrosine, phenylalanine, or leucine—at the target site in the absence of the intended non-standard amino acid. This misincorporation leads to heterogeneous protein populations, reduced yields of the desired product, and compromised functional integrity. While some efforts have sought to improve synthetase specificity through directed evolution, these approaches are often labor-intensive, limited by screening throughput, and do not address the fundamental issue of post-incorporation fidelity. The N-end rule pathway, a conserved proteolytic mechanism in prokaryotes and eukaryotes, governs protein stability based on the identity of the N-terminal residue, with certain amino acids acting as destabilizing signals that target proteins for rapid degradation by ATP-dependent proteases such as ClpAP. In Escherichia coli, the adaptor protein ClpS recognizes and delivers N-terminal aromatic and large hydrophobic residues to the ClpP protease, thereby enabling selective degradation. Despite this well-characterized mechanism, prior art has not exploited the N-end rule as a post-translational filter to discriminate between non-standard and standard amino acids incorporated at the N-terminus of a target protein. Furthermore, no method has been described that couples the controlled exposure of an N-terminal residue via a removable protecting group with the selective degradation of misincorporated species using engineered ClpS variants. There remains a critical need for a robust, scalable, and programmable system that can enhance the purity of non-standard amino acid-containing proteins by eliminating misfolded, misincorporated, or structurally aberrant variants after translation but before functional use.

## SUMMARY

- The present invention introduces a novel method for selectively degrading proteins that contain undesired amino acid residues at their N-termini, thereby enabling the enrichment of target polypeptides incorporating a desired non-standard amino acid. This method involves the genetic engineering of a host cell to express a target polypeptide fused to a removable protecting group at its N-terminus, such that upon cleavage of the protecting group, the first amino acid of the target polypeptide is exposed as the N-terminal residue. The host cell is further engineered to express an adapter protein, such as a variant of ClpS, and a corresponding protease, such as ClpP, which together form a degradation system that recognizes and eliminates polypeptides bearing destabilizing N-terminal residues. By designing the removable protecting group to be cleaved by a specific enzyme under controlled conditions, the N-terminal residue of the target polypeptide is revealed only after translation is complete, allowing the degradation system to act as a post-translational proofreading mechanism. The adapter protein is engineered to distinguish between standard amino acids and non-standard amino acids based on subtle differences in side-chain chemistry, enabling the selective degradation of proteins containing misincorporated residues while preserving those containing the desired non-standard amino acid. This approach increases the production of target polypeptides with the desired non-standard amino acid substitution while simultaneously decreasing the accumulation of polypeptides containing standard amino acids or other undesired analogs. The method is optimized by altering reaction conditions, modifying the aminoacyl-tRNA synthetase or its cognate tRNA, or introducing additional mutations into the adapter protein to fine-tune substrate specificity. The process may be repeated iteratively until a high-fidelity system is achieved, resulting in a cellular platform capable of producing homogenous, chemically precise proteins with non-standard amino acids at defined positions.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

- As used herein, the terms “polypeptide” and “protein” are used interchangeably to refer to a linear chain of amino acid residues linked by peptide bonds, regardless of whether the chain has been folded into a functional conformation. The invention encompasses the genetic modification of a host cell to express a target polypeptide in which a non-standard amino acid is substituted at a predetermined site, typically encoded by a reassigned codon such as UAG. To facilitate post-translational discrimination, a removable protecting group is covalently attached to the N-terminus of the target polypeptide, such that the first residue of the target sequence is masked until the protecting group is enzymatically cleaved. The protecting group may consist of a peptide sequence, a ubiquitin moiety, or a self-splicing intein domain, each of which is designed to be cleaved by a specific protease expressed within the same cell. Upon cleavage, the N-terminal residue of the target polypeptide is exposed and becomes accessible to an adapter protein that recognizes its chemical identity. The adapter protein, such as a mutant form of ClpS, binds to destabilizing N-terminal residues and delivers the target polypeptide to a protease complex, such as ClpAP, for unfolding and degradation. In contrast, polypeptides containing a desired non-standard amino acid at the N-terminus are not recognized by the adapter protein and remain stable. The system may be further enhanced by the use of an inducible promoter to control the timing and level of adapter protein and protease expression, ensuring that degradation occurs only after full-length translation and protecting group cleavage. In prokaryotic cells, ubiquitin may be employed as a removable protecting group, although its use in eukaryotic cells is limited due to the presence of endogenous deubiquitinating enzymes. To overcome this limitation, an altered ubiquitin E3 ligase domain may be introduced to create a synthetic degradation pathway that is orthogonal to native ubiquitin signaling. The method further includes the attachment of a detectable moiety, such as a fluorescent protein or epitope tag, to the C-terminus of the target polypeptide to enable quantification and purification. The host cell is genetically modified to express an orthogonal aminoacyl-tRNA synthetase and its cognate tRNA, which are specific for the desired non-standard amino acid and do not cross-react with endogenous synthetases. The synthetase and tRNA may be encoded on a plasmid or genomically integrated, and their expression may be driven by constitutive or inducible promoters. The cell may be further engineered to lack endogenous genes that interfere with the system, such as clpS or mutS, to reduce background degradation or mutation rates. The nucleic acid sequences encoding the target polypeptide, the protecting group, the adapter protein, the protease, and the synthetase/tRNA pair are delivered to the cell using vectors such as plasmids, bacteriophages, or viral vectors, and may include regulatory elements such as ribosome binding sites, terminators, and origin of replication sequences. The method may be applied to a wide range of prokaryotic and eukaryotic cells, including Escherichia coli, Bacillus subtilis, Saccharomyces cerevisiae, mammalian cells, and plant cells. The removable protecting group may be selected from a group consisting of ubiquitin, the MENLYFQ/* sequence, TEV protease recognition sites, or self-splicing inteins, each of which can be cleaved by a corresponding protease expressed in the host cell. The adapter protein may be derived from ClpS, and may be engineered to contain mutations at key residues in its binding pocket to alter its specificity for different non-standard amino acids. The protease system may be endogenous, such as ClpAP, or exogenous, such as the Lon protease or the 20S proteasome, depending on the host organism. The method enables the production of target polypeptides with a purity exceeding 95% for the desired non-standard amino acid, even in the presence of structurally similar standard amino acids. The system may be scaled for industrial production and adapted for use in biocontained strains to prevent the survival of genetically modified organisms in the environment.

### II. Removable Protecting Groups

- The removable protecting group is positioned adjacent to the amino acid substitution site within the target polypeptide and serves to mask the N-terminal residue until the appropriate time for degradation discrimination. In one embodiment, the protecting group is a peptide sequence produced by the cell itself, such as a ubiquitin domain fused to the N-terminus of the target polypeptide. In another embodiment, the protecting group is a foreign sequence introduced via genetic engineering, such as the MENLYFQ/* sequence derived from the human ubiquitin-specific protease 1 cleavage motif, which is recognized and cleaved by the TEV protease. The protecting group may also be an enzyme-cleavable domain, such as a self-splicing intein that excises itself upon induction of a specific trigger, such as a change in pH, temperature, or the addition of a chemical inducer. In prokaryotic cells, ubiquitin may be used as a removable protecting group because it is not naturally present in bacteria, thereby minimizing interference with endogenous proteolytic pathways. In eukaryotic cells, ubiquitin is less suitable due to the presence of endogenous deubiquitinases that may prematurely cleave the protecting group; therefore, alternative protecting groups such as the MENLYFQ/* sequence or intein domains are preferred. The cleavage of the protecting group is mediated by a protease that is co-expressed in the cell under the control of an inducible promoter, ensuring that the N-terminal residue is only exposed after translation is complete. Methionine aminopeptidases may be employed to remove the initiating methionine residue following translation, thereby exposing the true N-terminal residue of the target polypeptide. The timing and efficiency of protecting group removal are critical to the success of the method, and may be optimized by adjusting promoter strength, ribosome binding site efficiency, or the concentration of inducer molecules.

### III. Detectable Moiety

- A detectable moiety is attached to the C-terminus of the target polypeptide to facilitate quantification, purification, and monitoring of protein expression. The detectable moiety may be a peptide sequence encoded by the cell, such as a His6x tag, a FLAG tag, or a streptavidin-binding peptide, each of which allows for affinity-based isolation of the target protein. Alternatively, the detectable moiety may be a fluorescent protein, such as superfolder green fluorescent protein (sfGFP), which enables real-time, non-destructive measurement of protein abundance using fluorescence spectroscopy or flow cytometry. The detectable moiety may also be a reporter gene product, such as luciferase or β-galactosidase, which produces a measurable signal upon addition of a substrate. The moiety is genetically fused to the C-terminus of the target polypeptide via a flexible linker sequence to prevent steric interference with folding or function. The signal intensity of the detectable moiety correlates with the amount of full-length, correctly synthesized protein, allowing for high-throughput screening of synthetase variants or degradation system efficiency. In embodiments where the target polypeptide is degraded due to misincorporation, the detectable moiety is also degraded, resulting in a reduction in signal. This correlation enables the selection of cells producing high-fidelity proteins based on fluorescence or luminescence intensity, thereby facilitating the evolution of orthogonal translation systems with enhanced specificity.

### IV. Genetic Modifications

- The host cell is genetically modified to include foreign nucleic acid sequences encoding the target polypeptide, the removable protecting group, the detectable moiety, the adapter protein, the protease, and the orthogonal aminoacyl-tRNA synthetase and tRNA pair. These sequences are typically assembled into expression cassettes that include regulatory elements such as promoters, ribosome binding sites, and transcriptional terminators to ensure proper expression levels. The target polypeptide is encoded with a reassigned codon, such as UAG, at the desired substitution site, and the orthogonal tRNA is engineered to recognize this codon and deliver the desired non-standard amino acid. The aminoacyl-tRNA synthetase is selected or engineered to specifically charge the orthogonal tRNA with the non-standard amino acid and to avoid cross-reactivity with endogenous amino acids. The cell may be genomically recoded to remove all instances of the reassigned codon from its native genome, thereby preventing unintended suppression and increasing the efficiency of non-standard amino acid incorporation. The foreign nucleic acid sequences may be delivered to the cell using plasmids, cosmids, bacteriophages, or viral vectors, and may be integrated into the chromosome using homologous recombination, CRISPR-Cas systems, or λ Red recombineering. The expression of the adapter protein and protease may be controlled by an inducible promoter, such as the TET or arabinose-inducible promoter, to ensure that degradation occurs only after translation is complete. The cell may be further modified to inactivate endogenous genes such as clpS, mutS, or clpP to reduce background degradation or mutation rates. The nucleic acid constructs may be assembled using Gibson assembly, Golden Gate cloning, or other modular DNA assembly techniques to enable rapid prototyping and optimization.

### V. Adapter Protein Protease Systems

- The adapter protein is a key component of the degradation system and functions to recognize the N-terminal residue of the target polypeptide and deliver it to a protease for degradation. In one embodiment, the adapter protein is a variant of ClpS derived from Escherichia coli, engineered to have altered specificity for non-standard amino acids. The adapter protein may contain point mutations at residues such as V65, L32, V43, or V99, which are located within the hydrophobic binding pocket of ClpS and influence its recognition profile. These mutations enable the adapter protein to distinguish between standard amino acids and non-standard amino acids based on side-chain size, polarity, or chemical functionality. For example, the ClpS V65I variant stabilizes proteins containing large hydrophobic non-standard amino acids while destabilizing those containing tyrosine or phenylalanine. The adapter protein may be fused to a tag such as GFP or His6x for detection or purification. The protease system may consist of the ClpAP complex, which includes the AAA+ unfoldase ClpA and the proteolytic chamber ClpP, or may be replaced by other ATP-dependent proteases such as Lon or HslUV, depending on the host organism. The adapter protein and protease may be expressed as a single operon under a single promoter to ensure stoichiometric expression and coordinated function. The system may be further enhanced by overexpressing ClpP to increase degradation capacity or by introducing chaperones to improve substrate delivery.

### VI. Cells

- The method may be implemented in a wide variety of prokaryotic and eukaryotic cells. Prokaryotic cells include Escherichia coli, Bacillus subtilis, Pseudomonas putida, Salmonella enterica, and Streptomyces species, each of which may be genomically recoded to enhance the fidelity of non-standard amino acid incorporation. Eukaryotic cells include Saccharomyces cerevisiae, Candida albicans, Aspergillus nidulans, mammalian cells such as HEK293, CHO, and HeLa cells, and plant cells such as Nicotiana benthamiana and Arabidopsis thaliana. The choice of host cell depends on the intended application, with bacteria being preferred for high-yield industrial production and eukaryotic cells being preferred for the production of complex, post-translationally modified proteins. The host cell may be engineered to lack endogenous release factors, such as RF1, to prevent premature termination at the reassigned codon. The cell may also be engineered to express a biosynthetic pathway for the non-standard amino acid, thereby eliminating the need for exogenous supplementation. The host cell may be maintained in minimal media supplemented with the non-standard amino acid to ensure selective pressure for the desired phenotype.

### VII. Standard Amino Acid

- Standard amino acids are the twenty naturally occurring amino acids encoded by the universal genetic code, including alanine, arginine, asparagine, aspartic acid, cysteine, glutamic acid, glutamine, glycine, histidine, isoleucine, leucine, lysine, methionine, phenylalanine, proline, serine, threonine, tryptophan, tyrosine, and valine. The N-end rule pathway in Escherichia coli recognizes certain standard amino acids as destabilizing, including tyrosine, phenylalanine, tryptophan, leucine, and isoleucine, which are targeted for degradation by the ClpS-ClpAP system. The invention exploits this natural degradation pathway by engineering the adapter protein to enhance its recognition of destabilizing standard amino acids while reducing its affinity for non-standard amino acids. The N-end rule may be extended to include additional standard amino acids, such as aspartate, glutamate, asparagine, and glutamine, by introducing heterologous aminoacyl-transferases or modifying the adapter protein to recognize these residues. The system may be tuned to destabilize all twenty standard amino acids, thereby ensuring that any misincorporation event results in degradation of the target polypeptide.

### VIII. Non-Standard Amino Acid

- Non-standard amino acids are unnatural amino acids that are not among the twenty standard amino acids encoded by the universal genetic code. These include p-acetyl-phenylalanine, p-azido-phenylalanine, p-iodo-phenylalanine, p-bromophenylalanine, 5-hydroxytryptophan, and biphenylalanine, among others. Non-standard amino acids may be functionalized with chemical groups such as ketones, azides, alkynes, halogens, or photo-crosslinkers to enable bioorthogonal reactions, imaging, or crosslinking. Each non-standard amino acid is paired with a cognate orthogonal aminoacyl-tRNA synthetase and tRNA that do not cross-react with endogenous translation machinery. The non-standard amino acid must be non-toxic to the host cell and must be able to be incorporated efficiently at the target site. Commercially available non-standard amino acids may be used, or they may be synthesized in situ using engineered biosynthetic pathways. The invention enables the discrimination of structurally similar non-standard amino acids based on their N-terminal stability, allowing for the selection of variants that are resistant to degradation while eliminating those that are recognized as destabilizing.

## Exemplary Degradation Materials and Methods

- A method for producing a target polypeptide with a non-standard amino acid at its N-terminus involves providing a genetically modified cell containing a nucleic acid sequence encoding a fusion protein comprising a removable protecting group, a target polypeptide with a non-standard amino acid substitution, and a detectable moiety. The cell is further provided with a nucleic acid sequence encoding a ubiquitin cleavase, such as UBP1, and a non-standard amino acid, such as p-acetyl-phenylalanine. Upon induction, the cell expresses the orthogonal synthetase and tRNA, incorporates the non-standard amino acid at the UAG codon, and expresses the protecting group and detectable moiety. The protecting group is cleaved by the ubiquitin cleavase, exposing the N-terminal residue. The adapter protein ClpS binds to destabilizing residues and delivers the target polypeptide to the ClpP protease for degradation. Fluorescence from the detectable moiety is measured to approximate the level of correct incorporation. Screening methods are used to identify synthetase variants with improved specificity by selecting for high fluorescence in the presence of the non-standard amino acid and low fluorescence in its absence.

## Example II

- A method of making a protein having a non-standard amino acid incorporated at its N-terminus in an engineered Escherichia coli strain with orthogonal translation systems involves the use of post-translational proofreading to discriminate between non-standard and standard amino acids. A reporter construct is genomically integrated into a recoded E. coli strain lacking UAG codons and release factor 1. The reporter consists of a ubiquitin domain, a UAG codon, a destabilizing N-degron, and superfolder GFP. The orthogonal tRNA and synthetase are expressed from a plasmid, and the N-terminally truncated yeast ubiquitin cleavase UBP1 is co-expressed to expose the N-terminal residue. The expression of ClpS reduces fluorescence in the absence of the non-standard amino acid, confirming that misincorporated standard amino acids are degraded. Overexpression of ClpS enhances this effect, and substitution of the UAG codon with UGG or UAC confirms that degradation is dependent on N-terminal identity. The method is extended to other target proteins, and the promiscuity of multiple orthogonal systems is characterized. The “Off” and “On” states of the proofreading system are used to profile the N-end classification of diverse non-standard amino acids, and ClpS variants are engineered to alter this classification.

### Example III

- ClpS variants are rationally engineered to have tunable specificities for distinguishing between standard amino acids and non-standard amino acids. Single-point mutations at positions V65, L32, V43, and V99 in the ClpS binding pocket are introduced, and mutants are screened for their ability to stabilize proteins containing non-standard amino acids while destabilizing those containing tyrosine or phenylalanine. The ClpS V65I variant is identified as a highly selective mutant that stabilizes large hydrophobic non-standard amino acids while promoting the degradation of standard amino acids.

### Example IV

- Local sense codon reassignment is used to incorporate non-standard amino acids at sense codons rather than stop codons. This approach requires the reassignment of a rare sense codon, such as AGG or AGA, to encode a non-standard amino acid using an orthogonal synthetase-tRNA pair. The N-end rule proofreading system is adapted to recognize the N-terminal residue following reassignment, enabling high-fidelity incorporation at sense codons.

### Example V

- The E. coli strain C321.ΔA is used as a host for genomic integration of a TET promoter-driven Ub-UAG-sfGFP expression cassette. λ Red recombineering and tolC negative selection are used to integrate the cassette, and MAGE is used to inactivate endogenous mutS and clpS genes. The UBP1/clpS_V65I operon is integrated into the genome, and synthetase variants are generated using Gibson assembly and error-prone PCR. Flow cytometry and cell sorting are used to isolate variants with improved selectivity, and Sanger sequencing is used to verify genetic modifications.

### Example VI

- A method of making a target polypeptide involves genetically modifying a cell to express the target polypeptide with a non-standard amino acid substitution using an engineered aminoacyl-tRNA synthetase and tRNA pair. A removable protecting group is attached to the N-terminus of the target polypeptide, and a detectable moiety is attached to the C-terminus. The cell is provided with the non-standard amino acid and expresses the synthetase, tRNA, enzyme for cleaving the protecting group, and adapter protein for the protease. The protease degrades polypeptides containing standard amino acids or undesired non-standard amino acids, enriching for those containing the desired non-standard amino acid. The aminoacyl-tRNA synthetase is engineered to improve selectivity, and the engineered cell and nucleic acid constructs are used to produce high-purity target polypeptides.
