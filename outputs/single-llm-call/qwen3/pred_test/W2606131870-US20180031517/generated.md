# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNICAL FIELD

- define technical field of invention

The present invention relates to the field of electrochemical sensing, specifically to solid-liquid junction ion sensing interfaces for the selective detection of molecular ions in complex aqueous environments. The invention encompasses novel ionophoric materials based on metal-organic supercontainers (MOSCs) that enable high-fidelity, size-selective potentiometric measurements of large molecular cations, including but not limited to organic dyes, neurotransmitters, and pharmaceutical compounds. These materials are integrated into both conventional and solid-contact ion-selective electrodes, providing enhanced sensitivity, stability, and selectivity over traditional ionophores. The technology is particularly suited for applications in environmental monitoring, clinical diagnostics, food safety, and point-of-care medical devices where the discrimination of molecular ions from smaller elemental ions is critical and previously unattainable with conventional sensing platforms.

## BACKGROUND

- introduce ion sensing technology
- describe limitations of current ionophores
- identify need for improved ion sensing technology

Ion sensing technology has long relied on ion-selective electrodes (ISEs) that utilize ionophores embedded within polymeric membranes to selectively recognize and transduce the activity of specific ionic species into measurable electrical potentials. Conventional ionophores, typically derived from macrocyclic compounds such as crown ethers, calixarenes, or valinomycin analogs, have demonstrated utility in detecting elemental ions like potassium, sodium, calcium, and chloride. However, these materials exhibit fundamental limitations when applied to the detection of larger molecular ions, such as organic cations with dimensions exceeding one nanometer. Their binding pockets are often too small, lack structural rigidity, or possess insufficient spatial organization to accommodate and selectively bind bulky molecular species. As a result, existing ionophores suffer from poor selectivity, low binding affinity, and significant cross-reactivity with smaller, more abundant elemental ions, rendering them ineffective for molecular ion sensing in complex matrices such as biological fluids or environmental samples. Furthermore, many ionophores leach from the membrane over time, degrade under ambient conditions, or fail to maintain stable interfacial potentials, leading to signal drift and unreliable measurements. There exists a critical and unmet need for a new class of ionophoric materials that combine molecular recognition tailored to the size and geometry of large molecular ions with the chemical robustness and interfacial stability required for practical, long-term sensing applications.

The present invention introduces a solid-liquid junction sensing interface that employs metal-organic supercontainers (MOSCs) as size-selective ionophores in potentiometric sensors for the detection of molecular ions. Unlike conventional ionophores, MOSCs are three-dimensional, crystalline, supramolecular architectures composed of metal nodes and organic linkers that form well-defined internal cavities capable of encapsulating molecular ions with high specificity. The invention demonstrates that MOSCs, when incorporated into mixed-matrix membranes, enable potentiometric sensors to respond with near-Nernstian slopes to large molecular cations such as methylene blue, while exhibiting minimal response to smaller elemental ions such as potassium, silver, or ammonium. This unprecedented selectivity arises from the precise geometric complementarity between the molecular ion and the MOSC’s endo- and exo-cavities, which are structurally rigid and chemically tunable. The resulting sensing apparatus comprises a polymeric membrane containing the MOSC ionophore in contact with an aqueous sample and an underlying solid-state transducer, such as a pyrolyzed carbon surface or an ion-sensitive field-effect transistor (ISFET), which converts the interfacial potential change into an electrical signal. The invention enables detection limits as low as 300 nanomolar, response times under one second for concentrations above 30 micromolar, and exceptional stability in both aqueous and complex environmental matrices, with no measurable leaching of the MOSC or bound analyte over extended periods. Potential applications include real-time monitoring of pharmaceutical residues in wastewater, detection of neurotransmitters in cerebrospinal fluid, and identification of food contaminants in complex matrices. Example 1 demonstrates the use of a cobalt-based MOSC (1-Co) to detect methylene blue with a slope of 56.8 mV per decade of concentration, while Example 2 confirms the absence of leaching after prolonged immersion in water, and Example 3 validates the sensor’s performance in natural river water, demonstrating resilience to matrix interference and maintaining a near-Nernstian response despite environmental redox activity.

Metal-organic supercontainers (MOSCs) are a class of crystalline, porous materials formed by the self-assembly of metal ions and multifunctional organic ligands into extended, three-dimensional frameworks with well-defined internal cavities. The MOSC employed in the present invention, designated as 1-Co, consists of cobalt(II) nodes coordinated to p-tert-butylsulfonyl-calix[4]arene and 1,4-benzenedicarboxylate linkers, forming an octahedral geometry with an outer diameter of 3.3 nanometers and an internal volume of 1.2 cubic nanometers. As illustrated in FIG. 1, this architecture features a single central endo-cavity with a diameter of approximately 1.7 nanometers and six peripheral exo-cavities, each with a diameter of about 0.74 nanometers, creating a multi-pore architecture that enables simultaneous, cooperative binding of molecular ions. The endo-cavity is lined with aromatic walls that facilitate cation-π interactions, while the exo-cavities provide additional binding sites that enhance local ion concentration and recognition kinetics. The chemical structure of the MOSC is inherently tunable through variation of the metal node, ligand geometry, or functional group substitution, permitting the precise engineering of cavity size, polarity, and binding affinity for target molecular ions. These features enable MOSCs to function as highly selective host systems for large molecular cations whose dimensions match the cavity architecture, such as methylene blue, tetrabutylammonium, or other structurally analogous species. When incorporated into a mixed-matrix membrane (MMM), the MOSC is dispersed within a polymeric matrix composed of poly(vinyl chloride) and a plasticizer, forming a stable, ion-selective interface that responds potentiometrically to the target analyte. As shown in FIG. 4A-B, the MMM scaffold can be applied to conventional ISEs via capillary filling or to solid-contact electrodes via spin-coating onto pyrolyzed SU8-derived carbon surfaces, forming a MOSC-solid sensing surface (SSS) scaffold. The SSS scaffold enables direct electrical coupling between the ion-selective membrane and a solid-state transducer, eliminating the need for an internal filling solution and enabling miniaturization for integration with microelectronic systems. The tunability of the MOSC cavities allows for customization to detect a broad range of molecular ions by modifying the ligand structure to alter cavity dimensions and electronic properties. In the MMM scaffold, the MOSC facilitates ion transfer across the membrane-electrolyte interface, generating a measurable potential difference that is transduced by a high-impedance voltmeter or an ISFET. The use of an ISFET as the transducer, as illustrated in FIG. 6 and FIG. 7, allows for direct measurement of drain current shifts correlated with changes in interfacial potential, enabling highly sensitive, label-free detection. The MOSC molecules may be covalently anchored to the SSS surface via functionalized linkers, as shown in FIG. 8 and FIG. 9, further enhancing mechanical stability and preventing delamination during prolonged use. The resulting MOSC-based ion sensors exhibit superior selectivity, stability, and sensitivity compared to prior art, with negligible interference from elemental ions, no measurable leaching, and near-Nernstian response characteristics across a wide dynamic range.

The proof-of-concept study utilized a cobalt-based metal-organic supercontainer, designated as 1-Co, synthesized via a solvothermal reaction between cobalt(II) chloride, p-tert-butylsulfonyl-calix[4]arene, and 1,4-benzenedicarboxylate in tetrahydrofuran. The resulting molecule exhibited an octahedral framework with a central endo-cavity and six exo-cavities, as confirmed by single-crystal X-ray diffraction. A mixed-matrix membrane (MMM) was prepared by dissolving poly(vinyl chloride), bis(2-ethylhexyl)sebacate, potassium tetrakis(4-chlorophenyl)borate, and 1-Co in tetrahydrofuran, followed by the addition of methylene blue as the target molecular ion. The solution was drop-cast onto a disposable pipette tip to form a conventional ISE or spin-coated onto a pyrolyzed SU8-derived carbon substrate to form a solid-contact ISE. Impedance spectroscopy revealed a two-fold reduction in charge-transfer resistance when 1-Co was present, indicating enhanced ion transfer across the membrane-electrolyte interface. Potentiometric measurements showed a near-Nernstian response to methylene blue with a slope of 56.8 ± 2.5 mV per decade of concentration and a detection limit of approximately 300 nanomolar, as illustrated in FIG. 12. The sensor exhibited minimal response to potassium, silver, and ammonium ions, confirming size-selective recognition, as shown in FIGS. 13–16. The response time for concentrations above 30 micromolar was less than one second, with potential stability exceeding 95% over a period of one hour, as depicted in FIG. 15. Similar near-Nernstian behavior was observed for tetrabutylammonium, with a slope of 60.0 ± 1.5 mV per decade and a detection limit of one micromolar, demonstrating the versatility of the MOSC scaffold for structurally distinct but similarly sized molecular ions.

### EXAMPLE 2: LEECHING STUDY

- describe leeching test results with FIG. 17

A leaching test was conducted by immersing two identical mixed-matrix membranes—one containing the 1-Co MOSC and one without—in deionized water for a period of seven days. The solution in contact with the membrane lacking the MOSC turned distinctly blue, indicating the release of methylene blue into the aqueous medium. In contrast, the solution surrounding the membrane containing the MOSC remained colorless, demonstrating that the methylene blue remained encapsulated within the MOSC cavities and did not leach out. Visual inspection and UV-vis spectroscopy confirmed the absence of methylene blue in the supernatant of the MOSC-containing membrane, while no change in the color or integrity of the membrane itself was observed. This result confirms that the MOSC acts as a molecular trap, immobilizing the target ion within its cavities and preventing diffusion into the surrounding environment, thereby ensuring long-term sensor stability and eliminating signal drift caused by analyte loss.

### EXAMPLE 3: SIMULATED WASTE WATER SAMPLE

- describe response curve results in simulated wastewater sample with FIG. 18

To evaluate performance in a complex environmental matrix, a simulated wastewater sample was prepared using water collected from the Fyris River in Uppsala, Sweden. Controlled concentrations of methylene blue were added to the river water, and the potentiometric response of the 1-Co-based solid-contact ISE was measured. The sensor exhibited a near-Nernstian response with a slope of 54.2 ± 3.1 mV per decade, comparable to that observed in pure aqueous solutions. A slight shift in the detection limit was observed, attributed to partial reduction of methylene blue by microbial activity in the river water, which decreased the concentration of the cationic form of the dye. UV-vis spectroscopy confirmed a lower concentration of methylene blue in the river water compared to deionized water under identical conditions. Despite this redox interference, the sensor maintained its selectivity and responsiveness to the cationic species, demonstrating that the MOSC-based sensing platform is robust enough to function in real-world environmental samples without requiring extensive sample pretreatment.

A silicon nanowire ion-sensitive field-effect transistor (SiNW-ISFET) was functionalized with a methylene blue-selective mixed-matrix membrane (MB+-MMM1) containing the 1-Co MOSC. The device was conditioned in a 10-micromolar methylene blue solution for 24 hours to establish a stable interfacial potential. Electrical measurements were performed in a Faraday cage using a semiconductor parameter analyzer, with the gate exposed to varying concentrations of methylene blue in buffer solution. The transfer characteristics of the SiNW-ISFET showed a clear shift in threshold voltage (VTH) proportional to the logarithm of methylene blue concentration, with a maximum ΔVTH of 58.3 mV per decade, closely matching the Nernstian prediction. The shift was absent in control devices lacking the MOSC-containing membrane, confirming that the signal originated from the selective binding of methylene blue to the MOSC cavities. The binding constant of the 1-Co molecule for methylene blue was determined to be 1.42 × 10⁴ M⁻¹, consistent with single-crystal structural data. The VTH shift exhibited a near-linear dependence on the logarithm of methylene blue activity (αMB), with a slope of 57.1 mV per decade, demonstrating a near-Nernstian response. Minor deviations at high concentrations were attributed to saturation of binding sites and non-ideal activity coefficients. The detection limit for methylene blue was determined to be 280 nanomolar, surpassing the performance of conventional ISEs. Cross-sensitivity tests revealed negligible response to potassium, sodium, ammonium, and silver ions, with selectivity coefficients (KPot,MB) below 10⁻⁴, confirming exceptional molecular ion selectivity. The same MOSC scaffold was modified to detect sodium ions by replacing the methylene blue with sodium-selective ligands, yielding a near-Nernstian response to αNa with a slope of 55.4 mV per decade and a KNa,KPot value of 0.002. Simultaneous detection of methylene blue and sodium ions in a mixed solution demonstrated the capability for multiplexed sensing, with distinct VTH shifts corresponding to each ion, enabling real-time, label-free analysis of complex samples without cross-talk. The integration of MOSCs into ISFET platforms thus enables a new paradigm in molecular ion sensing, combining the selectivity of supramolecular recognition with the scalability and sensitivity of solid-state electronics.
