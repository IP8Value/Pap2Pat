# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

- The present invention relates to novel chemical and enzymatic methods for the direct, quantitative, and base-resolution detection of cytosine modifications in nucleic acids, particularly in DNA and RNA. Specifically, the invention encompasses a suite of bisulfite-free sequencing techniques that leverage TET enzyme-mediated oxidation and borane reductive conversion to transform modified cytosine derivatives into dihydrouracil, enabling their unambiguous identification through standard nucleic acid sequencing platforms. These methods provide a comprehensive, high-fidelity, and non-destructive approach to profiling 5-methylcytosine (5mC), 5-hydroxymethylcytosine (5hmC), 5-formylcytosine (5fC), and 5-carboxylcytosine (5caC) without the severe DNA degradation, sequence bias, or indirect inference inherent in prior technologies. The invention is particularly suited for applications in epigenetic research, clinical diagnostics, and biomarker discovery where accurate, reproducible, and sensitive detection of DNA modifications is critical.

## BACKGROUND

- Cytosine is one of the four canonical nucleobases in DNA and RNA, and its chemical modification plays a central role in regulating gene expression, genomic stability, and cellular differentiation. The most extensively studied modification, 5-methylcytosine (5mC), is established as a key epigenetic mark associated with transcriptional repression and is often referred to as the fifth base of the genetic code. Through the action of the ten-eleven translocation (TET) family of dioxygenases, 5mC undergoes iterative oxidation to form 5-hydroxymethylcytosine (5hmC), which is enriched in neuronal and pluripotent cells and is increasingly recognized as a stable epigenetic signal rather than merely a transient intermediate. Further oxidation yields 5-formylcytosine (5fC) and 5-carboxylcytosine (5caC), both of which are present at low but biologically significant levels and are implicated in active DNA demethylation pathways mediated by thymine DNA glycosylase and base excision repair. The detection and quantification of these modifications have long been hindered by the limitations of bisulfite sequencing, the historical gold standard, which chemically deaminates unmodified cytosine to uracil under harsh acidic and high-temperature conditions, resulting in the degradation of over 90% of the input DNA and the loss of sequence complexity due to the conversion of nearly all cytosines to thymines. Although alternative methods such as oxidative bisulfite sequencing (oxBS-seq) and TET-assisted bisulfite sequencing (TAB-seq) have been developed to distinguish 5mC from 5hmC, they retain the fundamental drawbacks of bisulfite treatment, including poor DNA recovery, reduced sequencing depth, and elevated sequencing artifacts. RNA bisulfite sequencing (RNA-BS-seq) has similarly been applied to detect cytosine modifications in RNA, but suffers from the same chemical instability and low conversion efficiency. Consequently, there remains a critical and unmet need for a method capable of directly, quantitatively, and simultaneously detecting all four oxidized cytosine derivatives in both DNA and RNA without inducing significant nucleic acid damage or requiring multiple independent assays and complex computational subtraction to resolve individual modification states.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

- The invention introduces a novel family of sequencing methods collectively termed TET-Assisted Pyridine Borane Sequencing (TAPS) and its derivatives, which enable the direct, quantitative, and subtraction-free detection of 5mC, 5hmC, 5fC, and 5caC in genomic and transcriptomic nucleic acids. The core innovation lies in the sequential application of TET enzyme-mediated oxidation to convert 5mC and 5hmC into 5fC and 5caC, followed by selective chemical reduction using pyridine borane or its derivatives to convert these oxidized derivatives into dihydrouracil (DHU), which is subsequently amplified and sequenced as thymine. This chemical transformation bypasses the need for bisulfite treatment entirely, preserving the integrity of the nucleic acid template and maintaining high sequence complexity. In a first embodiment, the method enables the specific identification of 5mC by blocking 5hmC with a glucosyltransferase enzyme that transfers a glucose moiety to the hydroxymethyl group, thereby preventing its oxidation by TET, while 5mC is oxidized and reduced to DHU. In a second embodiment, 5hmC is selectively identified by chemically oxidizing it to 5fC using potassium ruthenate or a related oxidant, followed by borane reduction to DHU, while 5mC remains unaltered. In a third embodiment, the method allows for the direct detection of 5fC and 5caC by reducing them to DHU without prior blocking, enabling the profiling of active demethylation intermediates. In a fourth embodiment, 5caC is specifically detected by blocking 5fC with O-ethylhydroxylamine prior to borane reduction, allowing for the selective conversion of 5caC to DHU. The invention further encompasses the use of these methods for quantitative analysis of modification frequencies across entire genomes or transcriptomes, with high mapping rates, superior base quality scores, and minimal PCR bias. The method is applicable to diverse nucleic acid types, including genomic DNA, cell-free DNA, mitochondrial DNA, and RNA, and can be implemented using standard next-generation sequencing platforms with minimal library preparation modifications.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

- The invention provides a comprehensive and chemically precise method for the detection of cytosine modifications in nucleic acids through a sequence of enzymatic and chemical reactions that convert modified bases into a uniform, sequencing-compatible form. The method begins with the provision of a purified nucleic acid sample, which may be derived from any biological source, including human, animal, plant, or microbial tissues, cells, or bodily fluids. In a first implementation for the identification of 5mC, the sample is treated with β-glucosyltransferase and UDP-glucose to selectively glucosylate 5hmC, thereby rendering it inert to subsequent TET oxidation. The glucosylated sample is then incubated with a TET enzyme, such as NgTET1 or mTET1CD, in the presence of α-ketoglutarate, iron(II), and ascorbate, which oxidizes 5mC to 5fC and subsequently to 5caC. The oxidized nucleic acid is then subjected to reduction using pyridine borane or pic-borane under controlled pH and temperature conditions, converting 5fC and 5caC into dihydrouracil (DHU). The resulting modified nucleic acid is amplified using polymerase chain reaction with a uracil-tolerant DNA polymerase, and the DHU residues are read as thymine during sequencing. The frequency of thymine incorporation at cytosine positions is then quantified to determine the absolute abundance of 5mC. In a second implementation for the identification of 5hmC, the nucleic acid sample is first denatured and treated with potassium ruthenate or a similar oxidizing agent to convert 5hmC to 5fC, while leaving 5mC unchanged. The oxidized sample is then treated with pic-borane, which reduces 5fC to DHU, while 5mC remains unreacted. The resulting nucleic acid is amplified and sequenced, with thymine signals corresponding exclusively to the original 5hmC positions. For the identification of 5fC and 5caC, the nucleic acid is directly treated with pyridine borane, which reduces both 5fC and 5caC to DHU, enabling their collective detection. To specifically identify 5caC, the sample is first treated with O-ethylhydroxylamine, which forms a stable oxime adduct with 5fC, preventing its reduction, while 5caC remains accessible to borane-mediated conversion to DHU. The method is further enhanced by the use of uracil-containing adaptor structures and USER enzyme treatment to protect ligated ends from oxidative damage during library preparation. The modified nucleic acid can be amplified using standard PCR protocols, cloned, or directly sequenced using next-generation sequencing platforms such as Illumina NovaSeq or PacBio systems. The invention further includes kits comprising all necessary reagents, including TET enzymes, glucosyltransferase, pyridine borane, pic-borane, potassium ruthenate, O-ethylhydroxylamine, buffer systems, and sequencing adaptors, pre-formulated for standardized and reproducible use. The method is validated across a range of input quantities, from picogram levels of cell-free DNA to microgram quantities of genomic DNA, and demonstrates superior DNA recovery, higher mapping rates, and improved coverage uniformity compared to bisulfite-based methods.

## EXAMPLES

- A model DNA construct was synthesized containing defined sequences with cytosine, 5-methylcytosine, 5-hydroxymethylcytosine, 5-formylcytosine, and 5-carboxylcytosine at known positions to serve as spike-in controls. These synthetic oligonucleotides were mixed with genomic DNA from mouse embryonic stem cells to evaluate the specificity and efficiency of the TAPS, TAPSβ, CAPS, PS, and PS-c reactions. The DNA was fragmented, ligated to adaptor sequences, and processed through each variant of the method. In TAPSβ, β-glucosyltransferase treatment resulted in near-complete blocking of 5hmC conversion (1.9% false conversion), while 5mC was oxidized and reduced to DHU with 97.6% efficiency. In CAPS, potassium ruthenate oxidation followed by pic-borane reduction achieved 97.2% conversion of 5hmC to DHU with a false-positive rate of 0.72%. In PS, direct borane reduction converted 5fC and 5caC to DHU with efficiencies of 76.8% and 93.8%, respectively, and a background conversion rate of 0.27%. In PS-c, O-ethylhydroxylamine blocking of 5fC reduced its conversion to 15.2%, while 5caC conversion remained at 95.3%. High-throughput sequencing of treated samples demonstrated mapping rates exceeding 90% and base quality scores comparable to unmodified controls. Comparison with whole-genome bisulfite sequencing revealed significantly higher DNA recovery, reduced PCR bias, and improved coverage of CpG islands and repetitive regions. The method was successfully applied to low-input cell-free DNA from human plasma, to mitochondrial DNA, and to RNA transcripts, demonstrating its versatility across nucleic acid types and sample sources. Statistical analyses confirmed high concordance with published datasets and eliminated the need for subtraction-based calculations to resolve 5mC and 5hmC levels. The invention was further validated through HPLC-MS/MS quantification of nucleoside derivatives, confirming the stoichiometric conversion of modified cytosines to DHU and the absence of off-target reactions. The resulting data enabled the generation of high-resolution epigenomic maps of cytosine modifications across the genome, revealing novel enrichment patterns at enhancers, promoters, and CTCF-binding sites, and providing a robust platform for clinical and research applications.
