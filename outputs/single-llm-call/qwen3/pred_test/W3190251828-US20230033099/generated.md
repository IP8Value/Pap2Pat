# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

Cancer remains one of the leading causes of mortality worldwide, with millions of new cases diagnosed annually and a substantial proportion of patients succumbing to disease progression despite advances in medical science. The burden of cancer is further exacerbated by the heterogeneity of tumor biology, the emergence of therapy-resistant clones, and the limited durability of responses to conventional treatments. Current therapeutic modalities—including surgery, radiation, chemotherapy, and targeted biologics—often yield transient benefits, with many patients experiencing disease recurrence or metastatic spread that proves fatal. Chemotherapy, while cytotoxic to rapidly dividing cells, lacks specificity and frequently damages healthy tissues, resulting in severe systemic toxicity that compromises patient quality of life and limits dosing regimens. Targeted therapies, though more precise, are only effective in cancers harboring specific molecular alterations, leaving large populations of patients without viable options, particularly those with tumors lacking well-defined driver mutations such as triple-negative breast cancer. Immunotherapies, including immune checkpoint inhibitors, have revolutionized the treatment landscape for certain malignancies; however, a significant fraction of patients remain non-responsive due to immunologically “cold” tumor microenvironments characterized by poor antigen presentation, low T cell infiltration, and dominant immunosuppressive cell populations. Moreover, the high cost, complex manufacturing, and logistical challenges associated with personalized neoantigen vaccines restrict their broad clinical applicability. Existing dendritic cell-based therapies, while promising, have been constrained by suboptimal antigen loading, insufficient co-stimulatory signaling, and inadequate DC activation, leading to weak or transient anti-tumor immunity. There is therefore an urgent and unmet need for a scalable, broadly applicable, and potent immunotherapeutic strategy capable of overcoming tumor immune evasion while minimizing systemic toxicity and enabling use in antigenically undefined or metastatic cancers.

## SUMMARY

The present invention discloses a novel dendritic cell-based cancer therapy that utilizes tumor membrane vesicles loaded with glycosyl phosphatidylinositol-anchored immunostimulatory molecules to enhance dendritic cell uptake, activation, and subsequent induction of tumor-specific adaptive immunity. The therapeutic approach involves the ex vivo pulsing of dendritic cells with tumor membrane vesicles derived from tumor tissue or cell lines, wherein the vesicles are surface-modified with glycosyl phosphatidylinositol-anchored cytokines, including granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor and interleukin-12, to simultaneously deliver tumor antigens and potent immune adjuvants. The dendritic cells, once pulsed with these modified vesicles, are administered to a subject suffering from cancer, thereby initiating a robust and sustained anti-tumor immune response. The tumor membrane vesicles are characterized by a diameter ranging from 200 to 600 nanometers, preserving the native tumor antigen repertoire, including transmembrane and glycosyl phosphatidylinositol-anchored proteins, while excluding intracellular components that may induce tolerance. The glycosyl phosphatidylinositol domain enables stable, non-covalent incorporation of immunostimulatory proteins into the lipid bilayer of the vesicles, ensuring their presentation on the vesicle surface in a biologically active conformation. The invention encompasses multiple embodiments, including the use of tumor membrane vesicles derived from autologous or allogeneic tumor sources, the incorporation of additional glycosyl phosphatidylinositol-anchored molecules such as B7-1 and B7-2 to enhance co-stimulation, and the combination of this dendritic cell vaccine with chemotherapy agents, immune checkpoint inhibitors, or other immunomodulatory therapies. The method is applicable to a wide spectrum of epithelial cancers, including but not limited to breast, ovarian, lung, colorectal, pancreatic, and head and neck carcinomas, and is particularly effective in cancers lacking defined target antigens, such as triple-negative breast cancer. The invention further includes formulations wherein the dendritic cells are administered intradermally, subcutaneously, or intranodally, and may be cryopreserved for off-the-shelf use or generated autologously from peripheral blood monocytes or bone marrow precursors.

### Tumor Membrane Vesicles (TMVs)

Tumor membrane vesicles are submicron, lipid bilayer particles derived from the plasma membrane of tumor cells, retaining surface proteins, glycoproteins, and lipids characteristic of the parent tumor. These vesicles serve as a natural reservoir of tumor-associated antigens, including overexpressed receptors, mutated proteins, and glycosyl phosphatidylinositol-anchored molecules, without containing intracellular components that may promote immunosuppression. The function of tumor membrane vesicles is to deliver a broad spectrum of tumor antigens to antigen-presenting cells in a particulate form that mimics the native tumor cell surface, thereby promoting efficient uptake and cross-presentation. Glycosyl phosphatidylinositol-anchored immunostimulatory molecules are proteins covalently attached to the lipid bilayer via a glycosyl phosphatidylinositol anchor, enabling stable, non-covalent integration into the vesicle membrane without denaturation or loss of biological activity. The protein transfer method involves incubating purified glycosyl phosphatidylinositol-anchored proteins with pre-formed tumor membrane vesicles under controlled conditions, allowing spontaneous insertion into the lipid bilayer. GPI-anchored protein incorporation is facilitated by the hydrophobic nature of the GPI anchor, which embeds into the vesicle membrane while the extracellular domain remains exposed and functional. The function of GPI-anchored proteins on tumor membrane vesicles is to provide co-stimulatory and cytokine signals directly at the site of antigen presentation, thereby activating dendritic cells and promoting their migration to lymphoid organs. The TMV-based vaccine immunotherapy approach leverages the natural immunogenicity of tumor membranes combined with the adjuvant properties of GPI-anchored cytokines to create a potent, multi-antigenic, and self-adjuvanting therapeutic. Cytokine-adjuvanted TMVs are vesicles that have been surface-modified with glycosyl phosphatidylinositol-anchored forms of granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor, interleukin-12, or other immunostimulatory cytokines. The dendritic cell vaccine platform consists of ex vivo-pulsed dendritic cells that have internalized cytokine-adjuvanted tumor membrane vesicles, resulting in enhanced antigen presentation, upregulation of co-stimulatory molecules, and secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines. Dendritic cell uptake of tumor membrane vesicles is significantly enhanced when the vesicles contain glycosyl phosphatidylinositol-anchored immunostimulatory molecules, as these molecules engage receptors on the dendritic cell surface, triggering endocytic pathways and promoting phagocytosis. Dendritic cell activation is evidenced by increased surface expression of CD80, CD86, and MHC class II, as well as secretion of interferon-gamma, tumor necrosis factor-alpha, and interleukin-6. Cytokine production by dendritic cells is directly correlated with the presence of GPI-GM-CSF and GPI-IL-12 on the vesicle surface, and is essential for the induction of cytotoxic T lymphocyte responses. Tumor growth inhibition occurs as a consequence of dendritic cell-mediated priming of tumor-specific T cells, leading to infiltration of the tumor microenvironment and direct killing of malignant cells. Metastasis reduction is observed due to the systemic activation of anti-tumor immunity, which targets disseminated cells and micrometastases. Immune cell infiltration into tumors is increased following dendritic cell vaccination, with elevated numbers of CD4+ and CD8+ T cells, natural killer cells, and mature dendritic cells detected within the tumor parenchyma. Particles with immunogenicity in the range of 200 to 600 nanometers are optimal for dendritic cell uptake and lymphatic drainage, and tumor membrane vesicles fall within this size range. Tumor membrane vesicle composition includes lipid bilayers, transmembrane proteins, glycosyl phosphatidylinositol-anchored proteins, and residual membrane-associated carbohydrates. Fusion proteins may be engineered to contain a tumor antigen fused to a glycosyl phosphatidylinositol anchor, enabling direct incorporation into the vesicle membrane. GPI domain anchoring ensures that cytokines remain stably associated with the vesicle surface during circulation and uptake. Cytokine surface protein refers to any immunostimulatory molecule, such as GM-CSF or IL-12, that is conjugated to a glycosyl phosphatidylinositol anchor and displayed on the vesicle surface. Dendritic cell derivation may be achieved from bone marrow precursors, peripheral blood monocytes, or circulating progenitors using cytokine-driven differentiation protocols. Immunostimulatory agents include cytokines, co-stimulatory ligands, Toll-like receptor agonists, and other molecules that enhance dendritic cell maturation. GM-CSF and IL-12 are cytokines critical for dendritic cell survival, activation, and T helper 1 polarization. B7-1 and B7-2 are co-stimulatory molecules that bind CD28 on T cells to provide the second signal required for T cell activation, and may be anchored to tumor membrane vesicles via glycosyl phosphatidylinositol domains. Tumor membrane vesicle pulsing refers to the incubation of dendritic cells with tumor membrane vesicles under controlled conditions to allow internalization and processing. Cancer antigen refers to any molecule expressed by tumor cells that can be recognized by the immune system, including HER2, MUC1, EGFR, WT1, and NY-ESO-1. HER2 antigen is a transmembrane tyrosine kinase receptor overexpressed in a subset of breast, gastric, and ovarian cancers. Other cancer antigens include mutated KRAS, p53, MAGE family proteins, and neoantigens derived from somatic mutations. Antigen molecule anchoring may be achieved through genetic fusion to a glycosyl phosphatidylinositol signal sequence or through post-isolation chemical conjugation. Tumor membrane vesicle extraction involves the mechanical disruption of tumor tissue or cell pellets, followed by differential centrifugation and density gradient purification. Cell fragmentation is achieved through repeated freeze-thaw cycles, homogenization, or sonication. Freezing and thawing cycles induce membrane rupture and vesicle formation. Dounce homogenization is performed using a glass homogenizer with tight-fitting pestles to gently disrupt cells while preserving membrane integrity. Centrifugation is employed at increasing speeds to remove cellular debris, nuclei, and organelles, leaving behind a vesicle-enriched supernatant. Density gradient centrifugation, particularly using sucrose gradients, separates vesicles based on buoyant density, yielding highly pure populations. Lipid ring detection is performed using fluorescent lipid dyes or electron microscopy to confirm vesicle integrity. Sonication and extrusion are used to standardize vesicle size and reduce polydispersity. Cytoplasmic protein and RNA inclusion is minimized through rigorous washing and gradient purification, ensuring that vesicles are enriched for membrane components. Dendritic cell administration may be performed via subcutaneous, intradermal, or intranodal injection. Tumor membrane vesicle source may be derived from autologous tumor tissue, allogeneic cell lines, or patient-derived xenografts. Cancer types suitable for this therapy include those with abundant tumor tissue or archived samples, including breast, ovarian, lung, and colorectal cancers. Combination therapy may include administration of chemotherapy, radiation, or immune checkpoint inhibitors prior to, during, or after dendritic cell vaccination.

### TMV Preparation, Characterization, and GPI-ISM Incorporation

Tumor membrane vesicles are prepared from frozen tumor tissue or cultured tumor cell pellets by first homogenizing the material in a buffered saline solution, followed by sequential centrifugation steps to remove cellular debris, nuclei, and mitochondria. The resulting supernatant is subjected to ultracentrifugation over a 41% sucrose density gradient, where tumor membrane vesicles migrate to a distinct lipid-rich band based on their buoyant density. This band is carefully harvested, diluted in phosphate-buffered saline, and subjected to additional ultracentrifugation to pellet the vesicles and remove residual sucrose. The vesicle pellet is resuspended in sterile saline and analyzed for particle size distribution using dynamic light scattering via a Zetasizer Nano ZS instrument. The incorporation of glycosyl phosphatidylinositol-anchored immunostimulatory molecules is achieved by incubating purified GPI-GM-CSF and GPI-IL-12 proteins with the vesicles at a ratio of 25 micrograms of GPI-protein per milligram of vesicle protein at 37 degrees Celsius for four hours under constant rotation. Following incubation, the mixture is centrifuged at 100,000 × g for one hour to pellet the vesicles, and the supernatant containing unbound GPI-proteins is discarded. The vesicle pellet is washed twice with phosphate-buffered saline to remove residual unincorporated proteins. Incorporation efficiency is confirmed by flow cytometry using fluorochrome-conjugated antibodies specific for GM-CSF and IL-12, with positive staining indicating successful surface display of the immunostimulatory molecules on the vesicle membrane.

### TMVs Containing the Tumor Antigen HER2 and Protein Transferred GPI-ISMs on Their Surface

Tumor membrane vesicles derived from HER2-expressing tumor cells are characterized using flow cytometry and nanoparticle tracking analysis. Flow cytometry confirms the retention of HER2 on the vesicle surface through staining with anti-HER2 monoclonal antibodies conjugated to fluorescent dyes, demonstrating that HER2 expression levels on vesicles mirror those of the parent tumor cells. Zetasizer analysis reveals a mean particle diameter of 220 to 280 nanometers with a polydispersity index below 0.3, indicating a homogeneous and stable vesicle population. Flow cytometric analysis of GPI-ISM-incorporated vesicles confirms the presence of GM-CSF and IL-12 on the vesicle surface, with no detectable signal in control vesicles lacking GPI-proteins. The co-localization of HER2 and GPI-ISMs on the same vesicle population is verified by dual-labeling experiments, demonstrating that each vesicle carries both tumor antigen and adjuvant signals in a single particulate unit, thereby enabling simultaneous delivery of antigen and immune stimulation to dendritic cells.

### Uptake of TMVs by DCs is Potentiated by GPI-ISMs

The uptake of tumor membrane vesicles by dendritic cells is quantified using pHrodo Deep Red, a pH-sensitive fluorescent dye that exhibits minimal fluorescence at neutral pH but emits strong fluorescence upon acidification within endosomes and phagolysosomes. Dendritic cells are incubated with pHrodo-labeled tumor membrane vesicles for 30, 90, and 180 minutes, after which cells are washed and analyzed by flow cytometry. Dendritic cells exposed to plain tumor membrane vesicles show modest uptake, with approximately 15% of cells positive for pHrodo signal after 180 minutes. In contrast, dendritic cells exposed to vesicles containing GPI-GM-CSF exhibit a 2.5-fold increase in uptake, with 38% of cells positive for pHrodo signal. Vesicles containing GPI-IL-12 induce a 2.2-fold increase in uptake, while vesicles containing both GPI-GM-CSF and GPI-IL-12 induce a 3.1-fold increase in uptake compared to plain vesicles. The mean fluorescence intensity of pHrodo signal is also significantly higher in dendritic cells exposed to GPI-ISM-containing vesicles, indicating greater internalization per cell. These findings demonstrate that glycosyl phosphatidylinositol-anchored immunostimulatory molecules enhance dendritic cell phagocytosis of tumor membrane vesicles, likely through receptor-mediated endocytosis triggered by cytokine-receptor interactions.

### DC Vaccine Therapy Reduces Tumor Growth

In a murine model of HER2-positive breast cancer, tumor-bearing mice are treated with dendritic cells pulsed ex vivo with HER2+ tumor membrane vesicles containing GPI-GM-CSF and GPI-IL-12. Control groups receive dendritic cells alone, dendritic cells pulsed with plain vesicles, or phosphate-buffered saline. Tumor growth is monitored over 50 days using caliper measurements. Mice treated with the dendritic cell vaccine exhibit a significant reduction in tumor volume compared to all control groups, with median tumor size reduced by 70% at day 35. Survival analysis reveals that 80% of mice receiving the dendritic cell vaccine survive beyond day 50, whereas all control mice succumb to tumor burden by day 35. Histological examination of tumors from vaccinated mice reveals increased infiltration of CD8+ T cells and decreased density of regulatory T cells, confirming the induction of a potent anti-tumor immune response. These data establish that dendritic cells pulsed with cytokine-adjuvanted tumor membrane vesicles effectively inhibit tumor growth and prolong survival in a HER2-positive cancer model.

### DC Vaccine Inhibits 4T1 Tumor Growth, Reduces Lung Metastasis, and Improved Immune Profile of Tumors

In a highly aggressive and metastatic triple-negative breast cancer model using 4T1 cells, mice are inoculated with tumor cells and subsequently treated with dendritic cells pulsed with 4T1-derived tumor membrane vesicles containing GPI-GM-CSF and GPI-IL-12. Tumor growth is monitored for 20 days, and lungs are harvested for metastasis quantification. Treatment with the dendritic cell vaccine results in a 60% reduction in primary tumor volume compared to controls. Lung metastatic burden is quantified by collagenase digestion and cell counting, revealing a 2.5-fold reduction in metastatic nodules in vaccinated mice. Flow cytometric analysis of tumor-infiltrating leukocytes demonstrates a 2.1-fold increase in MHC-II+ CD11c+ dendritic cells, a 1.8-fold increase in CD4+ T cells, and a 2.3-fold increase in CD8+ T cells within the tumor microenvironment of vaccinated mice. These findings demonstrate that the dendritic cell vaccine not only inhibits primary tumor growth but also suppresses metastasis and reprograms the tumor immune landscape toward a pro-inflammatory, anti-tumor state, even in the absence of a defined tumor antigen.

### Bone Marrow-Derived Dendritic Cell (BMDC) Production

Bone marrow-derived dendritic cells are generated from femurs of female BALB/c mice by flushing bone marrow cells into culture medium supplemented with recombinant murine granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor at a concentration of 20 nanograms per milliliter. Cells are cultured for ten days, with medium and cytokine replenishment on days 3, 6, and 8. On day 10, non-adherent and loosely adherent cells are harvested, washed, and assessed for dendritic cell markers CD11c and MHC-II by flow cytometry, with greater than 85% of cells expressing both markers. These cells are used immediately for uptake, pulsing, and therapeutic studies, ensuring optimal functional capacity and viability.

### 4T1 Lung Metastasis Assay

Lungs from tumor-bearing mice are collected at day 20 post-tumor inoculation, minced into small fragments, and digested in collagenase IV at 1 milligram per milliliter for two hours at 37 degrees Celsius under constant rotation. The resulting cell suspension is filtered through a 70-micron strainer and washed twice in selection medium containing 60 micromolar 6-thioguanine to eliminate fibroblasts. Tumor cells are then enumerated using an automated cell counter, and the number of viable 4T1 cells per lung is calculated. This method provides a quantitative measure of metastatic burden and demonstrates a significant reduction in lung metastases in mice treated with the dendritic cell vaccine compared to control groups.
