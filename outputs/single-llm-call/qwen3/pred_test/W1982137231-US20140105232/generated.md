## BACKGROUND

- Optical reference cavities serve as critical components in precision metrology, laser frequency stabilization, and coherent optical systems by providing a stable resonant frequency reference against which laser output can be locked. The performance of such systems is fundamentally limited by the thermal and mechanical noise inherent in the cavity structure, particularly when miniaturized for on-chip integration. Prior efforts to achieve high-stability optical references have relied heavily on bulk optical components, such as Fabry–Pérot cavities formed between mirrored mirrors suspended in vacuum, or long optical fiber loops coiled in temperature-controlled environments. While these systems offer excellent frequency stability, their size, fragility, and susceptibility to environmental perturbations render them unsuitable for portable, scalable, or field-deployable applications. The need for compact, robust, and high-performance optical references has motivated the development of chip-scale alternatives, particularly those based on whispering gallery mode resonators and ring waveguide structures fabricated in low-loss dielectric materials such as silicon nitride and silica-on-silicon.

- Prior art has demonstrated the use of microdisk and microring resonators with quality factors exceeding 10⁷, but these devices suffer from limited mode volumes and high power densities, which exacerbate thermorefractive and photothermal noise. The Hänsch–Couillaud method, a widely adopted technique for laser frequency stabilization, relies on detecting the error signal generated by phase-modulating the laser light and demodulating the reflected signal from a high-finesse cavity. Similarly, the Pound–Drever–Hall method employs sideband modulation and heterodyne detection to generate a feedback signal proportional to the detuning between the laser frequency and the cavity resonance. Both methods require a cavity with a high quality factor and minimal resonance frequency fluctuations to achieve sub-Hertz linewidths and long-term stability. Resonance frequencies in these systems are determined by the optical path length and refractive index of the cavity medium, making them sensitive to temperature drift, mechanical strain, and optical absorption-induced heating.

- The quality factor, or Q-factor, of a resonator is defined as the ratio of the resonant frequency to the full width at half maximum of its resonance peak, and it directly governs the system’s ability to discriminate small frequency shifts. However, even with high Q-factors, the intrinsic stability of the cavity is ultimately limited by thermal noise sources, including thermorefractive noise arising from fluctuations in the refractive index due to local temperature variations, thermomechanical noise resulting from thermal expansion of the substrate, and photothermal noise induced by absorption of circulating optical power. These noise mechanisms are particularly detrimental in small-mode-volume cavities, where energy density is high and thermal gradients are pronounced. The total root-mean-square frequency fluctuation in such systems scales inversely with the square root of the mode volume, meaning that increasing the physical size of the cavity while maintaining low optical loss is a powerful strategy for noise suppression.

- Previous attempts to scale cavity length on-chip have been constrained by fabrication limitations, waveguide scattering losses, and coupling inefficiencies. While fiber-based delay lines have achieved remarkable stability by exploiting meter-scale path lengths, their integration into photonic integrated circuits remains impractical due to bulkiness and coupling losses. The prior art has not successfully demonstrated a monolithic, chip-integrated optical reference cavity with a round-trip path length exceeding one meter while simultaneously achieving a Q-factor above 10⁸ and an RMS resonance frequency fluctuation below 10⁻¹² over integration times relevant to servo control. Furthermore, no prior system has combined low-loss waveguide morphology, spiral geometry, and adiabatic coupling to simultaneously suppress thermorefractive, photothermal, and thermomechanical noise across a broad frequency range.

## SUMMARY

- The present invention introduces an optical apparatus comprising a waveguide substrate upon which a closed-loop optical reference cavity is integrally formed, wherein the cavity has a round-trip optical path length greater than one meter and a quality factor exceeding one hundred million, enabling unprecedented frequency stability for on-chip laser stabilization. The apparatus further includes a laser source coupled to the cavity via a second optical waveguide, and a feedback mechanism that generates an error signal based on the interaction between the laser and the cavity resonance, thereby enabling active stabilization of the laser frequency. The optical reference cavity is designed to minimize thermal noise by maximizing mode volume while maintaining ultra-low optical loss, resulting in a reduction of thermorefractive and photothermal fluctuations by orders of magnitude compared to prior microresonators. The method of using this apparatus involves coupling a portion of the laser output into the cavity, detecting the transmitted or reflected optical signal, generating an error signal proportional to the detuning between the laser and the cavity resonance, and applying feedback to adjust the laser’s operating parameters to maintain resonance. The objects and advantages of this invention include the ability to achieve sub-hundred-hertz laser linewidths on a chip-scale platform, eliminate the need for bulky fiber delays or vacuum enclosures, and enable high-coherence optical systems in compact, scalable, and environmentally robust form factors. This summary is provided for the convenience of the reader and is not intended to limit the scope of the claims, which define the full extent of the invention. The invention represents a fundamental advancement in integrated photonics by enabling optical reference cavities to achieve performance previously attainable only in laboratory-scale systems.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF EMBODIMENTS

- The optical apparatus of the invention comprises a waveguide substrate fabricated from a dielectric material such as silicon dioxide or silicon nitride, upon which a closed-loop optical reference cavity is patterned using lithographic and etching techniques. The optical waveguide is formed within a thin film of low-loss dielectric material, which is deposited or grown atop the substrate, and is shaped into a continuous, unbroken loop that guides light via total internal reflection. The closed-loop optical reference cavity is characterized by a Q-factor greater than 10⁸ and an RMS resonance frequency fluctuation less than 10⁻¹² over integration times ranging from microseconds to seconds, achieved through a combination of extended path length, low optical loss, and optimized mode confinement. The performance characteristics of the cavity are defined by its ability to maintain resonance frequency stability despite ambient temperature variations, mechanical vibrations, and optical power fluctuations, with the RMS fluctuation being substantially lower than that observed in prior microcavities of comparable footprint.

- The substrate material may be selected from a group consisting of silicon, fused silica, or sapphire, and the waveguide morphology may be configured as a spiral, racetrack, or serpentine geometry to maximize path length within a compact area. The optical reference cavity may occupy an area less than five square centimeters while achieving a round-trip path length exceeding one meter, enabling integration into standard photonic integrated circuit packages. The center frequency noise is reduced by increasing the physical length of the cavity, which dilutes the energy density of the optical mode and reduces the magnitude of thermorefractive and photothermal fluctuations. More stringent performance parameters include a Q-factor exceeding 1.5 × 10⁸, an optical loss per meter below 0.05 dB/m, and an RMS frequency fluctuation below 5 × 10⁻¹³ at integration times of 400 microseconds. Low-loss waveguide morphologies are achieved through smooth sidewall profiles, minimal surface roughness, and precise control of cross-sectional dimensions during fabrication.

- The fabrication of the optical waveguide involves depositing a layer of silicon nitride or silicon dioxide on a silicon substrate, followed by electron-beam lithography and dry etching to define the waveguide pattern. The transverse cross section of the waveguide is rectangular or trapezoidal, with a height between 300 and 800 nanometers and a width between 500 and 1200 nanometers, optimized to support a single transverse mode while minimizing scattering losses. The dielectric material forming the waveguide may be surrounded by an ambient medium such as air, nitrogen, or a low-index polymer, and the substrate may be composed of silicon with a buried oxide layer to provide optical confinement. The on-chip optical reference cavity is formed without the use of mirrors or external reflectors, relying entirely on total internal reflection along the waveguide boundary. The area occupied by the cavity is less than 5.4 cm², enabling compatibility with standard semiconductor packaging and wafer-scale fabrication processes.

- The linked spiral waveguide design comprises two interleaved spirals connected by adiabatic S-turn couplers at the center, which suppress higher-order transverse modes and enhance coupling efficiency. A laser source, such as a distributed feedback laser or a fiber laser, is integrated onto the same substrate or coupled via a fiber taper, and emits light into a second optical waveguide that evanescently couples to the reference cavity. The feedback mechanism detects the transmitted or reflected optical power from the cavity, demodulates the signal using a phase-sensitive detector, and generates an error signal that is fed back to control the laser’s injection current or temperature, thereby stabilizing the laser frequency to the cavity resonance. The second optical waveguide is formed in the same dielectric layer as the reference cavity and is positioned within a coupling region where the evanescent field overlap is maximized. The laser source may be monolithically integrated using III-V materials or externally coupled using polarization-maintaining fiber tapers. The method of employing the optical reference cavity involves operating the laser source at a wavelength near the cavity resonance, coupling a portion of the optical signal into the cavity, generating an error signal proportional to the detuning, and adjusting the laser’s operating parameters to minimize the error signal, thereby locking the laser frequency to the cavity resonance with sub-Hertz precision.

### EXAMPLE 1

- The optical apparatus of Example 1 comprises a waveguide substrate fabricated from silicon dioxide on a silicon handle wafer, upon which a closed-loop optical reference cavity is defined in a silicon nitride layer with a thickness of 500 nanometers. The cavity has a round-trip path length of 120 centimeters and a Q-factor of 140 million, with an RMS resonance frequency fluctuation of 8 × 10⁻¹³ at a gate time of 400 microseconds. The waveguide has a rectangular cross section with a width of 800 nanometers and a height of 500 nanometers, and is surrounded by air as the ambient medium. The cavity is coupled to a fiber taper for input and output, and the system is operated at a wavelength of 1550 nanometers.
