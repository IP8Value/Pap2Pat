# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

### 1. Field

The present invention resides in the field of high-resolution optical imaging systems, particularly those requiring rapid, aberration-free axial re-focusing without mechanical movement of the objective or sample. This technology is critically applicable to live-cell microscopy, intravital imaging, neuroscience, and developmental biology, where dynamic processes occur on millisecond to microsecond timescales and demand volumetric data acquisition at high spatial fidelity. The invention specifically addresses the longstanding limitation of conventional axial scanning methods that rely on mechanical actuation or wavefront-modifying elements incapable of simultaneously achieving high scan rates, large axial ranges, and diffraction-limited resolution across the entire imaging volume.

### 2. Discussion of Related Art

Traditional optical microscopes rely on physically moving the objective lens or the specimen along the axial direction to change the focal plane. This approach is fundamentally constrained by the inertia of the components involved, resulting in scan rates that are too slow to capture fast biological dynamics such as neuronal spiking, vesicular trafficking, or cardiac contractions. While alternative methods such as electrically tuneable lenses (ETLs) and tuneable acoustic gradient index of refraction (TAG) lenses have been introduced to eliminate mechanical motion, they suffer from an inherent inability to correct higher-order aberrations beyond defocus. These devices approximate only a quadratic phase function, which introduces spherical aberrations as the focal plane shifts away from the nominal position, degrading resolution and contrast in thick or heterogeneous samples. Deformable mirrors offer greater wavefront control but are limited by a trade-off between actuator stroke and bandwidth: high-speed devices lack sufficient stroke for meaningful axial ranges, while those with large stroke operate at frequencies too low for live imaging. Remote focusing techniques that move a mirror in the conjugate plane of the specimen have demonstrated aberration-free performance but remain restricted by the mechanical limitations of the actuator, typically achieving no more than 1 kHz scan rates. More recently, multiplexed laser pulse techniques and reverberation loops have been proposed to achieve nanosecond-scale refocusing, yet these methods are severely limited in the number of resolvable focal planes—typically fewer than ten—due to pulse repetition constraints and fluorescence lifetime decay. Furthermore, they produce exponentially decaying intensity profiles across focal planes, rendering them unsuitable for quantitative imaging or uniform illumination. Projection imaging and spatial encoding of Z-position have been adopted as workarounds, but these sacrifice volumetric information or require sparse samples, respectively. Consequently, there exists a critical unmet need for a high-speed, aberration-free axial scanning technology capable of operating at multi-kilohertz frequencies over clinically and biologically relevant axial ranges without compromising spatial resolution or signal uniformity.

## SUMMARY

The present invention introduces a novel optical imaging system that transforms lateral scanning motion into precise, aberration-free axial re-focusing by leveraging the conjugate relationship between the remote-focusing arm and the imaging objective. This system eliminates the need for axial actuation of any optical component by instead scanning a laser beam laterally across a specially configured stationary mirror placed at the focal plane of a remote objective. The lateral displacement of the beam induces a controlled variation in the angle of incidence on the mirror, thereby generating a corresponding axial shift in the focused spot within the sample space. Crucially, because the mirror remains stationary and the lateral scan is fully compensated on the return path via a galvanometric scanner conjugate to the back focal plane of both objectives, the resulting motion is purely axial, with no lateral drift. This configuration enables diffraction-limited performance across the entire axial scan range, preserving resolution and contrast even at extreme defocus positions. The invention is applicable to both axially swept light-sheet microscopy and raster-scanning two-photon microscopy, where it enables volumetric imaging at frame rates previously unattainable. Two distinct embodiments are disclosed: a step mirror configuration that enables discrete, large-step axial jumps with arbitrary step sizes, and a tilted planar mirror configuration that permits continuous, fine-grained axial scanning over a limited range. Both configurations maintain aberration-free focus by ensuring pupil matching between the remote and imaging objectives. The system further extends to two-dimensional lateral scanning architectures, enabling multi-axis axial control. The invention achieves axial scan rates exceeding 12 kHz using resonant galvanometric mirrors, surpassing all prior art in speed while maintaining sub-500-nm resolution. The method is compatible with existing high-speed scanning technologies, including acousto-optic deflectors, and can be scaled to MHz-range axial modulation with no fundamental physical limitations.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The invention comprises an optical imaging system designed for high-speed, aberration-free axial re-focusing through the transformation of lateral scanning motion into axial displacement. Two exemplary configurations are described: a step mirror embodiment and a tilted planar mirror embodiment, each enabling distinct modes of axial control while preserving optical fidelity. In the step mirror configuration, a mirror with discrete, vertically offset planar surfaces is positioned at the focal plane of a remote objective. When a collimated laser beam is scanned laterally across these steps by a galvanometric mirror conjugate to the back focal plane of the remote objective, each step corresponds to a unique axial position in the sample plane. The returning beam is de-scanned by the same galvanometric mirror, ensuring that no lateral displacement is imparted to the final focus. This results in a series of discrete, diffraction-limited foci along the axial axis, with step sizes determined by the height difference between mirror surfaces. In the tilted planar mirror configuration, a single planar mirror is oriented at a small angle relative to the image plane, and the incoming laser focus is aligned normal to the mirror surface by laterally offsetting the remote objective. As the laser spot scans laterally across this tilted surface, the point of incidence moves along the mirror’s normal direction, effectively changing the distance between the mirror and the focal point of the remote objective. This generates a continuous, linear variation in axial focus position. The return path is again de-scanned, yielding pure axial motion. Numerical simulations and experimental measurements confirm that both configurations maintain aberration-free performance over their respective scan ranges, with point spread functions closely matching the theoretical diffraction limit. The step mirror allows for arbitrarily large axial jumps, limited only by the field of view of the remote scanning system and the physical number of steps that can be fabricated. The tilted mirror enables continuous scanning with step sizes limited only by the angular resolution of the lateral scanner, offering finer axial control but over a more restricted range. The axial scan range of the tilted mirror scales with the product of the field of view and the tangent of the tilt angle, favoring objectives with wide fields and high numerical apertures. Experimental results demonstrate axial scan ranges of up to 55 µm with sub-400-nm lateral resolution and axial resolutions below 2.1 µm in two-photon mode. When integrated into axially swept light-sheet microscopy, the system achieves a 20-fold increase in frame rate compared to prior art, enabling volumetric imaging of intracellular dynamics at 3.5 Hz with isotropic ~400-nm resolution. In two-photon raster scanning, the system achieves 12 kHz axial scan rates, permitting volumetric imaging of fixed brain tissue and live zebrafish heart dynamics at up to 156 Hz. The system is compatible with standard imaging components, including water immersion objectives, tube lenses, sCMOS cameras, and photomultiplier tubes, and requires no synchronized laser modulation or complex phase correction algorithms. The step mirror is fabricated using UV lithography on silicon wafers with SU-8 photoresist and metallized with platinum to ensure high reflectivity and durability under high-power laser illumination. The tilted mirror is constructed from protected gold-coated substrates to withstand high peak intensities in multiphoton applications. The system’s operation is fully compatible with existing microscope platforms and can be retrofitted into commercial two-photon or light-sheet systems with minimal modification. The optical train includes a collimated laser source, a polarizing beam splitter, a galvanometric scanning mirror, a remote objective, the step or tilted mirror, a 4F relay system, a water immersion imaging objective, a quarter-wave plate, a detection objective, and a camera or photomultiplier tube. The alignment of the system ensures that the back focal plane of the remote objective is conjugate to that of the imaging objective, enabling aberration compensation. The mirror is positioned such that its surface lies precisely at the focal plane of the remote objective, and the galvanometric scanner is calibrated to ensure that the lateral scan is fully compensated upon return. The system’s performance is validated through point spread function measurements, transmission imaging of laser foci, and fluorescence imaging of biological samples including RPE cells, MV3 cells expressing GEMs, fixed Thy1-GFP mouse brain tissue, and live zebrafish embryos. The step mirror configuration is particularly suited for applications requiring discrete axial sampling, such as multi-plane calcium imaging or structured illumination, while the tilted mirror configuration excels in continuous axial line scans, such as volumetric imaging of beating organs or fast neuronal activity. Both configurations enable the acquisition of high-resolution volumetric data at speeds orders of magnitude faster than conventional Z-stepping or ETL-based methods, without sacrificing resolution or signal uniformity.

### Experimental Setup

The imaging system is constructed using a 488 nm continuous-wave diode-pumped solid-state laser for light-sheet applications and a Ti:sapphire laser tuned to 920 nm for two-photon applications. The laser beam is spatially filtered using a pinhole and expanded by a Galilean telescope to match the input aperture of the remote-focusing arm. A half-wave plate is used to control polarization and laser power. The remote-focusing arm consists of a 60× 0.7 NA air objective for light-sheet microscopy and a 20× 0.8 NA air objective for two-photon microscopy, both equipped with 170-µm-thick coverslips to maintain optical performance. The beam is directed through two telecentric tube lenses to form a 4F relay system, ensuring pupil conjugation. A quarter-wave plate is inserted to convert linear polarization to circular polarization, minimizing polarization-dependent losses upon reflection. A galvanometric scanner with a resonant frequency of 12 kHz is used to laterally scan the beam across the step or tilted mirror. The mirror is mounted on a kinematic stage and aligned such that its surface lies precisely at the focal plane of the remote objective. For the step mirror, five micrometers of SU-8 3005 photoresist is spin-coated onto a silicon wafer, soft-baked, exposed through a microfluidic mask, post-baked, recoated, and re-exposed with a lateral offset to generate a staircase of steps with heights of 5–7 µm. The wafer is then sputter-coated with 100 nm of platinum to render it highly reflective. For the tilted mirror, a protected gold-coated substrate is mounted at an angle of 5–7.5° relative to the image plane, and the remote objective is laterally translated to ensure the incident beam is normal to the mirror surface. The illumination arm contains a water immersion objective with a numerical aperture of 0.8 or 1.05, depending on the application, and is matched to the remote objective via relay lenses to achieve a demagnification factor of 1.33–1.35. A custom sample chamber holds the specimen in aqueous medium and is mounted on a piezo stage for coarse axial positioning. The detection arm includes a second water immersion objective, a tube lens, a longpass filter to block excitation light, and a scientific CMOS camera or photomultiplier tube for fluorescence detection. Neutral density filters attenuate the laser intensity to prevent photodamage. A piezo stage is used to acquire transmission datasets for point spread function measurements by stepping the detection objective in 160-nm increments. The entire system is controlled via ScanImage software, with the galvanometric scanner driven by a triangular or sinusoidal waveform to generate continuous or discrete axial scans. The system’s performance is quantified through axial point spread function measurements, volumetric imaging of fluorescent beads, and live-cell imaging of genetically labeled cells and zebrafish embryos. The step mirror enables three discrete axial foci with equal intensity when the scan amplitude is adjusted to balance dwell time, while the tilted mirror produces a continuous axial line scan with homogeneous intensity over its linear region. The system achieves axial scan ranges of 43 µm with the tilted mirror and up to 55 µm with optimized optics, with lateral resolutions of 380–440 nm and axial resolutions of 1.7–2.1 µm. The system’s ability to perform aberration-free remote focusing is confirmed by comparing point spread functions obtained via mechanical Z-stepping with those obtained via the new method, showing no statistically significant difference in full width at half maximum across the entire scan range. The system’s compatibility with resonant scanning is demonstrated by achieving 12 kHz axial scan rates without signal degradation, and its utility in biological imaging is validated through volumetric imaging of GEMs in MV3 cells, dendritic spines in Thy1-GFP mouse brain tissue, and cardiac dynamics in zebrafish embryos at frame rates up to 156 Hz.
