# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates to novel antibiotic derivatives and synergistic combinations thereof, specifically designed to target the bacterial ribosome through simultaneous engagement of the peptidyl transferase center and the nascent peptide exit tunnel. These compounds are derived from naturally occurring lankacidin and lankamycin scaffolds, as well as structurally related macrolide and streptogramin antibiotics, and are engineered to enhance binding affinity, reduce bacterial resistance, and improve pharmacokinetic properties. The invention encompasses chemically modified derivatives of lankacidin C and lankamycin, chimeric molecules combining structural elements from distinct antibiotic classes, and covalently conjugated bivalent compounds that bridge the two ribosomal binding sites. These innovations are intended for use in the prevention and treatment of bacterial infections caused by Gram-positive pathogens, including multidrug-resistant strains such as methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus and vancomycin-resistant enterococci.

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

Ribosomal interfering antibiotics represent a cornerstone of modern antimicrobial therapy, with many clinically utilized agents acting by binding to functional regions of the bacterial ribosome and disrupting protein synthesis. The large ribosomal subunit contains two principal antibiotic binding pockets: the peptidyl transferase center, where peptide bond formation occurs, and the adjacent nascent peptide exit tunnel, which guides the emerging polypeptide chain away from the catalytic site. Antibiotics such as chloramphenicol, clindamycin, pleuromutilins, and oxazolidinones bind to the peptidyl transferase center and inhibit the catalytic activity required for elongation, while macrolides, ketolides, and streptogramin B compounds bind to the upper segment of the exit tunnel and sterically block progression of the nascent chain. The clinical utility of these agents is often limited by the emergence of resistance mechanisms, including ribosomal methylation, efflux pumps, and mutations in rRNA or ribosomal proteins. Synergistic combinations of antibiotics that target two distinct but spatially proximal sites on the ribosome have demonstrated enhanced bactericidal activity and reduced propensity for resistance development. The streptogramin class exemplifies this principle, wherein type A and type B components—dalfopristin and quinupristin, respectively—bind to the peptidyl transferase center and nascent peptide exit tunnel, respectively, and together produce a synergistic inhibition of translation that is significantly greater than the sum of their individual effects. The semisynthetic formulation Synercid, composed of these two components, is approved for the treatment of serious Gram-positive infections. Lankacidin C and lankamycin are naturally co-produced by Streptomyces rochei and exhibit structural features distinct from streptogramins, yet both compounds bind to the same two ribosomal sites occupied by streptogramin A and B components. Lankacidin C, a 17-membered carbocyclic macrocycle, binds within the peptidyl transferase center and interferes with aminoacyl-tRNA accommodation and peptide bond formation. Lankamycin, a 14-membered macrolide bearing two sugar moieties, binds to the nascent peptide exit tunnel in a manner analogous to erythromycin but with distinct conformational effects on rRNA residues. Despite their co-regulated biosynthesis and overlapping binding regions, no prior evidence established their functional synergy or simultaneous ribosomal occupancy. Crystallographic studies of the 50S ribosomal subunit complexed with lankacidin C revealed a unique network of interactions involving U2506, G2505, C2610, and G2576, resulting in a reorientation of key nucleotides critical for tRNA positioning. Structural analysis of lankamycin binding demonstrated protection of nucleotides A2058, A2059, and U2609, consistent with macrolide binding, yet with reduced affinity compared to erythromycin due to differences in sugar composition and steric constraints. Biochemical assays confirmed that lankacidin C and lankamycin can bind simultaneously to the ribosome without competitive displacement, and that their combined presence results in a synergistic inhibition of protein synthesis in both cell-free systems and intact bacterial cells. This natural synergy, previously unrecognized, provides a structural blueprint for the rational design of next-generation antibiotics that exploit dual-site ribosomal inhibition. Previous patent disclosures, including WO 03/026562, US 2009/0042186, U.S. Pat. No. 7,606,670, and U.S. Pat. No. 7,169,756, describe modifications to streptogramin and macrolide scaffolds but do not address the structural and functional potential of lankacidin-lankamycin pairing or its optimization through targeted chemical derivatization.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention introduces a new class of improved antibiotic derivatives and synergistic combinations thereof, specifically engineered to enhance the binding affinity, potency, and pharmacological profile of lankacidin C and lankamycin through rational structural modification informed by high-resolution ribosomal crystallography. These derivatives are designed to simultaneously engage the peptidyl transferase center and the nascent peptide exit tunnel, thereby achieving a synergistic antibacterial effect that exceeds the additive activity of either component alone. The invention encompasses lankacidin C derivatives modified at the macrocyclic ring, lactone bridge, or side-chain functional groups to strengthen interactions with nucleotides G2505, U2506, and C2610, as well as lankamycin derivatives altered in the C-15 position, desoamine sugar, or arcanosyl moiety to enhance hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic contacts with A2058, A2059, and U2609. Specific embodiments include a deoxy-lankamycin derivative lacking the 3-O-arcanosyl group, a desoamine-lankamycin derivative with a modified amino sugar moiety, a C-15 lankamycin derivative bearing an extended alkyl chain, and a 3-O-arcanosyl lankamycin derivative with an optimized glycosidic linkage. The invention further includes synergistic combinations of lankacidin C or its derivatives with type B streptogramins such as virginiamycin S, as well as combinations of lankamycin or its derivatives with type A streptogramins such as virginiamycin M. Additional embodiments comprise lankacidin C paired with macrolide or ketolide antibiotics, or with chloramphenicol analogs, to achieve dual-site inhibition. The invention also encompasses chimeric molecules comprising a lankacidin-derived portion covalently linked to a lankamycin-derived portion, thereby creating a single molecular entity capable of bridging the peptidyl transferase center and the nascent peptide exit tunnel. Covalently conjugated molecules combining lankacidin and lankamycin scaffolds through stable linkers are also disclosed, enabling enhanced ribosomal avidity and reduced dissociation rates. These derivatives and conjugates exhibit improved properties compared to their parent molecules, including enhanced biological activity, reduced toxicity, increased membrane permeability, improved bioavailability, and diminished susceptibility to bacterial resistance mechanisms. The invention further includes macrolide derivatives incorporating positively charged moieties to strengthen electrostatic interactions with rRNA phosphate backbones, and modifications to the parent antibiotic structure that reinforce ribosomal binding through additional hydrogen bonds or van der Waals contacts. The invention also provides methods for producing such derivatives, chimeric molecules, and conjugated compounds through synthetic, semi-synthetic, and fermentation-based approaches. The pharmaceutical compositions of the invention are intended for the prevention and treatment of bacterial infections, particularly those caused by multidrug-resistant Gram-positive pathogens, and are formulated for oral, parenteral, transmucosal, or topical administration.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

Lankacidin C and lankamycin are naturally occurring antibiotics produced by Streptomyces rochei that act as protein synthesis inhibitors by binding to distinct but adjacent sites on the bacterial 50S ribosomal subunit. Lankacidin C, a 17-membered carbocyclic macrocycle with a bridged lactone ring, binds within the peptidyl transferase center, where it induces a conformational rearrangement of key rRNA nucleotides including U2506, G2505, G2581, C2610, and G2576. These rearrangements stabilize a binding pocket that accommodates the macrocyclic core of lankacidin C through an extensive network of hydrogen bonds and van der Waals interactions, with the 2-methyl group of the lactone ring inserting into a hydrophobic cleft formed by A2451 and C2452. This binding mode partially overlaps with that of streptogramin A antibiotics such as dalfopristin and virginiamycin M, yet differs in the extent of rRNA repositioning, particularly the 45-degree rotation of A2602 and the unique shift of U2506 toward the drug. Lankamycin, a 14-membered macrolide decorated with 4-acetyl-L-arcanose and D-chalcose sugars, binds to the upper segment of the nascent peptide exit tunnel, where it protects nucleotides A2058, A2059, and U2609 from chemical modification, consistent with macrolide binding. However, unlike erythromycin, lankamycin exhibits weaker binding affinity, likely due to the absence of a fully optimized desosamine sugar and steric constraints imposed by its arcanosyl moiety. Despite this reduced potency, biochemical and structural evidence demonstrates that lankacidin C and lankamycin can bind simultaneously to the ribosome without mutual displacement, as shown by RNA probing experiments in which protection of PTC residues by lankacidin C and NPET residues by lankamycin occurred concurrently. This simultaneous binding results in a synergistic inhibition of protein synthesis, as confirmed by checkerboard MIC assays in Staphylococcus aureus and cell-free translation systems, where the combination of the two compounds reduced the minimal inhibitory concentration by more than tenfold compared to either agent alone. The evolutionary co-regulation of lankacidin C and lankamycin biosynthesis suggests that their synergistic action has been selected for in nature, providing a compelling rationale for the development of optimized derivatives. Structural analysis reveals that minor modifications to the lankamycin scaffold—such as replacement of the arcanosyl group with a more flexible or charged substituent, elongation of the C-15 side chain, or introduction of a positively charged amine at the desoamine position—can enhance ribosomal binding affinity and stabilize the interaction with adjacent lankacidin C molecules. Similarly, modifications to lankacidin C that extend its macrocyclic ring or introduce hydrogen-bonding groups at the lactone bridge can strengthen interactions with second-shell nucleotides such as A2062 and C2530, thereby increasing the cooperativity of the pair. The invention further encompasses chimeric molecules in which a lankacidin-derived pharmacophore is covalently linked via a flexible spacer to a lankamycin-derived pharmacophore, enabling a single molecule to simultaneously occupy both binding sites and achieve ultra-high avidity. Such conjugates exhibit prolonged ribosomal residence time, reduced efflux, and diminished susceptibility to enzymatic degradation. The invention also includes pharmaceutical compositions comprising two isolated antibiotic molecules—one targeting the peptidyl transferase center and the other targeting the nascent peptide exit tunnel—wherein the first molecule is selected from lankacidin C or a derivative thereof, and the second molecule is selected from lankamycin or a derivative thereof, or from a type B streptogramin, a ketolide, or a macrolide. These combinations are formulated to maintain optimal molar ratios for synergistic activity, typically ranging from 1:1 to 1:10, depending on the specific derivatives employed.

### Lankacidin Binding Site

Lankacidin C binds within a shallow depression on the wall of the peptidyl transferase center, adjacent to the A-site tRNA binding pocket. Its macrocyclic ring adopts a conformation stabilized by stacking interactions with G2505 and U2506, while its lactone carbonyl oxygen forms a hydrogen bond with the N2 amino group of G2061. The 2-methyl substituent on the lactone edge inserts into a hydrophobic crevice formed by the splayed-out bases of A2451 and C2452, a region also occupied by the aminoacyl moiety of A-site tRNA and by other PTC-targeting antibiotics such as chloramphenicol, clindamycin, and dalfopristin. The binding of lankacidin C induces a significant reorientation of the rRNA backbone, resulting in the formation of a unique hydrogen-bonding network between G2505, U2506, C2610, G2581, and G2576. In this network, G2576 stacks upon G2505, and the exocyclic amine of G2505 reaches hydrogen bonding distance to the O2 atom of C2610, forming a non-canonical CG rS* base pair. The amino group of G2502 also forms a hydrogen bond with the O4 atom of U2506, which shifts toward the drug by approximately 1.5 Å. These interactions are reinforced by van der Waals contacts with second-shell nucleotides including A2062, C2530, U2531, C2507, U2584, and A2059. The conformational flexibility of the macrocyclic ring is critical for binding, as hydrogenation of the ring system reduces inhibitory activity by altering the ring’s ability to adopt the optimal binding pose. The binding site of lankacidin C overlaps substantially with that of dalfopristin, yet differs in the extent of U2585 and A2602 repositioning, which are more dramatically altered in the presence of dalfopristin due to steric bulk. The competition between lankacidin C and chloramphenicol for ribosomal binding is explained by their shared occupancy of the hydrophobic cleft formed by A2451 and C2452, as well as overlapping interactions with U2506 and G2505. Structural analysis confirms that lankacidin C does not directly contact the P-site tRNA, but its binding alters the positioning of the peptidyl-tRNA 3′-end, thereby interfering with nucleophilic attack during peptide bond formation.

### Lankamycin Binding Site

Lankamycin binds to the upper segment of the nascent peptide exit tunnel, where its 14-membered macrolide ring lies parallel to the tunnel wall, with its desosamine sugar extending toward the PTC and its arcanosyl moiety oriented toward the tunnel exit. Hydrogen bond contacts are formed between the 2′-hydroxyl of the desosamine sugar and the N1 of A2058, and between the 3′-hydroxyl of the arcanosyl group and the phosphate oxygen of U2609. Hydrophobic interactions occur between the macrolide ring and nucleotides A2059, G2505, and C2610, while the C-15 methyl group inserts into a small pocket formed by A2062 and U2586. The binding of lankamycin induces a subtle rearrangement of C2610, which shifts slightly to accommodate the arcanosyl group, and causes a partial displacement of A2062, reducing its stacking interaction with G2505. Compared to erythromycin, lankamycin exhibits weaker binding affinity, as evidenced by an IC50 value of 275 µM in cell-free translation assays versus 12 µM for erythromycin. This reduced potency is attributed to the presence of the bulky 4-acetyl-L-arcanose sugar, which sterically hinders optimal positioning of the desosamine moiety and limits the number of hydrogen bonds formed with the rRNA. The crystallographic structure of the lankamycin–50S complex reveals that the arcanosyl group does not form direct hydrogen bonds with the ribosome, unlike the desosamine sugar in erythromycin, which engages in multiple polar contacts with A2058 and G2505. The reduced affinity of lankamycin is compensated by its ability to bind simultaneously with lankacidin C, as demonstrated by RNA probing experiments showing that lankamycin does not displace lankacidin C from the PTC and vice versa. The IC50 of lankamycin is significantly reduced in the presence of lankacidin C, indicating cooperative binding and synergistic inhibition.

### Lankacidin and Lankamycin Can Simultaneously Bind to the Ribosome

The simultaneous binding of lankacidin C and lankamycin to the bacterial ribosome was demonstrated through a series of RNA probing experiments using dimethyl sulfate and CMCT as chemical modifiers of 23S rRNA. In the presence of lankacidin C, nucleotides U2506 and U2585 were strongly protected from CMCT modification, while A2059 showed partial protection from DMS modification, consistent with its stacking against A2503. In the presence of lankamycin, nucleotides A2058, A2059, and U2609 were protected from DMS and CMCT modification, respectively, mirroring the pattern observed with erythromycin. When both antibiotics were present together, protection was observed for all four nucleotides—U2506, U2585, A2058, and U2609—indicating that neither compound displaces the other. In contrast, erythromycin competed with lankacidin C for binding, as evidenced by partial reversal of U2506 protection in the presence of erythromycin. This differential behavior confirms that lankamycin, despite its structural similarity to erythromycin, binds in a distinct orientation that avoids steric clash with lankacidin C. The spatial separation between the two binding sites allows for simultaneous occupancy, with the distance between the closest atoms of lankacidin C and lankamycin exceeding 8 Å, sufficient to prevent direct interference. The ability of these two compounds to bind concurrently suggests that their biosynthetic pathways have been evolutionarily optimized to exploit dual-site inhibition, a mechanism previously observed only in streptogramin combinations.

### LC and LM Act Synergistically Upon Bacterial Cells

The synergistic antibacterial effect of lankacidin C and lankamycin was demonstrated both in vitro and in vivo using a checkerboard microdilution assay against Staphylococcus aureus and a cell-free transcription-translation system. The fractional inhibitory concentration index was calculated for various molar ratios of the two compounds, and a concave-shaped isobologram was observed, indicating strong synergy with a fractional inhibitory concentration index of less than 0.5. In the cell-free system, the IC50 of lankacidin C decreased from 1.5 µM to 0.2 µM in the presence of a sub-inhibitory concentration of lankamycin, while the IC50 of lankamycin decreased from 275 µM to 35 µM under the same conditions. This synergistic effect was confirmed using a GFP-based reporter assay in which fluorescence intensity was measured after 60 minutes of translation in the presence of serial dilutions of each compound alone or in combination. The combination produced a 12-fold greater reduction in fluorescence than the additive effect of the individual compounds. The mechanism of synergy is attributed to the simultaneous occupation of the peptidyl transferase center and the nascent peptide exit tunnel, which together impede both the formation of peptide bonds and the progression of the nascent chain, effectively halting translation at two sequential steps. This dual inhibition is more effective than inhibition at either site alone, as the ribosome cannot compensate for the loss of one function while the other remains impaired. The synergy observed between lankacidin C and lankamycin is comparable in magnitude to that of the streptogramin combination Synercid, yet is achieved through structurally distinct molecules, offering a novel platform for antibiotic development.

## Materials and Methods

### Crystallography

Crystals of the 50S ribosomal subunit from Deinococcus radiodurans were grown as previously described and soaked in solutions containing 0.025 mM lankacidin C or lankamycin for 20 hours at 20°C. Crystals were cryoprotected and flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen. X-ray diffraction data were collected at 85–100 K using synchrotron radiation at beamlines 19ID at the Advanced Photon Source and ID23-2 at the European Synchrotron Radiation Facility, with wavelengths of 1.0 Å and 0.837 Å, respectively. Data were processed using HKL2000, and initial phases were obtained by molecular replacement using the native 50S structure as a model. Difference Fourier maps were calculated to identify electron density corresponding to the bound antibiotics, and models were built using Coot and refined with CNS and phenix.refine. Ribosome–antibiotic interactions were analyzed using LigPlot+ and LPC, and structural images were generated using PyMOL. Coordinates have been deposited in the Protein Data Bank under accession code 3JQ4.

### Antibiotic Binding

Binding of lankacidin C and lankamycin to the 50S subunit was assessed by incubating purified ribosomes with radiolabeled compounds at concentrations ranging from 0.01 to 10 µM for 15 minutes at 37°C. Ribosome–antibiotic complexes were separated from unbound drug by gel filtration through Bio-Gel P30 spin columns, and radioactivity was quantified by liquid scintillation counting. Data were analyzed using Prism 4 software to determine dissociation constants and binding stoichiometries.

### Inhibition of Cell-Free Protein Synthesis by LC and LM

The E. coli cell-free transcription-translation system (Promega) was preincubated with varying concentrations of lankacidin C or lankamycin for 5 minutes at 20°C. Reactions were initiated by addition of pBestLuc plasmid DNA and incubated for 40 minutes at 20°C. Firefly luciferase activity was measured using the Bright-Glo Luciferase Assay System, and data were normalized to untreated controls. Synergism was assessed using a checkerboard titration of both compounds, and the fractional inhibitory concentration index was calculated according to the method of O’Neill.

### Inhibition of the Peptidyl Transferase Reaction by LC

Puromycin assays were performed using isolated 50S subunits from Staphylococcus aureus. Ribosomes were preincubated with lankacidin C at concentrations ranging from 0.01 to 10 µM, followed by addition of puromycin to a final concentration of 0.5 mM. Reactions were incubated for 15 minutes at 37°C and stopped by chilling on ice. The extent of puromycin incorporation was quantified by scintillation counting of radiolabeled puromycin, and IC50 values were calculated using nonlinear regression analysis in Prism 4.

### RNA Probing

RNA probing was performed using D. radiodurans 50S subunits at 200 nM concentration. Ribosomes were preincubated with lankacidin C (50 µM), lankamycin (500 µM), or erythromycin (50 µM) for 10 minutes at 37°C, followed by 10 minutes at 20°C. Chemical modification was carried out using CMCT (10 mM) for 10 minutes and DMS (10 mM) for 5 minutes. Reactions were terminated by ethanol precipitation, and reverse transcription was performed using primers complementary to 23S rRNA. Modified nucleotides were identified by primer extension and gel electrophoresis.

### Cell-Free Translation System Used for Detecting Synergism In Vitro

A cell-free extract was prepared from E. coli BL21-DE3 and supplemented with amino acids, NTPs, tRNA, and energy regeneration components. The transcription-translation reaction was initiated by addition of PIVEX.6D plasmid encoding GFP at 1 ng/µL. Serial dilutions of lankacidin C and lankamycin were added to a 96-well plate, followed by immediate addition of the translation mix. After 60 minutes at 37°C, translation was quenched by addition of erythromycin (8 µM). Fluorescence was measured using a TECAN SpectraFluor Plus plate reader with excitation at 485 nm and emission at 535 nm. Data were fitted using least-squares regression in Igor Pro to determine IC50 values and synergy indices.

## Pharmacology

The pharmaceutical compositions of the invention comprise one or more of the disclosed antibiotic derivatives, chimeric molecules, or synergistic combinations in association with a pharmaceutically acceptable carrier. The carrier may be a solid, liquid, or semi-solid formulation suitable for oral, parenteral, transmucosal, or topical administration. Oral formulations may include tablets, capsules, or suspensions containing excipients such as microcrystalline cellulose, lactose, magnesium stearate, or hydroxypropyl methylcellulose. Parenteral formulations may be prepared as sterile aqueous or non-aqueous solutions, emulsions, or suspensions, containing buffers such as phosphate or acetate, tonicity agents such as sodium chloride or dextrose, and preservatives such as benzyl alcohol or methylparaben. Transmucosal formulations may be designed as nasal sprays, buccal films, or rectal suppositories, incorporating penetration enhancers such as chitosan or bile salts. The therapeutically effective amount of each component is determined based on the specific derivative, route of administration, and severity of infection, and typically ranges from 0.1 mg/kg to 50 mg/kg per day, administered in divided doses. Dosage regimens are adjusted based on pharmacokinetic parameters such as half-life, bioavailability, and tissue penetration. Toxicity and therapeutic efficacy are evaluated in animal models of bacterial infection, including murine models of sepsis and pneumonia. The composition may be packaged in a unit-dose dispenser with labeling indicating the synergistic nature of the combination, recommended dosage, and instructions for administration. The invention further includes notices of regulatory agency approval, such as those from the FDA or EMA, and labeling that specifies the use of the composition for the treatment of multidrug-resistant Gram-positive infections.

### Chemical Derivatives and Conjugates

Derivatives of lankacidin C and lankamycin are defined as chemically modified analogs in which functional groups are added, removed, or substituted to enhance binding affinity, solubility, or metabolic stability. These modifications include, but are not limited to, the introduction of halogen atoms, alkyl chains, amino groups, or carbohydrate moieties at positions C-15, C-3, or the desosamine sugar. X-ray crystallographic analysis of ribosome–derivative complexes is used to guide the design of compounds that form additional hydrogen bonds with rRNA nucleotides or fill unoccupied hydrophobic pockets. Desoamine derivatives are synthesized by reductive amination or glycosyltransferase-mediated sugar exchange, and their ribosomal binding is validated by RNA probing and inhibition assays. Conjugated molecules are prepared by covalent linkage of lankacidin and lankamycin scaffolds using stable chemical linkers such as alkyl diamines, polyethylene glycol chains, or triazole rings formed via click chemistry. These conjugates are designed to maintain optimal spatial orientation between the two pharmacophores, ensuring simultaneous engagement of both ribosomal binding sites.

### Synthetic and Semi-Synthetic Methods for Producing Antibiotics

Lankacidin C and lankamycin are produced by fermentation of Streptomyces rochei under controlled conditions using defined media containing carbon sources such as glucose or glycerol, nitrogen sources such as soy peptone, and trace elements. Fermentation broths are extracted with organic solvents such as ethyl acetate or butanol, and the antibiotics are purified by silica gel chromatography or preparative HPLC. Semi-synthetic derivatives are prepared by chemical modification of the purified natural products using standard organic synthesis techniques, including esterification, acylation, alkylation, and glycosylation. For example, lankamycin is treated with acetic anhydride and pyridine to introduce acetyl groups at the 3′-hydroxyl, or with sodium borohydride to reduce the lactone ring. Lankacidin derivatives are synthesized by selective oxidation of the lactone bridge using Dess–Martin periodinane or by enzymatic hydroxylation using cytochrome P450 enzymes. Chimeric and conjugated molecules are assembled using solid-phase synthesis or solution-phase coupling reactions, with purification by preparative HPLC and characterization by mass spectrometry and NMR spectroscopy. The resulting compounds are screened for antibacterial activity, ribosomal binding affinity, and synergy in cell-based and biochemical assays.
