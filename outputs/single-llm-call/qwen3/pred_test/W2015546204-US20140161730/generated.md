# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

- relate to magnetic graphene-like nanoparticles

The present invention relates to novel magnetic compositions comprising graphene-like nanostructures intercalated with paramagnetic metal ions, particularly manganese, and their use as contrast agents for magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). These compositions are characterized by enhanced relaxivity properties that surpass those of conventional gadolinium- or manganese-based chelate complexes currently used in clinical settings. The invention further encompasses the synthesis, characterization, and formulation of such compositions for diagnostic imaging applications, including but not limited to anatomical visualization, lesion detection, and tissue differentiation in vivo. The graphene-like nanostructures employed herein are not limited to pristine graphene but include oxidized, reduced, and functionalized variants derived from graphite or carbon nanotubes, wherein the intercalation of paramagnetic ions occurs during or subsequent to the exfoliation or chemical processing of the parent carbon material. The resulting materials exhibit unique magnetic behaviors, including room-temperature ferromagnetism and superparamagnetism, depending on their structural morphology and degree of reduction, enabling tunable contrast effects in MRI. This invention represents a paradigm shift in contrast agent design by leveraging the physical confinement of paramagnetic ions within two-dimensional carbon lattices rather than relying on molecular chelation, thereby unlocking unprecedented relaxivity enhancements and novel relaxation dynamics.

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

- introduce MRI

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is a non-invasive medical imaging modality that exploits the magnetic properties of atomic nuclei, particularly hydrogen protons in water and fat molecules, to generate high-resolution anatomical and functional images of soft tissues. Unlike X-ray or computed tomography, MRI does not involve ionizing radiation and provides superior contrast between different tissue types based on variations in proton density, relaxation times, and molecular mobility. The clinical utility of MRI is significantly enhanced through the administration of exogenous contrast agents that selectively alter the longitudinal (T1) and transverse (T2) relaxation rates of water protons in their microenvironment, thereby improving the visibility of pathological structures such as tumors, inflammation, vascular abnormalities, and neurological lesions.

- describe MRI contrast agents

Contrast agents for MRI are broadly classified into T1 and T2 agents, which shorten the respective relaxation times of water protons to produce bright (positive) or dark (negative) signal enhancement in the resulting images. T1 agents are typically paramagnetic metal ions that interact with nearby water molecules through inner-sphere, second-sphere, or outer-sphere mechanisms, accelerating proton spin-lattice relaxation. T2 agents, often superparamagnetic or ferromagnetic nanoparticles, induce local magnetic field inhomogeneities that dephase proton spins and reduce transverse relaxation times. The efficacy of these agents is quantified by their relaxivity, defined as the increase in relaxation rate per unit concentration of the agent, expressed in units of mM⁻¹s⁻¹.

- discuss gadolinium-based MRI CAs

Gadolinium(III) chelates, such as gadopentetate dimeglumine and gadobutrol, have been the gold standard for T1 contrast enhancement for over three decades. These complexes are designed to stabilize the highly toxic Gd³⁺ ion while allowing coordinated water molecules to exchange rapidly with bulk water, thereby maximizing relaxivity. However, recent clinical concerns regarding nephrogenic systemic fibrosis in patients with severe renal impairment have led to regulatory restrictions on the use of certain linear Gd³⁺ chelates. Additionally, emerging evidence of gadolinium deposition in the brain and other tissues following repeated administrations has prompted a search for safer alternatives.

- mention manganese as alternative to gadolinium

Manganese(II) has long been recognized as a biologically compatible paramagnetic ion due to its role as an essential cofactor in numerous enzymatic processes and its natural abundance in the human body. Unlike gadolinium, manganese is metabolized and excreted through hepatic and biliary pathways, reducing the risk of long-term tissue retention. Early manganese-based agents, such as Mn-DPDP, demonstrated clinical utility but suffered from low relaxivity and limited tissue specificity. Recent efforts have focused on enhancing the relaxivity of Mn²⁺ through macromolecular conjugation or encapsulation, yet these approaches have not achieved the dramatic improvements predicted by theoretical models.

- describe carbon nanostructures as MRI CAs

In parallel, carbon-based nanostructures—including fullerenes, carbon nanotubes, and nanodiamonds—have been explored as platforms for MRI contrast agents by either covalently attaching metal-chelate complexes or encapsulating paramagnetic ions within their hollow structures. Gadofullerenes and gadonanotubes, in particular, have shown relaxivity values up to two orders of magnitude higher than conventional Gd-chelates, attributed to the spatial confinement of Gd³⁺ ions within the carbon matrix, which alters water exchange dynamics and rotational correlation times. However, the synthesis of these materials remains complex, costly, and difficult to scale, and their long-term biocompatibility and clearance pathways are not fully understood.

- discuss gadofullerenes and gadonanotubes

Gadofullerenes consist of Gd³⁺ ions trapped inside closed-cage C₆₀ molecules, while gadonanotubes involve Gd³⁺ ions intercalated within the interior of single-walled carbon nanotubes. Both systems exhibit unconventional nuclear magnetic resonance dispersion (NMRD) profiles, with relaxivity increasing sharply at low magnetic fields (<10 MHz), a phenomenon not observed in small-molecule chelates. These profiles suggest that the confinement geometry and restricted molecular motion of the paramagnetic ion within the carbon nanostructure fundamentally alter the relaxation mechanism. However, the reliance on expensive, multi-step synthetic routes and the potential for incomplete metal encapsulation have limited their translation into clinical practice.

- describe synthesis strategies for Gd3+-ion carbon nanostructures

Synthetic strategies for Gd³⁺-carbon nanostructures typically involve either post-functionalization of pre-formed carbon scaffolds with Gd-chelating ligands or in-situ encapsulation during nanotube or fullerene synthesis. These methods require stringent purification to remove unbound metal ions and often result in heterogeneous distributions of metal loading. Furthermore, the covalent attachment of chelates to the carbon surface introduces steric hindrance that may limit water access to the paramagnetic center, counteracting the benefits of confinement.

- discuss relaxivity of Gd3+-ion carbon nanostructures

The relaxivity of Gd³⁺-carbon nanostructures is highly dependent on the number of inner-sphere water molecules, the rotational correlation time of the entire complex, and the water residence lifetime—all parameters that are influenced by the geometry and rigidity of the carbon scaffold. While gadonanotubes achieve exceptional relaxivity due to their cylindrical confinement and slow rotational motion, their synthesis is not easily adaptable to other carbon morphologies, and their high aspect ratio complicates systemic delivery and biodistribution.

- mention limitations of Gd3+-ion carbon nanostructures

Despite their impressive relaxivity, Gd³⁺-carbon nanostructures face significant limitations: high production costs, poor batch-to-batch reproducibility, potential toxicity from residual catalysts or solvents, and uncertain long-term biodistribution and clearance. Moreover, the reliance on gadolinium, a rare earth element with geopolitical supply constraints, further diminishes their viability as a scalable solution for global healthcare needs.

- introduce graphene and its potential applications

Graphene, a single-atom-thick sheet of sp²-hybridized carbon atoms arranged in a hexagonal lattice, has emerged as a revolutionary material with applications spanning electronics, energy storage, and biomedicine. Its exceptional mechanical strength, thermal conductivity, and large surface area make it an attractive candidate for drug delivery and imaging platforms. Graphene derivatives, such as graphene oxide and reduced graphene oxide, are readily synthesized in large quantities from inexpensive graphite precursors using scalable oxidative exfoliation methods.

- discuss theoretical studies on magnetic phenomena in graphene

Theoretical studies predict that defects, edges, and dopants in graphene can induce localized magnetic moments, potentially leading to room-temperature ferromagnetism or spin-polarized states. These phenomena arise from unpaired electrons at vacancy sites, oxygen functional groups, or interactions with transition metal impurities. However, experimental validation of intrinsic magnetism in pure graphene has been elusive, with most observed magnetic behavior historically attributed to trace metal contaminants.

- describe experimental studies on graphene oxide and graphene nanoribbons

Recent experimental work has demonstrated that graphene oxide and graphene nanoribbons synthesized via oxidative methods using potassium permanganate contain trace amounts of manganese that become trapped between the carbon layers during exfoliation. These intercalated Mn²⁺ ions are not merely surface contaminants but are structurally integrated into the lattice, leading to measurable magnetic properties distinct from those of free Mn²⁺ ions in solution.

- discuss confinement of Mn2+ ions within graphene sheets

The confinement of Mn²⁺ ions within the interlayer spaces of graphene-like nanostructures results in a unique electronic environment that modifies the spin dynamics and water interaction kinetics of the ion. Unlike chelated Mn²⁺, where water exchange is governed by ligand dissociation rates, the intercalated Mn²⁺ experiences restricted rotational motion and altered coordination geometry, leading to prolonged water residence times and enhanced spin-lattice relaxation efficiency.

- describe relaxivity of Mn2+-ion graphene nanostructures

Mn²⁺-intercalated graphene nanostructures exhibit longitudinal relaxivity (r₁) values up to 100-fold higher than clinically approved Mn²⁺ chelates at 0.47 T, with NMRD profiles showing a continuous increase in relaxivity down to 0.01 MHz—a feature previously observed only in gadonanotubes. This behavior is attributed to the combined effects of slow rotational correlation, high water coordination numbers, and optimal water residence lifetimes imposed by the graphene lattice confinement.

- discuss NMRD profiles of Mn2+-ion graphene nanostructures

The NMRD profiles of Mn²⁺-graphene systems differ fundamentally from those of traditional contrast agents. Instead of a bell-shaped curve peaking at mid-field frequencies, these materials exhibit a monotonic increase in relaxivity as the magnetic field decreases, suggesting that the dominant relaxation mechanism is dominated by slow molecular motion and strong magnetic anisotropy induced by the carbon lattice. This profile is particularly advantageous for low-field MRI systems, which are gaining traction in point-of-care and resource-limited settings.

- mention large-scale production of graphene nanoplatelets and graphene nanoribbons

Graphene nanoplatelets and nanoribbons can be produced in kilogram quantities using scalable, aqueous-phase chemical exfoliation methods derived from graphite or multi-walled carbon nanotubes. These methods are cost-effective, do not require high-temperature or vacuum conditions, and are amenable to continuous manufacturing, making them ideal candidates for industrial-scale production of MRI contrast agents.

- discuss physico-chemical properties of graphene oxide and graphene nanoribbons

Graphene oxide exhibits high water solubility due to its oxygen-containing functional groups, while reduced graphene nanoribbons display enhanced electrical conductivity and magnetic ordering. Both forms can be functionalized with biocompatible moieties to improve colloidal stability and reduce nonspecific protein adsorption. Their thickness, lateral dimensions, and surface chemistry can be precisely tuned during synthesis to optimize biodistribution, renal clearance, and contrast performance.

- describe experimental studies on graphene-like nanostructures

Experimental characterization using transmission electron microscopy, Raman spectroscopy, superconducting quantum interference device (SQUID) magnetometry, and electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) confirms the presence of Mn²⁺ ions intercalated within the graphene lattice, with no detectable free metal ions in solution. The magnetic behavior varies from superparamagnetic in oxidized nanoplatelets to weak ferromagnetic in nanoribbons, depending on the degree of reduction and defect density.

- summarize background of the invention

Collectively, these findings reveal that graphene-like nanostructures intercalated with Mn²⁺ ions represent a novel class of MRI contrast agents with superior relaxivity, tunable magnetic properties, scalable synthesis, and improved biocompatibility compared to existing technologies. The invention disclosed herein leverages this discovery to provide a new paradigm for high-performance, non-gadolinium-based contrast agents with broad clinical applicability.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

- introduce magnetic composition

The magnetic composition of the invention is a heterogeneous nanoscale material comprising a carbon-based matrix derived from graphene-like or graphitic structures, within which paramagnetic metal ions are intercalated and spatially confined. Unlike conventional contrast agents that rely on chelation chemistry to stabilize metal ions, the present composition exploits the physical entrapment of metal ions between the layers of carbon nanostructures, resulting in a synergistic enhancement of relaxivity that cannot be achieved through molecular design alone. The intercalation occurs during the chemical exfoliation process, wherein oxidizing agents such as potassium permanganate are used to separate graphite or carbon nanotubes into individual or few-layer sheets, and the manganese ions from the oxidizing agent become trapped in the interlayer spaces as the sheets restack or partially reduce.

- define paramagnetic, diamagnetic, ferromagnetic, and superparamagnetic

Paramagnetic materials exhibit weak, positive magnetization in the presence of an external magnetic field and lose their magnetization upon removal of the field. Diamagnetic materials generate a weak, negative magnetization opposing the applied field and are inherently non-magnetic. Ferromagnetic materials retain a permanent magnetization even in the absence of an external field due to strong alignment of magnetic moments within domains. Superparamagnetic materials behave as paramagnets at room temperature due to thermal fluctuations overcoming magnetic anisotropy barriers, but exhibit ferromagnetic-like behavior at low temperatures when the thermal energy is insufficient to flip the magnetic moments.

- specify relaxivity r1 and r2 values

The magnetic composition of the invention exhibits a longitudinal relaxivity (r₁) value of at least 50 mM⁻¹s⁻¹ at 0.47 Tesla and a transverse relaxivity (r₂) value of at least 100 mM⁻¹s⁻¹ at the same field strength, with preferred embodiments achieving r₁ values greater than 80 mM⁻¹s⁻¹ and r₂ values greater than 200 mM⁻¹s⁻¹. The r₂/r₁ ratio is between 1.5 and 4.0, indicating a balanced T1 and T2 contrast capability suitable for both bright and dark contrast imaging modalities.

- define graphene and graphitic nanostructures

Graphene is defined as a single layer of carbon atoms arranged in a two-dimensional honeycomb lattice with sp² hybridization. Graphitic nanostructures refer to multi-layered carbon assemblies derived from graphite, including stacked graphene sheets, carbon nanoribbons, and exfoliated graphite fragments, wherein the interlayer spacing is maintained between 0.335 nm and 1.0 nm, and wherein the structure retains sufficient crystallinity to support long-range magnetic ordering.

- describe graphene-like nanostructure

The graphene-like nanostructure encompasses oxidized, reduced, and functionalized variants of graphene that retain the fundamental two-dimensional carbon lattice but contain oxygen-containing functional groups, defects, or heteroatom dopants that enhance water solubility, stability, or magnetic interaction. These structures are synthesized through oxidative exfoliation of graphite or carbon nanotubes, followed by partial or complete reduction, yielding materials with controllable thickness, lateral dimensions, and surface chemistry.

- specify thickness of graphitic nanostructure or microstructure

The thickness of the graphitic nanostructure or microstructure is measured perpendicular to the plane of the carbon layers and ranges from 10 nanometers to 500 nanometers, with a preferred range of 50 to 200 nanometers, as determined by atomic force microscopy. This thickness range optimizes the balance between magnetic moment density and colloidal stability, ensuring that the particles remain dispersed in biological fluids without sedimentation or aggregation.

- describe carbon nanoplatelet and carbon nanoribbon

The carbon nanoplatelet is a flat, plate-like structure composed of multiple stacked graphene layers with a lateral dimension greater than 1 micrometer and a thickness less than 10 nanometers. The carbon nanoribbon is a narrow, elongated structure with a width between 5 and 200 nanometers and a length exceeding 1 micrometer, derived from the longitudinal unzipping of carbon nanotubes, and exhibiting high edge-to-area ratio and enhanced magnetic anisotropy.

- specify thickness and diameter of carbon nanoplatelet

The thickness of the carbon nanoplatelet is between 0.3 nanometers and 10 nanometers, and the diameter is between 1 micrometer and 10 micrometers, with a preferred embodiment wherein the thickness is less than 5 nanometers and the diameter is between 2 and 5 micrometers, enabling efficient renal clearance and uniform tissue distribution.

- specify width and length of carbon nanoribbon

The width of the carbon nanoribbon is between 5 nanometers and 200 nanometers, and the length is between 1 micrometer and 50 micrometers, with a preferred embodiment wherein the width is between 20 and 100 nanometers and the length is between 5 and 20 micrometers, optimizing magnetic field perturbation and relaxivity enhancement.

- describe water solubilizing moiety

The water solubilizing moiety is a hydrophilic chemical group or polymer chain covalently or non-covalently attached to the surface of the graphene-like or graphitic nanostructure to enhance dispersion in aqueous environments, reduce nonspecific protein binding, and prevent aggregation in physiological fluids. The moiety may be selected from polyethylene glycol, polyvinylpyrrolidone, dextran, chitosan, phospholipids, or carboxylated or sulfonated aromatic groups, and is typically present at a surface density sufficient to provide steric stabilization without masking the paramagnetic centers.

- specify attachment of water solubilizing moiety

The water solubilizing moiety is attached through covalent bonding to oxygen-containing functional groups on the surface of the graphene-like nanostructure, such as carboxyl, hydroxyl, or epoxy groups, or through non-covalent interactions including π-π stacking, electrostatic attraction, or hydrophobic embedding. The attachment is stable under physiological conditions and does not interfere with the magnetic properties of the intercalated metal ions.

- describe magnetic metal

The magnetic metal is selected from the group consisting of manganese, iron, cobalt, nickel, copper, and combinations thereof, wherein the metal is present in a +2 or +3 oxidation state, and wherein the metal is intercalated within the interlayer spaces of the graphene-like or graphitic nanostructure at a concentration of 0.01 to 5 weight percent, with a preferred concentration of 0.1 to 2 weight percent, sufficient to induce measurable paramagnetic or ferromagnetic behavior without inducing cytotoxicity.

- specify room temperature paramagnetic metallic elements

The room temperature paramagnetic metallic elements include manganese, iron, cobalt, and nickel, wherein manganese is the most preferred due to its biological compatibility, natural metabolic pathways, and high spin state (S = 5/2), which maximizes magnetic moment and relaxivity enhancement.

- specify room temperature ferromagnetic metallic elements

The room temperature ferromagnetic metallic elements include iron, cobalt, and nickel, wherein iron is the most preferred for its high saturation magnetization and potential for dual T1/T2 contrast, provided that its concentration is kept below 0.05 weight percent to avoid excessive T2 dominance and signal loss.

- specify rare earth metals

Rare earth metals such as gadolinium, dysprosium, and europium may be used in alternative embodiments, provided that they are intercalated within the graphene lattice and not chelated, and that their concentration is minimized to reduce toxicity risks, with manganese remaining the preferred metal for clinical translation.

- describe magnetic composition with multiple magnetic metals

The magnetic composition may contain a mixture of two or more magnetic metals, such as manganese and iron, wherein the metals are co-intercalated within the graphene lattice to produce synergistic relaxivity effects, with the ratio of metals optimized to balance T1 and T2 contrast, magnetic stability, and biocompatibility.

- specify intercalation of magnetic metal in graphene-like nanostructure

The magnetic metal is intercalated within the graphene-like nanostructure during the oxidative exfoliation process, wherein the metal ions are incorporated into the interlayer spaces as the carbon layers separate and partially re-stack, resulting in a structural integration that is not achievable through post-synthesis doping or surface binding.

- specify amount of magnetic metal in composition

The amount of magnetic metal in the composition is between 0.01 and 5 weight percent, with a preferred range of 0.1 to 2 weight percent, as determined by inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectroscopy, and wherein the metal is present in a dispersed, non-aggregated state within the carbon lattice.

- describe preferred embodiments of magnetic composition

A preferred embodiment comprises reduced graphene nanoribbons intercalated with manganese at a concentration of 0.8 weight percent, exhibiting a longitudinal relaxivity of 92 mM⁻¹s⁻¹ at 0.47 Tesla and a transverse relaxivity of 210 mM⁻¹s⁻¹, with a saturation magnetization of 0.1 emu/g at 300 K and a coercive field of 250 Oe, and wherein the composition is stabilized with polyethylene glycol chains covalently attached to surface carboxyl groups.

- describe method of performing magnetic resonance imaging

The method of performing magnetic resonance imaging comprises administering to a mammalian subject an effective amount of the magnetic composition, wherein the composition is delivered intravenously, orally, or via localized injection, and subsequently acquiring magnetic resonance images using a clinical MRI scanner operating at a field strength between 0.1 Tesla and 7 Tesla, wherein the magnetic composition enhances the contrast between normal and diseased tissues by shortening the T1 and/or T2 relaxation times of water protons in the vicinity of the composition.

- specify administration of magnetic composition

The magnetic composition is administered in a pharmaceutically acceptable aqueous formulation, wherein the concentration of the magnetic metal is between 0.1 mM and 10 mM, and wherein the composition is sterile, pyrogen-free, and isotonic, and may be administered as a single bolus injection, continuous infusion, or targeted delivery via catheter or nanoparticle carrier.

- describe kit for use in MRI imaging

The invention further provides a kit for use in magnetic resonance imaging, comprising a vial containing the magnetic composition in lyophilized or liquid form, a vial containing a sterile diluent, and instructions for reconstitution and administration, wherein the kit further includes a certificate of analysis confirming the concentration of magnetic metal, relaxivity values, and absence of endotoxins or heavy metal contaminants.

- determine toxicity of magnetic composition

The toxicity of the magnetic composition is evaluated in vitro using cell viability assays in human hepatocytes, renal tubular cells, and macrophages, and in vivo using rodent models, wherein the composition shows no significant cytotoxicity at concentrations up to 10 mM of magnetic metal, and wherein no histopathological abnormalities are observed in major organs following single or repeated dosing.

- estimate effective dose of magnetic composition

The effective dose of the magnetic composition is estimated to be between 0.01 and 0.5 mL per kilogram of body weight, corresponding to a manganese dose of 0.05 to 2.5 mg/kg, which is well below the known toxic threshold for manganese and comparable to or lower than doses used for clinical gadolinium agents.

- formulate magnetic composition for oral administration

The magnetic composition may be formulated for oral administration by encapsulating the particles in enteric-coated capsules or suspending them in a biocompatible gel matrix, wherein the composition remains stable in gastric and intestinal fluids and is absorbed primarily through the lymphatic system, enabling targeted imaging of gastrointestinal pathologies.

- formulate magnetic composition for parenteral administration

For parenteral administration, the magnetic composition is formulated as a sterile aqueous suspension or solution containing a surfactant such as polysorbate 80 or Pluronic F127, buffered to physiological pH, and optionally containing a tonicity agent such as dextrose or sodium chloride, wherein the particle size distribution is controlled to ensure renal clearance and minimize reticuloendothelial system uptake.

- describe targeted drug delivery system

The magnetic composition may be functionalized with targeting ligands such as antibodies, peptides, or aptamers specific to disease biomarkers, enabling active targeting of tumors, inflamed tissues, or atherosclerotic plaques, wherein the targeting ligand is conjugated to the water solubilizing moiety without compromising the relaxivity or magnetic properties of the intercalated metal ions.

- describe method of producing magnetic composition

The method of producing the magnetic composition comprises the steps of: (a) providing a graphite or multi-walled carbon nanotube precursor; (b) subjecting the precursor to oxidative exfoliation using a permanganate-based oxidizing agent in acidic aqueous medium; (c) isolating the resulting graphene-like nanostructure by centrifugation and washing; (d) optionally reducing the oxidized nanostructure using a reducing agent; and (e) purifying the product to remove unbound metal ions and residual reagents, wherein the intercalation of the magnetic metal occurs during step (b) as a result of the chemical reaction conditions.

- specify reducing agent for magnetic composition

The reducing agent is selected from the group consisting of hydrazine hydrate, sodium borohydride, ascorbic acid, and thermal treatment at temperatures between 150°C and 300°C, wherein the reduction step is performed to restore sp² hybridization, enhance electrical conductivity, and promote ferromagnetic ordering in the graphene lattice.

- describe alternative method of producing magnetic composition

An alternative method of producing the magnetic composition involves the direct synthesis of graphene-like nanostructures from metal-doped carbon precursors, wherein the metal ions are incorporated into the carbon lattice during chemical vapor deposition or laser ablation, followed by exfoliation and purification to yield intercalated nanostructures with controlled metal loading and morphology.

### EXAMPLES

- introduce examples of present invention

The following examples illustrate the preparation, characterization, and performance of the magnetic compositions of the invention, including graphene nanoplatelets and graphene nanoribbons intercalated with manganese, and demonstrate their superior relaxivity, magnetic behavior, and suitability as MRI contrast agents.

- describe materials and methods for GNP synthesis

Graphene nanoplatelets were synthesized by oxidizing analytical grade micro-graphite using a modified Hummers method, followed by ultrasonic exfoliation in water, centrifugation, and isolation of the oxidized nanoplatelets. A portion of the oxidized material was reduced using hydrazine hydrate under reflux to yield reduced graphene nanoplatelets.

- detail exfoliation procedure for GNP synthesis

In a typical exfoliation procedure, 200 mg of oxidized micro-graphite was suspended in 200 mL of deionized water and sonicated for one hour in an ultrasonic bath. The resulting suspension was centrifuged at 5000 rpm for one hour, and the supernatant was collected and lyophilized to obtain oxidized graphene nanoplatelets.

- outline reduction process for GNP synthesis

To reduce the oxidized nanoplatelets, 150 mL of the supernatant was treated with 1.5 mL of hydrazine hydrate and heated at 100°C for 12 hours under reflux. The resulting black precipitate was washed with water and methanol, then dried under vacuum to yield reduced graphene nanoplatelets.

- describe materials and methods for GNR synthesis

Graphene nanoribbons were synthesized by unzipping multi-walled carbon nanotubes using a modified oxidative method involving concentrated sulfuric acid and potassium permanganate, followed by extensive washing and centrifugation to isolate the nanoribbon product.

- detail modified procedure for GNR synthesis

MWCNTs (150 mg) were suspended in 30 mL of concentrated sulfuric acid and stirred for two hours. Potassium permanganate (750 mg) was added slowly, and the mixture was heated at 55–70°C for one hour. The reaction was quenched with water, and the product was washed with ethanol and ether, then isolated by centrifugation.

- perform sample analysis using TEM and Raman spectroscopy

Transmission electron microscopy revealed that the graphene nanoplatelets consisted of stacked layers with thicknesses ranging from 2 to 8 nanometers, while the nanoribbons exhibited elongated, ribbon-like morphologies with widths of 20–100 nanometers and lengths exceeding 10 micrometers. Raman spectroscopy confirmed the presence of D and G bands indicative of sp² carbon and defect density, with the D/G ratio increasing upon reduction.

- characterize magnetic behavior using SQUID magnetometer

SQUID magnetometry demonstrated that oxidized nanoplatelets exhibited paramagnetic behavior, while reduced nanoplatelets showed superparamagnetic characteristics with a blocking temperature of 40 K. Nanoribbons displayed weak ferromagnetism at room temperature with a saturation magnetization of 0.1 emu/g and a coercive field of 250 Oe.

- measure relaxivity of graphene samples

Relaxivity measurements at 0.47 Tesla showed that oxidized nanoplatelets had an r₁ of 78 mM⁻¹s⁻¹, reduced nanoplatelets had an r₁ of 85 mM⁻¹s⁻¹, and nanoribbons had an r₁ of 92 mM⁻¹s⁻¹, all significantly higher than clinical Mn-chelates (4–10 mM⁻¹s⁻¹).

- analyze metal content using ICPOES

Inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectroscopy confirmed the presence of manganese at concentrations of 0.8 wt% in nanoribbons and 0.6 wt% in nanoplatelets, with no detectable iron contamination in the nanoribbon samples.

- perform in vitro phantom MRI experiments

In vitro MRI phantoms containing varying concentrations of the magnetic compositions were imaged using a 1.5 Tesla clinical scanner with spin-echo sequences. All samples produced significantly higher signal intensity than control solutions, with nanoribbons providing the greatest contrast enhancement.

- obtain MRI images using 2D spin-echo imaging

Two-dimensional spin-echo images clearly demonstrated dose-dependent signal enhancement in phantoms containing the magnetic compositions, with the nanoribbon formulation producing the highest contrast-to-noise ratio, exceeding that of commercial Gd-based agents by more than fivefold.

- introduce results and discussion section

The results demonstrate that intercalation of manganese within graphene-like nanostructures produces a novel class of MRI contrast agents with unprecedented relaxivity, tunable magnetic behavior, and scalable synthesis. The structural confinement of Mn²⁺ within the carbon lattice fundamentally alters its relaxation dynamics, resulting in NMRD profiles unlike any known contrast agent.

- show TEM images of graphene nanoplatelets

TEM images revealed well-defined, layered structures with interlayer spacing consistent with oxidized and reduced graphene, confirming successful exfoliation and absence of large aggregates.

- describe structural and morphological information

The nanoplatelets exhibited a flake-like morphology with lateral dimensions of 1–5 micrometers and thicknesses of 2–8 nanometers, while the nanoribbons were elongated with high aspect ratios, consistent with the unzipping of MWCNTs.

- analyze AFM section of graphene oxide nanoplatelets

Atomic force microscopy confirmed the thickness of oxidized nanoplatelets to be approximately 3.5 nanometers, corresponding to approximately 10–12 graphene layers, with surface roughness indicative of oxygen functionalization.

- discuss thickness of graphene oxide nanoplatelets

The thickness of the graphene oxide nanoplatelets was found to be directly correlated with the degree of oxidation and exfoliation efficiency, with thinner platelets showing higher relaxivity due to increased surface exposure of intercalated Mn²⁺ ions.

- show TEM images of graphene nanoribbons

TEM images of the nanoribbons revealed uniform, ribbon-like structures with smooth edges and consistent widths, confirming the effectiveness of the unzipping procedure and the absence of residual nanotube fragments.

- describe structural and morphological information

The nanoribbons exhibited a highly anisotropic morphology with lengths exceeding 10 micrometers and widths between 20 and 100 nanometers, providing a large surface area and high edge density favorable for magnetic interactions.

- analyze Raman spectra of oxidized and reduced graphene nanoplatelets

Raman spectra of oxidized nanoplatelets showed prominent D and G bands with a D/G ratio of 1.1, indicating moderate defect density. Upon reduction, the D/G ratio increased to 1.3, confirming restoration of sp² domains and increased structural order.

- discuss G and D bands of Raman spectra

The G band at 1580 cm⁻¹ corresponds to the in-plane vibration of sp² carbon atoms, while the D band at 1350 cm⁻¹ arises from structural defects.
