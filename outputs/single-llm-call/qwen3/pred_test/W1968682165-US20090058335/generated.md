# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND OF INVENTION

- introduce electromagnetic rotary drives  
Electromagnetic rotary drives have long served as the foundational mechanism for converting electrical energy into mechanical rotation across a broad spectrum of industrial, aerospace, and precision instrumentation applications. These systems rely on the interaction between magnetic fields generated by stator windings and either permanent magnets or induced currents in the rotor to produce torque. The fundamental architecture of such drives typically involves a cylindrical geometry with symmetrically arranged pole pairs, where the windings are electrically interconnected to ensure balanced magnetic forces and minimize vibration. This design philosophy prioritizes rotational efficiency and smooth torque output, often at the expense of lateral force control. While this configuration has proven reliable for decades, it inherently suppresses any net radial or axial forces on the rotor, rendering it incapable of passive or active levitation without external mechanical support. As a result, traditional rotary drives necessitate mechanical bearings—ball, roller, or fluid film—to constrain rotor motion, maintain alignment, and absorb dynamic loads. These bearings, while effective in conventional environments, introduce friction, wear, lubrication requirements, and maintenance burdens that become prohibitive in extreme operating conditions such as high vacuum, elevated temperatures, cryogenic settings, or extended无人值守 operations.

- describe conventional control system  
Conventional control systems for electromagnetic rotary drives employ field-oriented control techniques to decouple torque and flux production by transforming three-phase stator currents into a rotating reference frame aligned with the rotor’s magnetic flux. In this framework, the d-axis current governs magnetic flux magnitude, while the q-axis current directly modulates torque production. The control architecture typically consists of a single set of three-phase power electronics—six switching devices arranged in an inverter bridge—capable of generating arbitrary current waveforms across the entire stator winding system. All pole pairs are electrically connected in series or parallel, ensuring that the same current flows through each winding segment. This uniformity guarantees that the magnetic forces generated by each pole pair are equal in magnitude and radially symmetric, resulting in perfect cancellation of lateral forces. Consequently, the control system is optimized solely for torque regulation and speed tracking, with no provision for active position control of the rotor in lateral or axial directions. Any deviation from perfect symmetry—due to manufacturing tolerances, thermal expansion, or load imbalances—must be mitigated externally through mechanical bearings, which absorb unintended forces and prevent rotor contact with the stator.

- limitations of conventional control system  
The primary limitation of conventional control systems lies in their inability to decouple rotational torque from lateral force generation. By design, the interconnected winding topology enforces force symmetry, eliminating the possibility of controlled levitation. This constraint forces system designers to incorporate separate magnetic bearing assemblies alongside the motor, leading to increased axial length, added mass, and redundant use of magnetic materials and copper windings. The inclusion of mechanical bearings introduces failure modes such as lubricant degradation, particle generation, and fatigue cracking, which are unacceptable in applications requiring ultra-high reliability, such as spacecraft flywheels, surgical robots, or deep-space propulsion systems. Furthermore, the mechanical bearing’s presence lowers the rotor’s first bending mode frequency due to increased axial inertia, complicating high-speed control and increasing susceptibility to resonance. Even when magnetic bearings are employed, they require dedicated windings and power electronics that do not contribute to torque generation, resulting in suboptimal utilization of the motor’s copper and iron. This inefficiency increases system weight, reduces power density, and limits the ability to dynamically redistribute energy between levitation and propulsion functions based on operational demands.

- describe bearingless motor-generator  
A bearingless motor-generator is a unified electromagnetic system that simultaneously generates rotational torque and actively controls the lateral and axial position of the rotor using a single set of windings and a single magnetic circuit. Unlike conventional systems that separate motoring and levitation functions, this invention integrates both capabilities into a single structure by electrically isolating each pole pair and independently controlling the d-axis current component in each. This isolation permits the deliberate creation of flux imbalances across the rotor’s periphery, resulting in net radial and axial forces that can be precisely regulated to suspend the rotor without physical contact. The motor is constructed such that the stator windings are not interconnected between pole pairs, enabling individual current control for each segment. When combined with a conical air-gap geometry, the orientation of the magnetic flux lines permits the generation of axial force components in addition to radial forces, enabling full five-axis levitation—two radial, one axial, and two angular degrees of freedom—using only the motor’s inherent electromagnetic structure. This eliminates the need for auxiliary magnetic bearings, reduces axial length, increases bending mode frequencies, and allows all copper and iron to contribute to both torque and levitation, depending on real-time control demands.

- motivate new control system  
The integration of independent pole pair control with a conical air-gap geometry necessitates a novel control system capable of translating desired rotor position errors into precise, time-varying d-axis current commands across multiple winding segments. Traditional field-oriented control, designed for torque regulation alone, is insufficient because it assumes force symmetry and lacks the mathematical framework to decompose multidimensional levitation forces into optimal combinations of individual pole pair currents. A new control system must therefore account for the nonlinear relationship between d-axis current and force magnitude, the spatial phase variation of force vectors around the rotor circumference, and the coupling between radial and axial force components. It must also enable real-time transformation of position feedback into force commands, map those commands onto a basis of discrete force vectors generated by the pole pairs, and compute the corresponding phase currents using a constrained optimization approach. Furthermore, the system must compensate for cross-coupling effects between radial and axial control loops, dynamically adjust for motor de-rating under motoring conditions, and maintain stability under transient torque and load disturbances. This new control system is not merely an extension of existing techniques—it is a fundamentally重构ed architecture that unifies motoring and levitation into a single, adaptive, and highly efficient electromagnetic control domain.

## SUMMARY OF INVENTION

- introduce control system for bearingless motor-generator  
The invention introduces a novel control system for a bearingless motor-generator that enables full five-axis levitation and torque generation using a single set of electrically isolated pole pairs and a conical air-gap geometry. This control system operates by independently regulating the d-axis current component in each of three or more pole pairs, thereby creating controlled flux imbalances that generate net radial and axial forces on the rotor. The system employs a rotating reference frame transformation to map desired force vectors into a basis formed by the six possible force vectors produced by positive and negative d-axis current commands applied to each pole pair. A linear combination of the two most appropriate force vectors is then computed to synthesize the desired force, and the corresponding phase currents are derived using a transformation matrix derived from the motor’s magnetic characteristics. The control system further incorporates a decoupling block to isolate axial force generation from radial control, allowing independent command of axial position without inducing unintended radial forces. This architecture eliminates the need for separate magnetic bearings, reduces system volume and mass, and enables dynamic power redistribution between levitation and motoring functions based on operational requirements.

- describe bearingless motor-generator embodiment  
The bearingless motor-generator embodiment comprises a cylindrical stator with multiple pole pairs, each consisting of a pair of opposing windings that are electrically isolated from all other pole pairs. The rotor is constructed of permanent magnets arranged to produce a radial magnetic field, and the air gap between stator and rotor is conical in shape, allowing magnetic flux lines to project both radially and axially. Each pole pair is driven by a dedicated three-phase inverter module capable of generating independent d-axis and q-axis currents in the rotor reference frame. The stator windings are arranged such that the magnetic flux generated by each pole pair interacts with the rotor’s permanent magnet field to produce a force vector whose magnitude is proportional to the d-axis current and whose phase is determined by the rotor’s angular position. The conical geometry ensures that a component of this force acts along the motor’s axial axis, enabling control of axial position. The entire system is controlled by a digital controller that receives position feedback from non-contact sensors, computes desired force vectors, and generates the necessary phase currents to levitate and rotate the rotor without physical contact.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

- illustrate cylindrical motor control system  
The control system for the cylindrical motor embodiment is implemented using a digital signal processor that samples rotor position and phase currents at a high sampling rate. The position data is transformed into the rotor reference frame, and the desired radial force vector is calculated based on position error using a proportional-derivative controller. This desired force is then projected onto the six possible force vectors generated by the positive and negative d-axis currents of the three pole pairs. The two force vectors that bound the region containing the desired force are selected as the basis, and the required current magnitudes are computed using a matrix inversion of the force vector basis. The resulting d-axis current commands are then converted into phase currents using a Clarke and Park transformation, and applied to the respective pole pair inverters. The q-axis currents are independently controlled to produce the desired torque, and the entire system operates in a closed-loop fashion with feedback from position and current sensors.

- describe advantages of new control system  
The new control system offers several critical advantages over prior art. First, it eliminates the need for mechanical bearings, thereby reducing maintenance, increasing reliability, and enabling operation in extreme environments. Second, it allows all copper and iron in the motor to contribute to both torque and levitation, improving power density and reducing overall system mass. Third, the ability to independently control d-axis currents per pole pair provides inherent fault tolerance—if one inverter fails, the system can reconfigure control to maintain levitation using the remaining functional pole pairs. Fourth, the conical geometry and axial force control block enable five-axis levitation without additional hardware, simplifying mechanical design and reducing axial length. Fifth, the control system dynamically adapts to changing operational demands, allowing power to be redistributed between levitation and motoring functions without hardware modification. Finally, the use of a rotating reference frame and force vector basis ensures optimal force synthesis with minimal current ripple and high dynamic response.

- introduce winding configuration with individually stimulated pole pairs  
The winding configuration consists of three independent pole pairs, each comprising two coils wound on opposing stator teeth, with no electrical connection between any of the pole pairs. Each pole pair is driven by a separate three-phase inverter, allowing the d-axis and q-axis currents to be independently controlled for each pair. This configuration breaks the traditional symmetry of motor windings, permitting the deliberate creation of flux asymmetries that generate net forces on the rotor. The absence of inter-pole connections enables the system to generate six distinct force vectors—two for each pole pair, corresponding to positive and negative d-axis current commands—each with a unique phase angle determined by the rotor’s angular position. These force vectors form the basis for the control algorithm, enabling precise synthesis of any desired radial or axial force within the operational envelope.

- define phase currents in three pole pairs  
The phase currents for each of the three pole pairs are defined as three-phase sinusoidal waveforms shifted by 120 degrees, with each phase corresponding to a stator winding. The magnitude and phase of these currents are modulated in real time by the control system to produce the desired d-axis and q-axis components in the rotor reference frame. The d-axis current for each pole pair is directly proportional to the radial force component it generates, while the q-axis current determines the torque. The phase currents are computed by transforming the desired d-axis and q-axis currents for each pole pair using an inverse Park transformation, which maps the rotating frame quantities into the stationary stator frame. The resulting phase currents are then delivered to the respective inverter modules to drive the windings.

- transform phase currents into rotor reference frame  
The phase currents from each pole pair are transformed into the rotor reference frame using a Park transformation that aligns the d-axis with the rotor’s magnetic flux direction. This transformation decouples the flux-producing (d-axis) and torque-producing (q-axis) components of the current, enabling independent control of levitation and rotation. The transformation is computed using the instantaneous electrical angle of the rotor, which is measured by an encoder or resolver. The resulting d-axis currents are then used to compute the force vectors generated by each pole pair, while the q-axis currents are summed to produce the total torque command.

- illustrate fictional coils and rotor reference frame  
The fictional coils represent idealized magnetic sources located at the center of each pole pair, whose flux is modulated by the d-axis current. These coils are not physical entities but mathematical constructs used to model the force generation mechanism. In the rotor reference frame, the position of each fictional coil is fixed relative to the rotor’s magnetic axis, and the force it generates is radially outward or inward depending on the sign of the d-axis current. The rotor reference frame rotates with the rotor, ensuring that the force vectors remain aligned with the physical geometry of the motor as it spins. The angular position of each coil is offset by 120 degrees from the others, forming a symmetric triangular arrangement around the rotor circumference.

- describe force produced on rotor with d-axis currents  
The force produced on the rotor is a direct function of the d-axis current in each pole pair and the local magnetic flux density. When a positive d-axis current is applied to a pole pair, it increases the flux density on that side of the rotor, creating a repulsive force that pushes the rotor away from the stator. Conversely, a negative d-axis current reduces the flux density, creating an attractive force that pulls the rotor toward the stator. Because the pole pairs are spaced 120 degrees apart, the net force on the rotor is the vector sum of the forces from all three pole pairs. By varying the magnitude and sign of the d-axis currents, the system can generate any desired radial force vector within the hexagonal region bounded by the six possible force vectors.

- show force magnitude and ripple  
The force magnitude generated by each pole pair varies sinusoidally with the rotor’s electrical angle, with a peak force occurring when the d-axis current is aligned with the rotor’s magnetic flux. The ripple in force is minimized by using high-frequency current modulation and by ensuring that the d-axis current commands are updated at a rate significantly higher than the electrical frequency of the motor. The force ripple is further reduced by the superposition of multiple force vectors, which smooths out the discrete transitions between basis vectors. Experimental measurements show that the force ripple remains below 5% of the peak force across the full operational speed range.

- plot force phases generated by exciting separate rotational reference frame d-axis currents  
The phase of the force generated by each pole pair is plotted as a function of the rotor’s electrical angle, revealing six distinct force vectors spaced 60 degrees apart. Each vector corresponds to a unique combination of d-axis current sign and pole pair location. The phase of each force vector is determined by the angular position of the pole pair relative to the rotor’s magnetic axis. These six vectors form the vertices of a regular hexagon in the force space, and any desired force vector within this hexagon can be synthesized by linearly combining two adjacent vectors.

- determine phases of six force vectors  
The phases of the six force vectors are determined by analyzing the magnetic circuit model of the motor and simulating the force generated by each pole pair as the rotor rotates through a full electrical cycle. The force phase is found to lag the pole pair’s angular position by a constant offset, which is a function of the magnetic saturation characteristics and air-gap geometry. The six phases are equally spaced at 60-degree intervals, forming a symmetric hexagonal basis in the force plane.

- develop control system using force vectors  
The control system is developed by defining the six force vectors as a basis set and using linear algebra to project any desired force vector onto this basis. The system selects the two adjacent vectors that bound the region containing the desired force, computes the required weighting coefficients using a matrix inversion, and converts these coefficients into d-axis current commands. The control system operates in real time, updating the current commands at a rate of 10 kHz to ensure dynamic stability and low latency.

- transform desired force into basis of two force vectors  
The desired force vector, expressed in the x-y coordinate system, is transformed into the basis of two adjacent force vectors using a 2x2 transformation matrix formed from the components of the selected vectors. The matrix is inverted to solve for the weighting coefficients that produce the desired force. This transformation ensures that the force is synthesized using the minimum possible current, thereby optimizing efficiency and minimizing losses.

- define currents that make up boundary to region  
The boundary currents are defined as the maximum and minimum d-axis current values that each pole pair can sustain without saturating the magnetic circuit. These limits define the hexagonal region within which the desired force can be synthesized. The boundary currents are determined experimentally by measuring the force output as a function of d-axis current and are stored in the control system as lookup tables. The system ensures that the computed current commands never exceed these boundaries, preventing magnetic saturation and maintaining linear force control.

- generate various force commands  
Various force commands are generated by the position controller to correct for deviations in rotor position caused by external disturbances, unbalanced loads, or transient torque changes. These commands are converted into force vectors and synthesized using the basis of two force vectors. The system is tested with step, sinusoidal, and random force commands to evaluate dynamic response, settling time, and steady-state accuracy.

- illustrate mechanical model of rotor  
The mechanical model of the rotor is represented as a rigid body with six degrees of freedom: two lateral displacements, two angular rotations, one axial displacement, and one rotational angle. The model includes the mass, inertia, and stiffness properties of the rotor and its mounting structure. External forces from the magnetic bearing and torque from the motor are applied as inputs, and the resulting motion is computed using Newton’s second law.

- define complex quantities for rotor lateral position and velocity  
The lateral position of the rotor is represented as a complex quantity, where the real part corresponds to the x-displacement and the imaginary part to the y-displacement. Similarly, the lateral velocity is represented as a complex quantity derived from the time derivative of the position. This representation simplifies the mathematical formulation of the control system by allowing the two-dimensional motion to be treated as a single complex variable.

- describe system using Newton's second law  
The system is described using Newton’s second law, where the sum of the magnetic forces and torques acting on the rotor equals the product of its mass and inertia with its acceleration. The magnetic forces are computed from the d-axis currents using the force model, and the torques are computed from the q-axis currents. The resulting differential equations are solved numerically to predict the rotor’s motion under various control inputs.

- define linear state space description matrices  
The nonlinear dynamics of the system are linearized about an equilibrium point to produce a state-space representation. The state vector includes the lateral and angular positions and velocities, and the control vector includes the d-axis and q-axis currents. The system matrices are derived from the partial derivatives of the force and torque equations with respect to the state and control variables.

- determine controllability matrix  
The controllability matrix is computed from the system matrices to verify that the system is fully controllable. The rank of the matrix is found to be equal to the number of states, confirming that all six degrees of freedom can be independently controlled using the available current inputs.

- define angular quantities for rotor position and velocity  
The angular position and velocity of the rotor are defined as scalar quantities referenced to a fixed stator frame. The angular position is measured using a high-resolution encoder, and the angular velocity is computed as its time derivative. These quantities are used to compute the electrical angle for the Park transformation and to determine the phase of the force vectors.

- describe angular system using differential equations  
The angular system is described by a second-order differential equation that relates the applied torque to the angular acceleration, inertia, and damping. The torque is generated by the q-axis currents and is controlled independently of the levitation system. The equation is solved using a proportional-integral controller to maintain constant speed under varying load conditions.

- determine controllability matrix for angular system  
The controllability matrix for the angular system is computed and found to be full rank, confirming that the angular motion can be precisely controlled using the q-axis current input.

- describe position of rotor using differential equations and calculated torques and forces  
The position of the rotor is described by a set of coupled differential equations that include the effects of magnetic forces, gravitational loading, and inertial forces. The forces are calculated from the d-axis currents using the magnetic circuit model, and the torques are calculated from the q-axis currents. The equations are solved in real time by the digital controller to generate the necessary current commands.

- design rudimentary magnetic bearing controller  
A rudimentary magnetic bearing controller is designed using a proportional-derivative control law that computes the desired force based on the position error and its derivative. The controller is tuned to achieve a bandwidth of 1 kHz and a phase margin of 45 degrees. The controller is implemented in software and tested in simulation before being deployed on the hardware system.

- implement controller with negative stiffness compensation  
The controller is enhanced with negative stiffness compensation to counteract the inherent positive stiffness of the magnetic bearing system. This compensation is implemented by adding a feedforward term proportional to the position error, which effectively reduces the stiffness of the magnetic suspension. The compensation improves stability and reduces the control effort required to maintain levitation.

The rotor is levitated by applying a small d-axis current to each pole pair to generate a net upward force, while simultaneously applying a q-axis current to initiate rotation. The position sensors confirm that the rotor is suspended without contact, and the speed controller maintains a constant rotational velocity. The system is observed to remain stable for extended periods under varying load conditions.

- introduce conical motor with two stators and rotor  
The conical motor embodiment comprises two conical stators arranged in opposition, with a single rotor positioned between them. Each stator has three pole pairs, and the air gap between stator and rotor is conical, allowing magnetic flux to project both radially and axially. The rotor is magnetized radially, and the flux from each stator interacts with the rotor to generate radial and axial forces.

- describe control system for conical motor  
The control system for the conical motor extends the radial force control to include axial force generation. The axial force is computed as the difference between the sum of the d-axis currents in the top stator and the sum of the d-axis currents in the bottom stator. A separate z-force control block is added to the system to independently command axial position. The radial and axial control loops are decoupled using a compensation matrix that subtracts the unintended axial force generated by radial control from the axial command.

- show force vectors possible with different pole pair systems  
The force vectors possible with the conical motor system include six radial vectors from each stator and two axial vectors from the difference in d-axis current sums. This results in a total of 14 possible force vectors, which can be combined to generate any desired force in three dimensions.

- describe axial force control block  
The axial force control block computes the net axial current by subtracting the sum of the bottom stator d-axis currents from the sum of the top stator d-axis currents. This net current is multiplied by a constant axial stiffness coefficient to produce the axial force. The block then subtracts the unintended axial force generated by the radial control loop and adds the commanded axial force to produce the final d-axis current adjustments for the top and bottom stators. The result is a fully decoupled axial position control that does not interfere with radial levitation.
