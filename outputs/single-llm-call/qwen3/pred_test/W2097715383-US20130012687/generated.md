# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

The purification of recombinant proteins remains a fundamental and often rate-limiting step in structural biology, proteomics, and biopharmaceutical development. Despite decades of innovation in affinity tagging strategies, the production of soluble, stable, and functionally intact proteins from bacterial expression systems continues to present significant challenges. Traditional approaches rely heavily on the fusion of target proteins to affinity tags such as poly-histidine (His6), glutathione S-transferase (GST), or maltose-binding protein (MBP), which facilitate initial capture via immobilized metal affinity chromatography (IMAC) or resin-based ligand interactions. However, these tags frequently interfere with protein folding, reduce solubility, or mask functional domains, necessitating their subsequent removal. The standard method for tag excision involves the exogenous addition of site-specific proteases—such as thrombin, TEV, or Factor Xa—engineered to cleave at defined recognition sequences between the tag and the target protein. This approach introduces multiple complications: the proteases themselves are expensive, often poorly soluble, and require stoichiometric or excess quantities to achieve complete cleavage. Furthermore, prolonged incubation times increase the risk of nonspecific proteolytic degradation of the target protein at cryptic cleavage sites, resulting in heterogeneous products that compromise downstream applications such as crystallization, functional assays, or therapeutic formulation. Additional purification steps are then required to remove the exogenous protease, further reducing yield, increasing processing time, and elevating overall costs. These limitations are particularly acute when working with intrinsically disordered proteins, membrane-associated domains, or proteins derived from non-model organisms, which are notoriously prone to aggregation, instability, or low expression levels. Consequently, there exists a critical and unmet need for a streamlined, self-contained purification system that simultaneously enhances protein expression and solubility while enabling precise, controlled, and tag-free release of the target protein in a single operational step, without reliance on costly or promiscuous exogenous enzymes.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention introduces a novel self-cleaving protease tag derived from the Vibrio cholerae MARTX toxin cysteine protease domain (CPD), which enables a one-step, on-bead purification and tag-removal system for recombinant proteins expressed in bacterial hosts. This tag is genetically fused C-terminally to a target protein and is equipped with a C-terminal hexahistidine (His6) affinity handle, allowing for efficient capture of the full-length fusion protein on nickel-nitrilotriacetic acid (Ni-NTA) resin under native conditions. Crucially, the CPD remains catalytically inert in the bacterial cytoplasm due to its absolute dependence on the eukaryotic signaling molecule inositol hexakisphosphate (InsP6) for activation, which is absent in prokaryotic cells. Upon binding of InsP6 to the immobilized CPD fusion protein, the protease undergoes a highly specific autocleavage event immediately C-terminal to a conserved leucine residue positioned at the junction between the target protein and the CPD tag. This cleavage liberates the target protein into the supernatant in an untagged, native form, while the His6-tagged CPD fragment remains tightly bound to the Ni-NTA resin, enabling complete separation without additional chromatographic steps. The system thus integrates affinity capture, site-specific proteolysis, and tag removal into a single, rapid, and scalable procedure. The CPD tag not only facilitates purification but also enhances the expression and solubility of otherwise intractable proteins, likely through stabilization of folding intermediates or suppression of aggregation-prone conformations. This dual functionality—enabling both improved biophysical properties and seamless tag excision—represents a marked advancement over existing self-cleaving systems, which either lack the capacity to enhance solubility or require complex environmental triggers such as temperature shifts or pH changes.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE PREFERRED EMBODIMENTS

The invention encompasses an on-bead cleavage purification system wherein a recombinant protein of interest is genetically fused to the Vibrio cholerae MARTX toxin cysteine protease domain (CPD) bearing a C-terminal His6 affinity tag. The fusion construct is expressed in a bacterial host, typically Escherichia coli, under the control of an inducible promoter such as the T7/lac system. Following induction and cell lysis, the CPD-His6 fusion protein is captured on Ni-NTA resin under mild, non-denaturing conditions, preserving the integrity of the target protein. The resin-bound fusion is then treated with inositol hexakisphosphate (InsP6) at concentrations ranging from 10 to 200 micromolar, which induces conformational activation of the CPD and triggers precise autocleavage at the conserved leucine residue located at the target protein–CPD interface. This results in the release of the untagged target protein into the supernatant, while the His6-tagged CPD fragment remains immobilized. The supernatant is collected and subjected to minimal downstream processing, such as buffer exchange or gel filtration, to yield highly pure, tag-free protein. The system is compatible with a broad range of expression vectors, including pET22b, pET28a, and derivatives thereof, and can be adapted to generate fusion proteins with varying C-terminal extensions by altering the cloning site to control the number of residual residues left on the target protein after cleavage. For instance, cloning into the SalI site results in the addition of Val-Asp-Ala-Leu, while BamHI cloning with a supplemental leucine codon yields a single leucine residue. The CPD exhibits exceptional specificity, with no detectable transcleavage activity observed even in intrinsically disordered proteins containing multiple leucine residues, ensuring the fidelity of the cleavage event. The system has been successfully applied to diverse target proteins, including green fluorescent protein, biotin ligase, the intracellular domain of gp130, the CRAC-activation domain of STIM1, the parasitic peptidase PfSENP1, and the notoriously insoluble mouse metalloelastase MMP12. In each case, CPD fusion dramatically increased expression yield and solubility compared to His6-tagged or GST-tagged counterparts. Furthermore, CPD fusion conferred resistance to proteolytic degradation during expression and purification, enabling the isolation of proteins previously deemed unstable or intractable. The CPD-mediated cleavage occurs efficiently within one to two hours at temperatures between 4°C and 37°C, in the presence or absence of salt, and is unaffected by standard protease inhibitor cocktails, allowing for maximal flexibility in buffer composition. The system outperforms existing self-cleaving technologies such as intein-CBD or sortase-based systems, which are either too bulky to enhance solubility or lack the ability to improve expression levels. The CPD tag’s small size, InsP6-dependent activation, and autoprocessing nature make it uniquely suited for high-throughput applications, robotic automation, and large-scale production of therapeutic proteins.

## METHODS FOR DETERMINING PRESENCE OF CPD OR ANTI-CPD ANTIBODIES

The presence of the CPD tag or antibodies directed against it can be determined using a variety of established immunological and biochemical techniques. To detect the CPD tag in recombinant protein samples, polyclonal or monoclonal antibodies raised against purified CPD domain can be employed in Western blotting, enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays (ELISA), or immunoprecipitation experiments. These antibodies specifically recognize epitopes within the conserved core of the CPD, enabling detection of the full-length fusion protein prior to cleavage and the residual His6-tagged CPD fragment following cleavage. For quantification of CPD expression levels, competitive ELISA or surface plasmon resonance can be utilized with immobilized CPD as a capture agent and labeled anti-CPD antibodies as detection reagents. To detect anti-CPD antibodies in biological samples such as serum or cell lysates, recombinant CPD-His6 protein can be immobilized on a solid support, incubated with the sample, and bound antibodies detected using enzyme-conjugated secondary antibodies against human or animal immunoglobulins. The specificity of antibody binding can be confirmed by pre-adsorption with purified CPD or by competition with excess soluble CPD antigen. Additionally, the functional activity of the CPD tag can be assessed by its ability to undergo InsP6-dependent cleavage in vitro; the release of the target protein from Ni-NTA resin in the presence of InsP6, but not in its absence, serves as a definitive biochemical assay for the presence of an intact, functional CPD domain. In cases where CPD is expressed as a fusion with an epitope tag such as HA or FLAG, these tags can be used as alternative detection handles in conjunction with anti-HA or anti-FLAG antibodies, providing orthogonal confirmation of CPD fusion expression. The detection of anti-CPD antibodies may also serve diagnostic or immunological purposes, particularly in contexts where exposure to Vibrio cholerae or its toxins is suspected, as CPD-specific antibodies may indicate prior infection or immune response to MARTX toxin components.
