## BACKGROUND

- The blood-brain barrier presents a formidable obstacle to the delivery of therapeutic agents to the central nervous system, as its tightly regulated endothelial cell junctions and efflux transporters severely restrict the passage of large molecules such as antibodies and protein-based drugs. Conventional passive diffusion mechanisms are ineffective for most biologics, necessitating the development of active transport strategies that exploit endogenous receptor-mediated transcytosis pathways. Among these, the transferrin receptor has emerged as a compelling target due to its high expression on brain endothelial cells and its natural role in shuttling iron-bound transferrin across the barrier via receptor-mediated endocytosis. The transferrin receptor undergoes a well-characterized cycle of ligand binding, internalization, acidification, ligand release, and recycling, a process that is critically dependent on pH-induced conformational changes in both the receptor and its ligand. At physiological pH, transferrin binds with high affinity to its receptor, facilitating uptake into clathrin-coated vesicles; upon endosomal acidification to approximately pH 5.5, iron is released and the apotransferrin form exhibits altered binding dynamics, enabling efficient recycling of the receptor back to the cell surface. This pH-dependent binding behavior has inspired efforts to engineer therapeutic molecules that mimic or enhance this natural mechanism, particularly for the purpose of delivering cytotoxic payloads or gene therapies across the blood-brain barrier. Histidine residues, with their side-chain pKa values centered near the endosomal pH threshold, play a pivotal role in mediating these pH-sensitive interactions, as protonation of these residues alters electrostatic and hydrogen-bonding networks critical for molecular recognition. Prior attempts to exploit this phenomenon have focused on modifying the Fc region of antibodies to alter FcRn binding or engineering ligands such as transferrin itself to resist iron release, thereby prolonging receptor engagement. However, these approaches have not fully decoupled the internalization process from the natural recycling fate of the receptor-ligand complex. The development of a novel class of pH-sensitive antibodies that bind their target with high affinity at neutral pH but rapidly dissociate under acidic endosomal conditions represents a significant advancement, enabling enhanced intracellular retention of therapeutic cargo without being sequestered into lysosomal degradation pathways. Such molecules offer a mechanistic advantage over conventional antibodies that remain tightly bound to their target throughout the endocytic journey, often leading to lysosomal degradation and reduced payload delivery efficiency. The transferrin receptor remains a particularly attractive target for brain delivery due to its abundance on the luminal surface of brain capillaries and its capacity to mediate transcytosis without permanent internalization. Natural ligands such as transferrin are internalized and recycled with high fidelity, suggesting that an engineered antibody capable of mimicking this behavior could serve as a superior delivery vehicle. Conjugates of transferrin with cytotoxins have demonstrated efficacy in preclinical models, but their clinical utility has been limited by poor pharmacokinetics, immunogenicity, and insufficient endosomal release. Similarly, anti-transferrin receptor antibodies have shown promise but are often trapped in endosomes due to persistent binding, resulting in lysosomal degradation rather than productive transcytosis. The need for a therapeutic antibody that binds its target with high affinity at blood pH, internalizes efficiently, and then rapidly dissociates within acidic endosomes to avoid degradation and enable cargo release has remained unmet. A solution to this challenge requires precise molecular engineering that introduces pH-sensitive dissociation without compromising target engagement at physiological conditions, a feat that has not been achieved through rational design alone. The invention described herein fulfills this unmet need by providing a novel antibody with engineered histidine residues in its complementarity-determining regions that confer precise pH-dependent binding behavior, enabling enhanced delivery of therapeutic agents across biological barriers and into target cells.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

- The invention provides a pH-dependent antibody that binds with high affinity to the transferrin receptor at neutral pH and exhibits rapid dissociation at acidic pH, thereby enhancing intracellular delivery and reducing lysosomal degradation of conjugated therapeutic agents. The antibody is designed to maintain sub-nanomolar binding affinity to the transferrin receptor at pH 7.4 while dissociating by more than 90% within 10 minutes at pH 5.5, a condition that mimics the endosomal environment. The pH sensitivity is conferred by the presence of multiple histidine residues strategically positioned within the heavy chain complementarity-determining region 1, particularly at positions corresponding to VH31 through VH34, where protonation at low pH disrupts key interactions with the transferrin receptor. Preferred embodiments of the antibody include a single-chain variable fragment (scFv) derived from an anti-transferrin receptor parental clone, wherein the CDRH1 loop contains at least three histidine substitutions, with at least one adjacent residue mutated to proline to stabilize the loop conformation. The antibody may be further engineered as a human monoclonal antibody, a humanized antibody, or a chimeric antibody, and may be expressed as a full-length immunoglobulin, a Fab fragment, or a multivalent format such as a tandem scFv or diabody. The invention further encompasses methods of delivering a therapeutic agent to a target cell by administering a pharmaceutical composition comprising the pH-dependent antibody linked to the agent, wherein the antibody facilitates receptor-mediated endocytosis and subsequent intracellular release of the agent due to its pH-sensitive binding properties. Embodiments of the invention include the use of the antibody to deliver chemotherapeutics, toxins, nucleic acids, enzymes, or imaging agents across the blood-brain barrier or into proliferating cancer cells expressing elevated levels of the transferrin receptor. The invention also includes pharmaceutical compositions comprising the pH-dependent antibody in combination with pharmaceutically acceptable carriers, as well as kits and devices for controlled administration. The antibody may be conjugated covalently or non-covalently to the therapeutic agent, or expressed as a fusion protein, or displayed on the surface of liposomes or nanoparticles for targeted delivery. The invention further includes vectors encoding the antibody, host cells expressing the antibody, and methods for producing the antibody via histidine saturation mutagenesis and yeast surface display screening under controlled pH conditions.

An “effective amount” is that quantity sufficient to achieve a therapeutic or diagnostic outcome, such as enhanced intracellular delivery of a conjugated agent, increased target cell accumulation, or improved survival in a disease model. The pharmaceutical compositions may be formulated for controlled or sustained release using polymeric matrices, liposomes, nanoparticles, or microspheres. “Pharmaceutically acceptable carriers” include those that are non-toxic, non-immunogenic, and compatible with biological systems, such as saline, dextrose, glycerol, ethanol, or combinations thereof. Aqueous carriers include phosphate-buffered saline, Ringer’s solution, or isotonic saline, while non-aqueous carriers may include oils, esters, or dimethyl sulfoxide. “Treating” or “treatment” refers to the administration of the invention for the purpose of ameliorating, preventing, or diagnosing a disease state, including cancer, neurodegenerative disorders, or infectious diseases involving the central nervous system. The antibody may be a monoclonal antibody, a human monoclonal antibody, a chimeric antibody, a humanized antibody, a human antibody, or a recombinant human antibody produced via transgenic animals, phage display, or yeast display technologies. Antibody fragments may be generated by enzymatic cleavage, recombinant expression, or chemical synthesis, and may retain full binding capacity. The antibody light chain variable region (VL) and heavy chain variable region (VH) each contain three complementarity-determining regions (CDRs) that define antigen specificity, with the heavy chain CDRs designated CDRH1, CDRH2, and CDRH3. The heavy chain constant region may be derived from human IgG1, IgG2, IgG3, or IgG4 isotypes, while the kappa or lambda light chain constant regions may be selected based on expression stability. A “fragment” refers to any portion of the antibody that retains binding function, including single-domain antibodies or minimal binding units. The complementarity-determining regions are defined by Kabat or Chothia numbering systems and are critical for antigen recognition. A “pH-sensitive antibody” is defined as an antibody that exhibits a dissociation constant at pH 5.5 that is at least five-fold greater than its dissociation constant at pH 7.4, as measured by surface plasmon resonance, flow cytometry, or magnetic bead binding assays. The transferrin receptor is a transmembrane glycoprotein that mediates cellular uptake of iron-bound transferrin and is overexpressed in many cancers and on the luminal surface of brain capillary endothelial cells. Endocytosis refers to the process by which the receptor-ligand complex is internalized via clathrin-coated pits into early endosomes, followed by acidification and ligand release. Yeast display is a method for expressing antibody fragments on the surface of Saccharomyces cerevisiae, enabling high-throughput screening of binding properties under controlled environmental conditions. A single-chain variable fragment (scFv) is a recombinant protein comprising the variable regions of the heavy and light chains connected by a flexible peptide linker. Saturation mutagenesis involves the systematic replacement of amino acid residues at defined positions with all possible amino acids, enabling the discovery of novel functional variants. The term “protein” and “polypeptide” are used interchangeably to refer to chains of amino acids, whether naturally occurring or synthetically produced. A “therapeutic agent” includes small molecule drugs, toxins, radioisotopes, nucleic acids, peptides, enzymes, or imaging agents. The present invention introduces a pH-dependent antibody that binds the transferrin receptor with high affinity at pH 7.4 and rapidly dissociates at pH 5.5, thereby enhancing intracellular accumulation of conjugated agents. The antibody exhibits enhanced delivery properties due to its ability to escape lysosomal degradation and promote transcytosis or intracellular release. The binding affinity of the antibody at pH 7.4 is in the sub-nanomolar range, while dissociation at pH 5.5 occurs within minutes. The carrier protein is the transferrin receptor, which serves as the target for cellular uptake. The pH values relevant to the invention include physiological pH 7.2–7.4 and endosomal pH 5.0–6.0, with optimal dissociation occurring at pH 5.5. Histidine residues are introduced into the complementarity-determining regions of the antibody, particularly within CDRH1, where their protonation at low pH disrupts hydrogen bonding and electrostatic interactions with the transferrin receptor. ### EXAMPLES

- The following examples illustrate the preparation, characterization, and application of the pH-dependent antibodies described herein. These examples are provided to demonstrate the invention and are not intended to limit its scope. The first example demonstrates the histidine saturation mutagenesis of an anti-transferrin receptor scFv, wherein the CDRH1 region of the parental clone H7 was subjected to comprehensive mutagenesis using a degenerate oligonucleotide library encoding all possible combinations of histidine residues at each position within the loop. The resulting library of approximately 3 × 10⁷ clones was displayed on the surface of Saccharomyces cerevisiae and screened under pH 7.4 conditions to enrich for functional binders, followed by a stringent pH 5.5 dissociation step to isolate clones exhibiting rapid antigen release. Flow cytometric analysis revealed a distinct population of clones with significantly reduced TfR binding at pH 5.5 compared to the wild-type H7, which retained approximately 56% binding under the same conditions. One such clone, designated M16, exhibited less than 10% residual binding at pH 5.5, corresponding to a dissociation efficiency of greater than 90%. Sequence analysis of M16 revealed four histidine substitutions within CDRH1 at positions VH31, VH32, VH33, and VH34, with an additional proline substitution at VH35. The apparent equilibrium binding affinity of M16 at pH 7.4 was determined to be 420 pM, compared to 160 pM for H7, indicating a moderate reduction in affinity that did not compromise target engagement. In contrast, a pH-insensitive mutant, N5, retained 75% binding at pH 5.5 and exhibited an affinity of 190 pM at pH 7.4. Soluble forms of M16 and N5 were purified and tested in a magnetic bead-based dissociation assay, confirming the pH-dependent behavior observed in yeast. M16 retained only 44% of bound TfR after 10 minutes at pH 5.5, while H7 retained 74% and N5 retained 80%. When applied to SK-BR-3 breast cancer cells, M16 demonstrated a 2.6-fold increase in internalized fraction compared to H7, as measured by trypsin-based removal of surface-bound antibody. Co-localization studies with endosomal and lysosomal markers showed that M16 accumulated in distinct, non-overlapping vesicular structures, with significantly reduced Pearson coefficients for EEA1, LAMP1, and LAMP2 compared to H7 and N5. Treatment with bafilomycin A1, an inhibitor of endosomal acidification, completely abolished the enhanced internalization of M16, confirming that its behavior is strictly pH-dependent. These results demonstrate that the introduction of multiple histidine residues into CDRH1 confers a novel pH-sensitive phenotype that enhances intracellular delivery without altering the receptor’s natural trafficking pathway. The invention thus provides a platform for engineering antibodies with tunable endosomal release properties for improved therapeutic efficacy.
