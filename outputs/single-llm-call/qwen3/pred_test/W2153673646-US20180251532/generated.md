## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

Apoptosis is a highly regulated form of programmed cell death essential for maintaining tissue homeostasis, eliminating damaged or infected cells, and shaping organ development during embryogenesis. Dysregulation of apoptotic pathways contributes to a broad spectrum of human diseases, including cancer, neurodegenerative disorders, autoimmune conditions, and ischemic injuries. Central to the intrinsic apoptotic pathway is the mitochondrial outer membrane permeabilization event, which permits the release of pro-apoptotic factors such as cytochrome c into the cytosol, triggering caspase activation and the execution phase of cell death. The BCL-2 protein family governs this critical step through a delicate balance of pro-apoptotic and anti-apoptotic members. Among the pro-apoptotic effectors, BAX plays a pivotal role as a molecular switch that, upon activation, undergoes profound conformational rearrangements to oligomerize and form pores in the mitochondrial outer membrane. In its inactive state, BAX resides as a monomeric protein in the cytosol, where it is held in a closed, autoinhibited conformation. Upon receiving apoptotic signals from BH3-only proteins such as tBID, BIM, or PUMA, BAX undergoes structural transitions that expose its N-terminal domain and C-terminal transmembrane helix, enabling its translocation to mitochondria and subsequent insertion into the membrane. The activation process involves the displacement of the α1–α2 loop, reorientation of helices α5 and α6, and exposure of the α9 helix for membrane integration. These conformational changes are tightly controlled and represent a key node for therapeutic intervention. Aberrant suppression of BAX activation is a hallmark of many cancers, where tumor cells evade apoptosis through overexpression of anti-apoptotic BCL-2 family members or mutations that impair BAX function. Conversely, excessive or uncontrolled BAX activation contributes to neuronal loss in stroke and Alzheimer’s disease, cardiomyocyte death following myocardial infarction, and tissue damage in ischemia-reperfusion injury. Despite the therapeutic potential of modulating BAX activity, the development of pharmacological agents targeting BAX has been hindered by the lack of high-affinity, conformation-specific tools capable of distinguishing between inactive monomeric BAX and its activated, membrane-inserted oligomeric forms. Traditional antibody generation methods have proven inadequate for capturing transient, dynamic conformations of BAX, and small molecules have struggled to achieve sufficient specificity and potency due to the shallow, flexible nature of BAX’s activation interfaces. Consequently, there remains a critical unmet need for novel molecular probes that can selectively bind and modulate BAX in its monomeric, cytosolic state to either inhibit or monitor its activation pathway with high precision.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention discloses novel synthetic antigen-binding fragments (Fabs) that specifically bind to monomeric BAX with high affinity and inhibit its activation by blocking the N-terminal activation site. These Fabs are capable of preventing mitochondrial translocation and oligomerization of BAX, thereby suppressing mitochondrial outer membrane permeabilization and downstream apoptotic events. The invention further encompasses methods of using these Fabs for the diagnosis, prognosis, and therapeutic management of diseases characterized by dysregulated apoptosis, including cancer, neurodegenerative disorders, and ischemic conditions. Additionally, the invention provides recombinant expression systems, conjugation strategies, and screening assays based on these Fabs for the discovery of small-molecule modulators of BAX activity.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

The invention provides a synthetic antigen-binding fragment (Fab) that specifically binds to the monomeric form of human BAX with high affinity and inhibits its activation by obstructing the N-terminal activation site comprising helices α1 and α6 and the intervening α1–α2 loop. This Fab is generated through in vitro selection from a synthetic phage display library designed to maximize diversity in the complementarity-determining regions, particularly within the heavy and light chain CDR3 loops. The Fab binds to BAX in a 1:1 stoichiometric complex, stabilizing the inactive conformation and preventing the structural rearrangements required for mitochondrial translocation and pore formation. The binding interface of the Fab engages solvent-exposed residues on BAX that are critical for interaction with pro-apoptotic BH3-only proteins such as tBID, BIM, and PUMA, thereby competitively inhibiting their ability to trigger BAX activation. The Fab exhibits a dissociation constant in the low nanomolar range, as determined by biolayer interferometry, and retains binding to both full-length BAX and the α9-truncated variant, indicating that its epitope resides distal to the C-terminal transmembrane domain. The Fab does not induce conformational changes in BAX but rather locks it in a closed, cytosolic state, as evidenced by hydrogen-deuterium exchange mass spectrometry and nuclear magnetic resonance spectroscopy. The Fab is produced recombinantly in Escherichia coli using periplasmic expression systems, purified through sequential nickel affinity and protein A chromatography, and formulated in physiologically compatible buffers for therapeutic or diagnostic use. Multiple embodiments of the Fab are disclosed, each differing in the amino acid sequences of their complementarity-determining regions while retaining the capacity to bind BAX and inhibit its activation. The heavy chain complementarity-determining region 1 (CDRH1) comprises the amino acid sequence GYTFTSYWMH, the CDRH2 region comprises the sequence IYSGSGSTYYADSVKG, and the CDRH3 region comprises the sequence ARDGYGYYGMDV. The light chain complementarity-determining region 3 (CDRL3) comprises the sequence QGNSYPLTF. These CDR sequences are derived from the lead clone 3G11, which demonstrates the highest binding affinity and functional inhibition among the panel of Fabs identified. The Fab may be expressed as a full-length immunoglobulin fragment or engineered into single-chain variable fragment (scFv) formats for improved tissue penetration or fusion with effector domains. The Fab may be conjugated to cytotoxic agents, radionuclides, fluorescent dyes, or enzyme tags to enable targeted delivery, imaging, or detection of cells exhibiting elevated levels of monomeric BAX. Administration of the Fab may be achieved via intravenous, intratumoral, intrathecal, or localized delivery routes, depending on the disease context. The Fab is useful in the treatment of cancers characterized by defective apoptosis, including but not limited to lymphoma, leukemia, breast cancer, lung cancer, and melanoma, as well as in neurodegenerative disorders such as Alzheimer’s disease, Parkinson’s disease, and amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, where excessive BAX activation contributes to neuronal loss. The invention further provides a method for measuring the levels of inactive monomeric BAX in biological samples by employing the Fab as a capture or detection reagent in enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays, flow cytometry, or immunohistochemistry. The Fab may be labeled with fluorophores, enzymes, or radioisotopes to facilitate detection in clinical or research settings. The invention also encompasses a method for screening small molecules that modulate BAX activity by employing the Fab in a competitive binding assay, wherein test compounds are evaluated for their ability to displace the Fab from BAX. Small molecules identified through this method are characterized by their capacity to bind the N-terminal activation site of BAX with high specificity and to inhibit BAX-mediated mitochondrial permeabilization. The invention further provides a method for predicting the efficacy of cancer therapies by quantifying the levels of monomeric BAX in patient tumor biopsies using the Fab as a diagnostic reagent, thereby identifying patients most likely to respond to BAX-activating agents or those who may benefit from BAX-inhibiting therapies. The Fab may be labeled with biotin, digoxigenin, or other detectable moieties for use in high-throughput screening platforms.

### Introduction

Synthetic antibody technology has revolutionized the discovery of high-affinity, conformation-specific binders to challenging protein targets, particularly those with dynamic or transient structural states. Traditional methods of antibody generation rely on immunization of animals, which often fails to elicit responses against conserved, non-immunogenic epitopes such as those found on intracellular proteins like BAX. The rationale for utilizing in vitro selection techniques, such as phage display, lies in the ability to directly screen vast libraries of human-derived antibody fragments against purified target antigens under controlled conditions, bypassing immune tolerance mechanisms and enabling the isolation of binders to cryptic or conformationally restricted epitopes. The challenges of raising conformation-specific antibodies against BAX stem from its structural plasticity, the transient nature of its activation intermediates, and the lack of stable, well-defined surfaces for antibody recognition. By selecting against the monomeric, cytosolic form of BAX, the disclosed invention overcomes these limitations and provides a novel class of molecular tools that can distinguish between inactive and active states of BAX, a feat previously unattainable with conventional antibodies.

### Materials and Methods

Recombinant full-length human BAX was produced in Escherichia coli BL21 CodonPlus (DE3)-RIPL cells using the pTYB1 vector system, which fuses BAX to a chitin-binding domain for affinity purification. Protein expression was induced with isopropyl β-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside, and cells were lysed via microfluidization. The BAX fusion protein was captured on chitin beads, cleaved overnight at 4°C, and purified by size-exclusion chromatography to isolate the monomeric fraction. Phage display vectors encoding the 14 selected Fab clones were converted to soluble Fab expression vectors by introducing a stop codon and a C-terminal hexahistidine tag upstream of the p3 gene fusion. Fab proteins were expressed periplasmically in E. coli BL21(DE3) under low-phosphate conditions for 18–22 hours at 30°C. Cells were harvested by centrifugation and lysed using BugBuster reagent. The lysate was clarified by centrifugation and applied to a Ni-NTA resin column equilibrated in TBS buffer. After washing with 20 mM imidazole, bound Fabs were eluted with 250 mM imidazole, dialyzed into PBS (pH 8.0), and further purified using a Protein A affinity column. Eluted Fabs were neutralized with Tris buffer and buffer-exchanged into PBS (pH 7.0) for downstream applications. Protein concentrations were determined by absorbance at 280 nm using extinction coefficients calculated from amino acid composition via ExPASy. The DNA sequences encoding the complementarity-determining regions of the Fabs were derived from phage display output and confirmed by Sanger sequencing. Selected Fab genes were cloned into mammalian expression vectors containing a Myc epitope tag for detection and expression in HEK293 cells. Fab expression and binding to BAX were confirmed by direct and competitive ELISAs, biolayer interferometry, and Western blotting. Binding kinetics were determined using the ForteBio BLItz system with Ni-NTA biosensors. Size-exclusion chromatography was performed using Superdex 75 and 200 columns. Liposome permeabilization assays were conducted using ANTS/DPX-labeled liposomes composed of phosphatidylcholine, phosphatidylinositol, dioleoyl phosphatidylserine, phosphatidylethanolamine, and tetraoleoyl cardiolipin. BAX translocation and mitochondrial release assays were performed using mitochondria isolated from Bak−/− mouse livers. Western blotting was carried out using anti-BAX and anti-cytochrome c antibodies. NMR samples of uniformly 15N-labeled BAX were prepared in sodium phosphate buffer with 5% D2O, and 1H,15N HSQC and TROSY spectra were acquired on a 600 MHz Bruker spectrometer. Hydrogen-deuterium exchange mass spectrometry was performed using an Orbitrap Elite mass spectrometer with online pepsin digestion and peptide separation via reverse-phase chromatography. Structure calculations for the 3G11-BAX complex were performed using HADDOCK with ambiguous restraints derived from HXMS data and CDR residues. Models were generated using CNS and visualized with PyMOL.

### Results

A synthetic phage display library designated Library F was screened against monomeric BAX immobilized on streptavidin-coated or direct-binding plates. After three rounds of selection, 14 distinct Fab clones were isolated, each exhibiting unique complementarity-determining region sequences with no significant homology between clones. These Fabs bound BAX with EC50 values ranging from 2.3 nM to 250 nM, as determined by ELISA. The top three binders—3G11, 3E8, and 2B1—demonstrated dissociation constants below 5 nM by biolayer interferometry. Competitive ELISAs revealed that 13 of the 14 Fabs competed for overlapping epitopes on the surface of BAX, with the exception of Fab 2A5, which bound with significantly lower affinity. Fab 3G11 was selected for detailed mechanistic studies due to its high affinity and potent functional inhibition. In liposomal assays, Fab 3G11 completely inhibited tBID-triggered membrane permeabilization at 2 μM, whereas Fab 2A5 showed no effect. In mitochondrial release assays, Fab 3G11 dose-dependently suppressed cytochrome c release and prevented BAX translocation to mitochondria. NMR titration confirmed the formation of a stable 1:1 complex between 3G11 and BAX without inducing conformational changes. Hydrogen-deuterium exchange mass spectrometry demonstrated significant protection of backbone amides in helices α1 and α6 and the α1–α2 loop upon 3G11 binding, indicating direct engagement of the N-terminal activation site. HADDOCK modeling generated a high-confidence structural ensemble consistent with 3G11 binding to the N-terminal surface of BAX, with key interactions involving residues Lys-21 and Arg-134. Mutagenesis of these residues to glutamate abolished 3G11 binding and functional inhibition, validating the predicted epitope. The Fab did not bind to activated, membrane-inserted BAX, confirming its specificity for the monomeric, cytosolic conformation.

### DISCUSSION

The discovery of synthetic Fabs that bind with high affinity and specificity to monomeric BAX represents a significant advance in the field of apoptosis regulation. Unlike previous inhibitors that target allosteric sites or rely on peptide mimetics, the disclosed Fabs engage the N-terminal activation site—the same interface used by pro-apoptotic BH3-only proteins to initiate BAX activation. This direct competitive inhibition mechanism provides unprecedented precision in modulating BAX function and offers a novel strategy for therapeutic intervention. The ability of Fab 3G11 to block mitochondrial translocation without inducing conformational changes suggests that it stabilizes BAX in a pre-activation state, preventing the cascade of structural rearrangements necessary for pore formation. The fact that these Fabs bind full-length and α9-truncated BAX with comparable affinity confirms that their epitope lies outside the C-terminal transmembrane domain, making them uniquely suited for targeting cytosolic BAX. The competitive ELISA platform developed herein enables the screening of small-molecule libraries for compounds that mimic the inhibitory action of the Fab, thereby accelerating the discovery of orally bioavailable therapeutics. The disclosed Fabs also serve as powerful diagnostic tools for quantifying monomeric BAX levels in patient samples, enabling stratification of patients for apoptosis-targeted therapies. The success of this approach underscores the power of synthetic antibody technology to uncover novel biological interfaces and provides a blueprint for targeting other conformationally dynamic proteins involved in disease.
