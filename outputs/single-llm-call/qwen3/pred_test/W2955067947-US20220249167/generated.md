# DESCRIPTION

- define epilepsy  
Epilepsy is a chronic neurological disorder characterized by the recurrent occurrence of spontaneous seizures, which arise from abnormal, hypersynchronous discharges of neuronal populations within the brain. These seizures manifest as transient alterations in consciousness, motor function, sensory perception, or autonomic activity, depending on the brain regions involved in the initiation and spread of the pathological activity. The condition is not defined by a single etiology but rather by a common clinical phenotype resulting from diverse structural, metabolic, genetic, or acquired disturbances in neural circuitry. Epilepsy affects approximately 1% of the global population and persists despite pharmacological intervention in a substantial subset of patients, necessitating alternative therapeutic strategies. The disorder is typically diagnosed following the occurrence of two or more unprovoked seizures separated by at least 24 hours, and its clinical course is often complicated by cognitive, emotional, and social comorbidities that significantly impair quality of life. The underlying pathophysiology involves a disruption of the balance between excitatory and inhibitory neurotransmission, leading to hyperexcitability and hypersynchrony within localized neural networks that propagate beyond their origin to engage broader cortical and subcortical systems.

- describe seizure characteristics  
Seizures in epilepsy are marked by a stereotyped sequence of electrophysiological events that begin with the abrupt, localized hyperexcitability of a specific brain region known as the epileptogenic zone, followed by the progressive recruitment of connected neural populations through anatomical pathways. The propagation of seizure activity is not random but follows the structural connectivity of the brain, with certain regions acting as critical hubs that facilitate rapid dissemination across distant areas. The clinical expression of a seizure is directly correlated with the spatial extent and temporal dynamics of this recruitment, with involvement of fronto-parietal networks often associated with loss of consciousness, while limbic or temporal lobe activation may produce automatisms, déjà vu, or olfactory hallucinations. The transition from interictal to ictal states is accompanied by a reorganization of network topology, wherein the normally segregated, modular architecture of the brain collapses into a large-scale, highly synchronized state. This transformation is observable in intracranial recordings as a shift from low-amplitude, high-frequency background activity to rhythmic, high-amplitude discharges that spread with predictable latency across connected regions. The duration, intensity, and final spread of the seizure determine its clinical severity and potential for neurological consequence, including post-ictal confusion, focal deficits, or prolonged impairment of cognitive function.

- introduce treatment options  
The primary therapeutic approach for epilepsy involves the administration of antiepileptic drugs, which aim to suppress neuronal hyperexcitability through modulation of ion channels, synaptic transmission, or neuroinflammatory pathways. For patients who fail to achieve seizure control with pharmacological agents, surgical intervention is considered a viable option, particularly when a well-defined epileptogenic zone can be identified and safely resected. Surgical strategies are broadly categorized into resection, which entails the physical removal of the seizure-generating tissue, and disconnection, which involves severing the neural pathways responsible for seizure propagation without removing the tissue itself. Resection is most effective when the epileptogenic zone is localized to non-eloquent cortex, whereas disconnection procedures such as corpus callosotomy or multiple subpial transections are employed when the seizure origin overlaps with critical functional areas. Other emerging interventions include neurostimulation devices, such as vagus nerve stimulators or responsive neurostimulation systems, which deliver electrical pulses to interrupt seizure activity in real time. These approaches, while beneficial in select cases, are not universally effective and often require long-term management with variable outcomes.

- discuss limitations of current treatments  
Despite advances in medical and surgical therapies, a significant proportion of patients with epilepsy remain refractory to existing interventions, primarily due to the multifocal or diffuse nature of epileptogenicity, the involvement of eloquent brain regions, or the inability to precisely delineate the seizure onset zone using current imaging and electrophysiological techniques. Resection of the epileptogenic zone frequently results in incomplete seizure control when multiple foci coexist or when the zone extends into areas critical for language, memory, or motor function, where surgical removal carries unacceptable risks of permanent neurological deficit. Disconnection procedures, while less destructive, often yield suboptimal outcomes due to incomplete interruption of propagation pathways or the emergence of compensatory circuits that re-establish seizure transmission. Furthermore, pharmacological treatments are burdened by systemic side effects, including cognitive slowing, sedation, and metabolic disturbances, which reduce patient adherence and long-term quality of life. The absence of a systematic, patient-specific framework for identifying alternative surgical targets beyond the epileptogenic zone has left a critical therapeutic gap for those who are not candidates for conventional resection or disconnection.

- motivate need for new surgical options  
There is an urgent and unmet clinical need for novel surgical strategies that can effectively suppress seizure propagation without requiring the resection of the epileptogenic zone itself, particularly in cases where the zone is located in eloquent or surgically inaccessible regions. Current approaches are constrained by their reliance on identifying and eliminating the seizure origin, which is often not feasible without causing significant neurological morbidity. A paradigm shift is required toward interventions that target the network mechanisms of seizure spread rather than its source, thereby enabling seizure control even in the presence of an inoperable epileptogenic zone. Such an approach would expand the population of eligible patients, reduce the risk of postoperative deficits, and provide a more flexible, personalized framework for surgical planning. The development of computational methods capable of simulating individual brain connectivity and predicting the functional consequences of network modulation offers a promising pathway toward this goal, enabling the identification of minimally invasive targets that disrupt propagation while preserving normal brain function.

- summarize network analysis of epilepsy  
Recent advances in neuroimaging and computational neuroscience have revealed that the epileptic brain exhibits distinct topological properties compared to the healthy brain, characterized by increased local connectivity, reduced global integration, and a heightened degree of modular segregation. Network analysis based on graph theory has demonstrated that during seizure onset, the brain transitions from a fragmented, modular state to a large-scale, synchronized network, suggesting that seizure control may be achieved by preventing this reorganization rather than eliminating its origin. Structural connectivity maps derived from diffusion MRI have shown that epileptic networks are often organized around a small number of highly connected hub regions, particularly in the temporal and paralimbic cortices, which serve as critical conduits for seizure propagation. These hubs are not necessarily the sites of seizure initiation but are pivotal in amplifying and disseminating activity across distant regions. The persistence of this altered network architecture during interictal periods indicates that the epileptic brain is not merely hyperexcitable but fundamentally rewired, with propagation pathways that are both predictable and modifiable.

- describe graph theory application  
Graph theory provides a mathematical framework for modeling the brain as a network of nodes and edges, where nodes represent anatomical regions and edges represent structural or functional connections between them. In the context of epilepsy, this approach enables the quantification of network properties such as modularity, centrality, path length, and clustering coefficient, which reflect the organization and efficiency of information flow within the brain. By applying algorithms that detect communities or modules—groups of nodes with dense internal connections and sparse external links—researchers have identified distinct subnetworks that remain segregated during interictal states but collapse into a unified structure during seizures. These modular boundaries serve as natural targets for intervention, as their disruption can prevent the global synchronization necessary for seizure manifestation. The application of graph theory thus transforms the clinical problem of epilepsy from a localized lesion to a systemic network disorder, enabling the identification of strategic nodes and edges whose removal can fragment the propagation network without requiring resection of the epileptogenic zone.

- discuss segregation features of epileptic brain  
The epileptic brain exhibits a pronounced segregation of functional modules, with neural activity confined to tightly interconnected subnetworks during interictal periods, in contrast to the more distributed and integrated architecture observed in healthy individuals. This segregation is not merely a passive consequence of pathology but an active organizational principle that facilitates the initiation and containment of seizure activity within specific regions. The modular structure is stabilized by strong intra-module connections and weakened inter-module links, which limit the spread of excitation under normal conditions. However, during seizure onset, these boundaries break down, allowing the hyperexcitable core to recruit distant regions through a small number of high-weight connections that bridge modules. This transition from segregation to integration is a hallmark of epileptiform activity and represents a critical window for therapeutic intervention. By preserving the internal integrity of modules while severing the inter-module connections that enable global synchronization, it becomes possible to suppress seizure propagation without altering the functional architecture of the brain.

- introduce personalized brain network models  
Personalized brain network models are computational representations of an individual’s brain anatomy and connectivity, constructed from high-resolution structural and functional neuroimaging data. These models integrate patient-specific information regarding the location of the epileptogenic zone, the strength and delay of white matter tracts, and the intrinsic excitability of each brain region to simulate the dynamics of seizure initiation and propagation. Unlike population-based models, personalized models account for individual variability in cortical folding, subcortical morphology, and connectivity patterns, enabling the generation of highly accurate simulations of seizure behavior unique to each patient. By incorporating clinical data such as electroencephalographic recordings and lesion maps, these models become not merely descriptive but predictive, allowing clinicians to test the effects of hypothetical surgical interventions in silico before any physical procedure is performed. This capability transforms surgical planning from a heuristic process into a data-driven, individualized optimization problem.

- describe simulation of seizure propagation patterns  
Seizure propagation patterns are simulated using large-scale neural mass models that replicate the dynamics of neuronal populations across the entire brain, coupled through anatomical connectivity derived from diffusion MRI. These simulations incorporate parameters that govern the excitability of each region, the strength and conduction delay of inter-regional connections, and the presence of background noise to mimic physiological variability. By initializing the model with hyperexcitability in the clinically identified epileptogenic zone, the system evolves over time to reproduce the spatiotemporal spread of seizure activity as observed in intracranial recordings. The resulting patterns reveal which regions are recruited, the latency of their activation, and the trajectory of propagation through the structural connectome. These simulations allow for the systematic testing of surgical interventions—such as the removal of specific nodes or disconnection of fiber tracts—by comparing pre- and post-intervention propagation dynamics. The suppression ratio, defined as the percentage reduction in the number of recruited regions, serves as a quantitative metric for evaluating the effectiveness of each proposed intervention.

- motivate modularity analysis  
Modularity analysis is motivated by the observation that the epileptic brain is not uniformly hyperexcitable but instead organized into discrete functional modules that remain segregated during interictal states. The disruption of inter-module connections, rather than the resection of the epileptogenic zone itself, offers a mechanism to prevent the global synchronization that underlies seizure manifestation. By applying modularity algorithms to patient-specific structural networks, it becomes possible to identify the minimal set of connections that bridge these modules and serve as critical conduits for seizure propagation. These connections represent ideal surgical targets because their removal fragments the propagation network without requiring the excision of eloquent tissue. The modularity approach thus provides a principled, data-driven method for identifying surgical interventions that are both effective and minimally invasive, offering a viable alternative for patients who are not candidates for conventional resection.

- introduce method of identifying target zones  
The method of identifying target zones involves the application of modularity analysis to a patient-specific brain network model, with constraints imposed to exclude the epileptogenic zone and other inoperable regions from being selected as surgical targets. The brain is first divided into modules based on the density of structural connections, and the module containing the epileptogenic zone is further subdivided into sub-modules to isolate the core region responsible for seizure initiation. Target zones are then defined as the nodes and edges that connect these sub-modules to other modules, representing the critical pathways through which seizure activity spreads. By systematically varying the resolution parameter of the modularity algorithm, multiple candidate target zones are generated, each corresponding to a different level of network fragmentation. These candidates are subsequently evaluated through simulation to determine their effectiveness in suppressing seizure propagation and their safety in preserving normal brain function.

- provide computerized platform for brain modeling  
A computerized platform for brain modeling is implemented using a validated neuroinformatics framework that integrates structural connectivity data, neural population dynamics, and simulation algorithms into a unified environment. This platform enables the reconstruction of the patient’s brain connectome from diffusion MRI, the assignment of biologically plausible parameters to each node based on clinical and electrophysiological data, and the execution of high-fidelity simulations of seizure propagation under various surgical conditions. The platform supports the automated derivation of target zones through modularity analysis, the simulation of intervention outcomes, and the iterative refinement of surgical plans based on feedback from safety and effectiveness evaluations. It is designed to be user-friendly for clinical neurologists and neurosurgeons, providing visualizations of network structure, propagation trajectories, and candidate targets in anatomical space, thereby bridging the gap between computational neuroscience and clinical decision-making.

- model epileptogenic zone and propagation  
The epileptogenic zone is modeled as a region of elevated excitability within the brain network, characterized by a specific parameter value that induces spontaneous, self-sustained oscillations representative of seizure onset. Surrounding regions are assigned lower excitability values consistent with a propagation zone, allowing them to be recruited by the epileptogenic zone through anatomical connections. The coupling between regions is defined by the strength and conduction delay of white matter tracts, which determine the speed and magnitude of signal transmission. The model is initialized with seizure activity in the epileptogenic zone and allowed to evolve according to the dynamics of a neural mass model that reproduces the characteristic waveforms of local field potentials observed in intracranial recordings. The resulting spatiotemporal pattern of recruitment reveals the propagation pathways and identifies regions that are most consistently activated following seizure initiation.

- load models into computerized platform  
Patient-specific brain network models, including structural connectivity matrices, excitability parameters, and anatomical labels, are loaded into the computerized platform through standardized data interfaces that ensure compatibility with neuroimaging formats and simulation engines. The platform automatically aligns the model with a standardized brain atlas, assigns physiological parameters based on clinical input, and initializes the simulation environment with the defined epileptogenic zone. The entire model, including all nodes, edges, and dynamic parameters, is encapsulated within a computational container that preserves the integrity of the patient’s unique network architecture. This allows for seamless execution of modularity analysis, simulation of surgical interventions, and evaluation of outcomes without manual reconfiguration or data loss.

- identify estimated epileptogenic zone  
The estimated epileptogenic zone is identified through a combination of clinical evaluation, electroencephalographic recordings, and structural neuroimaging findings, which are integrated into the computational model as a predefined set of brain regions with elevated excitability. This estimation is not assumed to be absolute but serves as the initial condition for simulation and target derivation, with the understanding that the true epileptogenic zone may extend beyond the clinically identified region. The model accounts for this uncertainty by allowing the zone to be adjusted within a plausible anatomical range during iterative refinement, ensuring that the derived target zones remain robust to variations in the initial estimation.

- personalize computerized platform  
The computerized platform is personalized by incorporating the patient’s unique structural connectivity, anatomical landmarks, and clinical history into the simulation environment. This includes the precise mapping of white matter tracts derived from diffusion MRI, the assignment of region-specific excitability parameters based on intracranial EEG findings, and the inclusion of known inoperable zones identified through pre-surgical mapping. The platform adapts its algorithms and visualization tools to reflect the individual’s network topology, ensuring that all subsequent analyses—modularity, simulation, and safety evaluation—are grounded in the patient’s own neuroanatomy rather than population averages. This personalization is essential for generating clinically relevant and actionable surgical recommendations.

- carry out modularity analysis  
Modularity analysis is carried out using a spectral algorithm that partitions the brain network into non-overlapping modules by maximizing the density of connections within modules while minimizing connections between them. The analysis is performed iteratively across a range of resolution parameters to generate multiple modular configurations, each representing a different scale of network segregation. Constraints are applied to ensure that the epileptogenic zone and other inoperable regions remain within the same module as their anatomical neighbors, preventing them from being selected as target zones. The resulting modular structure is used to identify the nodes and edges that serve as bridges between the epileptogenic sub-module and other modules, which are then proposed as candidate surgical targets.

- derive potential target zones  
Potential target zones are derived by identifying the nodes and edges that connect the epileptogenic sub-module to other modules in the modular structure obtained from modularity analysis. These nodes represent brain regions whose resection would disrupt the propagation pathway, while the edges represent the white matter tracts whose disconnection would prevent seizure spread. Each candidate target zone is associated with a specific modular configuration and resolution parameter, resulting in a diverse set of potential interventions that vary in the number of regions or tracts involved and the degree of seizure suppression they achieve. This diversity allows clinicians to select a target zone that balances effectiveness with surgical feasibility and safety.

- evaluate target zones' effectiveness  
The effectiveness of each target zone is evaluated by simulating seizure propagation in the patient-specific network model before and after the removal of the proposed nodes or edges. The suppression ratio is calculated as the percentage reduction in the number of brain regions recruited following seizure initiation, providing a quantitative measure of how well each target zone prevents the spread of activity. Target zones that achieve a suppression ratio exceeding a predefined threshold are deemed effective, while those with minimal impact are discarded. The simulation accounts for the dynamic interactions between regions, ensuring that the evaluation reflects the true network-level consequences of the intervention rather than isolated anatomical changes.

- evaluate target zones' safety  
The safety of each target zone is evaluated by simulating the brain’s response to controlled stimulation of key regions known to activate resting-state networks, such as the default mode, memory, and motor networks. The spatiotemporal patterns of activation before and after removal of the target zone are compared using a similarity coefficient derived from principal component analysis. A high similarity coefficient indicates that the stimulation-induced network dynamics remain intact, suggesting minimal disruption to normal brain function. A low coefficient, indicating substantial alteration of the response pattern, signals potential risk of postoperative cognitive or neurological deficits. Target zones that reduce similarity below a predefined threshold are classified as unsafe and excluded from further consideration.

- identify safe target zones  
Safe target zones are identified through an iterative feedback process in which candidate zones are evaluated for both effectiveness and safety. Those that achieve high seizure suppression while preserving the integrity of resting-state network responses are retained, while those that compromise safety are flagged for refinement. If a target zone contains multiple nodes, the node whose removal most significantly alters the stimulation response is identified as a critical node and designated as inoperable. The modularity analysis is then repeated with this updated constraint, yielding a new set of target zones that avoid the critical node. This process continues until a target zone is derived that meets both effectiveness and safety criteria, ensuring that the final recommendation is both therapeutically potent and clinically viable.

- discuss in-silico surgical approach  
The in-silico surgical approach represents a paradigm shift in the planning of epilepsy surgery by replacing empirical decision-making with a computational framework that simulates the effects of intervention on an individual’s unique brain network. Rather than relying on broad anatomical guidelines or population-based statistics, this approach derives patient-specific surgical targets through the integration of structural connectivity, network dynamics, and functional constraints. The method does not assume that the epileptogenic zone must be removed but instead focuses on disrupting the propagation pathways that transform a focal seizure into a generalized event. By simulating the consequences of each proposed intervention, the approach enables the identification of minimally invasive targets that offer seizure control without compromising eloquent function. This strategy is particularly valuable for patients with multifocal or eloquent epileptogenic zones who are currently deemed unsuitable for conventional surgery.

This connectome serves as the anatomical scaffold upon which all subsequent simulations and analyses are performed.

The method is sensitive to subtle changes in network dynamics that may not be apparent through conventional imaging or electrophysiological measures.

- evaluate safety of target zone  
The safety of the target zone is evaluated by determining whether the removal of the proposed nodes or edges causes a significant deviation in the brain’s response to stimulation, as measured by the similarity coefficient. A target zone is deemed safe if the mean similarity coefficient across all stimulated regions exceeds a predefined threshold, indicating that the intervention preserves the integrity of resting-state networks. If the coefficient falls below this threshold, the target zone is flagged as unsafe, and the critical node responsible for the greatest deviation is identified and excluded from future analyses. This iterative process ensures that the final target zone is both effective and safe.

- illustrate target zone evaluation  
Target zone evaluation is illustrated through a series of simulations that visualize the spatiotemporal dynamics of seizure propagation and network response before and after intervention. These visualizations include heatmaps of recruitment patterns, trajectories of network activation, and overlays of target zones on anatomical brain maps. The simulations demonstrate how the removal of a specific node or edge fragments the propagation network, reduces the number of recruited regions, and preserves the integrity of resting-state responses. These illustrations provide clinicians with an intuitive understanding of the proposed intervention and its expected outcomes.

- discuss effectiveness evaluation  
Effectiveness evaluation is conducted by simulating seizure propagation in the patient-specific network model and quantifying the suppression ratio—the percentage reduction in the number of brain regions recruited following seizure initiation. A target zone is deemed effective if the suppression ratio exceeds a predefined threshold, indicating that the intervention significantly limits the spread of seizure activity. The evaluation accounts for the dynamic interactions between regions and is validated across multiple simulation conditions to ensure robustness. The results are presented alongside safety scores to enable a balanced assessment of each candidate target zone.

- discuss personalized in-silico surgical approach  
The personalized in-silico surgical approach represents a transformative methodology for planning epilepsy surgery by leveraging patient-specific network models to identify surgical targets that are both effective and safe. Unlike conventional approaches that focus on resecting the epileptogenic zone, this method targets the propagation pathways that enable seizures to spread, allowing for seizure control without compromising eloquent brain regions. The approach integrates structural connectivity, modularity analysis, and network simulation into a unified framework that iteratively refines surgical options based on feedback from effectiveness and safety evaluations. This personalized, data-driven method expands the pool of eligible patients, reduces the risk of postoperative deficits, and provides a systematic alternative to empirical surgical planning.
