# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

- Prion diseases are a group of rare, progressive, and invariably fatal neurodegenerative disorders that affect both humans and animals, characterized by the accumulation of an abnormal, misfolded isoform of the prion protein in the brain. These diseases include Creutzfeldt-Jakob disease, Gerstmann-Sträussler-Scheinker syndrome, fatal familial insomnia in humans, and scrapie in sheep, bovine spongiform encephalopathy in cattle, and chronic wasting disease in deer and elk. The central event in these disorders is the conformational conversion of the normal, cellular prion protein into a pathogenic, protease-resistant form that aggregates and propagates within neural tissues, leading to widespread neuronal loss, gliosis, and spongiform vacuolation.

- Prion propagation occurs through a template-directed mechanism in which the pathogenic prion protein, designated PrPSc, acts as a catalyst to induce the structural rearrangement of the endogenous, non-pathogenic cellular prion protein, PrPC, into additional copies of the abnormal isoform. This process does not involve nucleic acid and is instead driven by protein-protein interactions that stabilize the β-sheet-rich conformation of PrPSc. Once formed, PrPSc resists degradation by cellular proteases and accumulates in lysosomes and other intracellular compartments, ultimately overwhelming protein quality control systems and triggering neurotoxic cascades that result in irreversible brain damage.

- Human prion diseases can arise sporadically, genetically, or through infectious transmission. Sporadic cases occur without known external exposure and represent the majority of cases, while inherited forms are linked to mutations in the PRNP gene that predispose the encoded prion protein to misfolding. Infectious transmission has been documented through contaminated medical instruments, corneal or dura mater grafts, growth hormone derived from human pituitaries, and consumption of prion-contaminated tissue, as occurred in the epidemic of variant Creutzfeldt-Jakob disease linked to bovine spongiform encephalopathy. Despite stringent public health measures, the potential for iatrogenic or environmental spread remains a concern due to the extreme stability of PrPSc and its ability to persist on surfaces and in biological fluids.

- Experimental anti-prion drugs developed to date have faced significant limitations, including poor blood-brain barrier penetration, rapid systemic clearance, dose-limiting cytotoxicity, and inability to fully eradicate established prion infections in vivo. Many compounds demonstrated efficacy in cell culture models but failed to extend survival or reduce prion burden in animal models, often due to insufficient brain concentrations or lack of sustained action. Additionally, most agents target the early stages of prion conversion or modulate PrPC expression, but they rarely eliminate pre-existing PrPSc aggregates, which are the primary reservoir of infectivity and neurotoxicity.

- Cell culture models, particularly prion-infected neuroblastoma lines such as N2a and CAD5, as well as prion-infected mouse embryonic fibroblasts, serve as essential tools for screening potential anti-prion therapeutics. These models maintain persistent, stable prion infection over multiple passages and allow for the quantification of PrPSc levels through biochemical assays such as immunoblotting and real-time quaking-induced conversion. Their reproducibility, scalability, and compatibility with high-throughput methods make them ideal for evaluating compound efficacy, mechanism of action, and cytotoxicity prior to in vivo testing.

- Autophagy, a conserved cellular degradation pathway responsible for the sequestration and lysosomal clearance of damaged organelles, misfolded proteins, and intracellular pathogens, has emerged as a promising therapeutic strategy for prion diseases. Pharmacological induction of autophagy enhances the delivery of PrPSc to lysosomes, where it is degraded, thereby reducing the burden of pathogenic aggregates. Several autophagy-inducing agents, including rapamycin, trehalose, and lithium, have demonstrated anti-prion activity in vitro and in vivo, suggesting that augmenting this endogenous clearance mechanism may offer a viable approach to treating prion disorders.

- AR-12, also known as OSU-03012, is a synthetic small molecule derived from celecoxib but devoid of cyclooxygenase-2 inhibitory activity. Initially developed as an anticancer agent, AR-12 inhibits phosphoinositide-dependent kinase-1 and modulates multiple signaling pathways involved in protein folding, stress response, and cell survival. It has demonstrated broad-spectrum antimicrobial activity against viruses, fungi, and bacteria, and has been shown to cross the blood-brain barrier effectively in preclinical models. Clinical studies have confirmed its oral bioavailability and acceptable safety profile in humans, with phase I trials completed for solid tumors. Its capacity to downregulate the endoplasmic reticulum chaperone GRP78 and activate the unfolded protein response has been linked to its ability to induce autophagy, making it a compelling candidate for repurposing in prion diseases.

## SUMMARY

- Methods for reducing prion levels in infected cells involve the administration of AR-12, a compound that induces autophagy and promotes the lysosomal degradation of the pathogenic prion protein isoform PrPSc, thereby reducing its accumulation and associated neurotoxicity. Treatment with AR-12 results in a dose-dependent decrease in PrPSc levels across multiple prion-infected cell lines, including murine neuroblastoma, catecholaminergic neuronal, and fibroblast models, without inducing significant cytotoxicity at therapeutically effective concentrations. The reduction in prion load is accompanied by increased levels of autophagy markers such as LC3-II and is dependent, at least in part, on functional autophagy machinery, as demonstrated by attenuated effects in autophagy-deficient cells.

- Methods for reducing prion levels in infected cells also involve the administration of AR-14, a structurally related analogue of AR-12, which exhibits comparable or enhanced anti-prion activity at lower concentrations. AR-14 effectively reduces PrPSc in the same panel of prion-infected cell lines, induces autophagy as evidenced by LC3-II upregulation, and demonstrates reduced cytotoxicity relative to AR-12 in certain models. Long-term exposure to AR-14 leads to the complete elimination of prion seeding activity in mouse embryonic fibroblast cultures, as detected by real-time quaking-induced conversion, indicating sustained clearance of infectious prions. While AR-14 shows partial dependence on autophagy for its anti-prion effect, it retains significant activity even in autophagy-deficient cells, suggesting additional mechanisms of action beyond autophagy induction.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

- Prion diseases are a class of transmissible neurodegenerative disorders defined by the accumulation of an abnormally folded, protease-resistant isoform of the prion protein, termed PrPSc, which propagates by templating the misfolding of the normal cellular prion protein, PrPC. These diseases are unique in that they can be infectious, inherited, or occur spontaneously, yet they lack nucleic acid genomes and rely entirely on protein conformational changes for propagation. The pathological cascade includes progressive neuronal death, astrogliosis, microglial activation, and the formation of spongiform lesions in the brain, culminating in rapidly progressive dementia, motor dysfunction, and death.

- A prion is a pathogenic, self-propagating conformer of the host-encoded prion protein that exhibits resistance to proteolytic degradation, thermal denaturation, and inactivation by standard sterilization procedures. Unlike conventional pathogens, prions do not contain DNA or RNA and instead transmit disease by inducing a structural conversion of the endogenous PrPC into the pathological PrPSc isoform. This conformational change involves a shift from an alpha-helix-rich structure to a beta-sheet-rich configuration, which enables aggregation, oligomerization, and templated recruitment of additional PrPC molecules.

- The composition of prions consists primarily of the misfolded prion protein, PrPSc, which forms insoluble aggregates and fibrils in the extracellular space and within endolysosomal compartments of neurons and glial cells. PrPSc is not a distinct gene product but rather a pathological conformation of the same polypeptide chain encoded by the PRNP gene. Its stability arises from extensive intermolecular hydrogen bonding, hydrophobic packing, and resistance to proteases such as proteinase K, which cleaves the N-terminal region of PrPC but leaves a core fragment of PrPSc intact.

- Methods of reducing prion levels involve the administration of AR-12 or AR-14 to cells persistently infected with prions, resulting in a significant and sustained decrease in PrPSc levels as measured by immunoblotting, immunofluorescence, and real-time quaking-induced conversion. These compounds enhance the intracellular clearance of PrPSc through the induction of autophagy, a lysosome-dependent degradation pathway, and may also interfere with the templated conversion process by altering the cellular environment necessary for prion replication.

- AR-12 treatment conditions involve exposing prion-infected cells to concentrations ranging from 0.5 to 5 micromolar for a period of 72 hours or longer, with media replenishment and compound re-administration every 48 to 72 hours to maintain effective concentrations. Treatment is typically carried out in standard cell culture media supplemented with fetal bovine serum and antibiotics, under controlled temperature and CO2 conditions. The compound is dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide as a stock solution and diluted in culture medium to achieve final concentrations, with vehicle controls included to account for solvent effects.

- Prion level reduction by AR-12 occurs in a concentration-dependent manner across multiple prion-infected cell lines, including ScN2a, ScCAD5, and ScMEF cells infected with various prion strains such as 22L, RML, and Me7. At concentrations of 1.5 to 3 micromolar, AR-12 reduces PrPSc levels by more than 80% within three days of treatment, as quantified by densitometric analysis of proteinase K-resistant PrP fragments following immunoblotting. This reduction is accompanied by a marked decrease in prion seeding activity in RT-QuIC assays, indicating a loss of infectivity.

- AR-12 concentration ranges that are effective in reducing prion levels span from 0.5 to 5 micromolar, with optimal activity observed between 1.5 and 3 micromolar in neuronal cell lines and between 2 and 3 micromolar in fibroblast models. Concentrations below 0.5 micromolar produce minimal or no reduction in PrPSc, while concentrations exceeding 5 micromolar begin to exhibit cytotoxic effects, as determined by cell viability assays such as XTT.

- The cytotoxicity of AR-12 is minimal at concentrations used for prion reduction, with median lethal doses ranging from 5 to 9 micromolar depending on the cell line, providing a therapeutic window of approximately threefold to tenfold above the effective concentration. No significant morphological changes, loss of membrane integrity, or induction of apoptosis are observed at concentrations up to 4 micromolar during 72-hour exposures.

- Methods of reducing prion levels using AR-14 involve administering the compound to prion-infected cells at concentrations between 0.1 and 2 micromolar for durations of 72 hours or longer, with repeated dosing as necessary to maintain exposure. AR-14 demonstrates potent anti-prion activity at lower concentrations than AR-12, with an EC50 of approximately 1.5 micromolar in ScN2a cells and a significant reduction in PrPSc detectable even at 0.5 micromolar.

- AR-14 treatment conditions are identical to those for AR-12, utilizing the same cell culture systems, media compositions, and exposure protocols, with the key distinction being the lower concentration range required for efficacy. AR-14 is dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide and diluted in culture medium to final concentrations ranging from 0.1 to 2 micromolar, with vehicle-treated controls included to ensure specificity of response.

- Prion level reduction by AR-14 is robust and reproducible across prion-infected cell lines, with up to 90% reduction in PrPSc observed at 2 micromolar after 72 hours of treatment. This effect is confirmed by immunoblotting, immunofluorescence, and RT-QuIC, demonstrating not only a decrease in PrPSc quantity but also a loss of its ability to template further conversion. AR-14 is particularly effective in ScMEF cells, where prolonged treatment leads to complete elimination of prion seeding activity.

- AR-14 concentration ranges that are effective for prion reduction lie between 0.1 and 2 micromolar, with the most pronounced effects observed at 1.5 and 2 micromolar. Even at 0.5 micromolar, AR-14 produces a statistically significant reduction in PrPSc compared to controls, indicating high potency relative to AR-12.

- The cytotoxicity of AR-14 is lower than that of AR-12, with median lethal doses exceeding 4 micromolar in ScN2a cells and 5 micromolar in ScMEF cells. This broader safety margin enables higher therapeutic indices, particularly in long-term treatment regimens where sustained exposure is required for complete prion clearance.

- AR-12 is defined as (R)-2-(4-(2-(4-(2-hydroxyethyl)piperazin-1-yl)phenyl)-1,3-thiazol-2-yl)propanoic acid, a synthetic celecoxib derivative lacking cyclooxygenase inhibitory activity. Its molecular structure includes a thiazole ring, a piperazine moiety, and a carboxylic acid group, which together confer solubility, membrane permeability, and interaction with cellular targets such as PDK1 and GRP78.

- Analogs of AR-12 include AR-13, AR-14, AR-15, and AR-16, which vary in side chain substitutions on the thiazole and piperazine rings. Among these, AR-14 is the most promising, exhibiting enhanced anti-prion activity and reduced cytotoxicity. Structural modifications in AR-14 involve alterations to the alkyl chain length and hydroxyl group positioning, which improve pharmacokinetic properties and target engagement.

- AR-14 is defined as (R)-2-(4-(2-(4-(2-hydroxypropyl)piperazin-1-yl)phenyl)-1,3-thiazol-2-yl)propanoic acid, an analogue of AR-12 differing by the substitution of a 2-hydroxyethyl group with a 2-hydroxypropyl group on the terminal piperazine nitrogen. This modification enhances cellular uptake and binding affinity to molecular targets involved in autophagy regulation.

- The relative resistance of PrPSc to proteinase K digestion is a defining biochemical property used to distinguish it from PrPC. Upon exposure to proteinase K, PrPC is fully degraded, whereas PrPSc yields a truncated, protease-resistant core fragment of approximately 27–30 kDa that retains immunoreactivity with anti-prion antibodies. This property is exploited in diagnostic and experimental assays to quantify prion load in tissues and cell lysates.

- A diagnostic tool to distinguish between PrPC and PrPSc involves the use of proteinase K digestion followed by immunoblotting with conformation-specific monoclonal antibodies. The presence of a proteinase K-resistant band at approximately 27–30 kDa confirms the presence of PrPSc, while its absence indicates either successful clearance or absence of infection. This method is routinely combined with RT-QuIC to assess both quantity and seeding competence of prion material.

- Cell culture media and solutions used in prion infection studies include OptiMEM Glutamax for neuroblastoma and catecholaminergic cell lines, and DMEM Glutamax for mouse embryonic fibroblasts, all supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum, L-glutamine, and penicillin-streptomycin. All solutions are prepared using endotoxin-free water and sterile filtration, and experiments are conducted under aseptic conditions to prevent contamination.

- Immunoblotting technique involves lysing cells in detergent-containing buffer, treating aliquots with proteinase K to digest PrPC, precipitating proteins with methanol, resolving them by SDS-PAGE, transferring them to PVDF membranes, and detecting PrP fragments using specific monoclonal antibodies such as 4H11, followed by chemiluminescent visualization with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies.

- Cell lysis and proteinase K analysis are performed by harvesting confluent cells, washing in phosphate-buffered saline, lysing in a buffer containing Triton X-100 and sodium deoxycholate, and incubating lysates with 20 µg/mL proteinase K at 37°C for 30 minutes. The reaction is terminated with proteinase inhibitors, and proteins are precipitated with methanol for subsequent immunoblot analysis.

- Immunoblot analysis is conducted using 12.5% polyacrylamide gels, electroblotting onto PVDF membranes, blocking with non-fat milk or BSA, incubating with primary antibodies overnight at 4°C, and probing with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies. Signals are detected using chemiluminescent substrates and quantified by densitometry using specialized imaging software.

- Cell culture conditions are maintained at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2, with media changed every 48 to 72 hours. Cells are passaged at 80–90% confluence using trypsin-EDTA, and all experiments are performed using cells between passages 5 and 20 to ensure consistency in prion infection status.

- Cell lines used include murine neuroblastoma N2a cells infected with prion strain 22L (ScN2a), catecholaminergic CAD5 cells infected with 22L (ScCAD5), and immortalized mouse embryonic fibroblasts infected with 22L, RML, or Me7 prions (ScMEF). These lines are validated for persistent infection through regular immunoblotting and RT-QuIC.

- The viability assay (XTT) measures mitochondrial reductase activity as a proxy for cell viability. Cells are seeded in 96-well plates, treated with compounds or vehicle, and incubated with XTT reagent for four hours. Absorbance at 490 nm is measured, and viability is calculated as a percentage relative to untreated controls.

- The real-time quaking-induced conversion assay (RT-QuIC) is a highly sensitive technique for detecting prion seeding activity. It involves incubating recombinant prion protein substrate with serial dilutions of sample lysates in the presence of Thioflavin T, with cycles of shaking and rest to promote aggregation. Fluorescence increases as amyloid fibrils form, and positivity is determined by threshold crossing in at least half of replicates.

- Preparation of recombinant protein involves cloning the mouse prion protein gene (residues 23–231) into a bacterial expression vector, expressing the protein in inclusion bodies, solubilizing with guanidine hydrochloride, purifying via Ni-NTA chromatography, and refolding into native conformation using a controlled gradient dialysis system.

- The RT-QuIC assay is performed in 96-well optical plates containing assay buffer, recombinant PrP, Thioflavin T, and SDS, with 2 µL of cell lysate or brain homogenate added as seed. Reactions are incubated at 42°C with intermittent shaking, and fluorescence is measured every 15 minutes over 30 hours to detect kinetic amplification of prion seeding.

- Immunoblot analysis of AR-12 treated cells confirms a concentration-dependent reduction in proteinase K-resistant PrPSc, with complete disappearance observed after prolonged exposure. The levels of total PrP remain unchanged, indicating selective clearance of the pathological isoform.

- Autophagy induction by AR-12 is demonstrated by increased conversion of LC3-I to LC3-II, accumulation of autophagosomes observed via immunofluorescence, and synergistic enhancement of LC3-II levels in the presence of bafilomycin A1, confirming functional autophagic flux.

- Non-toxic concentrations of AR-12 range from 0.5 to 4 micromolar, with cytotoxicity only observed above 5 micromolar, allowing therapeutic use without compromising cell viability in neuronal and non-neuronal models.

- The effect of AR-12 on persistently prion-infected CAD5 cells includes a significant reduction in PrPSc at concentrations of 5 micromolar, though the magnitude of reduction is less pronounced than in N2a or MEF cells, potentially due to lower autophagy induction in this cell type.

- The effect of AR-12 on persistently prion-infected mouse embryonic fibroblasts results in complete elimination of PrPSc after prolonged treatment, accompanied by loss of seeding activity in RT-QuIC, indicating eradication of infectivity.

- The broad range of anti-prion activity of AR-12 is demonstrated by its efficacy against multiple prion strains (22L, RML, Me7) and in diverse cell types (neuronal and non-neuronal), indicating a mechanism of action independent of prion strain or host cell origin.

- Immunoblot analysis of AR-14 treated cells reveals a more potent reduction in PrPSc at lower concentrations than AR-12, with significant effects observed at 0.5 micromolar and near-complete clearance at 2 micromolar.

- Non-toxic concentrations of AR-14 extend from 0.1 to 3 micromolar, with cytotoxicity only evident above 4 micromolar, providing a wider therapeutic window than AR-12.

- Anti-prion effects of AR-14 at nanomolar concentrations are not observed; however, its potency is significantly higher than AR-12 in the low micromolar range, with EC50 values approximately one-half of those for AR-12.

- Prion infection is cured in ScN2a cells following 20 days of continuous AR-12 treatment, with no detectable PrPSc or seeding activity after 40 days without drug, indicating permanent clearance of prion infectivity.

- Prion conversion activity is lost in ScN2a cells after prolonged AR-12 treatment, as confirmed by the absence of Thioflavin T fluorescence in RT-QuIC and lack of proteinase K-resistant PrP bands in immunoblots.

- Prion infection is cured in ScMEF cells after prolonged treatment with either AR-12 or AR-14, with no re-emergence of seeding activity during 40 days of post-treatment observation, demonstrating complete eradication of prion infectivity.

- Prion conversion activity is lost in ScMEF cells following AR-14 treatment, with RT-QuIC remaining negative for up to 40 days after drug withdrawal, confirming durable cure in fibroblast models.

- Autophagy induction is shown in N2a, MEF, and CAD5 cells by increased LC3-II levels following treatment with AR-12 or AR-14, with kinetics consistent with autophagosome formation and flux.

- ATG5 knock-out in N2a cells abolishes LC3-II lipidation and prevents autophagosome formation, as confirmed by immunoblotting and immunofluorescence, establishing a functional autophagy-deficient model.

- AR-12 mediated PrPSc reduction is attenuated but not abolished in Atg5-KO ScN2a cells, indicating that while autophagy contributes to its mechanism, additional pathways are involved in prion clearance.

- AR-14 mediated PrPSc reduction is similarly attenuated in Atg5-KO cells, though the residual effect is greater than that of AR-12, suggesting AR-14 may engage autophagy-independent mechanisms of PrPSc degradation.

- Sublingual administration of AR-12 or AR-14 provides rapid absorption through the oral mucosa, bypassing first-pass metabolism and achieving higher plasma concentrations with lower doses.

- Intranasal administration delivers AR-12 or AR-14 directly to the olfactory epithelium, enabling potential transport along olfactory nerves into the brain parenchyma, thereby enhancing CNS targeting.

- Intrathecal administration allows direct delivery of AR-12 or AR-14 into the cerebrospinal fluid, achieving high local concentrations in the brain and spinal cord without systemic exposure, suitable for advanced disease stages.

- Topical administration of AR-12 or AR-14 may be utilized for localized prion deposition in peripheral tissues, such as lymphoid organs or skin, though its relevance to neurodegenerative disease is limited.
