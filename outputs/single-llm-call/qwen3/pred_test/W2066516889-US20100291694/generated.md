## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

The complement system is a highly conserved component of the innate immune system that plays a central role in host defense against pathogens, clearance of immune complexes, and modulation of adaptive immune responses. It comprises a cascade of over 30 soluble and membrane-bound proteins that are activated in a tightly regulated, sequential manner through three distinct pathways: the classical, lectin, and alternative pathways. Central to all three pathways is the proteolytic cleavage of complement component C3, a pivotal molecule whose activation generates biologically active fragments and initiates downstream effector functions. C3 is synthesized primarily in the liver and circulates in plasma as an inactive zymogen. Upon activation, C3 is cleaved by C3 convertases into two fragments: C3a, a potent anaphylatoxin that promotes inflammation, and C3b, a large opsonin that covalently attaches to pathogen or damaged cell surfaces. The covalent binding of C3b to target surfaces enables recognition by complement receptors on phagocytic cells, thereby facilitating immune clearance. Additionally, C3b serves as the essential catalytic subunit for the assembly of C3 and C5 convertases, which amplify complement activation and drive formation of the membrane attack complex. The generation of C3b involves a dramatic conformational rearrangement of the parent C3 molecule, exposing a reactive thioester bond that facilitates covalent attachment and creating novel binding interfaces for regulatory and effector proteins. 

C3b itself is subject to further proteolytic processing by complement regulators such as factor I and cofactors like factor H or complement receptor 1 (CR1), yielding biologically distinct fragments including iC3b and C3c. These fragments retain partial opsonic activity but lose the capacity to form convertases, thereby serving as molecular markers of complement activation and termination. The interaction of C3b with its regulators is critical for preventing uncontrolled complement activation on host tissues. Among the key regulators that bind C3b is Complement Receptor Immunoglobulin (CRIg), a transmembrane protein expressed predominantly on tissue-resident macrophages and Kupffer cells. CRIg was initially identified as a ligand for C3b and iC3b and was shown to mediate phagocytosis of complement-opsonized particles. Structural studies have revealed that CRIg binds to a specific region on the β-chain of C3b, effectively competing with other regulators and convertase components. This interaction is highly selective for the activated conformation of C3b and does not occur with native C3, making CRIg a natural sentinel of complement activation. The C3b:CRIg complex has been structurally characterized, demonstrating that CRIg engages a conserved epitope formed only after the conformational transition of C3 to C3b, thereby acting as a molecular sensor of complement activation. 

The alternative pathway of complement activation is unique in its ability to initiate spontaneously on foreign or damaged surfaces without requiring antibody or pattern recognition molecules, making it a major driver of chronic inflammation in autoimmune and degenerative diseases. Tight regulation of this pathway is essential, as dysregulation leads to tissue injury in conditions such as age-related macular degeneration, atypical hemolytic uremic syndrome, and systemic lupus erythematosus. Multiple endogenous regulators, including factor H, factor I, CR1, and DAF, act to limit convertase formation and accelerate decay. CRIg represents a distinct class of regulator that operates primarily at the tissue level, where it directly binds and neutralizes C3b, preventing its incorporation into convertases. Recent structural and biochemical studies have elucidated the molecular basis for CRIg’s specificity and affinity, revealing that its binding interface overlaps with sites required for factor B and C5 binding, thereby providing a dual mechanism of inhibition. The discovery that CRIg recognizes a conformational epitope on C3b that is absent in native C3 has opened new avenues for therapeutic intervention. Furthermore, the identification of C3c, a stable cleavage product of C3b, as a high-affinity ligand for CRIg has expanded the understanding of how complement activation is terminated and how regulatory signals are propagated. The functional significance of the C3b:CRIg and C3c:CRIg complexes lies in their ability to block both the formation and propagation of the alternative pathway convertases, thereby suppressing amplification loops that drive chronic inflammation. The precise structural architecture of these complexes provides a blueprint for the rational design of therapeutic agents that mimic or enhance this natural regulatory mechanism.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention discloses the first high-resolution crystal structure of the human complement receptor CRIg in its unliganded form, solved to a resolution of 2.4 Å, revealing a single immunoglobulin variable-like domain with a characteristic β-sandwich fold stabilized by a conserved disulfide bond between cysteine residues at positions 37 and 98. The crystal belongs to space group P2₁2₁2₁ with cell constants a = 52.3 Å, b = 68.7 Å, and c = 89.1 Å, and contains two independent molecules per asymmetric unit. The structure exhibits a well-defined N-terminal region and a rigid core domain with minimal conformational flexibility, confirming its suitability as a stable scaffold for therapeutic engineering. The crystal structure of the C3b:CRIg complex was determined at a resolution of 3.1 Å and belongs to space group C2 with cell constants a = 216.4 Å, b = 180.4 Å, c = 154.6 Å, and β = 115.73°. In this complex, a single CRIg molecule binds to the β-chain of C3b at a well-defined interface that encompasses residues from the MG6 and MG7 domains of the C3b α-chain, with a buried surface area of approximately 1930 Å². The binding interface is dominated by hydrophobic interactions centered on phenylalanine 898 of C3b, which inserts into a complementary pocket formed by the heavy chain complementarity-determining region 3 of CRIg, supported by hydrogen bonds involving histidine 897 and backbone atoms of CRIg’s CDR-H1 and CDR-H3 loops. The structure reveals that CRIg binds exclusively to the activated conformation of C3b and does not recognize native C3 due to steric occlusion of the binding epitope in the unactivated state. 

The crystalline form of the C3c:CRIg complex was also obtained and solved to a resolution of 2.9 Å, with cell constants a = 209.8 Å, b = 176.5 Å, c = 152.3 Å, and β = 114.9°, confirming that CRIg maintains an identical binding mode with C3c as it does with C3b. The binding interface between CRIg and C3c is structurally indistinguishable from that observed in the C3b:CRIg complex, indicating that C3c retains the critical conformational epitope necessary for high-affinity recognition. The functional fragment of CRIg responsible for binding C3b and C3c corresponds to the entire extracellular domain spanning amino acids 22 to 144, which includes the immunoglobulin variable-like domain and the conserved disulfide bond. This fragment retains full binding affinity and specificity in solution-phase assays, confirming its sufficiency for biological activity. The structural coordinates of the CRIg:C3b complex have been deposited in the Protein Data Bank under accession code 8K7M, and those of the CRIg:C3c complex under accession code 8K7N. The resolution of the X-ray diffraction data for the CRIg:C3b complex was 3.1 Å, with an R-factor of 21.7% and a free R-factor of 28.3%, while the CRIg:C3c complex was refined to 2.9 Å with an R-factor of 19.4% and a free R-factor of 26.1%. The conformational stability of the CRIg domain, as evidenced by its well-ordered electron density and lack of disorder in loop regions, enables its use as a template for the design of engineered variants, fusion proteins, and small-molecule mimetics. The structural insights provided by these complexes reveal that the binding site for CRIg on C3b and C3c overlaps with the binding sites for factor B and C5, providing a mechanistic basis for the inhibition of convertase assembly. These crystal structures form the foundation for the rational design of therapeutics that selectively target the alternative pathway of complement activation by mimicking CRIg’s natural inhibitory function.

Complement Receptor Immunoglobulin (CRIg), also known as Z39Ig, VSIG4, or B7H6, refers to a type I transmembrane protein belonging to the immunoglobulin superfamily that functions as a high-affinity receptor for C3b and its cleavage products, including iC3b and C3c. A native sequence CRIg polypeptide is defined as a polypeptide comprising the amino acid sequence of human CRIg as encoded by the gene located on chromosome 1q32.2, with the full-length mature protein consisting of 144 amino acids excluding the signal peptide. A CRIg variant is a polypeptide that differs from the native sequence by one or more amino acid substitutions, insertions, or deletions, provided that the variant retains the ability to bind C3b or C3c with an affinity equal to or greater than 10⁻⁸ M. The extracellular domain (ECD) of CRIg encompasses residues 22 through 144 and includes the entire IgV-like domain responsible for ligand recognition. ### MODES OF CARRYING OUT THE INVENTION

Conventional techniques of molecular biology, including recombinant DNA technology, protein expression, purification, and structural analysis, are employed to produce and characterize CRIg and its functional variants. The three-dimensional structure of CRIg and its complexes with C3b and C3c were determined using X-ray crystallography, which requires the preparation of highly pure, homogeneous, and stable protein samples. Crystalline proteins and polypeptides were prepared by expressing recombinant CRIg in Escherichia coli, followed by purification using nickel-affinity chromatography and size exclusion chromatography. Polypeptides were crystallized using the hanging drop vapor diffusion method with optimized conditions of polyethylene glycol, magnesium chloride, and buffer pH. Crystals of sufficient size and clarity were obtained within one week and flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen for data collection. Crystalline polypeptides provide unparalleled purification quality, free of protease contamination, and are stable at ambient temperatures, making them suitable for pharmaceutical applications. Structural information derived from these crystals reveals conformational changes in C3b upon activation, including the rotation of the MG3 domain and the movement of the LNK region helix, which together expose the CRIg binding site. The binding site for CRIg on C3b is located on the β-chain and involves residues from the MG6 and MG7 domains, with a critical interaction mediated by histidine 897 and phenylalanine 898. The binding interface between CRIg and C3c or C3b is structurally identical, confirming that C3c retains the functional epitope necessary for high-affinity recognition. CRIg crystals were obtained with cell constants a = 52.3 Å, b = 68.7 Å, c = 89.1 Å, and space group P2₁2₁2₁. The C3b:CRIg complex crystals had cell constants a = 216.4 Å, b = 180.4 Å, c = 154.6 Å, and β = 115.73°, while the C3c:CRIg complex crystals had cell constants a = 209.8 Å, b = 176.5 Å, c = 152.3 Å, and β = 114.9°. These structural coordinates enable the rational design of CRIg agonists and antagonists through structure-based drug design, virtual screening, and epitope mapping. Agonists are designed to mimic the CRIg binding interface and inhibit convertase assembly, while antagonists are designed to block CRIg’s natural regulatory function for immunostimulatory applications. Immunoadhesins comprising the CRIg extracellular domain fused to the Fc region of human IgG1 were generated and shown to inhibit alternative pathway activation in human serum. Peptide mimetics were synthesized using solid-phase peptide synthesis with protected amino acids and cleaved from resin using trifluoroacetic acid. Side chain deprotection was performed using scavenger cocktails, and peptides were purified by reverse-phase HPLC. Non-peptide small organic molecules were screened using high-throughput docking against the CRIg binding pocket on C3b. Agonist antibodies were generated by immunizing mice with C3b and selecting clones that bind C3b but not C3, followed by humanization and affinity maturation. The preparation of monoclonal antibodies involved immunization with recombinant C3b, fusion of splenocytes with SP2/0 myeloma cells, selection of hybridomas by ELISA, and subcloning to ensure monoclonality. Binding affinity was determined by surface plasmon resonance, and antibodies were purified by protein A affinity chromatography. Recombinant DNA methods were used to express monoclonal antibodies in CHO cells, with DNA encoding the variable regions cloned into expression vectors under the control of the CMV promoter. Humanized antibodies were prepared by grafting murine CDRs onto human framework sequences and back-mutating key framework residues to restore affinity. Transgenic animals expressing human immunoglobulin loci were used to generate fully human antibodies. Bispecific antibodies were constructed using knobs-into-holes technology to enable dual targeting of C3b and another immune checkpoint molecule. Antibody effector function was modified by introducing cysteine residues into the Fc region to enable site-specific conjugation to cytotoxic agents. Immunoconjugates were prepared by linking monoclonal antibodies to ricin A-chain, doxorubicin, or radionuclides such as yttrium-90. Conjugation was achieved using heterobifunctional crosslinkers such as SMCC or NHS-PEG-Maleimide. Tissue pretargeting methods were employed to enhance tumor specificity by administering a bispecific antibody first, followed by a radiolabeled hapten. In vitro assays for CRIg activity included ELISA-based binding assays, fluid-phase convertase inhibition assays, and hemolytic assays using rabbit erythrocytes. In vivo assays utilized murine models of collagen-induced arthritis, age-related macular degeneration, and ischemia-reperfusion injury. The utility of CRIg and its agonists in preventing or treating complement-associated diseases is demonstrated by their ability to inhibit alternative pathway activation without affecting classical pathway function. Examples of complement-associated diseases include age-related macular degeneration, atypical hemolytic uremic syndrome, systemic lupus erythematosus, rheumatoid arthritis, and membranoproliferative glomerulonephritis. Age-related macular degeneration is characterized by chronic inflammation in the retina driven by C3b deposition and convertase activation. Non-exudative AMD involves drusen formation and geographic atrophy, while exudative AMD involves choroidal neovascularization. Both forms are associated with elevated levels of C3b and C3c in the subretinal space. Category 3 and category 4 high-risk AMD patients exhibit the greatest complement activation and are ideal candidates for CRIg-based therapy. Inflammatory conditions such as systemic lupus erythematosus involve immune complex deposition and complement-mediated tissue injury, while rheumatoid arthritis is driven by synovial complement activation and macrophage infiltration. Juvenile chronic arthritis, spondyloarthropathies, systemic sclerosis, idiopathic inflammatory myopathies, Sjögren’s syndrome, systemic vasculitis, and sarcoidosis all exhibit complement dysregulation. Autoimmune hemolytic anemia and autoimmune thrombocytopenia involve complement-mediated destruction of blood cells. Thyroiditis, including Hashimoto’s and Graves’ disease, is associated with local complement activation. Animal models for disease testing include collagen-induced arthritis, antibody-mediated arthritis, transgenic mice expressing human C3, psoriasis models using imiquimod, xenotransplantation models, and recombinant models with humanized complement components. Techniques for producing transgenic animals include pronuclear microinjection, CRISPR-Cas9 gene editing, and embryonic stem cell manipulation. ### Example 1

Mature human CRIg protein was produced by cloning the cDNA encoding residues 22–144 into a pET-28a vector with an N-terminal hexahistidine tag and expressing the protein in Escherichia coli BL21(DE3) cells. Cells were lysed by sonication in 50 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0, 300 mM NaCl, and 10 mM imidazole. The lysate was clarified by centrifugation and loaded onto a Ni-NTA affinity column. Bound protein was eluted with 250 mM imidazole and further purified by size exclusion chromatography on a Superdex 75 column equilibrated in 20 mM HEPES, pH 7.4, 150 mM NaCl. Purity was confirmed by SDS-PAGE and mass spectrometry. Crystallization of CRIg was performed using the hanging drop vapor diffusion method with a protein concentration of 10 mg/mL and a mother liquor containing 18% polyethylene glycol 3350, 0.2 M ammonium sulfate, and 0.1 M sodium citrate, pH 5.5. Crystals were cryoprotected in 25% glycerol and flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen. Diffraction data were collected at the Advanced Photon Source beamline 24-ID-C and processed using HKL-3000. The structure was solved by molecular replacement using the program Phaser with a monomer of the human IgV domain (PDB 1IGT) as the search model. Refinement was performed using REFMAC5 and COOT, resulting in a final model with an R-factor of 19.2% and a free R-factor of 23.8%. The overall structure of C3b was determined from crystals of purified C3b generated by cleavage of C3 with CVF, fB, and fD in the presence of MgCl₂. Conformational changes upon C3 activation involve the release of the anaphylatoxin domain and the reorientation of the CUB and TED modules, exposing the MG6 and MG7 domains. The α'NT domain undergoes a significant displacement, creating a binding surface for regulators. Potential binding sites for receptors and regulators are located on the lateral face of C3b, opposite the TED domain. Differences between C3 and C3b structures are most pronounced in the orientation of the MG6 and MG7 domains, which rotate approximately 15 degrees relative to each other upon activation. The CUB and TED modules in C3b are rigidly positioned, with the TED domain adopting a closed conformation that shields the thioester bond. The interaction between arginine 80 and the TED domain stabilizes this conformation. The conformation of the TED domain in C3b is distinct from that in C3, with a 7 Å shift in the C-terminal helix. The binding of CRIg to the β-chain of C3b and C3c occurs at a site that overlaps with the binding epitope for factor B and C5. The N-terminal domain of CRIg is an IgV-like domain with a β-sandwich fold stabilized by a conserved disulfide bond between Cys37 and Cys98. The arrangement of contacting domains in the CRIg:C3b complex reveals that CRIg binds perpendicular to the long axis of C3b, engaging both MG6 and MG7. The affinity of CRIg for C3b is 1.8 nM and for C3c is 2.1 nM, as determined by surface plasmon resonance. The binding site for CRIg on C3b is centered on phenylalanine 898 and histidine 897, which form a hydrophobic pocket and hydrogen bonding network, respectively. The binding interface between CRIg and C3c or C3b is nearly identical, with a root mean square deviation of 0.3 Å for Cα atoms. The MG3 domain acts as a molecular gate that must rotate to expose the CRIg binding site. The rotation of MG3 in C3b is accompanied by a helical shift in the LNK region, which repositions residues 895–900 into an accessible conformation. The importance of this binding interface is underscored by the fact that mutations at histidine 897 abolish CRIg binding. ### CRIg Variants

CRIg mutants were designed based on the crystal structure of the CRIg:C3b complex to enhance binding affinity, stability, or solubility. Mutations were introduced into the CDR-like loops of CRIg using site-directed mutagenesis. Specifically, residues in the CDR-H1 loop (positions 28–30), CDR-H2 loop (positions 52–56), and CDR-H3 loop (positions 95–102) were substituted with amino acids predicted to improve hydrophobic packing or hydrogen bonding. The mutant proteins were expressed in E. coli and purified using the same protocol as wild-type CRIg. Binding studies were performed using surface plasmon resonance to measure affinity for C3b and C3c. The variant CRIg-F898Y exhibited a 5-fold increase in affinity for C3b (K_D = 0.36 nM) due to enhanced π-stacking with tyrosine 900 on C3b. The variant CRIg-H897A lost all binding activity, confirming the critical role of histidine 897. The variant CRIg-T31R showed improved solubility and retained full binding affinity. The variant CRIg-D95A exhibited reduced affinity, indicating that this residue contributes to the hydrogen bonding network. All variants were tested for stability by thermal shift assay, and those with melting temperatures above 65°C were selected for further development. The most stable variant, CRIg-F898Y, was fused to the Fc region of human IgG1 to generate an immunoadhesin with a half-life of 14 days in cynomolgus monkeys. This variant was shown to inhibit C3 convertase formation in human serum by 98% at 10 nM concentration.

### Example 3

The inhibition of the alternative pathway of complement by CRIg was demonstrated using fluid-phase convertase assays. C3b was immobilized on microtiter plates and incubated with factor B and factor D to form the C3 convertase. Addition of recombinant CRIg at concentrations as low as 1 nM completely blocked the formation of C3a and C3b fragments, as measured by ELISA and SDS-PAGE. CRIg did not inhibit the classical pathway, as evidenced by normal hemolytic activity in C1q-depleted serum supplemented with C4 and factor D. Selective inhibition of the alternative pathway was confirmed using a hemolytic assay with rabbit erythrocytes, where CRIg inhibited lysis in factor B-containing serum but not in factor B-depleted serum. Molecular mechanisms of inhibition were elucidated using surface plasmon resonance, which showed that CRIg binds C3b with a 1:1 stoichiometry and prevents factor B binding with a Ki of 0.8 nM. CRIg does not accelerate decay of pre-formed C3 convertase, indicating that its mechanism is purely preventive. CRIg also inhibits the binding of C5 to C3b-C3b dimers, thereby blocking formation of the C5 convertase. The inhibition of C5 binding was confirmed using a C5 binding ELISA, where CRIg reduced C5 binding by 95% at 5 nM. These findings demonstrate that CRIg acts as a dual inhibitor of both C3 and C5 convertase assembly by sterically blocking access to the C3b binding site required for substrate recruitment.

### Example 4

An arthritis model was established using collagen-induced arthritis in DBA/1 mice. Mice were immunized with bovine type II collagen in complete Freund’s adjuvant and boosted two weeks later. Disease onset was monitored by paw swelling and clinical scoring. CRIg-Fc fusion protein was administered intravenously at 10 mg/kg every three days starting at day 14. Bone volume was measured by micro-CT, and histological examination of joint sections revealed reduced cartilage erosion, pannus formation, and inflammatory cell infiltration in treated mice compared to controls. Cytokine concentrations in hind paw homogenates were measured by ELISA and showed a 70% reduction in TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 levels. CRIg treatment inhibited complement activation in the synovial fluid, as evidenced by decreased C3a and C5a levels. Binding of CRIg to convertases locally was demonstrated by immunohistochemistry, where CRIg colocalized with C3b deposits in the synovial membrane. The therapeutic potential of CRIg was further confirmed by its ability to reduce serum levels of anti-collagen IgG and suppress T-cell proliferation in draining lymph nodes. These results demonstrate that CRIg not only inhibits complement activation but also dampens the adaptive immune response, suggesting a dual mechanism of action in autoimmune arthritis.

### Example 5

Decay acceleration assays were performed by pre-incubating C3b with factor B and factor D to form the C3 convertase, followed by addition of CRIg or control proteins. The decay of convertase activity was measured by quantifying C3a generation over time. CRIg did not accelerate decay, confirming that it does not function as a decay-accelerating factor. C3b-C4b and C3b-C3b dimers were prepared by incubating C3b with C4b or excess C3b in the presence of Mg²⁺. Binding and competition assays were conducted by coating plates with C3b-C4b or C3b-C3b and measuring binding of CRIg in the presence or absence of C5. CRIg bound to both dimers with similar affinity, and pre-incubation with CRIg blocked C5 binding by 92% and 89%, respectively. Results showed that CRIg inhibits C5 binding to both forms of the C5 convertase. The mechanism of CRIg inhibition of AP complement activation involves steric occlusion of the C5 binding site on C3b, as confirmed by cryo-EM and molecular modeling. CRIg interferes with C3b function by preventing its interaction with factor B and C5, thereby blocking convertase assembly. The effect of CRIg on C5 convertase activity was quantified using a hemolytic assay, which showed complete inhibition at 5 nM CRIg. The inhibition of C5 convertase was dose-dependent, with an IC₅₀ of 1.2 nM. The steric or allosteric inhibition of C5 binding to C3b by CRIg is mediated by direct competition for overlapping binding sites on the MG6 and MG7 domains, as revealed by structural superposition of the CRIg:C3b complex with the C5:C3b complex. These findings establish CRIg as a potent and selective inhibitor of the alternative pathway at the level of both C3 and C5 convertases.
