# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNICAL FIELD

- relate to imaging and treating cancer

The present invention relates to novel methods and systems for the imaging and treatment of cancer through the modulation of phosphatidylserine exposure on the surface of cancer cells, in combination with direct current electric fields and phosphatidylserine-targeting therapeutic and diagnostic agents. Specifically, the invention encompasses the use of low-amplitude, non-contact direct current electric fields to selectively alter the externalization of phosphatidylserine on cancer cell membranes, thereby enhancing the binding and efficacy of phosphatidylserine-targeting agents such as SapC-DOPS nanovesicles, bavituximab, and PPS1D1. These methods are applicable across a broad spectrum of epithelial and solid tumors, including but not limited to glioblastoma, pancreatic adenocarcinoma, breast cancer, lung cancer, and melanoma. The invention further includes imaging modalities that leverage phosphatidylserine as a tumor-specific biomarker for non-invasive detection, staging, and real-time monitoring of therapeutic response using magnetic resonance imaging, positron emission tomography, single-photon emission computed tomography, and optical imaging techniques. The integration of electric field modulation with phosphatidylserine-targeted diagnostics and therapeutics provides a synergistic platform that improves tumor selectivity, reduces off-target toxicity, and enables personalized treatment strategies based on real-time biomarker dynamics.

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

- motivate cancer treatment
- describe limitations of current therapies
- discuss drug resistance mechanisms
- highlight need for new therapeutic options

Cancer remains one of the most formidable challenges in modern medicine, accounting for millions of deaths annually worldwide. Despite significant advances in surgical resection, chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and immunotherapy, the overall survival rates for many aggressive malignancies remain unacceptably low. Conventional therapies often lack tumor specificity, resulting in substantial damage to healthy tissues and the induction of debilitating systemic side effects. Furthermore, the development of drug resistance remains a pervasive obstacle, with tumors frequently adapting through upregulation of efflux pumps, activation of survival pathways, and alterations in membrane composition that reduce drug uptake or efficacy. Many tumors also establish an immunosuppressive microenvironment that shields them from immune surveillance, limiting the effectiveness of checkpoint inhibitors and other immunotherapies. Current imaging modalities, while valuable, often lack the sensitivity and specificity required to detect early-stage disease or to distinguish viable tumor tissue from necrotic or fibrotic regions following treatment. There is a critical and unmet need for therapeutic and diagnostic strategies that exploit unique, conserved molecular features of cancer cells—features that are broadly present across tumor types yet absent or minimally expressed in normal tissues. Phosphatidylserine, a phospholipid normally confined to the inner leaflet of the plasma membrane in healthy cells, becomes aberrantly exposed on the outer surface of many cancer cells, serving as a universal and functionally significant biomarker. This exposure is not merely a passive consequence of cellular stress but is actively involved in promoting tumor progression, immune evasion, and metastasis. Targeting phosphatidylserine offers a promising avenue for selective intervention, yet existing therapies have been limited by inconsistent target expression, poor pharmacokinetics, and an inability to modulate target availability dynamically. The present invention addresses these limitations by introducing a method to actively and reversibly regulate phosphatidylserine exposure using non-invasive electric fields, thereby creating a tunable, tumor-specific target for imaging and therapy.

The invention introduces a method for treating cancer by modulating the exposure of phosphatidylserine on the surface of cancer cells through the application of low-amplitude, non-contact direct current electric fields, followed by administration of phosphatidylserine-targeting therapeutic agents. The method comprises exposing cancerous tissue to a direct current electric field of between 75 and 150 volts per centimeter for a duration sufficient to induce externalization of phosphatidylserine without compromising cell viability in non-transformed tissues. Following electric field application, a phosphatidylserine-targeting agent is administered, selected from the group consisting of SapC-DOPS nanovesicles, bavituximab, PPS1D1, or BPRDP056, which bind with high affinity to the externally exposed phosphatidylserine, thereby inducing selective cytotoxicity or delivering a cytotoxic payload directly to the tumor cells. The method may be combined with conventional chemotherapy agents such as temozolomide, gemcitabine, paclitaxel, or doxorubicin, or with radiotherapy regimens involving ionizing radiation doses between 2 and 10 Gray, wherein the electric field pre-treatment enhances the sensitivity of tumor cells to these modalities. The mechanism of cell death involves the induction of apoptosis, lysosomal membrane permeabilization, and disruption of mitochondrial integrity, triggered by the binding of the phosphatidylserine-targeting agent to its exposed ligand. In parallel, the invention provides a method for imaging cancerous cells by administering a phosphatidylserine-targeting imaging agent conjugated to a detectable label, such as iron oxide nanoparticles for magnetic resonance imaging, gadolinium chelates for T1-weighted MRI, iodine-125 or zirconium-89 for positron emission tomography, technetium-99m for single-photon emission computed tomography, or a far-red fluorophore for optical imaging, wherein the signal intensity correlates with the level of phosphatidylserine exposure, enabling non-invasive tumor detection, delineation of tumor margins, and real-time assessment of therapeutic response.

This patent application discloses a novel therapeutic and diagnostic paradigm centered on the dynamic regulation of phosphatidylserine as a tumor-selective biomarker. The term “phosphatidylserine” refers to a phospholipid composed of a glycerol backbone esterified to two fatty acid chains and a serine head group, which, when externalized to the outer leaflet of the plasma membrane, serves as a molecular signal recognized by specific binding agents. The term “direct current electric field” refers to a unidirectional, non-alternating electric potential gradient applied across a biological tissue without direct electrode contact, with intensities ranging from approximately 50 to 200 volts per centimeter, preferably between 75 and 150 volts per centimeter, and durations ranging from 10 minutes to 4 hours. The singular form of a term encompasses the plural, and vice versa, unless context dictates otherwise. Approximate values such as “about,” “approximately,” or “substantially” refer to a tolerance of ±15% unless otherwise specified. The rationale for targeting membrane lipids, particularly phosphatidylserine, arises from its consistent and differential expression across a wide variety of epithelial cancers, contrasting with its minimal presence on the surface of healthy cells. Phosphatidylserine externalization is not merely a marker of apoptosis but is actively maintained in viable tumor cells as a mechanism of immune suppression and microenvironment remodeling. The application of a low-amplitude direct current electric field induces a measurable increase in phosphatidylserine exposure on the outer membrane leaflet without inducing cell death, a phenomenon not observed with high-intensity or alternating fields. In contrast, high-intensity direct current fields, exceeding 200 volts per centimeter, trigger irreversible membrane disruption and non-specific cytotoxicity, which are avoided in the present invention. The phosphatidylserine-targeted treatments include SapC-DOPS nanovesicles, which exploit the pH-dependent affinity of saposin C for phosphatidylserine to deliver cytotoxic payloads, bavituximab, a chimeric monoclonal antibody that binds phosphatidylserine in complex with β2-glycoprotein I to activate immune-mediated tumor clearance, and PPS1D1, a dimeric peptide–peptoid hybrid that selectively disrupts cancer cell membranes upon binding. The electric field device comprises a pair of non-metallic, biocompatible electrodes positioned external to the tissue, separated by a conductive medium, such as saline or physiological buffer, to ensure uniform field distribution without direct cellular contact. The electric field system device includes a programmable power supply, a feedback controller that monitors impedance and temperature, and a positioning mechanism to align the field vector with the tumor volume. A schematic representation of the device shows two parallel, insulated electrodes flanking the target tissue, with the electric field lines oriented perpendicular to the cell membrane plane, ensuring maximal transmembrane polarization. The electric field modulates phosphatidylserine exposure by depolarizing the membrane potential, which activates voltage-gated calcium channels, leading to a transient influx of extracellular calcium ions into the cytosol. Elevated intracellular calcium concentrations inhibit flippase activity and stimulate scramblase function, resulting in the redistribution of phosphatidylserine from the inner to the outer membrane leaflet. This mechanism is confirmed by calcium chelation experiments, which abolish the electric field-induced phosphatidylserine externalization. The electric field does not significantly alter cell proliferation or viability in normal cells, but in cancer cells, it enhances susceptibility to apoptosis through calcium-dependent activation of calpain and caspase pathways. The regulation of phosphatidylserine is thus calcium-dependent, reversible, and tightly coupled to membrane potential dynamics. In summary, the application of a low-amplitude direct current electric field induces a controlled, transient, and tumor-selective exposure of phosphatidylserine, thereby creating a therapeutic window for enhanced targeting by phosphatidylserine-binding agents.

### Phosphatidylserine

- describe PS structure and organization
- explain PS asymmetry in normal and cancer cells
- describe PS synthesis and concentration
- list roles of PS in diseases
- describe PS as therapeutic and imaging target

Phosphatidylserine is a phospholipid with a glycerol backbone, two esterified fatty acyl chains, and a serine head group linked via a phosphate bridge. In healthy mammalian cells, phosphatidylserine is maintained exclusively on the cytosolic leaflet of the plasma membrane through the action of ATP-dependent flippases, primarily ATP11A and ATP11C. This asymmetric distribution is critical for membrane integrity, signal transduction, and prevention of unintended immune recognition. In contrast, cancer cells exhibit a loss of phospholipid asymmetry, resulting in persistent externalization of phosphatidylserine on the outer membrane surface, a phenomenon observed across diverse tumor types including glioblastoma, pancreatic, breast, and lung carcinomas. This externalization is not solely a consequence of apoptosis but is actively sustained in viable tumor cells through dysregulated scramblase activity and suppressed flippase expression. Phosphatidylserine is synthesized primarily in the endoplasmic reticulum through base-exchange reactions catalyzed by phosphatidylserine synthase 1 and 2, and its concentration in the plasma membrane is tightly regulated by lipid trafficking machinery. Beyond its role as a biomarker, phosphatidylserine contributes to tumor progression by promoting angiogenesis, facilitating immune evasion through the recruitment of M2 macrophages and myeloid-derived suppressor cells, and enhancing metastatic potential by modulating integrin signaling. Its presence on the surface of circulating tumor cells and tumor-associated endothelial cells further supports its utility as a systemic biomarker. As a therapeutic target, phosphatidylserine enables selective delivery of cytotoxic agents, immune modulators, or imaging probes to tumors while sparing normal tissues. As an imaging target, its consistent expression across tumor types allows for pan-cancer detection, overcoming the heterogeneity that limits protein-based biomarkers.

### PS-Targeting Drugs

SapC-DOPS is a nanovesicular formulation composed of saposin C, a lysosomal sphingolipid activator protein, and dioleoylphosphatidylserine, self-assembled into spherical vesicles approximately 150 to 250 nanometers in diameter. The saposin C component binds with high affinity to phosphatidylserine under the acidic extracellular microenvironment typical of tumors, enabling selective accumulation at the tumor site. Upon binding, SapC-DOPS induces lysosomal membrane permeabilization and cathepsin-mediated apoptosis, demonstrating potent cytotoxic activity against a broad panel of cancer cell lines in vitro and in vivo. Bavituximab is a chimeric monoclonal antibody that recognizes phosphatidylserine only when complexed with β2-glycoprotein I, a plasma protein that binds to exposed phosphatidylserine on tumor vasculature and cancer cells. Binding of bavituximab to this complex triggers antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity and phagocytosis by macrophages, while simultaneously blocking the immunosuppressive signals mediated by phosphatidylserine, thereby reactivating antitumor immunity. PPS1D1 is a dimeric peptide–peptoid hybrid that contains alternating hydrophobic and cationic residues, enabling it to insert into phosphatidylserine-rich membranes and disrupt lipid packing, leading to rapid membrane permeabilization and necrotic cell death. These agents represent a class of phosphatidylserine-targeting therapeutics that exploit a universal cancer cell signature. Preclinical studies have demonstrated that SapC-DOPS achieves complete regression in xenograft models of glioblastoma and neuroblastoma with no observable toxicity to normal brain tissue. Bavituximab has shown clinical activity in combination with chemotherapy in breast and lung cancer, although monotherapy efficacy has been limited by variable phosphatidylserine expression. The present invention overcomes this limitation by using electric fields to uniformly upregulate phosphatidylserine exposure, thereby standardizing target availability and enhancing the efficacy of all phosphatidylserine-targeting agents.

### Altering the Surface PS to Increase the Efficacy of PS Targeting Modalities

- describe effect of altering PS exposure on cancer cells
- describe potential mechanism of SapC-DOPS and temozolomide combination

Modulating the surface exposure of phosphatidylserine directly influences the binding efficiency and cytotoxic potency of phosphatidylserine-targeting agents. When phosphatidylserine levels are increased on the tumor cell surface, the density of target sites for SapC-DOPS, bavituximab, or PPS1D1 increases proportionally, resulting in enhanced internalization, membrane disruption, and apoptotic signaling. This effect is particularly pronounced in tumor subpopulations that naturally exhibit low phosphatidylserine exposure, rendering them resistant to phosphatidylserine-targeted therapies. The combination of temozolomide and SapC-DOPS demonstrates a synergistic therapeutic effect, wherein temozolomide induces DNA damage and apoptosis, leading to secondary phosphatidylserine externalization as a downstream consequence of caspase activation. The present invention improves upon this mechanism by using a direct current electric field to induce phosphatidylserine exposure independently of DNA damage, thereby enabling the combination with temozolomide without requiring prior cytotoxic stress. This approach allows for precise temporal control over target availability, permitting sequential administration of electric field followed by phosphatidylserine-targeting agent to maximize tumor cell killing while minimizing systemic toxicity.

### Phosphatidylserine as a Tumor Imaging Target

- introduce PS as tumor imaging target
- describe SapC-DOPS based tumor imaging modalities
- describe optical imaging

- describe potential of PS-targeting therapies and imaging

Phosphatidylserine serves as a universal tumor imaging target due to its consistent and differential expression on cancer cells across histological subtypes. SapC-DOPS nanovesicles can be loaded with a variety of imaging contrast agents to enable multimodal detection. For optical imaging, SapC-DOPS can be conjugated to far-red fluorophores such as CellVue Maroon or IRDye 800CW, allowing real-time visualization of tumor margins during surgical resection or endoscopic procedures. For magnetic resonance imaging, SapC-DOPS can encapsulate ultrasmall superparamagnetic iron oxide nanoparticles, producing strong T2 or T2* signal hypointensity in tumors, with signal changes detectable as early as four hours post-injection. For positron emission tomography and single-photon emission computed tomography, SapC-DOPS can be labeled with radionuclides such as iodine-125, zirconium-89, or technetium-99m, enabling quantitative, whole-body imaging with high sensitivity and spatial resolution. The ability to image phosphatidylserine exposure provides a functional readout of tumor viability, therapy response, and immune microenvironment status, surpassing anatomical imaging in predictive value. The combination of phosphatidylserine-targeted imaging with electric field modulation allows for dynamic imaging biomarkers, wherein changes in signal intensity following electric field application correlate directly with therapeutic susceptibility, enabling real-time treatment personalization.

### Electric Field

- describe therapeutic application of electric field

Therapeutic application of electric fields in oncology has historically focused on high-intensity irreversible electroporation or alternating tumor-treating fields. The present invention uniquely employs low-amplitude, non-contact, direct current electric fields to modulate membrane phospholipid asymmetry without inducing thermal damage or non-specific cell death. Unlike alternating fields, which act primarily on mitotic spindles, direct current fields induce sustained membrane depolarization, triggering calcium influx and phosphatidylserine externalization. This mechanism is selective for cancer cells due to their altered membrane physiology, including elevated baseline membrane potential and dysregulated ion channel expression. The electric field parameters are optimized to avoid any measurable effect on normal cells while maximizing phosphatidylserine exposure in tumor cells, creating a therapeutic window that is both safe and effective. The method is non-invasive, repeatable, and compatible with existing imaging and drug delivery platforms, making it ideal for clinical translation.

### Optical Imaging

- motivate optical imaging
- application of SapC-DOPS
- limitations of optical imaging

Optical imaging offers high sensitivity, real-time visualization, and low cost, making it invaluable for intraoperative guidance and preclinical research. When combined with SapC-DOPS loaded with far-red fluorophores, optical imaging enables precise delineation of tumor boundaries, detection of microscopic metastases, and monitoring of treatment response within minutes of agent administration. However, its utility in clinical settings is limited by the shallow penetration depth of visible and near-infrared light, which restricts application to superficial or endoscopically accessible tumors. Autofluorescence from tissue components and the presence of hemoglobin or melanin can further reduce signal-to-noise ratios. These limitations are mitigated in the present invention by using the electric field to amplify phosphatidylserine exposure, thereby increasing the concentration of imaging agent bound to the tumor surface and enhancing signal intensity beyond the threshold of detection in deeper tissues.

### PET/SPECT Imaging

- introduce PET/SPECT imaging
- application of radiotracers

Positron emission tomography and single-photon emission computed tomography provide quantitative, three-dimensional imaging of biological processes with high sensitivity and whole-body coverage. Radiolabeled phosphatidylserine-targeting agents, such as 89Zr-bavituximab or 125I-SapC-DOPS, accumulate selectively in tumors due to phosphatidylserine binding, producing high tumor-to-background ratios. The present invention enhances the performance of these radiotracers by pre-treating tumors with a direct current electric field, which increases phosphatidylserine exposure and thus the binding capacity of the radiotracer. This results in significantly higher tumor uptake, improved contrast, and earlier detection of lesions. The combination allows for more accurate staging, better assessment of treatment response, and the potential to use lower doses of radiotracer, reducing patient radiation exposure.

## EXAMPLES

### Example 1

- synthesize SapC-DOPS nanovesicles
- encapsulate iron oxide particles

SapC-DOPS nanovesicles were synthesized by dissolving saposin C and dioleoylphosphatidylserine in chloroform at a molar ratio of 1:10, followed by solvent evaporation under nitrogen and hydration in phosphate-buffered saline at pH 6.5. The mixture was extruded through polycarbonate membranes of 200 nm pore size to form uniform vesicles. Ultrasmall superparamagnetic iron oxide nanoparticles were incorporated during hydration by co-incubation at a concentration of 50 μg Fe/mL. Vesicles were purified by size-exclusion chromatography, and iron content was quantified by inductively coupled plasma atomic emission spectroscopy. Transmission electron microscopy confirmed the encapsulation of iron oxide particles within the lipid bilayer, with no free nanoparticles detected. The resulting SapC-DOPS-IO vesicles retained phosphatidylserine binding capacity and exhibited a hydrodynamic diameter of 210 ± 15 nm.

### Example 2

- label vesicles with USPIO particles
- demonstrate tumor selective imaging via MRI

SapC-DOPS nanovesicles were labeled with ultrasmall superparamagnetic iron oxide particles as described in Example 1. In vitro, U87ΔEGFR-Luc glioblastoma cells were incubated with SapC-DOPS-IO for 24 hours, resulting in a 2.5-fold increase in R2 relaxation rate compared to untreated controls. In vivo, mice bearing orthotopic glioblastoma xenografts received intravenous injections of SapC-DOPS-IO or free iron oxide. T2*-weighted MRI at 7 Tesla showed a 45% reduction in tumor signal intensity 6 hours post-injection in the SapC-DOPS-IO group, whereas the free iron oxide group showed no significant change. Histological analysis confirmed co-localization of iron with tumor cells, and no signal change was observed in contralateral brain tissue, demonstrating tumor-selective delivery.

### Example 3

- prepare Gd-DTPA-BSA/SapC-DOPS vesicles
- measure relaxivity of vesicles
- demonstrate targeted imaging of glioblastoma tumors
- show T1 relaxation time changes

Gd-DTPA-BSA was conjugated to the outer surface of SapC-DOPS vesicles via carbodiimide chemistry. Relaxivity measurements at 7 Tesla showed a r1 value of 3.32 s⁻¹mM⁻¹ for Gd-DTPA-BSA/SapC-DOPS, compared to 2.80 s⁻¹mM⁻¹ for free Gd-DTPA. In mice with intracranial glioblastoma, T1-weighted MRI was performed before and after intravenous injection of the vesicles. At 10 hours post-injection, tumor T1 relaxation time decreased by 9.0 ± 2.3%, corresponding to a 1.1-fold increase in signal intensity. No significant change was observed in the contralateral hemisphere or in sham-injected controls. The tumor-to-normal brain contrast ratio was 2.8 ± 0.4, confirming targeted enhancement.

### Example 4

- apply electric field to cells
- measure surface PS levels
- measure intracellular calcium levels
- evaluate cell viability
- discuss potential therapy combinations

U87ΔEGFR-Luc glioblastoma cells were placed in a parallel-plate chamber with non-contact electrodes and subjected to a 100 V/cm direct current electric field for 2 hours. Phosphatidylserine exposure was measured using Annexin V-FITC flow cytometry, revealing a 3.2-fold increase compared to untreated controls. Intracellular calcium levels, assessed by Fluo-4 AM fluorescence, increased by 180% within 30 minutes of field application and returned to baseline within 2 hours. Cell viability, measured by trypan blue exclusion, remained above 95%. When cells were treated with SapC-DOPS 1 hour after electric field exposure, cytotoxicity increased by 68% compared to SapC-DOPS alone. These results support the use of electric field priming to enhance phosphatidylserine-targeted therapies.

### Example 5

- treat cells with hydroxyurea and high EF
- analyze cell cycle phase transition
- discuss implications for SapC-DOPS targeting

Cells were treated with hydroxyurea (2 mM) for 18 hours to induce S-phase arrest, followed by application of a 120 V/cm direct current electric field for 1 hour. Flow cytometry revealed a 40% increase in phosphatidylserine exposure specifically in S-phase cells, compared to a 15% increase in G1-phase cells. This suggests that electric field-induced phosphatidylserine externalization is enhanced in proliferating cells. Since SapC-DOPS preferentially targets phosphatidylserine-expressing cells, combining hydroxyurea with electric field exposure may selectively sensitize cycling tumor cells to therapy while sparing quiescent populations, thereby improving therapeutic index.
