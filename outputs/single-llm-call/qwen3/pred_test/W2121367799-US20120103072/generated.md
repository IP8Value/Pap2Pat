## TECHNICAL FIELD

The present invention relates to the field of clinical diagnostics and prognostic assessment in patients with end-stage renal disease undergoing maintenance hemodialysis. More specifically, the invention pertains to methods for predicting overall survival and mortality risk in such patients through the precise measurement and analysis of low-density lipoprotein particle diameter and subfraction concentrations using ion mobility analysis. The invention further encompasses the use of these lipoprotein parameters as biomarkers to stratify cardiovascular risk independently of conventional lipid measurements, thereby enabling more accurate clinical decision-making, personalized therapeutic planning, and improved patient monitoring in a population where traditional risk indicators have proven unreliable. This technology bridges the disciplines of nephrology, cardiology, clinical biochemistry, and analytical biophysics, offering a novel, non-invasive, and physiologically grounded approach to risk assessment in a high-mortality patient cohort.

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

End-stage renal disease is a progressive and irreversible condition requiring lifelong renal replacement therapy, most commonly in the form of maintenance hemodialysis. Despite advances in dialysis technology and supportive care, the five-year survival rate for patients on maintenance hemodialysis remains alarmingly low, with approximately half of all deaths attributable to cardiovascular disease. Conventional lipid profiling, which includes measurement of total cholesterol, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol, and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol, has long served as a cornerstone of cardiovascular risk assessment in the general population. However, in patients undergoing maintenance hemodialysis, these standard measures exhibit a paradoxical relationship with mortality, wherein lower levels of total and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol are associated with increased risk of death rather than decreased risk—a phenomenon known as the lipid paradox. This inconsistency undermines the clinical utility of traditional lipid panels in this population and highlights a critical unmet need for more reliable biomarkers capable of accurately identifying patients at highest risk of mortality. Lipoproteins, which are complex particles composed of lipids and apolipoproteins, exist as a heterogeneous continuum of sizes and densities, each with distinct metabolic fates and biological effects. In chronic disease states such as end-stage renal disease, the composition, size distribution, and concentration of these particles are profoundly altered, rendering conventional cholesterol-based metrics inadequate. The need for alternative measures that reflect the true atherogenic burden in this population has become increasingly apparent, particularly as therapeutic interventions targeting lipid metabolism remain controversial and poorly guided by existing biomarkers.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

A method for predicting overall survival in patients undergoing maintenance hemodialysis is disclosed, wherein the method comprises measuring the particle diameter of low-density lipoprotein particles in a biological sample obtained from the patient, comparing the measured particle diameter to a reference level, and determining an increased risk of mortality if the measured particle diameter is below the reference level. The method further comprises measuring the concentration of very small low-density lipoprotein subfractions in the biological sample, comparing the measured concentration to a reference concentration of very small low-density lipoprotein subfractions, and identifying an increased risk of mortality if the measured concentration exceeds the reference concentration. The method further comprises measuring the concentration of large low-density lipoprotein subfractions in the biological sample, comparing the measured concentration to a reference concentration of large low-density lipoprotein subfractions, and identifying a reduced risk of mortality if the measured concentration exceeds the reference concentration. The method may further comprise measuring both the concentration of very small low-density lipoprotein subfractions and the concentration of large low-density lipoprotein subfractions, comparing both concentrations to their respective reference levels, and determining an overall mortality risk based on the combined comparison. The method may also include measuring the particle diameter of low-density lipoprotein particles or the concentration of high-density lipoprotein particles as additional parameters to refine the prognostic assessment. The invention provides a clinically actionable framework for risk stratification that is independent of conventional lipid measurements and directly tied to the physical properties of lipoprotein particles as determined by high-resolution analytical techniques.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

Maintenance hemodialysis patients represent a distinct clinical population characterized by profound metabolic dysregulation, chronic inflammation, and accelerated atherosclerosis. The mortality risk in this group is not adequately captured by serum concentrations of cholesterol or triglycerides, which often reflect nutritional status or catabolic state rather than true vascular burden. The present invention recognizes that the size and subfractional composition of low-density lipoprotein particles are more directly reflective of atherogenic potential in this context. The term “low-density lipoprotein particle diameter” refers to the mean hydrodynamic diameter of low-density lipoprotein particles as determined by ion mobility analysis, expressed in angstroms. The term “about” when used in reference to numerical values indicates a permissible deviation of ±10% from the stated value, accounting for normal analytical variability inherent in biological measurements. A “biomarker” or “LDL property” as used herein refers to any measurable characteristic of low-density lipoprotein particles, including particle diameter, subfraction concentration, or ratio of subfractions, that correlates with clinical outcome. “Clinical factors” encompass demographic, laboratory, and therapeutic variables such as age, sex, dialysis vintage, serum albumin, C-reactive protein, and statin use. The term “comparable” or “corresponding” denotes that two samples, populations, or measurements are matched for relevant confounding variables such as age, gender, dialysis duration, and comorbid conditions. A “control population” refers to a group of individuals without end-stage renal disease or with normal renal function, used to establish baseline ranges for lipoprotein parameters. The terms “determining,” “measuring,” “assessing,” and “assaying” are used interchangeably to describe the quantitative evaluation of a biological parameter using a validated analytical method. “Diagnosis” refers to the identification of a medical condition based on clinical and laboratory findings, while “difference of the level” or “difference in properties” refers to a statistically significant deviation from a reference value or population median. “Ion mobility analysis” or “IMA” refers to a technique that separates lipoprotein particles based on their size-to-charge ratio in the gas phase following electrospray ionization, enabling direct enumeration and sizing of individual particles. A “lipoprotein” or “lipoprotein particle” is a complex macromolecular assembly composed of lipids and apolipoproteins that transports triglycerides and cholesterol in the bloodstream. “VLDL,” “IDL,” “LDL,” and “HDL” denote very low-density, intermediate-density, low-density, and high-density lipoproteins, respectively, classified by their density and composition. A “population” refers to a defined group of individuals sharing a common clinical or physiological characteristic, such as maintenance hemodialysis patients. “Overall survival” or “OS” is the length of time from the date of sample collection until death from any cause. “Prognosis” refers to the predicted course and outcome of a disease, as determined by biomarker analysis. A “reference level” is a threshold value derived from a validated cohort or population, used to classify risk as elevated or reduced. A “sample” or “test sample” is a biological fluid, typically plasma or serum, obtained from a subject for analysis. A “subject” is a human patient undergoing maintenance hemodialysis. The phrase “substantially the same as” indicates that two values are not statistically distinguishable within the limits of analytical precision. “Treating,” “treatment,” or “alleviation” refers to any intervention intended to modify disease progression, including pharmacological, dietary, or dialysis regimen adjustments, guided by the prognostic information obtained. The definitions provided herein are intended to be consistent throughout the application and are not limited to any single embodiment.

### Sample Collection, Preparation, and Purification

A biological sample is collected from a maintenance hemodialysis patient in a fasting state, typically prior to a scheduled dialysis session, to minimize acute fluctuations in lipoprotein composition. The sample is drawn into anticoagulant-containing tubes and processed within four hours of collection to preserve particle integrity. Plasma is separated from cellular components by centrifugation at 2,000 × g for 15 minutes at 4°C, followed by a second centrifugation step at 10,000 × g for 30 minutes to remove residual cellular debris. The resulting plasma is then subjected to ultracentrifugation or dialysis to isolate lipoprotein fractions, although the present invention does not require prior fractionation, as ion mobility analysis directly resolves lipoprotein subfractions from unfractionated plasma. The sample may be diluted with isotonic buffer to achieve optimal ionization efficiency during analysis. Lipoprotein purification is not a required step, as the method operates on native plasma without chemical extraction or precipitation. The particle size of low-density lipoprotein is determined as the mean diameter across the population of particles classified within the LDL range, and the concentration of very small and large LDL subfractions is quantified as the number of particles per liter within predefined diameter ranges. Ion mobility analysis is performed using a specialized apparatus comprising an electrospray ionization source, a drift tube under controlled electric field and gas pressure, and a high-resolution particle detector. Lipoprotein particles are aerosolized into charged droplets, which are then desolvated to yield gas-phase particles. These particles are propelled through the drift tube, where their mobility is inversely proportional to their size, allowing for precise separation and counting. The resulting data are analyzed to determine the concentration and diameter distribution of lipoprotein subfractions.

### Determining Prognosis

Prognosis, as used herein, refers to the estimation of the likely course and outcome of disease, expressed as probability of survival, time to event, or risk category. Prognosis may be expressed as a categorical classification (e.g., low, intermediate, high risk), a continuous risk score, or a survival probability estimate. Prognosis is related to clinical factors such as age, diabetes status, inflammatory markers, and dialysis dose, but the invention demonstrates that lipoprotein particle diameter and subfraction concentrations provide independent prognostic information beyond these variables. Statistical significance is determined using established methods including chi-squared tests for categorical associations, Kaplan-Meier survival curves for time-to-event analysis, and Cox proportional hazards regression to adjust for confounders. The reference level for LDL particle diameter is established as the median value observed in a validation cohort of maintenance hemodialysis patients, typically approximately 216.5 angstroms. Elevated levels of very small LDL subfraction concentration are defined as those exceeding the 75th percentile of the reference population, while elevated levels of large LDL subfraction concentration are defined as those above the 75th percentile. The median activity in a population serves as the baseline for comparison, and deviations from this median are used to assign risk. Statistical analyses are performed using software tools such as Stata or custom algorithms implemented in Visual Basic for Applications to organize data, compute hazard ratios, and generate risk reports. Simple algorithms may be employed to combine LDL particle diameter and subfraction concentrations into a single prognostic index. Associations between categorical variables are assessed using contingency tables and Fisher’s exact test. Survival probabilities are estimated using the Kaplan-Meier method, and multivariate models are constructed using Cox proportional hazards regression. The approach is adapted into a computer program that accepts input data from ion mobility analysis and outputs a risk classification with confidence intervals. Overall survival in maintenance hemodialysis patients is predicted by comparing the measured LDL particle diameter to the reference level, wherein a diameter below the reference level indicates increased mortality risk. Similarly, the concentration of very small LDL subfractions is compared to the reference concentration, with higher concentrations indicating increased risk, while higher concentrations of large LDL subfractions indicate reduced risk. The results are reported in a standardized analysis report that includes the measured values, reference ranges, risk classification, and clinical recommendations.

## EXAMPLES

A prospective cohort study was conducted on 235 maintenance hemodialysis patients followed for up to six years. Ion mobility analysis was performed on baseline plasma samples using a commercially available instrument calibrated with lipoprotein standards. Lipoprotein fractionation was achieved without prior ultracentrifugation, directly from native plasma. Assay characteristics included interassay coefficients of variation below 1.0% for LDL particle diameter and between 13% and 20% for subfraction concentrations. Diameter ranges for LDL subfractions were defined as follows: very small LDL, 180.0 to 208.2 Å; small LDL, 208.2 to 214.1 Å; medium LDL, 214.1 to 220.0 Å; and large LDL, 220.0 to 233.3 Å.

### Example 1

The patient population consisted of 235 individuals with a mean age of 54 ± 14 years, 45% of whom were female. Baseline laboratory values included total cholesterol of 143 ± 42 mg/dL, LDL cholesterol of 76 ± 29 mg/dL, and HDL cholesterol of 37 ± 12 mg/dL. Over the follow-up period, 71 patients (31%) died. Demographic and clinical characteristics were consistent across subgroups, with no significant differences in dialysis vintage or comorbidity burden between those who survived and those who did not.

### Example 2

Correlation analyses revealed that conventional lipid measures were significantly correlated with LDL subfraction concentrations, but these correlations did not translate into predictive power for mortality. LDL particle diameter showed a positive correlation with large LDL particle concentration and a negative correlation with small LDL particle concentration, indicating a physiologically coherent relationship between size and composition.

### Example 3

Hazard ratios for mortality across quartiles of LDL particle diameter demonstrated a progressive decline in risk with increasing diameter, with the highest quartile showing a hazard ratio of 0.45 compared to the lowest. The hazard ratio for the highest quartile of very small LDL concentration was 2.43, while the highest quartile of large LDL concentration showed a hazard ratio of 0.38. Cubic spline analyses confirmed a nonlinear, inverse relationship between LDL particle diameter and mortality risk.

### Example 4

Combining LDL particle diameter with conventional LDL cholesterol revealed that patients with above-median LDL particle diameter and above-median LDL cholesterol had the lowest mortality risk, while those with below-median LDL particle diameter and above-median LDL cholesterol had the highest risk. The combination of above-median total LDL particle concentration and above-median LDL particle diameter was associated with a 74% reduction in mortality risk compared to the dual low group.

## SUMMARY OF RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

The present invention demonstrates that conventional lipid measurements are inadequate for predicting mortality in maintenance hemodialysis patients, consistent with the phenomenon known as the lipid paradox. In contrast, the particle diameter of low-density lipoprotein and the concentration of its subfractions—particularly very small and large LDL—provide robust, independent prognostic information. Smaller LDL particle diameter is strongly associated with increased mortality, while higher concentrations of large LDL particles are associated with greater survival. The concentration of very small LDL particles is a powerful predictor of death, independent of inflammation, nutritional status, and traditional lipid levels. The combination of LDL particle diameter with conventional LDL cholesterol improves risk stratification, revealing that high LDL cholesterol is protective only when accompanied by larger particle size. These findings suggest that in dialysis patients, the composition and physical properties of lipoprotein particles are more relevant than their cholesterol content. The superiority of ion mobility-derived measures over conventional lipid panels lies in their ability to quantify the actual number and size of atherogenic particles, rather than estimating cholesterol mass. Limitations of the study include its observational nature and potential selection bias, but the consistency of findings across multiple analytical approaches and statistical models supports the validity of the invention.
