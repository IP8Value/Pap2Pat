# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

### Technical Field

The present invention relates to the field of metallurgy and materials science, specifically to the production of low-carbon steels with a gradient microstructure that exhibits ultra-high surface hardness without the need for post-casting heat treatment, surface hardening, or alloying. The invention encompasses a novel manufacturing process that leverages controlled free oxygen content and rapid solidification to induce the formation of fine oxide inclusions, which act as heterogeneous nucleation sites for acicular ferrite and martensite, resulting in a surface region with exceptional mechanical properties while preserving the ductility and toughness of the steel’s interior.

### Description of the Related Art

Low-carbon steels are widely utilized in structural, automotive, and industrial applications due to their excellent formability, weldability, and cost-effectiveness. However, their inherent limitation lies in their relatively low surface hardness and yield strength, which restrict their use in wear-resistant or high-stress environments. Conventional methods to enhance surface hardness, such as carburizing, nitriding, induction hardening, and laser surface treatment, require additional processing steps that increase manufacturing complexity, energy consumption, and cost. These methods often introduce residual stresses, dimensional distortion, or surface brittleness, compromising the overall integrity of the component. Alternative approaches involving alloying with chromium, molybdenum, or vanadium to promote hardenability are economically prohibitive and may reduce ductility. Furthermore, traditional casting processes yield coarse microstructures with limited control over phase distribution, resulting in homogeneous hardness profiles that fail to deliver the desired combination of hard surface and tough core. There remains a critical unmet need for a single-step, energy-efficient manufacturing method capable of producing low-carbon steels with a self-formed, gradient-hardened surface layer that achieves martensitic-level hardness without altering the bulk composition or requiring post-processing.

## SUMMARY

The present invention introduces a novel class of low-carbon steels characterized by a surface region with ultra-high hardness achieved through a controlled solidification process that promotes the formation of fine oxide inclusions and a mixed microstructure of acicular ferrite, upper bainite, and lath martensite. The steel contains no more than 0.03 wt% carbon and is produced without alloying elements beyond trace amounts of titanium, manganese, and silicon necessary for deoxidation and inclusion control. The invention provides a steel material with a surface hardness exceeding 400 HV, comparable to that of high-carbon martensitic steels, while maintaining a core hardness below 200 HV and preserving the ductility and impact resistance typical of conventional low-carbon steels. A key embodiment of the technique involves the simultaneous control of free oxygen content and cooling rate during solidification to generate submicron oxide inclusions rich in titanium and oxygen, which serve as potent nucleation sites for acicular ferrite and facilitate the diffusionless transformation of carbon-enriched austenite into ultrafine lath martensite. The resulting material exhibits a distinct property gradient from surface to interior, enabling applications in gears, bearings, cutting tools, and wear-resistant components without the need for secondary processing.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The improved low-carbon steels of the present invention are produced through a single-step direct-cast hardening method that eliminates the necessity for post-casting heat treatment or surface modification. The process begins with the melting of a precursor steel material in an induction furnace at temperatures exceeding 1550 °C, followed by the controlled addition of iron oxide (FeO) to adjust the free oxygen content to a range of 30 to 60 parts per million by weight. Titanium is introduced in the form of ferrotitanium to achieve a final titanium concentration between 0.01 and 0.02 wt%, while manganese and silicon are added to maintain deoxidation balance and refine grain structure. The molten steel is then cast between two copper plates separated by a narrow gap, inducing rapid heat extraction and sub-rapid solidification with cooling rates ranging from 400 to 1000 K/s at depths of 0 to 200 micrometers. The resulting steel sheet exhibits a surface layer with a microstructure composed of lath martensite, upper bainite, and acicular ferrite, with the relative proportions of each phase determined by the local cooling rate and the density of oxide inclusions. Fine oxide inclusions, predominantly composed of Ti–O complexes with diameters less than 500 nanometers, are uniformly dispersed throughout the surface region and act as heterogeneous nucleation sites for acicular ferrite during the austenite-to-ferrite transformation. The presence of these inclusions fragments the austenite grains into smaller regions, promoting the formation of fine, high-angle misoriented ferrite needles that inhibit dislocation motion and enhance strength. Simultaneously, localized carbon enrichment occurs in austenite adjacent to bainitic ferrite laths, leading to the diffusionless transformation of carbon-saturated austenite into lath martensite with lath widths as narrow as 128 nanometers. The resulting martensite exhibits a body-centered tetragonal lattice distortion consistent with carbon concentrations up to 0.14 wt%, despite the bulk carbon content remaining below 0.03 wt%. The acicular ferrite morphology is distinguished from polygonal ferrite by its needle-like, non-equiaxed geometry and high-angle grain boundaries, which provide superior resistance to crack propagation compared to the more ductile but weaker polygonal ferrite. The critical size for effective heterogeneous nucleation of acicular ferrite is determined to be less than 600 nanometers, with inclusions below this threshold exhibiting significantly higher nucleation efficiency. The single-step Direct-Cast Hardening (DiCH) method enables the formation of a property-gradient structure wherein the surface region achieves a Vickers hardness of 420 to 480 HV, while the interior remains below 180 HV, creating a natural transition zone that minimizes stress concentrations. The composition of oxide inclusions varies with depth: near the surface, inclusions are rich in titanium and oxygen, while deeper regions exhibit higher silicon and manganese content, reflecting the differential segregation behavior during solidification. The presence of titanium-containing inclusions is essential for stabilizing the nucleation of acicular ferrite and suppressing the formation of coarse polygonal ferrite. The resulting steel structure exhibits a surface hardness that exceeds that of conventional low-carbon steels by a factor of two to three, while maintaining the same chemical composition and without the addition of expensive alloying elements. The technique for producing this steel involves precise control of the free oxygen content during melting, followed by rapid solidification under constrained thermal conditions, ensuring the formation of a dense population of submicron inclusions. The addition of FeO and other oxide-forming components to the melt ensures the generation of fine, stable inclusions that remain dispersed throughout the solidifying matrix, promoting uniform nucleation and microstructural refinement. The resulting steel exhibits a unique combination of ultra-high surface hardness, retained ductility, and resistance to abrasive wear, making it suitable for applications demanding both toughness and surface durability.

### EXAMPLES

An ultra-hard low-carbon steel was produced using the DiCH method with four distinct variations in free oxygen content and cooling rate. Each sample was cast from a melt containing 0.023 wt% carbon, 0.5 wt% manganese, 0.2 wt% silicon, and 0.015 wt% titanium. Three levels of free oxygen—35 ppm, 45 ppm, and 55 ppm—were established by controlled addition of FeO, and cooling rates were adjusted by varying the thickness of the copper mold and the dwell time before quenching. Four samples, designated S1 through S4, were prepared with differing combinations of oxygen content and cooling rate. The average cooling rate was calculated using ProCAST simulation software and verified by thermal imaging, yielding values between 400 and 1000 K/s in the surface region. Microstructural analysis via scanning electron microscopy and transmission electron microscopy revealed a consistent presence of submicron oxide inclusions at the surface, with particle density increasing with free oxygen content. Sample S1, with 35 ppm oxygen and a cooling rate of 950 K/s, exhibited the highest density of titanium-rich inclusions and the finest acicular ferrite morphology. Sample S4, with 55 ppm oxygen and a cooling rate of 600 K/s, showed a more uniform distribution of inclusions and a higher volume fraction of lath martensite. The particle density variation (PDV) was quantified across the cross-section, with S1 and S3 showing higher PDV near the surface, indicating localized nucleation enhancement. The microstructure at the surface of S2 and S4 consisted predominantly of acicular ferrite and upper bainite, whereas S1 and S3 displayed a mixed layer of martensite and bainite. Energy-dispersive spectroscopy confirmed the Ti–O composition of inclusions in S4, with minor contributions from Mn and Si. Selected area electron diffraction patterns confirmed the bcc structure of ferrite and the bct distortion of martensite. The size distribution of inclusions in S4 showed a peak at 320 nm, within the critical nucleation range. Vickers hardness testing revealed surface hardness values of 420 HV for S1, 440 HV for S2, 460 HV for S3, and 480 HV for S4, while the interior hardness remained below 170 HV in all cases. Nanoindentation tests using a Berkovich tip confirmed nanohardness values of 9.87 GPa for martensite, 4.18 GPa for upper bainite, and 2.93 GPa for acicular ferrite. The yield strength of the surface layer was calculated to be 2378 MPa for martensite, demonstrating that the steel achieves performance comparable to high-carbon steels without alloying or heat treatment. The coupling effect of free oxygen content and cooling rate was shown to be critical: higher oxygen promoted inclusion density, while higher cooling rates favored martensite formation. The resulting material exhibited a continuous gradient in hardness, microstructure, and mechanical properties from surface to core, enabling the fabrication of components with superior wear resistance and impact toughness in a single manufacturing step.
