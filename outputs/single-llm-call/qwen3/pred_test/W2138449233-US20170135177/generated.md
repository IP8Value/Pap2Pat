## TECHNICAL FIELD

The present invention relates to smart lighting systems for indoor environments, particularly those employing non-imaging color sensors and controllable LED fixtures to estimate spatial occupancy distributions without compromising privacy. The invention integrates perturbation-modulated illumination with passive optical sensing to infer the presence and approximate location of objects or individuals within a room, enabling dynamic adjustment of lighting conditions based on real-time occupancy data. This technology is particularly suited for applications in office spaces, residential dwellings, healthcare facilities, educational institutions, and commercial buildings where energy efficiency, occupant comfort, and privacy preservation are paramount. Unlike conventional systems that rely on imaging sensors such as cameras or depth scanners, the disclosed system utilizes low-resolution, non-visual photometric measurements derived from color-filtered photodiodes, thereby eliminating the capture of personally identifiable visual information. The system operates under the principle that changes in the spatial transport of visible light—induced by the presence of objects or people—can be quantified through controlled modulation of light sources and subsequent analysis of sensor responses, enabling occupancy estimation without direct observation. The invention further encompasses the computational framework for modeling light transport, reconstructing occupancy distributions from sparse sensor data, and translating those estimates into actionable control signals for lighting fixtures, all while maintaining imperceptible perturbations to human occupants.

## BACKGROUND

Existing occupancy-sensitive lighting systems have relied heavily on imaging sensors such as webcams, infrared arrays, and depth cameras to detect the presence and position of individuals within a space. While these technologies offer high spatial resolution and detailed scene understanding, they inherently capture visual data that raises significant privacy concerns, particularly in environments such as restrooms, bedrooms, and private offices where individuals expect a reasonable expectation of confidentiality. Even when image data is processed locally and not transmitted or stored, the mere presence of a camera can induce psychological discomfort and deter usage of the space. Alternative non-imaging sensors, including passive infrared (PIR), ultrasonic, microwave, and acoustic detectors, have been employed to mitigate these concerns; however, each suffers from inherent limitations. PIR sensors are prone to false negatives when occupants remain still and false positives due to thermal drift or non-human heat sources. Ultrasonic and microwave sensors are susceptible to environmental interference from moving objects such as curtains or fans, and often lack the spatial granularity required for nuanced lighting control. Acoustic sensors are highly vulnerable to ambient noise, resulting in unreliable detection. Furthermore, none of these conventional sensors are capable of distinguishing between different types of obstructions—such as a person, a piece of furniture, or a moving curtain—nor can they provide any indication of the spatial extent or shape of the obstruction. The absence of a sensing modality that is both privacy-preserving and capable of capturing spatially distributed information has created a critical gap in the field of intelligent lighting. There exists a persistent need for a system that can infer occupancy with sufficient spatial fidelity to enable adaptive lighting control, while simultaneously avoiding the capture, storage, or transmission of any visual or biometric data that could compromise personal privacy. The present invention fulfills this need by leveraging the inherent properties of visible light transport and the controllability of modern LED fixtures to construct a non-invasive, non-imaging occupancy sensing architecture.

## SUMMARY

The invention introduces a novel occupancy-sensitive smart lighting system that operates through the modulation of base illumination with imperceptible perturbations and the subsequent analysis of photometric sensor responses to estimate spatial occupancy. The system employs a network of color-controllable LED fixtures as both illumination sources and active transmitters of encoded light signals, paired with a sparse array of non-imaging color sensors that measure changes in luminous flux across multiple spectral bands. Rather than capturing images, the sensors output low-dimensional numeric values representing the intensity of red, green, blue, and white light components, ensuring no visual information is retained. A control strategy module governs the system’s operation by alternating between an adjustment stage, wherein the base lighting condition is set according to predefined comfort or energy goals, and a sensing stage, during which small, temporally varying perturbations are superimposed upon the base light. These perturbations are designed to be below the human perceptual threshold for flicker or color change, yet sufficient to induce measurable variations in sensor output. The aggregate sensor responses across multiple perturbation patterns are used to construct a light transport matrix that encodes the spatial relationship between each fixture and each sensor. This matrix is then analyzed using a computational model that interprets deviations from a reference transport state as indicators of occupancy. Two distinct estimation methods are employed: one based on the blockage of direct light paths for wall-mounted sensors, and another based on the modulation of reflected light for ceiling-mounted sensors. The resulting occupancy estimates are coarse, low-resolution representations of spatial occupancy that are sufficient for lighting control but insufficient for identifying individuals or discerning fine details. The system further incorporates an optimization framework for selecting perturbation sequences that minimize perceptibility while maximizing information content, and employs a pseudo-inverse solution to recover the light transport matrix from underdetermined or overdetermined measurement sets. The entire process is implemented through a modular architecture comprising a perturbation manager, a sensor data manager, a light transport modeler, and an occupancy estimation system, all coordinated by a strategy manager that ensures seamless transitions between sensing and adjustment phases. The system is designed to operate continuously and adaptively, periodically recalibrating its reference model and updating lighting conditions in response to changing occupancy, thereby achieving energy savings, enhanced comfort, and robust privacy protection.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The invention comprises an occupancy-sensitive smart lighting system that dynamically adjusts illumination based on inferred spatial occupancy without capturing visual imagery. Occupancy, as used herein, refers to the presence and approximate spatial distribution of objects or individuals within an enclosed environment, as inferred from perturbations in visible light transport, rather than from direct observation. The system is composed of two primary functional modules: a control strategy module and an occupancy sensing module, which operate in alternating sensing and adjustment stages. During the adjustment stage, the control strategy module determines an optimal base lighting condition based on predefined criteria such as energy efficiency, circadian alignment, or task-specific illumination requirements. During the sensing stage, the occupancy sensing module introduces a sequence of imperceptible perturbations to the base light and records the resulting changes in sensor output across multiple spectral channels. The perturbations are applied rapidly and sequentially, ensuring that the ambient conditions and spatial occupancy remain effectively static during data acquisition. The sensor data is then processed to construct a light transport matrix that characterizes the relationship between the input signals to the LED fixtures and the output signals from the color sensors. This matrix is compared against a reference matrix acquired during a prior calibration phase to generate a difference matrix, which encodes spatial variations in light attenuation caused by the presence of objects. The difference matrix is then subjected to a reconstruction algorithm that estimates the occupancy distribution, either as a three-dimensional volume using a light blockage model for wall-mounted sensors or as a two-dimensional confidence map using a light reflection model for ceiling-mounted sensors. The estimated occupancy is then fed back to the control strategy module to inform the next lighting adjustment, completing a closed-loop feedback cycle.

The control strategy module comprises a strategy manager, a lighting controller, a base light manager, and a perturbation manager. The strategy manager coordinates the overall operation of the system, determining when to transition between sensing and adjustment phases and selecting the target lighting condition for the next adjustment. The lighting controller translates the target lighting condition into specific intensity values for each color channel of each LED fixture. The base light manager maintains the current base illumination level and ensures that perturbations are applied relative to this baseline. The perturbation manager generates a sequence of perturbation vectors, each representing a small, temporally distinct variation in the input to the fixtures, and orders them according to an optimization criterion that minimizes perceptibility while maximizing information content. The occupancy sensing module comprises a sensor data manager, a light transport modeler, and an occupancy estimation system. The sensor data manager collects and synchronizes the output from each color sensor, compensating for temporal delays and sensor drift. The light transport modeler computes the light transport matrix by solving the linear system defined by the perturbation inputs and the corresponding sensor responses, using a Moore-Penrose pseudo-inverse when the system is overdetermined or a sparse recovery technique when underdetermined. The occupancy estimation system applies either the light blockage model or the light reflection model to the difference matrix to generate an occupancy estimate. In the light blockage model, the system assumes that direct line-of-sight paths between fixtures and wall-mounted sensors dominate light transport, and that a reduction in measured flux along such a path indicates obstruction by an occupant. In the light reflection model, the system assumes that ceiling-mounted sensors detect only diffuse reflections, and that changes in reflected flux correlate with the presence of occupants on the floor plane. The system further employs a Gaussian kernel function to compute confidence values for each spatial location, normalizing for non-uniform fixture and sensor distributions. The resulting occupancy estimate is a low-resolution spatial map that indicates regions of probable occupancy without revealing identity, shape, or motion.

The system design is highly flexible, allowing for arbitrary configurations of fixtures and sensors, and accommodating different room geometries and lighting requirements. The perturbation patterns are generated randomly and then reordered using a genetic algorithm to minimize perceptible flicker, based on the principle that neighboring perturbations should be as similar as possible. The magnitude of each perturbation is carefully calibrated to exceed the noise floor of the sensors while remaining below the human visual detection threshold, typically set at approximately 2.5% deviation from the base intensity. The light transport matrix is computed under the quasi-static assumption that neither the occupancy nor the ambient light changes during the brief sensing interval. The system eliminates the influence of ambient light by subtracting the sensor response under a reference condition from the response under perturbed conditions. The matrix inversion process may involve rank minimization or sparse recovery techniques to improve robustness in low-sensor-density environments. The system further incorporates precomputed reflection kernels for ceiling-mounted configurations, enabling rapid generation of 2D confidence maps through weighted summation. All computational processes are implemented on a dedicated computing system comprising a processor, memory, input/output interfaces, and a communication pathway to the lighting hardware. The system may be implemented as a standalone unit or integrated into existing building automation infrastructure. The entire process—base light setting, perturbation application, sensor data collection, matrix computation, occupancy estimation, and lighting adjustment—is repeated periodically to adapt to dynamic occupancy conditions. The system is designed to be self-calibrating, allowing users to define a new reference state by manually initiating a calibration sequence, thereby accommodating changes in furniture, decor, or room layout. The invention thus provides a complete, privacy-preserving, and energy-efficient solution for intelligent lighting control in any indoor environment.
