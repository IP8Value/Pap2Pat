# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNICAL FIELD

- introduce invention

The present invention relates to a novel composition and method for achieving sustained, cytoplasmic inhibition of gene expression in eukaryotic cells without requiring nuclear entry of genetic material. Specifically, the invention provides a hybrid mRNA/DNA system that enables prolonged suppression of target gene expression through the self-amplifying production of bacteriophage T7 RNA polymerase in the cytoplasm, followed by continuous transcription of short hairpin RNA under the control of a T7 promoter. This system overcomes the transient nature of conventional RNA interference therapies by establishing a self-sustaining enzymatic cascade that generates mature small interfering RNA over an extended period, thereby enabling durable gene silencing in both in vitro and in vivo settings. The invention further encompasses the formulation of this hybrid system into neutral liposomal carriers for systemic delivery, particularly to solid tumors, without inducing significant innate immune activation or cytotoxicity.

## BACKGROUND ART

- define RNA interference

RNA interference is a conserved biological mechanism in eukaryotic cells that enables sequence-specific degradation of messenger RNA through the action of small non-coding RNA molecules. This process is initiated by the introduction of double-stranded RNA, which is processed by the enzyme Dicer into short interfering RNA duplexes approximately 21 to 23 nucleotides in length. These duplexes are loaded into the RNA-induced silencing complex, where one strand, known as the guide strand, directs the complex to complementary mRNA targets, resulting in their cleavage and subsequent degradation. RNA interference has emerged as a powerful tool for functional genomics and holds significant therapeutic potential for the selective silencing of disease-associated genes.

- explain small interference RNA

Small interfering RNA refers to synthetic or endogenously derived double-stranded RNA molecules that mediate RNA interference by binding to complementary mRNA transcripts and triggering their cleavage. These molecules are typically chemically synthesized and administered exogenously to cells, where they rapidly enter the RNAi pathway. However, their efficacy is limited by rapid degradation by nucleases, inefficient cellular uptake, and dilution during cell division, resulting in a transient suppression of gene expression that typically lasts no longer than four days in cultured cells.

- describe siRNA applications

Small interfering RNA has been widely explored for therapeutic applications in oncology, virology, and genetic disorders due to its ability to silence specific genes with high precision. Clinical trials have investigated siRNA for the treatment of conditions such as macular degeneration, amyloidosis, and various cancers. Despite promising early results, the clinical translation of siRNA has been hindered by the need for frequent administration, poor biodistribution to non-hepatic tissues, and the induction of off-target or immune-mediated toxicities.

- limitations of siRNA duration

The transient nature of siRNA-mediated gene silencing represents a fundamental limitation for its therapeutic utility. Once delivered into the cytoplasm, siRNA molecules are subject to enzymatic degradation, partitioning into daughter cells during mitosis, and competition with endogenous RNAi components. As a result, repeated dosing is required to maintain suppression of target gene expression, which increases the risk of immune activation, accumulation of delivery vehicle components, and patient non-compliance. This short duration renders siRNA unsuitable for chronic diseases requiring long-term gene modulation.

- conventional siRNA delivery methods

Conventional delivery methods for siRNA include lipid-based nanoparticles, polymer conjugates, and viral vectors. Cationic lipids and polymers form electrostatic complexes with negatively charged siRNA to facilitate cellular uptake but often induce cytotoxicity and nonspecific organ accumulation. Viral vectors, such as adenoviruses and lentiviruses, provide efficient transduction and prolonged expression but carry inherent risks of insertional mutagenesis, immunogenicity, and limitations in scalable manufacturing.

- problems of cationic transporters

Cationic transporters, while effective at complexing siRNA and promoting endocytosis, exhibit high affinity for serum proteins and endothelial cell surfaces, leading to rapid clearance from circulation and reduced delivery to target tissues. Their positive surface charge promotes aggregation, enhances uptake by the reticuloendothelial system, and triggers inflammatory responses through activation of Toll-like receptors and complement pathways. These properties limit their utility for systemic administration and necessitate high dosing regimens that exacerbate toxicity.

- limitations of viral vectors

Viral vectors, although capable of achieving long-term transgene expression, are associated with significant safety concerns. Adenoviral vectors elicit strong innate and adaptive immune responses that can lead to hepatotoxicity and systemic inflammation. Lentiviral vectors integrate into the host genome, posing a risk of oncogenic activation through insertional mutagenesis. Additionally, the production of high-titer, clinical-grade viral vectors remains complex, costly, and subject to regulatory constraints that impede widespread clinical adoption.

- nucleus-dependent shRNA expression

An alternative to direct siRNA delivery involves the intracellular expression of short hairpin RNA from plasmid DNA vectors transcribed by RNA polymerase III promoters such as U6 or H1. These promoters drive the synthesis of RNA hairpins that are processed by Dicer into functional siRNA. However, this strategy is inherently dependent on the nuclear import of plasmid DNA, a process that is inefficient in non-dividing or post-mitotic cells, where the nuclear envelope remains intact and limits passive diffusion of large DNA molecules.

- limitations of plasmid DNA method

The requirement for nuclear entry severely restricts the applicability of plasmid-based shRNA expression systems, particularly in vivo. Only a small fraction—typically less than one percent—of internalized plasmid DNA reaches the nucleus, resulting in low and inconsistent shRNA expression. Furthermore, prolonged nuclear expression of shRNA can saturate the nuclear export machinery, leading to the sequestration of endogenous microRNAs and unintended cellular toxicity. These limitations diminish the therapeutic potential of plasmid-based approaches despite their ability to provide longer duration than synthetic siRNA.

- motivate nucleus-independent shRNA expression

To overcome these constraints, a nucleus-independent system for shRNA expression is needed—one that operates entirely within the cytoplasm, bypasses the nuclear barrier, and sustains enzymatic production of shRNA over an extended period. Such a system would enable durable gene silencing in a broader range of cell types, including quiescent and primary cells, while minimizing the risks associated with nuclear integration and immune activation. The development of a self-amplifying, cytoplasmic transcriptional platform represents a critical advancement toward achieving clinically viable, long-term RNA interference therapy.

## DISCLOSURE

### Technical Problem

- identify technical problem of gene expression inhibition

The technical problem addressed by this invention is the inability of existing RNA interference technologies to achieve sustained, high-efficiency gene silencing in vivo without repeated administration, nuclear translocation, or the use of immunogenic viral vectors. Current methods suffer from transient suppression, poor biodistribution, cytotoxic delivery vehicles, and insufficient expression levels in non-dividing cells. There exists a critical unmet need for a non-viral, non-nuclear, self-sustaining system capable of producing therapeutic levels of small interfering RNA over a period exceeding ten days following a single administration.

### Technical Solution

- provide composition for sustained inhibition of gene expression

The invention provides a composition for sustained inhibition of gene expression comprising a hybrid mRNA/DNA system designed for cytoplasmic operation. This composition includes a 5′-capped messenger RNA encoding bacteriophage T7 RNA polymerase, a plasmid DNA encoding a self-amplifying T7 RNA polymerase gene under the control of a T7 promoter and an internal ribosome entry site, and a separate DNA fragment encoding a short hairpin RNA under the control of a T7 promoter. Together, these components function in concert to initiate and perpetuate the production of T7 RNA polymerase in the cytoplasm, leading to continuous transcription of shRNA and subsequent generation of mature siRNA through endogenous cellular processing.

- define T7 RNA polymerase

T7 RNA polymerase is a single-subunit, DNA-dependent RNA polymerase derived from bacteriophage T7 that exhibits high transcriptional efficiency and specificity for its cognate T7 promoter sequence. Unlike eukaryotic RNA polymerases, T7 RNA polymerase does not require cellular transcription factors or nuclear localization signals to function and is capable of initiating RNA synthesis directly in the cytoplasm. Its ability to transcribe long RNA transcripts with high fidelity makes it uniquely suited for sustained, high-yield expression of RNA-based therapeutics.

- describe self-amplification of T7 RNA polymerase

The self-amplification of T7 RNA polymerase occurs through a positive feedback loop wherein an initial supply of T7 RNA polymerase protein, translated from the delivered 5′-capped mRNA, binds to T7 promoters present on the plasmid DNA encoding the polymerase gene. This binding initiates transcription of a new mRNA encoding additional T7 RNA polymerase, which is then translated into more enzyme, further amplifying the reaction. This autocatalytic process continues until substrate depletion or cellular degradation limits the system, resulting in exponential accumulation of T7 RNA polymerase over time.

- detail plasmid DNA for self-amplification of T7 RNA polymerase

The plasmid DNA for self-amplification of T7 RNA polymerase comprises a T7 promoter sequence positioned upstream of a coding sequence for T7 RNA polymerase, which is flanked downstream by an internal ribosome entry site and a polyadenylation signal, followed by a T7 transcription termination sequence. The internal ribosome entry site enables cap-independent translation of the T7 RNA polymerase mRNA in the cytoplasm, ensuring efficient protein synthesis even in the absence of a 5′ cap. The polyadenylation signal enhances mRNA stability, and the T7 terminator ensures precise termination of transcription, preventing read-through and non-specific RNA production.

- describe mRNA fragment of T7 RNA polymerase

The mRNA fragment of T7 RNA polymerase is a synthetic, in vitro transcribed RNA molecule containing the full-length coding sequence of T7 RNA polymerase, modified to include a 5′-cap structure at its terminus to enhance translational initiation and stability. This mRNA is delivered as a separate component from the plasmid DNA and serves as the initial trigger for the self-amplification cascade. The cap structure is synthesized using an anti-reverse cap analog to ensure proper orientation and efficient recognition by the cellular translation machinery.

- describe 5'-cap structure of mRNA fragment

The 5′-cap structure of the mRNA fragment is a modified guanosine nucleotide linked via a 5′-5′ triphosphate bridge to the first transcribed nucleotide, with a methyl group added to the 7-position of the guanine ring. This cap structure protects the mRNA from exonucleolytic degradation and facilitates recruitment of the eukaryotic translation initiation complex, thereby significantly enhancing the efficiency of T7 RNA polymerase protein synthesis upon cytoplasmic delivery.

- synthesize mRNA fragment of T7 RNA polymerase

The mRNA fragment of T7 RNA polymerase is synthesized by in vitro transcription using a DNA template containing a T7 promoter upstream of the T7 RNA polymerase coding sequence. The transcription reaction is performed in the presence of an anti-reverse cap analog and nucleoside triphosphates, followed by purification via LiCl precipitation and DNase digestion to remove template DNA. The resulting mRNA is quantified spectrophotometrically and assessed for integrity by gel electrophoresis.

- describe plasmid DNA for self-amplification of T7 RNA polymerase

The plasmid DNA for self-amplification of T7 RNA polymerase is constructed by cloning the T7 RNA polymerase coding sequence downstream of a T7 promoter and an internal ribosome entry site derived from encephalomyocarditis virus, followed by a polyadenylation signal and a T7 transcription terminator. The plasmid is maintained in an E. coli expression system and purified by endotoxin-free alkaline lysis and anion-exchange chromatography to ensure suitability for in vivo use.

- describe IRES domain in plasmid DNA

The internal ribosome entry site domain in the plasmid DNA is a structured RNA element that allows ribosomes to bind and initiate translation independently of the 5′ cap structure. This feature ensures that the T7 RNA polymerase mRNA transcribed in the cytoplasm from the plasmid is efficiently translated, thereby sustaining the autocatalytic loop even in the absence of nuclear-derived capped mRNAs.

- describe gene encoding T7 RNA polymerase

The gene encoding T7 RNA polymerase is derived from the bacteriophage T7 genome and encodes a 99-kDa protein with high processivity and promoter specificity. The coding sequence is codon-optimized for expression in mammalian cells and cloned into the plasmid vector without introns, as nuclear splicing is not required for cytoplasmic function.

- describe poly A tail in plasmid DNA

The poly A tail in the plasmid DNA is a sequence of 100 to 200 adenine nucleotides positioned downstream of the T7 RNA polymerase coding sequence to enhance mRNA stability and translational efficiency. This tail is transcribed as part of the mRNA and protects the transcript from rapid degradation by cytoplasmic exonucleases.

- describe T7 termination sequence in plasmid DNA

The T7 termination sequence is a 19-nucleotide stem-loop structure located downstream of the coding region that signals the T7 RNA polymerase to terminate transcription precisely. This sequence prevents read-through into plasmid backbone regions and ensures the production of discrete, functional mRNA transcripts.

- describe self-amplification of T7 RNA polymerase

Self-amplification of T7 RNA polymerase is achieved through the iterative binding of newly synthesized T7 RNA polymerase protein to T7 promoter sequences on the plasmid DNA, resulting in the continuous transcription of additional T7 RNA polymerase mRNA. This creates a positive feedback loop that exponentially increases intracellular concentrations of the enzyme over time, enabling sustained transcription of downstream shRNA constructs.

- examine cytoplasmic expression of T7 polymerase

Cytoplasmic expression of T7 RNA polymerase is confirmed by Western blot analysis of cell lysates following delivery of the hybrid system. Expression is detectable within hours and persists for up to nine days, with significantly higher levels observed when the 5′-capped mRNA is co-delivered with the self-amplifying plasmid, compared to systems relying on nuclear promoters such as CMV.

- describe DNA fragment encoding gene expression inhibitor

The DNA fragment encoding the gene expression inhibitor is a synthetic, double-stranded oligonucleotide containing a T7 promoter sequence upstream of a short hairpin RNA sequence designed to target a specific mRNA transcript. The hairpin structure includes a 19-nucleotide stem complementary to the target mRNA and a 9-nucleotide loop, forming a substrate for Dicer-mediated cleavage into functional siRNA.

- describe T7 promoter in DNA fragment

The T7 promoter in the DNA fragment is a 23-base pair sequence derived from bacteriophage T7 that is specifically recognized by T7 RNA polymerase. This promoter is positioned immediately upstream of the shRNA sequence and is essential for initiating transcription of the hairpin RNA in the cytoplasm.

- describe gene expression inhibitor

The gene expression inhibitor is a short hairpin RNA transcribed from the DNA fragment, which is processed by the cellular RNAi machinery into a mature small interfering RNA that binds to and induces degradation of a complementary target mRNA. The inhibitor is designed to be non-immunogenic and to avoid off-target effects through careful sequence selection and bioinformatic filtering.

- describe siRNA, shRNA, microRNA, and aptamer

The gene expression inhibitor may take the form of small interfering RNA, short hairpin RNA, microRNA, or aptamer, each capable of modulating gene expression through distinct mechanisms. In this invention, the preferred form is short hairpin RNA, as it is efficiently transcribed by T7 RNA polymerase and processed into siRNA by endogenous Dicer, enabling prolonged silencing without requiring repeated delivery.

- evaluate long-term shRNA expression

Long-term shRNA expression is evaluated by quantitative reverse transcription PCR and small RNA-specific TaqMan assays, which detect the presence of shRNA intermediates and mature siRNA over time. Expression is sustained for more than thirteen days in vitro, with siRNA levels peaking at day ten and remaining detectable beyond day thirteen, demonstrating a duration far exceeding that of conventional siRNA delivery.

- describe limitations of existing shRNA expression method

Existing methods of shRNA expression rely on nuclear transcription from pol III promoters, which require plasmid DNA to enter the nucleus—a process that is inefficient in non-dividing cells and results in low and variable expression. These systems also risk saturation of nuclear export machinery and are incompatible with systemic delivery due to poor biodistribution and instability of naked plasmid DNA.

- describe advantages of nucleus-independent T7 RNA polymerase

The nucleus-independent T7 RNA polymerase system eliminates the need for nuclear entry, enabling robust gene silencing in quiescent and primary cells. It provides a self-sustaining enzymatic cascade that produces high levels of shRNA over an extended period, avoids immune activation associated with viral vectors, and permits formulation into neutral, non-toxic liposomal carriers for systemic administration.

- define target tissue

The target tissue refers to any tissue or organ in which sustained gene silencing is desired, including but not limited to solid tumors, liver, lung, brain, and sites of chronic inflammation. In preferred embodiments, the target tissue is a tumor site exhibiting enhanced permeability and retention, where the liposomal carrier accumulates passively due to its nanoscale size.

- describe delivery of composition to target tissue

The composition is delivered to the target tissue via systemic administration of a liposomal formulation, which protects the mRNA and DNA components from degradation and facilitates accumulation at the target site through the enhanced permeability and retention effect. The liposomes are administered intravenously, allowing circulation to reach disseminated tumor sites without the need for local injection.

- provide transporter of gene expression inhibitor

The transporter of the gene expression inhibitor is a liposomal nanoparticle composed of neutral phospholipids that encapsulate the hybrid mRNA/DNA system. The transporter shields the nucleic acids from nucleases, enhances cellular uptake, and minimizes immune recognition, enabling safe and effective delivery to target cells.

- describe composition of transporter

The composition of the transporter comprises 1,2-dioleoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphatidylcholine (DOPC) as the primary phospholipid, mixed with the hybrid mRNA/DNA system at a weight ratio of oligonucleotides to lipid of 1:10. The mixture is formed by solvent evaporation and hydration, followed by size exclusion filtration to remove unencapsulated components.

- describe liposome as transporter

The liposome serves as a biocompatible, non-immunogenic, and non-cationic transporter that encapsulates the mRNA and DNA components of the hybrid system. Its neutral surface charge prevents nonspecific binding to serum proteins and endothelial cells, prolonging circulation time and promoting passive accumulation in tumor tissues.

- prepare transporter by mixing composition and DOPC

The transporter is prepared by dissolving DOPC in tert-butanol, mixing with the hybrid mRNA/DNA system, lyophilizing the solution, and hydrating the resulting cake with isotonic saline. The suspension is then filtered through a 30 kDa cutoff membrane to remove free oligonucleotides, yielding a liposomal formulation with greater than 90% encapsulation efficiency.

- evaluate loading efficiency of oligonucleotides

Loading efficiency is evaluated by measuring the concentration of free oligonucleotides in the filtrate using spectrophotometry. The encapsulated fraction is calculated by subtracting the free fraction from the total input, with values consistently exceeding 90% under optimized conditions.

- describe inhibition of target gene expression

Inhibition of target gene expression is demonstrated by quantitative reduction of target mRNA and protein levels in treated cells and tissues. This is measured using quantitative RT-PCR, Western blot, and fluorescence-based assays, with suppression persisting for more than ten days following a single administration.

- evaluate inhibition of RFP mRNA and protein

Inhibition of RFP mRNA and protein is evaluated in B16F10/RFP melanoma cells and tumor-bearing mice. Following administration of the liposomal composition, RFP mRNA levels decrease by more than 50% at day seven and remain suppressed for thirteen days. RFP protein expression is similarly reduced, with fluorescence intensity declining by more than 60% and remaining significantly suppressed for ten days.

- compare with existing nucleus-dependent shRNA expression system

Compared to a nucleus-dependent system using an H1 promoter-driven shRNA plasmid, the T7-based cytoplasmic system achieves a 1.8-fold greater reduction in target mRNA at day three and sustains suppression for more than four additional days. The nuclear system shows complete loss of silencing by day four, whereas the cytoplasmic system remains effective beyond day thirteen.

- describe accumulation of transporter in cancer tissue

The liposomal transporter accumulates preferentially in cancer tissue due to its nanoscale size and the enhanced permeability and retention effect. Fluorescently labeled liposomes are detected in tumor tissue at significantly higher concentrations than in liver, spleen, or kidney, with tumor-to-liver ratios exceeding 3:1.

- evaluate accumulation level of Cy5.5-auto_shRFP@LS

The accumulation level of Cy5.5-labeled auto_shRFP@LS in tumor tissue is quantified using in vivo imaging and ex vivo fluorescence analysis. Tumor fluorescence intensity is 2.7-fold higher than in control animals receiving free siRNA, confirming targeted delivery and retention.

- describe inhibition of RFP gene expression in cancer tissue

Inhibition of RFP gene expression in cancer tissue is confirmed by noninvasive fluorescence imaging, immunohistochemistry, and qRT-PCR. Tumors from treated mice show a 43% reduction in RFP mRNA and near-complete absence of RFP protein, whereas control groups exhibit no significant suppression.

- provide method for preparing transporter

The method for preparing the transporter involves synthesizing the 5′-capped T7 RNA polymerase mRNA, constructing the self-amplifying plasmid DNA, and assembling the T7-driven shRNA DNA fragment. These components are mixed in a defined molar ratio, combined with DOPC in tert-butanol, lyophilized, hydrated with saline, and purified by ultrafiltration to remove unencapsulated material.

- describe preparation of mRNA fragment, plasmid DNA, and DNA fragment

The mRNA fragment is prepared by in vitro transcription using T7 RNA polymerase and an anti-reverse cap analog. The plasmid DNA is cloned into an endotoxin-free vector and purified by anion-exchange chromatography. The DNA fragment is assembled by PCR using overlapping oligonucleotides and verified by sequencing.

- mix composition with DOPC

The composition is mixed with DOPC at a weight ratio of 1:10 in tert-butanol under sterile conditions, followed by rapid freezing and lyophilization to form a dry powder.

- remove free oligonucleotides by filtration

The hydrated liposomal suspension is passed through a 30 kDa molecular weight cutoff filter to retain liposomes and remove unencapsulated oligonucleotides. The filtrate is analyzed to confirm removal of free components.

- describe ratio of oligonucleotides and DOPC

The optimal ratio of oligonucleotides to DOPC is 1:10 by weight, which ensures complete encapsulation, minimal aggregation, and maximal delivery efficiency without inducing cytotoxicity or immune activation.

### Advantageous Effects of the Invention

- summarize advantageous effects of the invention

The invention provides a non-viral, nucleus-independent, self-amplifying system for sustained gene silencing that achieves prolonged suppression of target gene expression following a single administration. It eliminates the need for repeated dosing, avoids the risks associated with viral vectors and cationic carriers, and enables efficient delivery to solid tumors via passive targeting. The system demonstrates high loading efficiency, low immunogenicity, and excellent biocompatibility, with silencing lasting over thirteen days in vitro and six days in vivo without toxicity. This represents a transformative advance in RNA interference therapeutics, enabling durable, systemic, and safe gene silencing for the treatment of chronic diseases and malignancies.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

- describe invention with reference to examples

The invention is further illustrated by the following examples, which demonstrate the design, preparation, characterization, and functional efficacy of the hybrid mRNA/DNA system and its liposomal formulation.

### Example 1. Design and Preparation of pT7/T7 Polymerase System Capable of Sustained Expression of shRNA in Cytoplasm

- design auto_T7pol plasmid capable of sustained cytoplasmic self-amplification of T7 polymerase

The auto_T7pol plasmid was designed to contain a bacteriophage T7 promoter upstream of a codon-optimized T7 RNA polymerase coding sequence, followed by an encephalomyocarditis virus internal ribosome entry site, a polyadenylation signal, and a T7 transcription terminator. The plasmid was cloned into a pUC-based vector and verified by sequencing.

- prepare pT7/shRNA DNA template for cytoplasmic shRNA expression

A 64-base pair DNA fragment encoding a short hairpin RNA targeting red fluorescence protein was synthesized by assembly PCR. The fragment contained a T7 promoter, a 19-nucleotide stem complementary to RFP mRNA, a 9-nucleotide loop, and a T7 terminator. The sequence was confirmed by Sanger sequencing.

- synthesize 5′-capped T7pol mRNA for supplying first T7 polymerase

A DNA template encoding T7 RNA polymerase was transcribed in vitro using T7 RNA polymerase and an anti-reverse cap analog. The resulting mRNA was purified by LiCl precipitation and quantified spectrophotometrically. The 5′ cap was confirmed by mass spectrometry.

- prepare pSuper_shRFP plasmid for nucleus-dependent shRNA expression as control group

A plasmid expressing shRNA under the control of the H1 promoter was constructed by cloning a 64-bp shRNA sequence into the pSuper vector. The construct was verified by sequencing and used as a nuclear-dependent control in comparative experiments.

- describe design of auto_T7pol plasmid

The auto_T7pol plasmid was engineered to enable cap-independent translation of T7 RNA polymerase via the IRES element and to ensure transcriptional termination via the T7 terminator, preventing read-through into plasmid backbone sequences.

- describe preparation of pT7/shRNA DNA template

The pT7/shRNA DNA template was assembled from four overlapping oligonucleotides using PCR with high-fidelity polymerase. The final product was gel-purified and quantified by fluorometry.

- describe synthesis of 5′-capped T7 polymerase mRNA

The 5′-capped T7 polymerase mRNA was synthesized using a commercial in vitro transcription kit with ARCA cap analog, followed by DNase digestion and ethanol precipitation. The mRNA integrity was confirmed by agarose gel electrophoresis.
