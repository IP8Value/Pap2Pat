# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

The field of diagnostic and biomedical support materials has long relied on synthetic polymeric substrates derived from nonrenewable petroleum-based sources. These materials, while widely adopted for immunoassays, biosensors, and lateral flow devices, present persistent challenges related to environmental sustainability, mechanical fragility, and uncontrolled biomolecular interactions. Their inherent hydrophobicity or inconsistent surface chemistry often necessitates extensive chemical modification to achieve sufficient binding capacity and specificity, leading to increased production complexity and cost. Moreover, many such substrates exhibit poor aqueous stability, limiting their utility in liquid-phase diagnostic platforms where consistent performance under hydration is critical.

Cellulose and its chemical derivatives have emerged as compelling alternatives due to their biocompatibility, biodegradability, and abundance in renewable biomass. Among these, nitrocellulose has been historically employed as a membrane material in diagnostic assays owing to its high protein-binding capacity. However, nitrocellulose suffers from critical limitations including low tensile strength, susceptibility to combustion, and excessive non-specific adsorption of proteins, which compromises assay sensitivity and reproducibility. These drawbacks have prompted the search for more controlled and robust cellulose-based platforms that retain the advantages of natural origin while overcoming the structural and chemical deficiencies of traditional derivatives.

Nanofibrillated cellulose (NFC), produced through mechanical disintegration of cellulose fibers, offers a unique combination of properties including high specific surface area, exceptional mechanical integrity, and a dense, hydrogen-bonded network structure. Unlike conventional cellulose membranes, NFC can be processed into thin, transparent, and mechanically resilient films with minimal porosity, enabling precise control over analyte diffusion and surface accessibility. Recent advances have demonstrated that NFC films derived from low-charge precursor pulps exhibit remarkable water resistance, a property attributed to their compact morphology and reduced inter-fibrillar void space. This structural characteristic renders them particularly suitable for applications requiring prolonged exposure to aqueous environments, such as point-of-care diagnostics and implantable biosensors.

Despite these advantages, the native surface chemistry of NFC remains suboptimal for targeted biomolecular immobilization. The abundance of hydroxyl groups on cellulose fibrils provides limited reactivity for covalent conjugation, and unmodified NFC surfaces often exhibit inconsistent binding behavior. TEMPO-mediated oxidation has been established as a selective method for converting primary hydroxyl groups on cellulose into carboxyl functionalities, thereby introducing negatively charged sites that can serve as anchors for subsequent chemical coupling. However, conventional TEMPO-oxidized NFC suspensions, when dried into films, tend to swell excessively in water due to electrostatic repulsion between carboxylate groups, undermining the very water resistance that makes NFC attractive. This paradox—where oxidation enhances reactivity but diminishes structural stability—has hindered the practical deployment of TEMPO-oxidized NFC in diagnostic applications.

Recent efforts to stabilize oxidized NFC films have focused on surface-limited oxidation strategies, where reaction kinetics are constrained to prevent penetration into the bulk of the film. This approach preserves the dense, water-resistant core while enabling controlled functionalization of the outermost layer. Such a strategy represents a critical advancement, as it permits the creation of films that are simultaneously hydrophobic enough to resist water ingress and chemically active enough to support stable, covalent biomolecule attachment. The integration of this concept with established coupling chemistries, such as EDC/NHS-mediated amine activation, opens a pathway to high-performance, sustainable diagnostic platforms that are both environmentally benign and functionally superior to existing technologies.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The invention pertains to a novel class of topographically modified nanocellulose films engineered for high-fidelity biomolecular immobilization in diagnostic and biomedical applications. The discovery that water-resistant NFC films, when subjected to controlled TEMPO-mediated oxidation, retain structural integrity while gaining a chemically active surface layer enables the development of a robust platform for covalent conjugation of proteins, antibodies, and other biomolecules. This innovation resolves the longstanding conflict between water resistance and surface reactivity in cellulose-based materials, which has previously precluded their use in liquid-phase diagnostic systems requiring both stability and specificity.

The invention introduces surface-modified nanocellulose films that are prepared by first forming a dense, low-porosity NFC film from low-charge precursor pulp, followed by a precisely timed TEMPO-mediated oxidation that selectively introduces carboxyl groups exclusively at the film’s surface. This surface-limited oxidation preserves the film’s water-resistant core while endowing its outer layer with sufficient negative charge to facilitate downstream activation. Subsequent treatment with EDC and NHS reagents converts these surface carboxyl groups into amine-reactive NHS-ester functionalities, creating a chemically defined and stable interface capable of covalently binding amine-containing biomolecules such as antibodies, enzymes, and peptides.

The method of functionalization is characterized by its precision, scalability, and compatibility with standard biochemical protocols. The oxidation step is conducted under controlled conditions—limited in duration and concentration—to ensure that carboxylation occurs only within the topmost nanometers of the film, preventing bulk swelling or structural degradation. The EDC/NHS activation is performed in aqueous buffer under mild conditions, preserving the integrity of the underlying cellulose matrix. The resulting activated film exhibits negligible non-specific protein adsorption, high binding capacity for target biomolecules, and exceptional stability upon drying and rehydration, making it uniquely suited for use in diagnostic devices that require storage, transport, and repeated use.

The advantages of the invention are manifold. First, the films are derived from renewable, non-toxic, and biodegradable materials, eliminating the environmental burden associated with petrochemical substrates. Second, the topographical modification yields a surface with uniform chemical functionality, enabling reproducible and quantitative immobilization of biomolecules. Third, the films maintain mechanical robustness and dimensional stability even after prolonged exposure to aqueous environments, a critical feature for diagnostic devices exposed to biological fluids. Fourth, the activated surface permits immobilization via both physical adsorption and covalent conjugation, allowing flexibility in assay design. Finally, the surface can be patterned using non-contact techniques such as inkjet printing, enabling high-resolution spatial control over biomolecule deposition without the need for photolithography or complex microfabrication.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF PREFERRED EMBODIMENTS OF THE INVENTION

The invention is practiced through a sequential process of film formation, surface oxidation, chemical activation, and biomolecule conjugation. The process begins with the preparation of nanofibrillated cellulose films from bleached hardwood pulp, typically birch, which is mechanically processed through multiple passes in a grinder and subsequently disintegrated using a microfluidizer to yield a stable NFC gel. The gel is filtered under controlled pressure to remove excess water, then consolidated by rolling and pressed between absorbent sheets to form a dense, homogeneous film with minimal internal porosity. This structural density is essential to the water resistance of the final product.

The TEMPO-mediated oxidation is carried out by immersing the NFC film in an aqueous solution containing 2,2,6,6-tetramethylpiperidin-1-oxyl (TEMPO), sodium bromide, and sodium hypochlorite, with the pH maintained at approximately 10 using hydrochloric acid. The reaction is conducted for a duration between 10 seconds and 5 minutes, with the optimal time selected to achieve sufficient surface carboxylation without inducing bulk swelling. The reaction is terminated by the addition of ethanol, followed by extensive rinsing with deionized water to remove residual oxidants and byproducts. The resulting film exhibits a surface enriched in carboxyl groups while retaining its structural integrity and water resistance, as confirmed by contact angle measurements and mechanical testing.

Subsequent to oxidation, the carboxylated film is activated via EDC/NHS chemistry. The film is immersed in a solution of 1-ethyl-3-(3-dimethylaminopropyl)carbodiimide hydrochloride (EDC) and N-hydroxysuccinimide (NHS) in 10 mM sodium acetate buffer at pH 5 for a period of 15 to 30 minutes. During this step, the carboxyl groups on the film surface are converted into NHS-ester intermediates, which are highly reactive toward primary amines. Excess reagents are removed by rinsing with buffer and water, and the film is dried under controlled conditions to prevent deformation.

The activated film is then ready for biomolecule conjugation. Biomolecules such as antibodies, enzymes, or peptides bearing primary amine groups may be immobilized either by physical adsorption or by direct covalent coupling. For covalent immobilization, the biomolecule is applied in aqueous solution at a pH near its isoelectric point to maximize reactivity. Following incubation, unreacted NHS esters are quenched by exposure to ethanolamine, and non-specifically bound proteins are removed by sequential rinsing with buffers of varying ionic strength and pH. The resulting biointerface is stable upon drying and retains full functionality upon rehydration.

The conjugation of anti-human IgG was demonstrated using both inkjet printing and direct adsorption. Inkjet printing was performed using a piezoelectric printer with cartridges loaded with fluorescein- or dansyl-labeled anti-human IgG solutions. The printed patterns exhibited high spatial resolution and retained fluorescence after ethanolamine quenching, confirming covalent attachment. The surface topography of the printed films was analyzed using atomic force microscopy, revealing a uniform layer of biomolecules covering the underlying fibrillar structure without disrupting the film’s integrity.

The functionalized films are suitable for a broad range of diagnostic applications, including immunoassays, biosensors, point-of-care testing devices, and microfluidic platforms. Their compatibility with standard biochemical protocols, scalability of production, and environmental sustainability render them superior to conventional nitrocellulose and synthetic polymer substrates.

### EXAMPLES

The materials used in the examples include bleached birch pulp, TEMPO, sodium bromide, sodium hypochlorite, EDC, NHS, ethanolamine, bovine serum albumin, anti-human IgG, human IgG, dansyl chloride, fluorescein isothiocyanate, and deionized water purified via Millipore Synergy UV filtration. An Epson R800 piezoelectric inkjet printer with CD-printing tray and unused cartridges were employed for patterning.

NFC films were prepared by mechanical disintegration of pulp followed by pressure filtration and rolling to consolidate structure. NFC model surfaces were prepared by spin-coating a dilute NFC suspension onto QCM-SiO₂ crystals coated with polyethylenimine. TEMPO oxidation was performed for 30 seconds under controlled pH and temperature, followed by rinsing and EDC/NHS activation in 10 mM sodium acetate buffer at pH 5 for 20 minutes.

Surface carboxylation was quantified via conductometric titration, revealing a two-fold increase in carboxyl content after oxidation. Water tolerance was confirmed by immersion in water for 72 hours without film disintegration. Activation was verified by XPS analysis, which detected a distinct nitrogen signal corresponding to NHS esters and amide groups formed during EDC/NHS coupling.

BSA and anti-human IgG were adsorbed onto unmodified, TEMPO-oxidized, and activated NFC surfaces. QCM-D measurements demonstrated that only the EDC/NHS-activated surfaces retained significant protein binding after alkaline rinsing, confirming covalent immobilization. Fluorescence imaging and CLSM confirmed spatially resolved antibody deposition following inkjet printing. AFM analysis revealed a reduction in surface roughness after antibody conjugation, indicating uniform coverage. The conjugated anti-human IgG retained biological activity, as demonstrated by specific binding of human IgG in QCM-D assays, with negligible non-specific adsorption after blocking with Superblock.

Inkjet-printed anti-human IgG patterns were analyzed by fluorescence microscopy and CLSM, showing high signal-to-noise ratios and precise feature resolution. The printed texture was confirmed to be covalently bound through ethanolamine quenching and XPS detection of nitrogen. The films remained functional after drying and rehydration, demonstrating suitability for storage and transport. The entire process is scalable, cost-effective, and environmentally sustainable.
