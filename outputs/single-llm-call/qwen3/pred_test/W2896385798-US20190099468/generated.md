# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

- define field of invention

The present invention relates to the field of ophthalmology and more specifically to the prevention, protection, and therapeutic management of ocular surface disorders characterized by epithelial barrier dysfunction, including but not limited to dry eye disease, solution-induced corneal staining, and other conditions involving sublethal epithelial damage. The invention encompasses novel methods and pharmaceutical compositions for preserving the structural and functional integrity of the corneal and conjunctival epithelia through the topical administration of compounds that mitigate plasma membrane and glycocalyx disruption induced by environmental, chemical, or oxidative stressors. The invention further provides for the use of dynamin inhibitors, including Dynasore and its structural analogs such as Dyngo-4a, as protective agents that act to stabilize the ocular surface epithelial barrier without requiring the inhibition of endocytic pathways, thereby offering a mechanistically distinct approach to the treatment of ocular surface pathology.

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

- describe ocular surface structure

The ocular surface is composed of stratified squamous epithelial tissues of the cornea and conjunctiva, which form a continuous, dynamic barrier between the external environment and the internal ocular structures. These epithelial cells originate from a basal layer of progenitor cells located at the limbus and undergo a precisely regulated process of upward displacement and terminal differentiation as they migrate toward the surface. During this maturation, cells progressively flatten, reduce metabolic activity, and acquire specialized surface features, including microplicae and membrane-associated mucins such as MUC16, which are critical for maintaining the integrity of the tear film and the underlying epithelial barrier.

- explain cell renewal process

Cell renewal on the ocular surface occurs through a continuous cycle of proliferation, differentiation, and desquamation, with complete epithelial turnover typically completed within five to seven days in healthy individuals. Basal cells divide to generate daughter cells that ascend through the epithelial layers, acquiring distinct biochemical and morphological characteristics as they approach the apical surface. This process ensures the maintenance of a functional barrier while simultaneously replacing damaged or aged cells. The transition from proliferative basal cells to terminally differentiated apical cells involves the downregulation of cell cycle markers and the upregulation of mucosal and barrier proteins essential for ocular surface homeostasis.

- describe glycocalyx barrier

The apical surface of the differentiated corneal and conjunctival epithelium is coated with a dense, highly organized glycocalyx formed by the interaction of transmembrane mucins, particularly MUC16, with soluble galectins such as LGALS3. This glycoprotein meshwork functions as a primary physical and biochemical barrier, preventing the penetration of pathogens, allergens, and exogenous substances into the underlying epithelial layers. The glycocalyx also contributes to the lubricating and hydrating properties of the tear film, ensuring smooth eyelid movement and minimizing friction-induced damage.

- discuss ocular surface damage

Disruption of the ocular surface epithelial barrier can occur due to a variety of environmental, mechanical, or chemical insults, including ultraviolet radiation, airborne irritants, prolonged contact lens wear, and exposure to preservatives in ophthalmic solutions. Such insults trigger sublethal cellular stress responses that compromise the structural integrity of both the plasma membrane and the glycocalyx, leading to increased permeability, altered surface protein expression, and aberrant shedding of barrier components. These changes are not necessarily associated with cell death but are sufficient to initiate inflammatory cascades and clinical manifestations of ocular surface disease.

- introduce dry eye disease

Dry eye disease, or keratoconjunctivitis sicca, is a prevalent, multifactorial disorder affecting more than twenty percent of the adult population in developed nations. It is characterized by tear film instability, hyperosmolarity, ocular surface inflammation, and epithelial damage, resulting in symptoms ranging from mild discomfort to severe visual impairment. The condition is often diagnosed clinically through the use of vital dyes, which reveal areas of epithelial barrier compromise as focal or diffuse staining patterns on the corneal and conjunctival surfaces.

- discuss vital dyes

Vital dyes such as fluorescein and rose bengal have been employed for over a century to visualize ocular surface epithelial defects. These water-soluble compounds are non-toxic at diagnostic concentrations and are selectively taken up by cells with compromised barriers, allowing clinicians to map regions of epithelial dysfunction. Despite their widespread clinical use, the precise mechanisms underlying their uptake into living epithelial cells have remained poorly understood, particularly in the context of sublethal injury.

- explain mechanism of staining

Traditional assumptions held that vital dye uptake resulted solely from physical breaches in the epithelial barrier caused by cell loss or desquamation. However, recent evidence indicates that viable, metabolically active cells can internalize these dyes through active, energy-dependent processes. Fluorescein, in particular, is concentrated within the cytoplasm of individual cells under conditions of sublethal stress, a phenomenon termed hyperstaining, which occurs even in the absence of overt cell death or membrane rupture.

- describe fluorescein uptake

Fluorescein uptake is not blocked by the presence of mucins or an intact glycocalyx, suggesting that the plasma membrane itself serves as the principal barrier to its entry. The uptake is temperature-sensitive and requires cellular metabolic activity, indicating that it is not a passive diffusion process. Furthermore, the concentration of fluorescein within cells is enhanced by oxidative stressors and certain contact lens cleaning solutions, suggesting a link between cellular stress responses and dye internalization.

- discuss hyperstaining

Hyperstaining refers to the abnormal accumulation of vital dyes within individual epithelial cells, producing a mosaic pattern of intensely fluorescent or colored cells against a background of unstained tissue. This phenomenon is not indicative of necrosis but rather reflects a state of cellular stress in which membrane permeability is altered without immediate loss of viability. The persistence of hyperstained cells in the absence of apoptotic markers suggests that these cells are undergoing repair or remodeling processes that inadvertently facilitate dye entry.

- explain fluorescein concentration

The concentration of fluorescein within stressed cells is an active process that may involve the sequestration of dye into intracellular compartments or the inhibition of its efflux. This process is distinct from simple membrane leakage, as it requires functional cellular machinery and is reversible under certain conditions. The ability of cells to retain and concentrate dye implies that the barrier defect is transient and localized, allowing for potential therapeutic intervention before irreversible damage occurs.

- hypothesize fluorescein uptake mechanism

It has been hypothesized that the uptake of vital dyes such as fluorescein may be mediated by endocytic pathways activated during plasma membrane repair. However, studies using stratified and differentiated epithelial models have revealed that endocytosis is not universally stimulated by stress and that inhibition of endocytosis does not consistently prevent dye uptake. This discrepancy suggests that alternative mechanisms, such as transient pore formation, lipid peroxidation-induced membrane fluidity changes, or altered ion channel activity, may contribute to dye internalization. The observation that certain dynamin inhibitors prevent dye uptake without blocking endocytosis further supports the existence of a novel, non-endocytic mechanism of barrier protection.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

- introduce invention

The present invention introduces a novel therapeutic strategy for the protection and restoration of the ocular surface epithelial barrier through the topical application of dynamin inhibitors, including Dynasore and its structural analogs such as Dyngo-4a. Unlike conventional approaches that target inflammation or tear production, this invention directly addresses the underlying mechanism of epithelial barrier disruption by stabilizing the plasma membrane and preventing the loss of glycocalyx components under conditions of oxidative or chemical stress.

- describe method of protecting ocular surface

The method comprises the topical administration of an effective amount of Dynasore or a structurally related analog to the ocular surface of a subject exhibiting or at risk of developing ocular surface epithelial damage. Administration is performed in a manner that allows sustained contact with the corneal and conjunctival epithelia, enabling the compound to penetrate the superficial layers and exert a protective effect on membrane integrity, lipid organization, and galectin retention. The invention is effective both as a prophylactic measure and as a therapeutic intervention for ongoing disease.

- discuss pharmaceutical composition

The invention further provides for pharmaceutical compositions comprising Dynasore or Dyngo-4a in a pharmaceutically acceptable vehicle suitable for topical ocular delivery. These compositions are formulated to maintain compound stability, enhance corneal residence time, and minimize systemic absorption. The active ingredient is present in concentrations sufficient to inhibit membrane permeabilization and glycocalyx shedding without inducing cytotoxicity or interfering with normal epithelial turnover.

- outline advantages of invention

The advantages of this invention include the ability to prevent ocular surface damage prior to its manifestation, the potential to resolve chronic epithelial dysfunction through repeated application, the absence of dependence on endocytic inhibition for efficacy, and the compatibility with existing diagnostic practices such as vital dye staining. Importantly, the invention does not rely on anti-inflammatory or immunomodulatory mechanisms, making it suitable for patients unresponsive to conventional therapies. The compounds used are rapidly acting, reversible, and exhibit a favorable safety profile in preclinical models, supporting their potential for long-term clinical use.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

- define vital dyes

Vital dyes are water-soluble, non-toxic compounds that are selectively taken up by cells with compromised membrane integrity or altered physiological states, without causing immediate cell death. In the context of ocular surface evaluation, the most commonly used vital dyes include fluorescein, rose bengal, and lissamine green. These dyes bind to exposed cellular components or accumulate within cells due to increased membrane permeability, allowing for the visualization of epithelial damage under slit-lamp or fluorescence microscopy.

- describe staining mechanisms

The staining mechanism of vital dyes involves the interaction of the dye molecule with cellular structures that become accessible following barrier disruption. Fluorescein, due to its small molecular size and hydrophilic nature, penetrates the plasma membrane of stressed cells and accumulates intracellularly, likely through transient pores or altered membrane dynamics. Rose bengal, being larger and more negatively charged, is initially excluded by the glycocalyx but gains access when mucin layers are shed or disorganized. The uptake of these dyes is not a passive process but is influenced by cellular metabolism, membrane fluidity, and the activity of membrane-associated proteins.

- hypothesize dye uptake mechanism

It is hypothesized that the uptake of vital dyes such as fluorescein occurs not through endocytosis but through the formation of transient, non-lethal breaches in the plasma membrane induced by oxidative stress, lipid peroxidation, or surfactant exposure. These breaches allow dye molecules to enter the cytoplasm, where they are retained due to the inability of the cell to rapidly efflux them. The requirement for cellular viability in this process suggests that active mechanisms, such as ion flux regulation or cytoskeletal tension, may contribute to dye concentration, rather than vesicular trafficking.

- describe oxidative stress model

An oxidative stress model was developed using tert-butyl hydroperoxide (tBHP) to simulate the cellular damage observed in dry eye disease and solution-induced corneal staining. tBHP induces lipid peroxidation, disrupts mitochondrial function, and triggers the shedding of glycocalyx components such as LGALS3, without inducing widespread apoptosis. This model reproduces the hyperstaining phenotype observed clinically and provides a reproducible system for evaluating barrier-protective agents.

- show damage stimulates dye uptake

Exposure of human corneal epithelial cells to tBHP results in a dose-dependent increase in fluorescein and rose bengal uptake, as quantified by fluorescence intensity and spectrophotometric analysis. This increase is observed in both undifferentiated monolayer cultures and stratified, differentiated cultures, although the pattern of staining differs, with monolayer cells exhibiting cytoplasmic concentration and stratified cells showing diffuse surface staining.

- show damage stimulates endocytosis

In undifferentiated monolayer cultures, oxidative stress stimulates the internalization of transferrin, a marker of clathrin- and caveolin-mediated endocytosis. This increase in endocytic activity correlates temporally with the rise in vital dye uptake, suggesting a potential mechanistic link. However, in stratified and differentiated cultures, which more accurately model the ocular surface, endocytosis is not elevated under the same conditions, indicating that endocytosis is not the primary driver of dye uptake in physiologically relevant epithelia.

- describe endocytosis inhibitors

Endocytosis inhibitors such as chlorpromazine, genistein, and Dynasore were employed to assess the role of endocytic pathways in dye uptake. Chlorpromazine inhibits clathrin-mediated endocytosis, genistein inhibits caveolin-mediated endocytosis, and Dynasore inhibits dynamin GTPases required for both pathways. In monolayer cultures, all three inhibitors significantly reduced vital dye uptake. However, in stratified cultures, only Dynasore and its analog Dyngo-4a retained this inhibitory effect, suggesting a non-endocytic mechanism of action.

- describe differentiated corneal epithelial cell cultures

Differentiated human corneal epithelial cell cultures were generated by culturing telomerase-immortalized limbal epithelial cells in high-calcium, serum-containing medium for seven days, resulting in stratified, polarized epithelia expressing mucin markers and forming a functional glycocalyx. These cultures exhibit reduced endocytic activity, mimicking the barrier properties of the native ocular surface, and are therefore superior to monolayer cultures for evaluating therapeutic agents.

- show dye uptake in differentiated cultures

In differentiated cultures, oxidative stress induces vital dye uptake without stimulating endocytosis, as confirmed by the absence of increased transferrin internalization. This demonstrates that dye uptake in physiologically relevant epithelia occurs independently of classical endocytic pathways and is instead linked to plasma membrane destabilization.

- show endocytosis not stimulated in differentiated cultures

Quantitative analysis of transferrin uptake in differentiated cultures revealed no significant increase following oxidative stress, in contrast to the robust stimulation observed in monolayer cultures. This finding underscores the importance of using differentiated models to study ocular surface pathology and highlights the limitations of monolayer systems in predicting therapeutic efficacy.

- describe Dynasore and its effects

Dynasore is a cell-permeable, reversible inhibitor of dynamin GTPases that has been widely used to study endocytosis. In the context of this invention, Dynasore was found to prevent vital dye uptake in both monolayer and differentiated cultures, despite its inability to inhibit endocytosis in the latter. This suggests that Dynasore exerts a protective effect on the ocular surface through a mechanism independent of endocytic blockade.

- describe alternative mechanism of Dynasore

The protective effect of Dynasore is hypothesized to involve the stabilization of plasma membrane lipids, modulation of mitochondrial dynamics, or inhibition of actin remodeling. Dynasore has been shown in other systems to reduce mitochondrial fragmentation and inhibit inflammasome activation, both of which may contribute to the preservation of epithelial barrier function. The compound may also alter membrane cholesterol distribution or lipid raft organization, thereby reducing susceptibility to oxidative damage.

- define treatment and treating

The term “treatment” or “treating” refers to the administration of a therapeutic agent to a subject with the intent of alleviating, reducing, or preventing the symptoms or progression of a disease or condition. In the context of this invention, treatment involves the topical application of Dynasore or its analogs to the ocular surface to preserve epithelial barrier integrity and prevent the uptake of vital dyes.

- define prevent and inhibit

“Prevent” refers to the administration of a compound prior to or concurrent with the onset of epithelial damage, thereby blocking the initiation of barrier disruption. “Inhibit” refers to the suppression of a biological process, such as dye uptake or glycocalyx shedding, regardless of whether the initiating stressor is present. The invention encompasses both preventive and inhibitory applications.

- define effective amount

An “effective amount” refers to the quantity of Dynasore or its analog sufficient to achieve a clinically meaningful reduction in vital dye uptake, preservation of glycocalyx components, or improvement in epithelial barrier function upon topical administration to the ocular surface. This amount is determined empirically and may vary based on the formulation, frequency of administration, and severity of the condition.

- describe carriers

Pharmaceutical carriers suitable for topical ocular delivery include aqueous solutions, suspensions, gels, ointments, and sustained-release formulations. Preferred carriers are isotonic, non-irritating, and compatible with the ocular surface, such as buffered saline, hyaluronic acid, or hydroxypropyl methylcellulose. The carrier must maintain the stability and bioavailability of the active compound during storage and application.

- define protein

A “protein” refers to a polypeptide chain composed of amino acids linked by peptide bonds, which may be naturally occurring or synthetically produced. In the context of this invention, proteins include endogenous ocular surface components such as LGALS3 and MUC16, as well as recombinant or synthetic analogs used for therapeutic or diagnostic purposes.

- define isolated polypeptide or protein

An “isolated polypeptide or protein” refers to a protein that has been separated from its native cellular environment, purified to substantial homogeneity, and rendered free of contaminating cellular components. Such proteins may be produced recombinantly or chemically synthesized and are suitable for use in pharmaceutical formulations.

- define substantially the same

The phrase “substantially the same” refers to a polypeptide or protein that retains the biological activity of a reference molecule, such as Dynasore or Dyngo-4a, despite minor structural modifications. Such modifications may include amino acid substitutions, deletions, or additions that do not impair the compound’s ability to protect the ocular surface epithelial barrier.

- describe homeostatic protein clusterin

Clusterin, also known as apolipoprotein J, is a secreted glycoprotein involved in cellular homeostasis, membrane repair, and the inhibition of protein aggregation. It is expressed in a variety of tissues, including the ocular surface, and has been shown to bind to damaged membranes and prevent their lysis. Clusterin functions as a molecular chaperone that stabilizes lipid bilayers and facilitates the clearance of cellular debris.

- describe CLU levels in human inflammatory disorders

Elevated levels of clusterin have been observed in human inflammatory disorders, including dry eye disease, where it is thought to act as a compensatory mechanism to mitigate epithelial damage. Its presence in tear fluid suggests a role in maintaining ocular surface integrity under stress conditions.

- describe CLU's sealing mechanism

Clusterin binds to exposed phospholipids and hydrophobic domains on damaged cell membranes, forming a protective shield that prevents further disruption and promotes membrane resealing. This mechanism is analogous to the protective effect observed with Dynasore, suggesting a potential synergistic relationship between the two agents.

- describe CLU's binding to ocular surface

Clusterin is present in the tear film and binds to the apical surface of corneal epithelial cells, particularly under conditions of stress. Its binding is mediated by interactions with membrane lipids and glycoproteins, and it may serve to stabilize the glycocalyx and reduce the permeability of the plasma membrane.

- describe combination therapy with Dynasore

The invention contemplates combination therapy in which Dynasore or its analogs are administered concurrently with clusterin or other barrier-stabilizing agents to enhance the protective effect on the ocular surface. Such combinations may be particularly beneficial in advanced or chronic cases of dry eye disease, where multiple pathways of epithelial damage are operative.

- describe human clusterin structure

Human clusterin exists as a heterodimeric glycoprotein composed of two disulfide-linked chains derived from a single precursor polypeptide. The mature protein contains multiple domains involved in lipid binding, protein interaction, and membrane association. The structure enables it to act as a molecular chaperone that recognizes and stabilizes damaged membranes.

- describe clusterin isoform 2 preproprotein sequence

Clusterin isoform 2, the predominant form expressed in epithelial tissues, is encoded by the CLU gene and consists of a 496-amino acid preproprotein that undergoes post-translational cleavage to yield the mature heterodimer. The sequence includes conserved regions critical for lipid binding and membrane stabilization.

- describe clusterin as used in the invention

In the context of this invention, clusterin refers to the naturally occurring or recombinantly produced human clusterin protein, or fragments thereof, that retain the ability to bind to and stabilize damaged ocular surface membranes. The protein may be administered as a therapeutic agent in combination with Dynasore or as a standalone treatment.

- describe recombinant human clusterin production

Recombinant human clusterin can be produced in mammalian cell expression systems such as CHO or HEK293 cells, followed by purification using affinity chromatography. The resulting protein is formulated in a sterile, isotonic buffer suitable for ocular administration.

- describe pharmaceutical compositions

Pharmaceutical compositions of the invention comprise Dynasore or Dyngo-4a in a therapeutically effective amount, combined with a pharmaceutically acceptable carrier. These compositions may be formulated as eye drops, gels, or sustained-release inserts and may include additional agents such as preservatives, viscosity enhancers, or antioxidants to improve stability and patient compliance.

- describe polypeptides substantially the same as human clusterin

Polypeptides substantially the same as human clusterin include variants with conservative amino acid substitutions, truncated forms retaining membrane-binding domains, or fusion proteins that enhance ocular retention. These variants must retain the ability to stabilize epithelial membranes and reduce dye uptake.

- describe administration of Dynasore or analogs

Dynasore or its analogs are administered topically to the ocular surface in the form of eye drops, gels, or ointments. Administration may be performed once daily or multiple times per day, depending on the severity of the condition. The compound is absorbed by the corneal and conjunctival epithelia, where it exerts its protective effect on membrane integrity.

- describe compositions and formulations for topical administration

Topical formulations are designed to maximize corneal residence time and minimize drainage. Preferred formulations include viscous gels containing hydroxypropyl methylcellulose or hyaluronic acid, which prolong contact with the ocular surface. The active ingredient is present at concentrations ranging from 1 μM to 100 μM, optimized to achieve barrier protection without cytotoxicity.

- describe pharmaceutical formulations

Pharmaceutical formulations are sterile, pyrogen-free, and pH-adjusted to match the physiological pH of the tear film (approximately 7.4). They may contain buffering agents such as phosphate or borate, tonicity modifiers such as sodium chloride or glycerol, and preservatives such as benzalkonium chloride or polyquad, provided they do not interfere with the activity of the active compound.

- describe dosages and desired drug concentrations

The desired concentration of Dynasore in topical formulations ranges from 5 μM to 50 μM, with a preferred concentration of 20 μM. Dosage volumes of 10–50 μL are administered one to four times daily, depending on the clinical indication. Sustained-release formulations may allow for less frequent administration, such as once every 12 to 24 hours.

- describe maintenance therapy

Maintenance therapy involves the long-term, periodic administration of Dynasore or its analogs to patients with chronic ocular surface disease. Over time, repeated application protects newly differentiated epithelial cells as they migrate to the surface, leading to a gradual resolution of barrier dysfunction and a reduction in clinical signs and symptoms. This approach is particularly effective in conditions such as dry eye disease, where epithelial turnover is accelerated and regeneration is impaired.

- describe experimental model

The experimental model employed human corneal epithelial cell lines and ex vivo mouse corneas subjected to oxidative stress induced by tert-butyl hydroperoxide. The model was validated for its ability to reproduce the hyperstaining phenotype observed in dry eye disease and solution-induced corneal staining.

- outline study design

The study design included comparisons between stressed and unstressed controls, with and without treatment with Dynasore, Dyngo-4a, or endocytosis inhibitors. Multiple endpoints were assessed, including vital dye uptake, endocytosis, membrane integrity, and glycocalyx component shedding.

- explain statistical analysis

Statistical significance was determined using Student’s t-test on triplicate biological replicates, with p-values less than 0.05 considered significant. All data are presented as mean ± standard deviation.

- hypothesize dye uptake mechanism

It was hypothesized that oxidative stress stimulates vital dye uptake through endocytic mechanisms. However, findings in stratified cultures contradicted this hypothesis, leading to the discovery of a novel, non-endocytic mechanism of barrier disruption and protection.

- choose oxidative stress as damaging stress

Oxidative stress was selected as the damaging stressor due to its established role in dry eye disease and its ability to induce sublethal epithelial damage without triggering widespread apoptosis.

- describe tBHP preparation

Tert-butyl hydroperoxide was prepared as a 100 mM aqueous stock solution and diluted to final concentrations of 3 mM or 10 mM in culture medium immediately prior to use.

- outline cell culture experiment

Human corneal epithelial cells were cultured in monolayer or stratified conditions and exposed to tBHP for 2–3 hours. Vital dye uptake was measured using fluorescein and rose bengal, and endocytosis was assessed via transferrin internalization.

- describe endocytic inhibitors

Endocytic inhibitors included chlorpromazine (8 μg/mL), genistein (200 μM), Dynasore (40–80 μM), and Dyngo-4a (15 μM), all dissolved in DMSO and applied concurrently with stress.

- outline inhibitor treatment

Inhibitors were added to cultures 30 minutes prior to tBHP exposure and remained present throughout the experiment. Vehicle controls received equivalent volumes of DMSO.

- describe vital dye assay

Vital dye uptake was quantified using a plate reader after 10 minutes of exposure to fluorescein or rose bengal, followed by washing and solubilization of retained dye.

- outline data analysis

Data were analyzed using ImageJ for fluorescence intensity and spectrophotometry for dye concentration. Statistical comparisons were made between treatment and control groups.

Trypan blue staining was used to evaluate plasma membrane integrity. Dynasore treatment nearly eliminated staining, indicating profound membrane protection.

PCR for ACTB was performed on culture medium to assess cellular lysis. No increase in DNA fragments was detected, confirming that cell death was not the cause of LGALS3 release.
