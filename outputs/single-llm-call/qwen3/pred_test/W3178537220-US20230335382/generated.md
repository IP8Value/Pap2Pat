# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD

- introduce plasma systems measurement

The field of this invention pertains to the real-time, non-invasive monitoring of plasma characteristics in industrial processing environments, particularly within semiconductor manufacturing systems where precise control of plasma parameters is critical to process repeatability and device yield. Conventional diagnostic techniques for plasma characterization have long relied on optical emission spectroscopy (OES) and electrical probes, both of which suffer from inherent limitations in deployment, sensitivity, or physical intrusiveness. This invention introduces a novel radio emission spectroscopy (RES) system capable of remotely detecting electromagnetic signatures generated by plasma dynamics without requiring direct contact with the plasma or unobstructed optical access to the process chamber. The RES technique leverages the natural radio frequency emissions produced by time-varying currents within the plasma bulk and at its boundaries, capturing these signals through a near-field magnetic loop antenna positioned external to the plasma chamber. Unlike optical methods, which are rendered ineffective by viewport fouling, particulate deposition, or optical window degradation, the RES system operates independently of optical transparency, enabling continuous, uninterrupted monitoring under real-world manufacturing conditions. The system is designed to be compatible with standard industrial plasma reactors, including capacitively coupled and multi-frequency configurations, and provides a robust, scalable solution for in situ process control in high-precision environments such as etching, deposition, and cleaning processes used in integrated circuit fabrication.

## BACKGROUND

- describe plasma usage

Plasma systems are indispensable in modern semiconductor manufacturing, where they enable precise material removal, surface modification, and thin-film deposition at the nanoscale. Capacitively coupled plasmas, inductively coupled plasmas, and multi-frequency configurations are routinely employed in etching and deposition tools to achieve anisotropic profiles, high selectivity, and uniformity across large-area wafers. These plasmas are sustained by radio frequency (RF) power applied to electrodes within a vacuum chamber, creating a highly dynamic environment characterized by electron density, electron temperature, ion flux, and sheath behavior—all of which must be tightly controlled to ensure process consistency and device performance. Variations in these parameters, even at the sub-percent level, can lead to critical defects such as line width variations, residue formation, or non-uniform etch rates, resulting in significant yield loss and economic impact.

- explain plasma diagnosis and monitoring

Accurate diagnosis and real-time monitoring of plasma conditions are essential for process qualification, endpoint detection, and fault prevention. Traditional diagnostic methods include optical emission spectroscopy, Langmuir probes, and voltage-current (V-I) probes. Optical methods rely on detecting photons emitted by excited species within the plasma, but their utility is severely compromised by the accumulation of dielectric films, particulates, or etch byproducts on viewport surfaces, which attenuate or completely block optical signals. Langmuir probes, while providing direct measurements of electron density and temperature, require physical insertion into the plasma, introducing contamination risks, perturbing the plasma itself, and limiting applicability in production environments. V-I probes offer quantitative insight into conduction currents but are typically installed at specific locations and are not easily scalable for multi-chamber deployment.

- motivate OES technique

Optical emission spectroscopy has been the industry standard for non-invasive plasma monitoring due to its simplicity, speed, and ability to identify specific atomic and molecular species. However, its reliance on optical path integrity renders it vulnerable to environmental degradation common in high-throughput manufacturing. Even minor clouding of viewports—often caused by polymer deposition from organic precursors or oxide residues from inorganic etchants—can lead to signal attenuation exceeding 90%, necessitating frequent chamber maintenance and process interruptions for optical window cleaning or replacement.

- describe OES limitations

The fundamental limitation of OES lies in its dependence on line-of-sight optical access. Any obstruction, whether from condensates, sputtered materials, or photoresist residues, disrupts the measurement, leading to data gaps, false endpoints, or uncorrected drift in process control. Furthermore, OES provides limited information about bulk plasma dynamics such as conduction current or sheath impedance, focusing instead on radiative transitions of excited species, which may not correlate directly with the underlying power dissipation or electron transport mechanisms driving the process.

- introduce RES technique

Radio emission spectroscopy (RES) emerges as a transformative alternative to optical methods by detecting electromagnetic emissions in the radio frequency range generated by time-varying currents within the plasma. These emissions arise from the acceleration of charged particles in the plasma bulk and at the sheath boundaries, producing detectable magnetic field fluctuations that can be captured by a near-field loop antenna placed externally to the chamber. The technique is entirely non-invasive, requires no optical access, and is insensitive to viewport contamination, making it uniquely suited for continuous, real-time monitoring in harsh industrial environments.

- describe RES benefits

The RES technique offers several compelling advantages: it provides direct insight into plasma conduction currents, which are directly linked to power dissipation and electron transport; it operates independently of optical window condition; it enables high temporal resolution measurements at kilohertz sampling rates; and it is compatible with existing plasma reactor architectures without requiring hardware modifications. Furthermore, RES signals exhibit strong sensitivity to variations in RF power, chamber pressure, and wall contamination, allowing for the detection of subtle process deviations before they manifest as yield-limiting defects.

- discuss prior art RES systems

Prior attempts at radio frequency plasma diagnostics have focused on far-field electromagnetic sensing or spectral analysis of harmonics generated by nonlinear plasma behavior. However, these approaches lacked the spatial resolution, signal-to-noise ratio, or sensitivity required for industrial deployment. Systems designed for laboratory environments often employed bulky, low-bandwidth receivers and were not engineered for integration into commercial plasma tools.

- criticize prior art RES systems

Existing RES systems suffered from poor signal fidelity due to inadequate antenna design, insufficient shielding from electromagnetic interference, and lack of calibration against known plasma parameters. Many were limited to single-frequency operation and could not resolve the complex mixing products generated in multi-frequency plasmas. Additionally, prior systems did not establish a quantitative correlation between RES signal amplitude and fundamental plasma variables such as electron density or collision frequency, limiting their utility as process control tools.

- introduce PCT/EP2018/057556

The international patent application PCT/EP2018/057556 disclosed a method for detecting plasma emissions using a loop antenna placed near a dielectric viewport, but it failed to demonstrate the technique’s sensitivity to sub-percent changes in process parameters, did not validate its independence from viewport transparency under realistic contamination conditions, and did not explore its application in multi-frequency systems or its ability to monitor chamber wall cleanliness.

- describe PCT/EP2018/057556 benefits

While PCT/EP2018/057556 introduced the conceptual framework of near-field radio emission detection, it provided no empirical evidence of the technique’s robustness in production environments, nor did it quantify its sensitivity or establish its superiority over optical methods under conditions of viewport fouling.

- introduce Mandelis publication

The publication by Mandelis et al. explored thermal wave diagnostics in plasmas using infrared and acoustic methods, proposing indirect inference of plasma properties from surface temperature fluctuations. While innovative, this approach relies on thermal coupling mechanisms that are slow, indirect, and highly sensitive to ambient conditions, making it unsuitable for real-time process control in high-speed manufacturing.

- criticize Mandelis publication

Mandelis’ method lacks the temporal resolution and direct physical linkage to plasma current dynamics required for industrial plasma monitoring. Its reliance on thermal signatures introduces significant lag, and the technique is not applicable to non-thermal plasmas where electron temperature and gas temperature are decoupled, which is the norm in semiconductor processing.

- describe RES system components

The RES system comprises a high-sensitivity magnetic loop antenna, a low-noise preamplifier, a broadband RF receiver with digital down-conversion capabilities, a high-speed analog-to-digital converter, and a signal processing unit configured to perform real-time Fast Fourier Transform analysis. The antenna is constructed from a copper loop with a diameter of 21.6 mm, shielded by a non-conductive, RF-transparent housing, and positioned within 2 mm of the plasma chamber viewport. The system is calibrated using known RF power inputs and validated against V-I probe measurements to establish quantitative correlations between signal amplitude and plasma conduction current.

- explain RES system operation

During plasma operation, time-varying currents within the plasma generate oscillating magnetic fields that induce a voltage in the loop antenna via Faraday’s law of induction. The induced signal is amplified, filtered to remove external electromagnetic interference, digitized at sampling rates exceeding 100 kHz, and processed to extract the amplitude and frequency content of the fundamental drive frequency and its harmonics. The resulting spectral data is analyzed to detect deviations in signal amplitude, frequency shift, or the emergence of sidebands indicative of changes in plasma parameters such as power, pressure, or sheath impedance.

- discuss RES system benefits

The RES system enables continuous, contact-free monitoring of plasma conditions without requiring optical access, physical probes, or process interruption. It provides real-time feedback on conduction current variations, which are directly linked to power dissipation and electron density, enabling precise control of etch rates and deposition uniformity. Its immunity to viewport contamination ensures uninterrupted operation during extended runs, reducing downtime and improving yield. The system’s high sensitivity allows detection of changes as small as 5 W in applied power or 1 mTorr in chamber pressure, far exceeding the resolution of conventional methods.

- introduce plasma chamber types

The RES system is compatible with a wide range of plasma chamber architectures, including single-frequency capacitively coupled plasmas, dual-frequency systems, and multi-frequency configurations used in advanced etching and deposition tools. It functions effectively in both symmetric and asymmetric electrode geometries, and is adaptable to chambers with grounded walls, dielectric linings, or metallic surfaces.

- describe plasma chamber operation

In a typical capacitively coupled plasma chamber, RF power is applied to one electrode while the opposing electrode or chamber wall serves as a ground. The resulting electric field accelerates electrons, which collide with neutral species to sustain ionization. The plasma sheath forms at each electrode boundary, acting as a nonlinear capacitor that modulates current flow in response to the applied voltage. These dynamics generate broadband radio frequency emissions that are captured by the RES antenna, providing a direct signature of the underlying plasma behavior.

- motivate RES system development

The development of the RES system was motivated by the persistent challenges in maintaining process stability in high-volume semiconductor manufacturing, where even minor deviations in plasma parameters lead to significant yield loss. Existing diagnostic tools are either too intrusive, too slow, or too vulnerable to environmental degradation. The RES system addresses these limitations by providing a robust, non-invasive, and highly sensitive method for real-time plasma monitoring that integrates seamlessly into existing production infrastructure, enabling closed-loop control and predictive maintenance without compromising process integrity.
