# DESCRIPTION

- introduce schizophrenia diagnosis method

A novel method for diagnosing schizophrenia is disclosed, wherein the diagnosis is based on the assessment of expression levels of the inwardly rectifying potassium channel Kir2.3 and/or the dopamine D2 receptor DRD2 in a biological sample derived from a subject. This method enables the detection of schizophrenia with high specificity and sensitivity, even in drug-naive or drug-free individuals, by identifying consistent and statistically significant upregulation of Kir2.3 and/or DRD2 mRNA or protein expression relative to a defined baseline level observed in healthy control populations. The invention leverages the discovery that aberrant expression of these two molecular entities in peripheral immune cells correlates strongly with the neurobiological underpinnings of schizophrenia, independent of pharmacological intervention. Unlike prior diagnostic approaches that rely solely on clinical symptomatology or indirect biochemical proxies, this method provides an objective, molecularly grounded diagnostic criterion that can be applied early in the disease course, prior to the full manifestation of psychotic symptoms. The method is applicable across diverse demographic groups and is not confounded by age, sex, or ethnic background, as demonstrated by the uniformity of expression patterns observed in both male and female subjects. The diagnostic utility of this method is further enhanced by its compatibility with standard laboratory techniques, including nucleic acid amplification, hybridization, and immunoassay platforms, allowing for integration into existing clinical workflows. By focusing on peripheral biomarkers that reflect central nervous system dysfunction, this approach circumvents the ethical and practical limitations associated with direct brain tissue sampling, thereby enabling repeated, non-invasive monitoring over time. The method may be employed not only for initial diagnosis but also for stratifying patients according to molecular subtype, predicting treatment response, and evaluating therapeutic efficacy in longitudinal studies.

## BACKGROUND ART

- describe schizophrenia

Schizophrenia is a chronic and debilitating neuropsychiatric disorder characterized by profound disruptions in thought processes, emotional regulation, and behavioral control. Affected individuals typically exhibit positive symptoms such as hallucinations and delusions, negative symptoms including social withdrawal and flattened affect, and cognitive impairments involving attention, memory, and executive function. The disorder usually emerges in late adolescence or early adulthood and carries a lifetime prevalence of approximately one percent worldwide. Despite decades of clinical and scientific investigation, the etiology of schizophrenia remains incompletely understood, though it is widely accepted to arise from a complex interplay of genetic susceptibility, neurodevelopmental abnormalities, and environmental stressors. The pathophysiology is strongly associated with dysregulation of dopaminergic neurotransmission, particularly in mesolimbic and mesocortical pathways, although contributions from glutamatergic, GABAergic, and cholinergic systems are increasingly recognized. Current diagnostic criteria rely entirely on standardized psychiatric interviews and behavioral assessments, lacking any objective biological correlate, which contributes to diagnostic delays, misclassification, and variability in clinical outcomes.

- motivate genetic linkage of schizophrenia

Extensive family, twin, and adoption studies have established a robust genetic component to schizophrenia, with heritability estimates exceeding 80 percent. Genome-wide association studies and linkage analyses have identified numerous susceptibility loci, many of which implicate genes involved in synaptic plasticity, neurodevelopment, and ion channel function. The presence of shared genetic risk factors among affected relatives, even in the absence of identical phenotypes, supports the notion that schizophrenia is a polygenic disorder with heterogeneous molecular manifestations. This genetic complexity has hindered the development of targeted therapies and precise diagnostic tools, necessitating the identification of downstream molecular signatures that reliably reflect the underlying pathophysiology. Peripheral tissues, particularly immune cells, have emerged as promising sources of such signatures due to their accessibility and demonstrated capacity to mirror central nervous system gene expression patterns, likely through shared developmental origins and systemic signaling pathways.

- summarize prior art on genetic markers

Prior efforts to identify molecular markers for schizophrenia have focused primarily on neurotransmitter receptors, particularly those within the dopamine system. The dopamine D3 receptor gene (DRD3) has been repeatedly reported to be upregulated in peripheral blood lymphocytes of schizophrenic patients, independent of antipsychotic medication. Similarly, reduced expression of the α7 nicotinic acetylcholine receptor gene (CHRNA7) has been documented in immune cells of affected individuals. These findings have been validated using quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction and ligand-binding assays. However, these markers have demonstrated limited sensitivity and specificity when used in isolation, and their diagnostic utility has not been consistently replicated across independent cohorts. Moreover, no single marker has yet achieved sufficient predictive power to serve as a standalone diagnostic tool.

- describe Johnston-Wilson et al. (WO 0144269)

Johnston-Wilson et al. disclosed a method for detecting schizophrenia based on the analysis of protein expression profiles in peripheral blood mononuclear cells using two-dimensional gel electrophoresis and mass spectrometry. The study identified a panel of differentially expressed proteins, including those involved in energy metabolism and cytoskeletal organization, but did not report any association with dopamine receptors or potassium channels. While innovative in its proteomic approach, the method required specialized equipment and complex data analysis, limiting its clinical applicability.

- describe Cochran et al. (EP 1 348963 A1)

Cochran et al. described a diagnostic assay for schizophrenia based on the measurement of dopamine D2 receptor binding affinity in lymphocyte membranes using radiolabeled ligands. Although the method demonstrated altered receptor affinity in patients, it was hampered by low reproducibility, high variability due to membrane preparation techniques, and an inability to distinguish between receptor density and functional state. Furthermore, the assay was not validated in drug-naive populations, leaving open the possibility that observed changes were secondary to medication exposure.

- describe WO02400661 (Kir2.3 animal model)

WO02400661 disclosed the generation of transgenic animal models with targeted disruption of the Kir2.3 gene, which exhibited behavioral abnormalities resembling aspects of schizophrenia, including impaired prepulse inhibition and social withdrawal. These findings suggested a causal role for Kir2.3 dysfunction in neuropsychiatric phenotypes. However, the publication did not extend these observations to human subjects, nor did it propose a diagnostic method based on Kir2.3 expression in peripheral tissues.

- motivate dopamine receptor dysfunction

Dysfunction of dopamine receptors, particularly the D2 subtype, is a central tenet in the neurochemical hypothesis of schizophrenia. Antipsychotic medications exert their therapeutic effects primarily through antagonism of D2 receptors, and positron emission tomography studies have demonstrated elevated striatal D2 receptor occupancy in untreated patients. This suggests that alterations in D2 receptor expression or signaling may precede and contribute to symptom onset. However, direct measurement of D2 receptors in the human brain is invasive and impractical for routine use. The discovery that D2 receptor mRNA and protein are detectable and quantifiable in peripheral immune cells offers a viable alternative, provided that the expression levels in these cells reliably reflect central nervous system pathology.

- describe Horn et al. (U.S. Pat. No. 4,931,270)

Horn et al. described a method for detecting schizophrenia by measuring the binding of dopamine to lymphocyte membranes using radiolabeled dopamine analogs. The method relied on the premise that dopamine receptor density in immune cells correlates with brain receptor density. While this approach provided early evidence of peripheral dopaminergic involvement, it lacked specificity due to the presence of multiple dopamine receptor subtypes and was susceptible to interference from endogenous catecholamines and pharmacological agents.

- summarize expression of neurotransmitters and dopamine receptors

Numerous studies have documented the expression of various neurotransmitter receptors—including dopamine, serotonin, and acetylcholine receptors—in peripheral immune cells. These receptors are not merely vestigial remnants of neural development but are functionally active, capable of modulating immune cell migration, cytokine release, and adhesion. The presence of dopamine receptors on T lymphocytes, for instance, has been shown to influence integrin-mediated adhesion and activation states. This functional relevance strengthens the biological plausibility of using immune cell expression profiles as biomarkers for neuropsychiatric disorders.

- describe DRD3 as a peripheral marker

The dopamine D3 receptor (DRD3) has been proposed as a peripheral biomarker for schizophrenia based on its consistent upregulation in peripheral blood lymphocytes of affected individuals. Multiple independent studies have confirmed elevated DRD3 mRNA levels in drug-naive patients, suggesting that this alteration is intrinsic to the disease process rather than a consequence of pharmacotherapy. However, DRD3 alone lacks sufficient discriminatory power to serve as a definitive diagnostic marker, as its expression varies widely among individuals and overlaps with levels observed in other psychiatric conditions such as bipolar disorder.

- describe α7AChR gene expression

Reduced expression of the α7 nicotinic acetylcholine receptor gene (CHRNA7) has been reported in peripheral blood cells of schizophrenic patients. This finding is consistent with postmortem brain studies showing decreased α7 receptor density in the hippocampus and prefrontal cortex. The α7 receptor is implicated in sensory gating deficits, a hallmark of schizophrenia, and its downregulation may reflect a broader disruption in cholinergic signaling. Nevertheless, like DRD3, α7AChR expression changes are not exclusive to schizophrenia and are influenced by factors such as smoking status and cognitive load, limiting their diagnostic specificity.

- motivate search for new markers

Given the limitations of existing biomarkers, there remains a critical unmet need for novel, highly specific, and quantitatively reliable molecular markers that can accurately distinguish schizophrenia from other psychiatric disorders and from healthy states. The identification of such markers would revolutionize early diagnosis, enable personalized treatment selection, and facilitate the development of novel therapeutics targeting specific molecular pathways. The present invention fulfills this need by introducing the combined assessment of Kir2.3 and DRD2 expression as a highly sensitive and specific diagnostic signature for schizophrenia.
