## DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

### 1. Field of the Invention

The present invention relates to non-destructive testing systems and methods for the detection, characterization, and quantitative measurement of internal defects in composite materials, particularly those composed of layered fiber-reinforced polymers. The invention is specifically directed to systems and techniques that enable high-resolution identification of foreign object inclusions, voids, disbonds, and other manufacturing anomalies within composite laminates using ultrasonic pulse-echo interrogation. The disclosed methods and apparatus are designed for use in industrial environments where precision, repeatability, and real-time feedback are critical to ensuring structural integrity, particularly in aerospace, automotive, and energy sectors where composite components are subject to stringent safety and performance standards.

### 2. Description of Related Art

Non-destructive testing (NDT) has become an indispensable component of modern manufacturing and maintenance protocols for advanced composite structures. Traditional NDT methods, including thermography, shearography, X-ray computed tomography, and acoustic emission, have been employed to detect internal flaws such as delaminations, porosity, and foreign object inclusions. However, these techniques often suffer from limitations in spatial resolution, depth penetration, accessibility, or cost. Ultrasonic testing, by contrast, offers a favorable balance of resolution, portability, and cost-effectiveness, making it the most widely adopted method for composite inspection. Despite its prevalence, conventional ultrasonic systems have struggled to reliably quantify the dimensions of small, thin, or irregularly shaped defects embedded within highly scattering woven fiber architectures.

Ultrasonic testing of composite materials remains challenged by the presence of structural noise arising from fiber tow undulations, resin-rich regions, and ply interfaces, which obscure defect signatures and reduce the reliability of amplitude-based detection thresholds. Current methods for porosity measurement, cure monitoring, adhesive inspection, and laminate characterization rely on manual gate selection, fixed amplitude thresholds, or low-resolution C-scan imaging, resulting in significant underestimation or overestimation of defect size. Automated calibration, curved surface compensation, and structural health monitoring systems have been proposed, but none integrate real-time signal processing with machine learning or gradient analysis to achieve the level of precision required for critical aerospace components. Interface-guided waves and workpiece inspection techniques have been explored, yet they are often limited to specific geometries or require specialized transducer arrays. The need for a system capable of direct material property determination, portable operation, and high-fidelity 3D visualization of internal anomalies remains unmet by existing technologies.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention introduces a novel non-destructive testing system and method for the high-resolution detection and quantification of internal defects in composite materials, particularly those comprising woven fiber-reinforced polymer laminates. The invention enables real-time visualization of foreign object inclusions, disbonds, voids, and other manufacturing anomalies with unprecedented accuracy by integrating advanced signal processing, spatial interpolation, and gradient-based edge detection techniques. The system operates using a pulse-echo ultrasonic configuration with a spherically focused transducer, a precisely controlled translation stage, and a fluid-coupled housing assembly that ensures consistent acoustic coupling across curved or irregular surfaces. 

A first embodiment of the system includes a portable transducer housing assembly comprising a central housing, a fluid connector, a lens housing, and a spherically focused ultrasonic transducer mounted within a coupling element designed to maintain a stable acoustic interface. The transducer is driven by a pulser/receiver unit that generates high-frequency, short-duration pulses, and the reflected signals are captured at a high sampling rate and processed by a digital processor. The processor performs a three-stage data enhancement: first, it applies a surface leveling algorithm to correct for minor variations in part topography by fitting a third-order polynomial to front-wall echo arrival times; second, it applies a two-dimensional Gaussian filter to suppress structural noise while preserving defect signatures; and third, it employs Fourier-based interpolation to increase the spatial sampling density by a factor of five, thereby enhancing edge definition without requiring physically finer scan steps.

A second embodiment of the system incorporates a material database that stores acoustic impedance profiles, wave velocity characteristics, and expected signal responses for known composite architectures. The processor matches acquired A-scan waveforms against stored signatures to identify defect types and assign probability metrics for inclusion, disbond, or porosity. This enables automated classification without operator intervention. A third embodiment integrates an artificial intelligence module trained on a dataset of labeled ultrasonic responses, enabling the system to autonomously detect, classify, and quantify defects based on amplitude gradients, frequency shifts, and wavelet transform features. 

The system further defines the characteristics of foreign objects as having an acoustic impedance distinct from the surrounding matrix, with dimensions ranging from 1.5 mm to 12.7 mm in diameter and thicknesses as low as 0.05 mm. The invention distinguishes such objects from air pockets and delaminations by analyzing the temporal shape of the reflected waveform, the magnitude of the amplitude gradient at the defect boundary, and the consistency of the defect’s geometric profile across multiple B-scans. The system outputs a three-dimensional graphical representation of the defect, including its lateral dimensions, depth location, and estimated volume, with color-coded intensity maps that highlight regions of maximum acoustic impedance mismatch. The invention enables detection and sizing of defects with an average error in diameter of less than 0.12 mm, a significant improvement over prior art methods that exhibit errors exceeding 0.5 mm.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention provides a comprehensive non-destructive testing system for the detection, characterization, and dimensional quantification of internal defects in composite materials, particularly those fabricated from woven carbon fiber-reinforced polymer laminates. The system comprises a portable transducer housing assembly, a digital signal processor, a data acquisition unit, and a graphical user interface that collectively enable high-resolution ultrasonic interrogation of composite structures from a single access surface. The transducer housing assembly includes a central housing constructed from a rigid, non-acoustically reflective polymer, a fluid connector that interfaces with a coupling medium such as water or gel, and a lens housing that securely retains a spherically focused ultrasonic transducer with a center frequency of 7.5 MHz and a focal length of 38.1 mm. The transducer is mounted within a coupling element formed from a flexible, acoustically transparent membrane or a fluid-filled chamber, ensuring consistent acoustic coupling regardless of surface curvature or minor surface irregularities.

The ultrasonic transducer operates in a pulse-echo configuration, emitting short-duration, high-frequency acoustic pulses into the composite material and capturing the reflected waveforms. The reflected signals are digitized at a sampling rate of 160 MHz and transmitted to a processor that performs a sequence of data enhancement operations. First, the processor identifies the front-wall echo at each spatial coordinate (x₁, x₂) and constructs a three-dimensional surface of arrival times, which is then fitted with a third-order polynomial to model surface topography. The time axis of each A-scan is adjusted to align all front-wall echoes to a common reference point, effectively compensating for minor variations in part flatness or transducer alignment. Second, the processor applies a two-dimensional Gaussian filter to the amplitude data, with standard deviations selected to be five-thirds of the scan step size, to suppress high-frequency structural noise arising from fiber tow undulations and ply interfaces. Third, the filtered data is interpolated using a Fourier-based method to increase the spatial sampling density by a factor of five, thereby enhancing the resolution of defect boundaries without increasing scan time or data volume.

The processor then generates a maximum gated amplitude (MGA) map by selecting the peak amplitude within a time gate defined as three-quarters of the peak-to-peak distance between adjacent plies, corresponding to the region where foreign object inclusions are expected. This MGA map forms the basis for the C-scan image. To determine the lateral dimensions of a defect, the processor computes the magnitude of the gradient of the interpolated C-scan data using a fourth-order central difference scheme, yielding a gradient magnitude map that highlights regions of maximal amplitude change. The boundary of the defect is identified by tracing the local maxima of this gradient along a radial projection from a manually selected interior point, with the process repeated iteratively around the defect perimeter to generate a closed contour. The area enclosed by this contour is then calculated, and an effective diameter is derived using the formula for the diameter of a circle with equivalent area. This method, termed Maximum Gradient Transition (MGT), is insensitive to defect shape and does not rely on amplitude thresholds, thereby overcoming the limitations of traditional 6 dB drop techniques.

The system further includes a material database that stores pre-characterized acoustic signatures for various composite architectures, including fiber orientation, resin type, and ply sequence. The processor matches acquired A-scan waveforms against entries in the database to assign a probability of defect type, enabling automated classification of foreign objects, disbonds, and porosity. In an alternative embodiment, an artificial intelligence module trained on a dataset of over ten thousand labeled ultrasonic responses is employed to recognize patterns in amplitude, frequency shift, and wavelet transform coefficients, allowing the system to detect and quantify defects with minimal operator input. The system generates a three-dimensional layered image of the composite, with color-coded depth slices that illustrate the location and extent of anomalies. The graphical user interface allows users to select z-gates, adjust amplitude filters, and apply edge delineation tools to refine defect boundaries. The system also calculates the curvature and roughness of bond lines, distinguishes between kissing bonds and true disbonds, and quantifies the size and shape of barely visible impact damage with sub-millimeter accuracy. The system's capabilities extend to the determination of fiber orientation, degree of cure, and layer thickness through the application of two-dimensional fast Fourier transforms and differential amplitude analysis. The entire system is operable in both manual and automated modes, with the transducer housing mountable on robotic arms, translation stages, or handheld platforms, and is communicatively linked to a networked computing environment for data storage, remote analysis, and integration with manufacturing control systems.
