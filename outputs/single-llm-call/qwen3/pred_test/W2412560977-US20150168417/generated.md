## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

- Histology has long served as a foundational tool in pathological diagnosis and biomedical research, relying on the preservation of tissue architecture through chemical fixation to enable microscopic examination. Protein detection within fixed tissues typically involves immunohistochemical or immunofluorescent staining, which requires the accessibility of epitopes that are often masked or altered during fixation. The most widely employed fixative in clinical and research settings is formaldehyde, which induces inter- and intra-molecular cross-links between amino groups on proteins, thereby stabilizing cellular structures but simultaneously obscuring antigenic sites critical for antibody binding. This phenomenon has necessitated the development of antigen retrieval techniques to reverse or mitigate the effects of fixation and restore immunoreactivity.

- Aldehyde-based cross-linking agents, including paraformaldehyde, glutaraldehyde, and formalin, are commonly used to immobilize cellular components and preserve morphology. These agents react primarily with primary amines on lysine residues and the N-termini of proteins, forming Schiff bases that may further rearrange into stable methylene bridges. While effective for structural preservation, these covalent modifications significantly reduce the availability of target epitopes, leading to diminished signal intensity, false-negative results, and inconsistent reproducibility across samples and laboratories.

- Current fixation methods, despite decades of refinement, remain fundamentally limited by their irreversible chemical alterations of protein structure. Conventional antigen retrieval protocols, such as heat-induced epitope retrieval in citrate or EDTA buffers, rely on thermal energy to disrupt non-covalent interactions and partially reverse cross-links. However, these approaches are often insufficient to fully restore antigenicity, particularly for epitopes deeply embedded within dense aldehyde-induced networks. Moreover, these methods exhibit variable efficacy depending on tissue type, fixation duration, and antibody specificity, resulting in unreliable outcomes that compromise diagnostic accuracy and experimental reproducibility.

- There exists a persistent and unmet need for improved antigen retrieval methods capable of chemically reversing aldehyde-mediated modifications rather than merely disrupting non-covalent associations. Prior art has largely focused on physical or pH-based strategies, neglecting the potential of targeted chemical scavenging to actively neutralize residual aldehydes and liberate masked epitopes. Existing techniques fail to address the root cause of epitope masking—the presence of reactive aldehyde groups covalently bound to proteins—and thus provide only partial and inconsistent restoration of antigenicity.

- Deficiencies in prior art are further compounded by the absence of standardized, chemically defined reagents designed specifically to reverse formaldehyde adducts. Commercially available retrieval solutions are often ill-defined mixtures lacking mechanistic clarity, and no prior method has demonstrated the ability to quantitatively restore free amino groups or recover native protein structure following formaldehyde fixation. Consequently, there remains a critical gap in the field for a reliable, broadly applicable, and chemically rational approach to antigen retrieval that directly targets and neutralizes aldehyde cross-links.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

- The invention discloses a novel antigen retrieval method that employs aldehyde scavenging agents to chemically reverse formaldehyde-induced protein modifications, thereby restoring epitope accessibility for antibody detection. This method fundamentally departs from conventional thermal or pH-based retrieval by directly neutralizing reactive aldehyde moieties through nucleophilic addition, thereby liberating masked antigens and enhancing immunoreactivity.

- The invention comprises a solution containing one or more aldehyde scavenging agents capable of reacting with formaldehyde adducts on fixed tissues, thereby cleaving methylene bridges and regenerating free amino groups on lysine residues and protein N-termini. The solution is formulated to optimize reactivity, stability, and tissue penetration, and may be applied to paraffin-embedded or frozen tissue sections following deparaffinization and rehydration.

- The aldehyde scavenging agents function by forming stable adducts with free aldehydes or Schiff bases, preventing their reformation and promoting the hydrolysis of existing cross-links. This chemical unmasking process is distinct from mere epitope exposure through denaturation and enables the recovery of antigenicity even in tissues subjected to prolonged fixation.

- An optional heating step may be employed to accelerate the reaction kinetics between the scavenging agent and aldehyde adducts, with temperatures ranging from approximately 80°C to 98°C for durations of 30 to 90 minutes. Heating enhances diffusion and molecular mobility, facilitating deeper penetration of the scavenging agent into dense tissue matrices.

- The formulations of the aldehyde scavenging agents include aqueous solutions buffered to a pH range of 3.0 to 5.0, optimized to promote nucleophilic attack while minimizing tissue degradation. The solutions may further include nonionic surfactants to improve wettability and stabilizing agents to prevent oxidation or degradation of the scavenging compounds during storage or use.

- The invention further encompasses a kit for antigen retrieval comprising a pre-formulated solution of an aldehyde scavenging agent, instructions for use, and optionally, controls for validation of retrieval efficacy. Additional methods include the use of the solution for identifying novel antigen retrieval agents through mass spectrometric analysis of peptide recovery and the lyophilization of fixed proteins for in vitro screening of unmasking efficacy.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

- For the purposes of this disclosure, the term “a” or “an” refers to one or more of the recited element unless otherwise specified. The term “comprise” and its grammatical variants, including “comprising,” are used in an open-ended sense to mean including but not limited to the elements listed, and do not exclude additional, unrecited components. The term “or” is used in its inclusive sense, meaning either one or both of the referenced options unless context dictates otherwise. The term “about” denotes a range of ±10% of the stated value, unless otherwise indicated, to account for normal experimental variation in measurement, preparation, or application.

- “Unmasking” refers to the chemical process by which aldehyde-induced cross-links are reversed, thereby restoring the native conformation or accessibility of epitopes on proteins that were previously obscured by formaldehyde adducts. This process is distinct from denaturation or epitope exposure through heat or pH alone, as it involves direct chemical modification of the cross-linking agent rather than disruption of secondary or tertiary structure.

- The method for retrieving antigens and improving detection involves the application of an aqueous solution containing an aldehyde scavenging agent to fixed tissue sections after deparaffinization and rehydration. The solution is maintained at a temperature between 80°C and 98°C for a period of 30 to 90 minutes, during which time the scavenging agent reacts with formaldehyde-derived adducts, cleaving methylene bridges and regenerating free amino groups. Following retrieval, tissues are cooled, washed, and subjected to standard immunohistochemical or immunofluorescent staining protocols.

- The heating step is performed using a pressure-assisted heating device or water bath maintained at a controlled temperature, ensuring uniform thermal exposure across the tissue section. The solution is maintained at a pH between 3.0 and 5.0 to optimize nucleophilic reactivity while minimizing tissue damage. Representative aldehyde-based cross-linking agents include paraformaldehyde, glutaraldehyde, formalin, and mixtures thereof.

- Representative aldehyde scavenging agents include ascorbic acid, 2-imidazolidinone, tris(hydroxymethyl)aminomethane, aminoethanol, hydrazine, hydroxylamine, and derivatives thereof. The concentration of the aldehyde scavenging agent in the solution ranges from 1% to 10% (w/v), with optimal efficacy observed at 5% (w/v) for ascorbic acid and 2-imidazolidinone.

- An optional nonionic surfactant may be included to enhance tissue penetration and reduce surface tension. Representative nonionic surfactants include Triton X-100, Tween 20, and Nonidet P-40, each used at concentrations between 0.1% and 1.0% (v/v). A stabilizing agent, such as sodium azide, EDTA, or butylated hydroxytoluene, may be added to prevent oxidation or microbial contamination of the solution during storage.

- A preferred formulation comprises 5% (w/v) ascorbic acid, 0.5% (v/v) Triton X-100, and 0.02% (w/v) sodium azide, buffered to pH 3.5 with citric acid. This formulation demonstrates superior antigen retrieval across multiple tissue types and fixation durations.

- The kit for retrieving protein of interest in fixed tissue includes a sealed container of the scavenging agent solution, a set of instructions detailing application parameters, a positive control tissue section, and a negative control buffer lacking the scavenging agent. The kit may further include pre-cut tissue slides, staining reagents, or detection antibodies.

- A method for identifying novel antigen retrieval agents involves lyophilizing a fixed protein, reconstituting it in water, and exposing it to candidate agents under controlled heating conditions. Protein recovery is then quantified using mass spectrometry to assess the restoration of free amino groups and peptide integrity.

- Compounds useful for unmasking include amino ethanol derivatives such as 2-aminoethanol and N-methylaminoethanol; amino acids and derivatives including glycine, lysine, and ornithine; amine and aniline-containing compounds such as aniline, benzylamine, and ethylenediamine; hydrazine and hydrazide derivatives including hydrazine, phenylhydrazine, and adipic acid dihydrazide; urea derivatives such as thiourea and methylurea; thiol derivatives including dithiothreitol and 2-mercaptoethanol; hydroxyl compounds such as glycerol and ethylene glycol; and keto derivatives such as acetone and cyclohexanone.

- In Example 2, formaldehyde-fixed tissue sections were treated with a 5% ascorbic acid solution at pH 3.5 and heated at 95°C for 60 minutes, resulting in marked enhancement of Collagen IV and Claudin-5 immunoreactivity compared to untreated controls.

- In Example 3, the retrieval method was shown to be compatible with multiple immunohistochemical detection systems, including chromogenic, fluorescent, and enzymatic labeling, without compromising signal specificity or background.

- In Example 4, vascular structures in human and murine tissues were clearly visualized following retrieval, with enhanced continuity and intensity of Collagen IV staining along basement membranes.

- In Example 5, vascular-associated antigens in adult mouse brain tissue were unmasked using the disclosed method, demonstrating recovery of epitopes previously undetectable after standard fixation.

- In Example 6, mass spectrometry revealed a significant increase in free amino acids following treatment with the scavenging agent, confirming chemical reversal of formaldehyde adducts.

- In Example 7, human brain vasculature exhibited robust antigen retrieval after prolonged formalin fixation, enabling reliable detection of endothelial markers in clinical samples.

- In Example 8, the addition of 0.5% Triton X-100 to a 5% ascorbic acid solution significantly improved staining evenness and intensity, while the inclusion of 5% ascorbic acid reduced tissue autofluorescence. Images captured under 488 nm excitation demonstrated superior signal-to-noise ratio compared to controls heated in water alone.
