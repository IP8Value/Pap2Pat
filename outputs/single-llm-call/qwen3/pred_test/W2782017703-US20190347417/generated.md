# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

System-on-chip (SoC) architectures have become the foundation of modern electronic systems, integrating dozens of digital and analog intellectual property (IP) cores to fulfill complex functional requirements. To accelerate development timelines and reduce design costs, SoC integrators routinely procure a significant portion of these IP cores from third-party vendors, a practice that introduces critical trust concerns. These third-party IP (3PIP) cores are often delivered without full transparency into their internal structure, leaving the integrator with no means to verify the absence of malicious circuitry embedded during fabrication or design. The potential for hardware Trojans—malicious modifications inserted covertly into an IP core—poses a severe threat to the security and integrity of the entire system. Such Trojans can remain dormant during normal operation, activating only under rare or specific conditions, thereby evading conventional functional testing and verification. Once triggered, these Trojans may exfiltrate sensitive data, disrupt system behavior, or grant unauthorized access to privileged operations, compromising confidentiality, integrity, and availability. The challenge of detecting such Trojans is exacerbated by the absence of a trusted golden reference model against which to compare the behavior or structure of the procured IP. Without this baseline, traditional verification methods struggle to distinguish between legitimate design functionality and stealthy malicious modifications. Existing approaches to Trojan detection have been broadly categorized into structural analysis, functional simulation, symbolic algebraic reasoning, formal verification, and information flow tracking. Structural analysis techniques rely on identifying circuits with low signal activity or unused logic paths as potential Trojan indicators. However, these methods are inherently flawed because Trojans can be designed to mimic normal operational patterns, ensuring high activation probabilities and full integration into the functional netlist, rendering them indistinguishable from legitimate components. Functional analysis methods attempt to detect anomalies by observing deviations in input-output behavior under simulated stimuli. While these techniques can uncover some forms of malicious behavior, they are easily circumvented by Trojans that activate only after prolonged sequences of inputs or under highly specific environmental conditions that are unlikely to be exercised during standard simulation. Symbolic algebra approaches attempt to model the mathematical relationships between inputs and outputs, but they require access to a known-good reference design, which is typically unavailable when IP is sourced from untrusted vendors. Formal verification techniques, including bounded model checking and property checking, have been employed to assert that sensitive data does not propagate to unauthorized outputs. However, these methods are limited by the number of clock cycles over which the circuit can be unrolled, and they are susceptible to false positives due to the inability to distinguish between direct information flow and coincidental value equivalence. Furthermore, attackers can evade detection by introducing non-resettable counters or extended finite state machines that delay Trojan activation beyond the verification horizon. Information flow tracking (IFT) methods, such as gate-level taint analysis, attempt to trace the propagation of sensitive data bits through Boolean gates. While more precise than structural or functional methods, IFT suffers from taint explosion in designs incorporating scan chains or other design-for-test structures, where the taint of a single sensitive bit propagates uncontrollably across hundreds of flip-flops, rendering the analysis meaningless. Additionally, IFT cannot differentiate between legitimate and malicious paths when the Trojan leaks data through the same output ports used by the intended functionality. Commercial tools based on security path verification attempt to identify unauthorized data flows by tagging sensitive signals and tracing their reach, but they require prior knowledge of which internal signals are sensitive, a requirement that cannot be met when the IP is provided as a black box. These limitations collectively demonstrate that no existing method is capable of reliably detecting hardware Trojans in third-party IP cores without access to internal design details or a trusted reference, leaving the SoC supply chain vulnerable to sophisticated and undetectable threats.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

A novel framework for detecting hardware Trojans in third-party IP cores is introduced, grounded in the principles of information flow security (IFS) verification. This framework operates at the gate-level netlist and does not require access to the original RTL source code or a trusted reference design. It is based on the fundamental observation that any hardware Trojan, regardless of its complexity or concealment strategy, must alter the legitimate flow of information within the system, thereby violating established confidentiality or integrity policies. The framework models the sensitive asset—such as a cryptographic key, private register, or control signal—as a stuck-at-0 or stuck-at-1 fault and employs an enhanced partial-scan automatic test pattern generation (ATPG) technique to detect whether this fault can be observed at unauthorized output points or controlled from unauthorized input points. This approach transforms the problem of Trojan detection into a systematic fault propagation analysis, leveraging decades of industrial ATPG infrastructure to identify information flow violations without relying on simulation, formal unrolling, or taint propagation. The SoC design flow begins with the specification of system-level functionality, followed by the integration of soft, firm, or hard IP cores from trusted and untrusted sources. In this framework, entities such as the SoC integrator and the CAD tools are considered trusted, while all third-party IP providers, including those responsible for design-for-test (DfT) insertion, are treated as untrusted. The Trojan structure is assumed to consist of two components: a trigger mechanism that activates under specific conditions and a payload that performs the malicious action, such as leaking a secret or altering a control signal. Two broad categories of Trojans are recognized: Type I Trojans, which leak or manipulate assets through legitimate functional paths by introducing bypass circuits, and Type II Trojans, which operate through functionally isolated circuits that have no legitimate connection to the asset’s intended domain. The detection framework is implemented as a software module executable on a computing device comprising a processor circuit and memory components, where the gate-level netlist, technology library, and policy definitions are stored in volatile or nonvolatile memory. The framework is embodied either as software executed by the processor or as dedicated hardware circuitry optimized for high-throughput ATPG analysis. Upon initialization, the framework first identifies all assets of interest, such as specific nets or flip-flops containing sensitive data, and then determines the set of potential observation and control points within the design. Using partial-scan ATPG, the framework dynamically enables scan chains for selected flip-flops to enhance controllability and observability without requiring full-scan insertion, thereby preserving the original design’s testability structure while enabling deep sequential fault propagation analysis. The framework performs two distinct verification phases: confidentiality verification and integrity verification. In confidentiality verification, the asset is modeled as a stuck-at fault, and the ATPG engine identifies all output points—primary outputs or scan flip-flops—through which the logical value of the asset can be observed. Each identified observation point is analyzed to determine whether it lies outside the authorized fan-out cone of the asset, indicating a potential Trojan leakage path. In integrity verification, the asset is modeled as a fault to be activated, and the ATPG engine identifies all input points from which the asset’s value can be controlled. Any control point located outside the authorized fan-in cone of the asset is flagged as a potential Trojan trigger. To distinguish between legitimate and malicious paths, the framework performs intersect analysis, comparing the fan-in or fan-out structures of identified observation or control points against those of known legitimate paths. For example, in an encryption module, if a secret key can be observed at a point not in the fan-out cone of the ciphertext output, a confidentiality violation is declared. Similarly, if a program counter can be influenced by a register not in the fan-in cone of the instruction decode unit, an integrity violation is detected. The framework further extracts the triggering condition of the Trojan by analyzing the stimulus vectors generated during ATPG. If the Trojan’s trigger is implemented as a finite state machine, the framework identifies state registers by detecting feedback loops and reconstructs the state transition graph using reverse-engineering techniques, thereby deriving the precise sequence of inputs required to activate the payload. The detection framework is implemented using commercially available ATPG tools such as Tetramax, enhanced with custom Tcl scripts to automate the IFS policy checks, asset modeling, and intersect analysis. The framework is independent of the underlying technology node and operates on synthesized gate-level netlists, making it compatible with any standard design flow. It can be applied to both DfT-inserted and non-DfT netlists, overcoming the taint explosion problem inherent in IFT methods. The confidentiality and integrity policies are formally defined as access restrictions: confidentiality policy prohibits unauthorized observation of sensitive assets, while integrity policy prohibits unauthorized control over critical system states. These policies are encoded as constraints within the framework and verified against the netlist using the ATPG-driven fault propagation model. The framework generates detailed reports including the propagation path, stimulus vector, and identification of malicious observation or control points, enabling the SoC integrator to isolate and remove the Trojan. Variations in execution order, the addition of counters or state variables to track multiple assets, and the use of disjunctive logic to express complex policy rules are supported. The framework may be embodied in software, firmware, or dedicated hardware, and may be distributed as a computer-readable medium containing instructions that, when executed, cause a processor to perform the IFS verification steps. The scope of this disclosure encompasses all embodiments and modifications that fall within the spirit and principles of the invention, including extensions to analog circuits, mixed-signal systems, and post-silicon validation environments. The framework’s ability to detect Trojans without requiring a golden reference, its resilience to extended triggering sequences, and its compatibility with industrial DfT flows represent a fundamental advancement in hardware security verification. The disclosure concludes with the recognition that while no detection method is infallible, this framework significantly raises the bar for Trojan insertion by eliminating the assumptions of invisibility and inevitability that have previously protected malicious actors in the IP supply chain.
