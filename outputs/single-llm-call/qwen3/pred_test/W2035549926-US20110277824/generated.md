## BACKGROUND

Solar cells are semiconductor devices that convert sunlight directly into electrical energy through the photovoltaic effect, making them a cornerstone of renewable energy systems. As global demand for clean, sustainable power continues to rise, solar energy has emerged as one of the most viable alternatives to fossil fuel-based generation due to its scalability, low operational emissions, and decreasing cost per watt. While various alternative energy sources such as wind, hydroelectric, and geothermal systems contribute to the energy mix, solar photovoltaics offer unique advantages in terms of modular deployment, minimal maintenance, and compatibility with distributed generation architectures. The fundamental structure of a typical crystalline silicon solar cell consists of a p-n junction formed within a silicon wafer, overlaid with an anti-reflective coating to minimize optical losses, front and rear metal contacts for current collection, and often a back-surface field layer to enhance carrier collection efficiency. The photovoltaic effect occurs when photons with sufficient energy strike the semiconductor material, generating electron-hole pairs that are separated by the built-in electric field of the p-n junction, thereby producing a flow of current through an external circuit. To maximize light absorption, the surface of the silicon wafer is typically textured to reduce specular reflection and increase the probability of photon trapping through multiple internal reflections. Selective emitter structures have been developed to optimize this process by creating regions of high doping under the metal contacts to reduce series resistance, while maintaining a lightly doped region between contacts to minimize recombination losses at the surface. Conventional methods for forming such selective emitters rely on photolithographic masking techniques, which involve the application of a photoresist layer, exposure to ultraviolet light through a patterned mask, development to remove exposed regions, and subsequent diffusion or implantation steps to create the desired doping profile. However, this process is inherently complex, requiring multiple high-precision steps, expensive equipment, and significant material consumption. The stripping process, used to remove the photoresist after patterning, often introduces contamination or surface damage, and may necessitate additional cleaning steps that further increase processing time and cost. Etching processes, particularly those involving wet chemical solutions or plasma-based techniques, are employed to remove unwanted material and define surface features, but they are frequently limited by poor selectivity, non-uniformity, or the introduction of crystallographic defects. The conventional method of forming a selective emitter using photolithography and diffusion is further constrained by its inability to achieve fine feature resolution without risking alignment errors, mask misregistration, or excessive edge roughness, all of which degrade cell performance. Moreover, the use of toxic chemicals, high-temperature processing, and multi-step fabrication sequences contribute to elevated manufacturing costs and environmental concerns. These limitations collectively hinder the scalability and economic viability of high-efficiency solar cells, particularly in mass production environments where throughput, yield, and cost efficiency are paramount. Consequently, there exists a critical and unmet need for a simplified, cost-effective, and scalable method of fabricating selective emitter structures that preserves optical performance while minimizing material waste, reducing process complexity, and eliminating reliance on photolithographic masking.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention introduces a novel solar cell and a method of manufacturing the same that overcomes the limitations of conventional fabrication techniques by enabling precise, mask-free patterning of the front surface with minimal material loss and reduced chemical consumption. The method achieves a significant reduction in etchant consumption by eliminating the need for photolithographic masks and replacing them with a direct, localized etching process that selectively removes only the regions intended for heavy doping, thereby minimizing the volume of aggressive chemical solutions required. This approach also minimizes material loss from the silicon wafer by avoiding the over-etching and undercutting commonly associated with traditional masking and stripping steps, preserving the structural integrity of the substrate and reducing yield loss. The invention enables easy patterning of the front surface through a simplified, single-step process that does not require ultraviolet exposure, photoresist application, or complex alignment procedures, thereby streamlining the manufacturing workflow. By precisely controlling the doping profile beneath the contact fingers, the invention reduces surface recombination velocity in the lightly doped regions between contacts, allowing for improved open-circuit voltage and fill factor without compromising short-circuit current density. The localized heavy doping under the metal contacts simultaneously reduces serial resistance by enhancing carrier conductivity in the current collection paths, leading to a more efficient extraction of photogenerated carriers. The resulting solar cell exhibits superior photoelectric conversion efficiency compared to conventional designs, achieving higher current output and improved voltage characteristics through the synergistic optimization of surface texturing, doping uniformity, and contact interface quality. The advantages of this invention include a substantial reduction in manufacturing complexity, a significant decrease in the use of hazardous chemicals, a lower overall production cost, and a measurable improvement in cell efficiency that is fully compatible with existing industrial screen-printing and firing lines. Furthermore, the method is scalable to large-area wafers and requires no specialized equipment beyond standard diffusion furnaces and etching stations, making it readily adoptable by existing solar cell manufacturers without capital-intensive upgrades.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF EXEMPLARY EMBODIMENTS

The emitter layer is formed by diffusing phosphorus into the surface of a boron-doped p-type silicon wafer using a phosphorus oxychloride (POCl₃) source in a high-temperature tube furnace, resulting in a uniformly doped shallow junction across the entire front surface. Following this initial diffusion, a thin silicon dioxide (SiO₂) layer is deposited over the emitter using plasma-enhanced chemical vapor deposition to serve as a diffusion barrier mask. This SiO₂ layer is then selectively removed in predetermined regions corresponding to the locations of future front metal contacts by applying a patterned etch paste that chemically dissolves the oxide only where contact fingers are intended to be formed. The etch paste is applied using a screen-printing technique, allowing for precise, non-lithographic patterning without the need for photomasks or UV exposure. After patterning, the wafer undergoes a second diffusion step under identical conditions to the first, resulting in a heavily doped region beneath the areas where the SiO₂ mask was removed, while the regions still covered by SiO₂ retain their shallow, lightly doped character. The remaining SiO₂ mask is then removed using a dilute hydrofluoric acid solution, leaving behind a precisely defined selective emitter structure with a low sheet resistance of approximately 20 Ω/□ under the contact fingers and a higher sheet resistance of approximately 80 Ω/□ in the inter-finger regions. An anti-reflective coating is subsequently deposited over the entire front surface using plasma-enhanced chemical vapor deposition, forming a silicon nitride (Si₃N₄) layer with a refractive index of 2.06 and a thickness of 85 nm to further reduce optical losses. Front electrodes are formed by screen-printing silver paste along the predefined contact finger patterns, followed by cofiring in a belt furnace to establish low-resistance ohmic contacts with the heavily doped regions. Rear electrodes are formed by screen-printing aluminum paste across the entire back surface, which simultaneously forms a back-surface field layer during the firing process by inducing aluminum-induced doping that creates a p⁺ region beneath the rear contact, enhancing carrier collection and reducing recombination at the rear interface. The formation of the back-surface field layer improves the open-circuit voltage and overall conversion efficiency by repelling minority carriers from the rear surface. The rear electrode also serves as a reflective layer, enhancing internal light trapping by reflecting unabsorbed photons back into the silicon substrate. Alternative configurations for the front and rear electrodes may include the use of copper plating or silver-coated copper wires for reduced material cost, while maintaining the same selective emitter architecture. The entire manufacturing process is significantly simplified compared to conventional methods, as it eliminates photolithography, resist stripping, and multiple etching cycles, thereby reducing the number of process steps and minimizing exposure to hazardous chemicals. This simplification directly translates into a reduction in manufacturing costs, improved throughput, and enhanced yield. The resulting solar cell demonstrates a marked improvement in photoelectric conversion efficiency, achieving a measured efficiency of 17.6% under standard test conditions, with a substantial gain in short-circuit current density exceeding 200 mA compared to conventionally textured and homogeneously doped cells, without any degradation in open-circuit voltage or fill factor.
