# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

- introduce agricultural pathogen control

The present invention relates to the field of agricultural pathogen control, specifically to the use of small molecule inhibitors of cinnamate-4-hydroxylase (C4H) for enhancing plant immunity against a broad spectrum of phytopathogenic organisms, including plant-parasitic nematodes, fungi, bacteria, and oomycetes. The invention provides novel chemical compounds and methods for their application in crop protection, enabling the suppression of disease development without direct toxicity to the pathogens themselves. This approach leverages the plant’s endogenous defense machinery by modulating key metabolic fluxes in the phenylpropanoid pathway, thereby inducing a state of heightened resistance that is effective across diverse crop-pathogen systems. The compounds of the invention are designed for agricultural use, compatible with existing application methods and formulation technologies, and offer a sustainable alternative to conventional chemical nematicides, fungicides, and bactericides.

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

- describe plant pathogens

Plant pathogens represent a persistent and escalating threat to global food security, causing substantial yield losses across major agricultural systems. These include a wide array of organisms capable of infecting roots, stems, leaves, and reproductive tissues of cultivated plants, leading to reduced productivity, diminished crop quality, and in severe cases, complete crop failure. Among the most destructive are plant-parasitic nematodes, fungal pathogens, bacterial pathogens, and oomycetes, each employing distinct mechanisms to colonize host tissues, extract nutrients, and suppress host defenses. Their combined impact results in annual economic losses estimated in the hundreds of billions of dollars, with many pathogens evolving resistance to conventional control measures.

- describe bacteria

Phytopathogenic bacteria are prokaryotic microorganisms that invade plant tissues through natural openings or wounds, often secreting effector proteins and cell wall-degrading enzymes to facilitate colonization. Notable examples include Pseudomonas syringae, Xanthomonas spp., Ralstonia solanacearum, and Agrobacterium tumefaciens, which cause diseases such as bacterial speck, bacterial wilt, and crown gall. These pathogens are particularly challenging to manage due to their rapid reproduction, ability to persist in soil and plant debris, and capacity to overcome host resistance through genetic adaptation.

- describe fungi

Fungal pathogens are eukaryotic organisms that colonize plant surfaces and penetrate internal tissues using specialized structures such as appressoria and hyphae. They include both biotrophic and necrotrophic species, with the former deriving nutrients from living cells and the latter killing host tissue prior to feeding. Major fungal pathogens include Fusarium oxysporum, Botrytis cinerea, Magnaporthe oryzae, and Rhizoctonia solani, which cause vascular wilts, gray mold, rice blast, and damping-off, respectively. Fungal spores are widely dispersed by wind and water, and many species form long-lived resting structures that persist in soil for years, complicating eradication efforts.

- describe nematodes

Plant-parasitic nematodes are microscopic, unsegmented roundworms that feed on plant roots, stems, or other tissues, disrupting nutrient and water transport and inducing the formation of specialized feeding sites. The most economically significant genera include Meloidogyne (root-knot nematodes), Heterodera and Globodera (cyst nematodes), and Pratylenchus (root-lesion nematodes). These organisms are ubiquitous in agricultural soils and are particularly damaging in monoculture systems. Their sedentary lifestyle and ability to evade host immune recognition make them exceptionally difficult to control with conventional methods.

- describe plant disease symptoms

Symptoms of plant pathogen infection vary depending on the pathogen type and host species but commonly include stunted growth, chlorosis, necrosis, wilting, galls, root rot, and reduced yield. Root-knot nematodes induce characteristic galls on roots, while cyst nematodes lead to swollen root tips and reduced root mass. Fungal pathogens often manifest as leaf spots, blights, or vascular discoloration, whereas bacterial pathogens may cause water-soaked lesions, oozing exudates, or systemic wilting. These symptoms frequently result from a combination of direct tissue damage, nutrient diversion, and the plant’s own defense responses, which can be energetically costly and lead to growth suppression.

- describe pathogenic bacteria

Pathogenic bacteria colonize plant tissues by exploiting natural openings such as stomata or wounds, and often produce extracellular enzymes and toxins that degrade cell walls and interfere with host signaling. Their infection cycles are typically rapid, with some species capable of completing multiple generations within a single growing season. Many are seed-borne or soil-borne, and their spread is facilitated by irrigation, machinery, and human activity. Control is further complicated by the limited availability of effective bactericides and the absence of resistant cultivars for many important crops.

- describe plant-pathogenic fungi

Plant-pathogenic fungi are among the most pervasive and destructive agents of crop disease. They secrete a diverse array of virulence factors, including cell wall-degrading enzymes, toxins, and effector proteins that manipulate host physiology. Many fungi form complex life cycles involving multiple spore types and host stages, enabling persistence and adaptation. Their ability to colonize both living and dead tissue allows them to survive in crop residues and soil organic matter, making crop rotation and sanitation only partially effective. Resistance breeding has yielded limited success due to the genetic complexity of fungal pathogenicity and the rapid evolution of avirulence gene variants.

- describe nematode lifecycle stages

The lifecycle of plant-parasitic nematodes typically includes an egg, four juvenile stages (J1–J4), and an adult. The second-stage juvenile (J2) is the primary infective stage, emerging from the egg and migrating through the soil toward host roots in response to chemical cues. Upon penetration, the J2 induces the formation of a feeding site, molts into J3 and J4 stages, and matures into an adult. Sedentary nematodes such as Meloidogyne and Heterodera become immobile after establishing feeding sites, while migratory species such as Pratylenchus continue to move and feed within root tissues. The entire lifecycle can be completed in as little as three weeks under favorable conditions, enabling rapid population buildup.

- describe plant parasitic nematodes

Plant-parasitic nematodes are obligate biotrophs that rely entirely on living plant tissue for nutrition and reproduction. They are classified as either ectoparasites, which feed externally with their stylet, or endoparasites, which enter the root and establish feeding sites. Sedentary endoparasites, including root-knot and cyst nematodes, induce the formation of giant cells or syncytia, which serve as nutrient sinks. These feeding sites are metabolically reprogrammed by nematode effectors to suppress host defenses and redirect resources, making them central to the success of the infection. The economic importance of these nematodes stems from their broad host range, high reproductive rates, and resilience to environmental stress.

- describe biotrophs and necrotrophs

Biotrophic pathogens derive nutrients from living host cells without immediately killing them, relying on sophisticated molecular manipulation to suppress host immunity and maintain cell viability. Necrotrophic pathogens, in contrast, kill host tissue through the secretion of toxins and cell wall-degrading enzymes, then feed on the resulting dead material. Many plant pathogens exhibit intermediate or facultative lifestyles, but the distinction is critical for understanding disease mechanisms and designing control strategies. Biotrophs such as cyst nematodes and powdery mildews are often more difficult to control because they actively evade immune recognition, whereas necrotrophs such as Botrytis and Fusarium may be more susceptible to compounds that enhance host defense responses.

- describe current control strategies

Current strategies for managing plant pathogens include chemical fumigants, nematicides, fungicides, and bactericides; cultural practices such as crop rotation, sanitation, and resistant varieties; and biological control agents. Chemical controls remain dominant in commercial agriculture but are increasingly restricted due to environmental toxicity, human health risks, and the development of resistance. Cultural practices are often labor-intensive and inconsistently effective, while biological control agents are limited by environmental stability and inconsistent performance. Resistant cultivars are the most sustainable option but are available for only a subset of pathogens and are frequently overcome by evolving pathogen strains.

- describe limitations of current control strategies

The limitations of current control strategies are profound and multifaceted. Chemical agents often lack specificity, harming non-target organisms and contaminating soil and water systems. Resistance to these agents is widespread and rapidly evolving, rendering many products obsolete within a few years. Cultural practices are constrained by land use, economic feasibility, and the polyphagous nature of many pathogens. Biological control agents are frequently unreliable under field conditions due to environmental variability and poor colonization. Resistant cultivars are often narrow in spectrum, susceptible to multiple pathogens, and subject to breakdown under high pathogen pressure. There is a critical need for novel, broadly effective, environmentally benign approaches that enhance the plant’s innate immune capacity without directly targeting the pathogen.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

- introduce compounds and methods for plant immunity

The present invention introduces a novel class of small molecule compounds and methods for enhancing plant immunity against phytopathogens by specifically inhibiting the enzyme cinnamate-4-hydroxylase (C4H), a key regulator in the phenylpropanoid pathway. Unlike conventional antimicrobial agents, these compounds do not directly kill or inhibit pathogens but instead trigger a state of heightened plant resistance by altering metabolic fluxes within the host. This leads to the accumulation of pathway intermediates and downstream defense metabolites that collectively impede pathogen establishment and reproduction. The invention provides a sustainable, resistance-breaking strategy applicable across a wide range of crops and pathogens.

- describe method of inhibiting cinnamate-4-hydroxylase

The method of the invention involves the exogenous application of a C4H inhibitor to a plant or its growing medium, thereby reducing the conversion of trans-cinnamic acid to para-coumaric acid. This inhibition results in the accumulation of trans-cinnamic acid and its conjugates, which are shown to interfere with the ability of pathogens to establish feeding sites, migrate through tissues, or suppress host defenses. The inhibition is achieved through direct binding to the active site of C4H, competitively or non-competitively, and is reversible under physiological conditions. The method does not require genetic modification of the plant and is compatible with conventional agricultural practices.

- describe exogenous compound application

The compounds of the invention are applied exogenously to the plant or its environment through methods including foliar spray, seed coating, soil drench, or incorporation into planting media. Application may occur prior to or at the time of pathogen exposure, and in some embodiments, multiple applications are used to maintain effective concentrations throughout critical infection windows. The compounds are absorbed by roots or foliage and translocated within the plant, achieving systemic distribution to sites of pathogen attack. The timing and dosage are optimized to induce resistance without impairing plant growth or development.

- describe small molecule characteristics

The compounds of the invention are low molecular weight organic molecules, typically less than 500 Daltons, with sufficient solubility in aqueous and organic solvents to enable formulation and uptake. They are stable under ambient storage conditions and exhibit low volatility, minimizing environmental loss. Their chemical structure is designed for high specificity toward C4H, with minimal off-target effects on other plant enzymes or microbial metabolism. They are non-phytotoxic at effective concentrations and do not accumulate to harmful levels in edible tissues.

- describe aromatic carboxylic acid or benzoic acid derivative

The compounds of the invention are derivatives of aromatic carboxylic acids, particularly benzoic acid analogs, featuring a carboxylic acid group directly attached to an aromatic ring. These derivatives are selected for their ability to mimic the structure of trans-cinnamic acid, the natural substrate of C4H, while resisting enzymatic conversion. The aromatic ring may be substituted with halogens, alkyl groups, alkoxy groups, or other electron-withdrawing or electron-donating moieties that enhance binding affinity and metabolic stability.

- describe benzodioxole comprising carboxylic acid group

In specific embodiments, the compounds contain a benzodioxole ring system fused to the aromatic core and bearing a carboxylic acid substituent. This structural motif enhances metabolic resistance to degradation and improves membrane permeability, facilitating uptake and translocation within the plant. The benzodioxole group also contributes to the compound’s ability to bind C4H with high affinity, as demonstrated by enzyme kinetics and structural modeling studies.

- describe compound formulas

The compounds of the invention are represented by general formulas I, II, and III, wherein the aromatic ring is substituted at specific positions with functional groups that modulate inhibitory activity, bioavailability, and stability. Formula I encompasses benzoic acid derivatives with halogen substitution at the ortho or para position. Formula II includes benzodioxole-containing analogs with alkynyl or alkyl extensions. Formula III describes substituted phenylpropenoic acids with modified side chains that enhance C4H binding without being substrates for the enzyme.

- describe use of compounds for preventing infection

The compounds of the invention are used to prevent infection by pathogens prior to their establishment in plant tissues. When applied before pathogen exposure, they induce a primed state of defense in which the plant responds more rapidly and robustly upon challenge. This prophylactic effect is particularly effective against sedentary nematodes and biotrophic fungi, which rely on prolonged interactions with living cells to establish infection.

- describe use of compounds for increasing plant resistance

The compounds increase plant resistance by altering the metabolic landscape of the host, leading to the accumulation of defense-related phenolic compounds and the downregulation of susceptibility factors. This results in impaired migration of nematodes, reduced formation of feeding sites, and decreased fungal sporulation. The enhanced resistance is broad-spectrum, affecting multiple pathogen types, and is maintained over multiple growth stages without requiring repeated application.

- describe composition and formulation of compounds

The compounds are formulated as agrochemical compositions comprising an active ingredient and one or more agriculturally acceptable carriers, surfactants, stabilizers, or adjuvants. Formulations include liquid concentrates, water-dispersible granules, seed coatings, and soil-incorporated pellets. The composition is designed for compatibility with standard application equipment and for optimal uptake and persistence in the rhizosphere or phyllosphere.

- introduce plant defense and immune responses

Plants possess a layered immune system capable of recognizing pathogen-associated molecular patterns and mounting inducible defense responses. These include the production of antimicrobial compounds, reinforcement of cell walls, activation of signaling pathways such as salicylic acid and jasmonic acid, and localized cell death to restrict pathogen spread. The phenylpropanoid pathway is a central component of this system, generating precursors for lignin, flavonoids, and other defense metabolites. Modulation of this pathway can profoundly influence the outcome of plant-pathogen interactions.

- describe compounds' effects on plant growth

The compounds of the invention, when applied at effective concentrations, do not impair plant growth, development, or reproductive capacity. In multiple crop systems, including wheat, rice, tomato, and sugar beet, no significant reduction in shoot height, root length, biomass, or seed yield was observed following treatment. In fact, in some cases, improved plant vigor was noted, likely due to reduced pathogen burden and enhanced metabolic efficiency.

- describe compounds' effects on pathogens

The compounds of the invention exert no direct inhibitory or lethal effects on phytopathogenic organisms when tested in vitro. Nematodes, fungi, and bacteria exposed to compound concentrations equivalent to those used in planta show no reduction in motility, germination, or growth rate. This confirms that the observed disease suppression is mediated solely through the plant’s enhanced defense capacity, not through direct pathogen toxicity.

- introduce cinnamate-4-hydroxylase (C4H) inhibitors

Cinnamate-4-hydroxylase (C4H) is a cytochrome P450-dependent monooxygenase that catalyzes the para-hydroxylation of trans-cinnamic acid to form para-coumaric acid, a critical step in the phenylpropanoid pathway. The compounds of the invention are potent and selective inhibitors of C4H, binding to the enzyme’s active site with high affinity and preventing the conversion of its natural substrate. This inhibition leads to the accumulation of trans-cinnamic acid and its conjugates, which are shown to interfere with pathogen invasion and feeding site formation.

- describe C4H's role in plant defense

C4H serves as a metabolic gatekeeper in the phenylpropanoid pathway, directing flux toward the biosynthesis of lignin, flavonoids, and other defense-related compounds. Its activity is upregulated during pathogen attack, and its suppression has been shown to alter the balance of phenolic metabolites in ways that enhance resistance. Inhibition of C4H does not abolish defense but redirects metabolic resources toward intermediates that are more effective at impeding pathogen progression.

- describe unexpected effects of C4H inhibition

Contrary to conventional expectations that inhibition of a defense-related enzyme would compromise resistance, the inhibition of C4H by the compounds of the invention leads to a significant and reproducible increase in resistance to multiple pathogens. This unexpected effect is attributed to the accumulation of trans-cinnamic acid and its derivatives, which act as signaling molecules or direct inhibitors of pathogen behavior, and to the compensatory upregulation of other defense genes, including those involved in salicylic acid biosynthesis.

- introduce method of enhancing plant immunity

The method of enhancing plant immunity comprises applying to a plant or its environment an effective amount of a C4H inhibitor, thereby inducing a state of systemic acquired resistance that is effective against nematodes, fungi, bacteria, and oomycetes. The method does not require prior pathogen exposure and can be applied prophylactically or at early stages of infection. The resistance conferred is broad-spectrum, durable, and independent of known resistance gene pathways.

- define C4H inhibitor

A C4H inhibitor is defined as a chemical compound that binds to the cinnamate-4-hydroxylase enzyme and reduces its catalytic activity by at least 50% under physiological conditions, as measured by the decrease in para-coumaric acid production from trans-cinnamic acid in enzyme assays. The inhibitor may be competitive, non-competitive, or uncompetitive, and may be reversible or irreversible.

- describe techniques for measuring C4H activity

C4H activity is measured by incubating plant microsomal fractions or recombinant enzyme with trans-cinnamic acid and NADPH, followed by quantification of para-coumaric acid formation using high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) or liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS). Inhibition is calculated as the percentage reduction in product formation relative to untreated controls.

- describe measurement of trans-cinnamic acid conversion

The conversion of trans-cinnamic acid to para-coumaric acid is quantified by extracting plant tissues or enzyme reaction mixtures at defined time points and analyzing them via LC-MS. A decrease in the ratio of para-coumaric acid to trans-cinnamic acid indicates inhibition of C4H. This ratio is used as a biomarker for compound efficacy in both in vitro and in planta assays.

- describe inhibition of C4H enzyme

Inhibition of the C4H enzyme is confirmed by enzyme kinetics studies showing a reduction in Vmax and/or an increase in Km for trans-cinnamic acid in the presence of the inhibitor. Structural modeling and docking simulations demonstrate that the inhibitors occupy the substrate-binding pocket, preventing proper orientation of trans-cinnamic acid for hydroxylation.

- describe accumulation of trans-cinnamic acid conjugates

Upon C4H inhibition, trans-cinnamic acid accumulates and is conjugated to glucose, malonate, or other small molecules, forming stable derivatives such as trans-cinnamic acid glucoside and trans-cinnamic acid malonate. These conjugates are detected in plant tissues via LC-MS and are shown to correlate with enhanced resistance. Their accumulation is not observed in untreated plants or in plants treated with inactive analogs.

- describe compensatory upregulation of C4H gene

Despite inhibition of C4H enzyme activity, transcript levels of the C4H gene are upregulated in response to inhibitor treatment, indicating a feedback mechanism triggered by metabolic imbalance. This upregulation does not restore enzyme function due to the presence of the inhibitor and instead leads to increased expression of other defense-related genes, including those involved in salicylic acid biosynthesis and pathogenesis-related protein production.

- describe primary root elongation assay

The primary root elongation assay is used to assess potential phytotoxicity of the compounds. Seedlings are grown on agar plates containing varying concentrations of the compound, and root length is measured after seven days. No significant reduction in root elongation is observed at concentrations up to 100 µM, confirming the absence of growth-inhibiting effects.

- describe lignification impairment assay

Lignification impairment is assessed by staining plant stems or roots with phloroglucinol-HCl, which binds to lignin and produces a red color. Treated plants show no reduction in lignin deposition compared to controls, indicating that the inhibition of C4H does not compromise structural integrity or mechanical strength.

- introduce in planta assays

In planta assays involve inoculating plants with pathogens after compound application and measuring disease symptoms, pathogen load, or reproduction. These assays are conducted under controlled environmental conditions and include quantification of nematode galls, fungal lesion area, bacterial colony counts, and oomycete sporulation.

- describe use of C4H inhibitor precursors

In certain embodiments, precursor compounds that are metabolized in planta to active C4H inhibitors are applied. These precursors, such as ester or amide derivatives, are more stable during storage and transport and are cleaved by endogenous plant enzymes to release the active inhibitor upon uptake.

The small molecule C4H inhibitors of the invention are synthetic organic compounds with molecular weights between 150 and 450 Da, designed for optimal uptake, translocation, and target binding. They are distinct from natural phenylpropanoids and are not recognized as substrates by C4H, allowing them to act as pure inhibitors without being metabolized.

In a specific embodiment, the invention is applied to rice plants infected with Meloidogyne graminicola, tomato plants infected with Meloidogyne incognita, and sugar beet plants infected with Heterodera schachtii.

- describe application to plant parts

Application is made to roots, stems, leaves, seeds, or soil surrounding the plant. Root application is preferred for nematode control, while foliar application is effective for fungal and bacterial pathogens.

- describe indirect application to soil

Indirect application involves treating the soil with the compound prior to planting, allowing uptake by developing roots. This method is particularly effective for soil-borne pathogens and provides long-lasting protection.

- list suitable application methods

Suitable application methods include spraying, drenching, seed coating, furrow application, and incorporation into irrigation water.

- describe seed coating

Seed coating involves applying a thin layer of the compound to the seed surface, which is absorbed upon germination and translocated to developing roots, providing early protection against soil-borne pathogens.

- describe soil drench application

Soil drench application involves pouring a solution of the compound around the base of the plant or into the planting hole, ensuring direct contact with the root zone and facilitating systemic uptake.

- describe combination with other compounds

The compounds may be combined with fungicides, nematicides, insecticides, or fertilizers in a single application, provided that no antagonistic interactions occur. Synergistic effects are observed with certain plant immunity primers.

- describe method for manufacturing composition

The method for manufacturing the composition involves blending the active compound with carriers, surfactants, and stabilizers under controlled temperature and humidity, followed by homogenization, drying, or granulation as appropriate for the final formulation.

- list manufacturing methods

Manufacturing methods include wet granulation, dry blending, spray drying, fluid bed coating, and emulsification.

- describe customary agrochemical auxiliary agents

Customary agrochemical auxiliary agents include fillers such as kaolin or silica, dispersants such as lignosulfonates, and anti-caking agents such as talc.

- describe examples of the invention

Examples of the invention include the use of 4-iodobenzoic acid to reduce root-knot galls in tomato, the use of 4-trifluoromethylbenzoic acid to suppress cyst nematode reproduction in sugar beet, and the use of 3-(prop-2-yn-1-yloxy)benzodioxole-5-carboxylic acid to reduce bacterial speck in pepper.

## EXAMPLES

- introduce plant material

Plant material used in the examples includes seeds of rice (Oryza sativa), wheat (Triticum aestivum), tomato (Solanum lycopersicum), sugar beet (Beta vulgaris), and carrot (Daucus carota), obtained from commercial sources and grown under controlled environmental conditions.

- describe rice cultivars

Rice cultivars used include Nipponbare (susceptible), Koshihikari (moderately resistant), and IR64 (susceptible), selected for their relevance to Meloidogyne graminicola infection.

- describe sugar beet cultivar

Sugar beet cultivar used is KWS2310, a commercially grown variety susceptible to Heterodera schachtii.

- describe wheat cultivars

Wheat cultivars used include Bobwhite (susceptible), Paragon (moderately resistant), and Cadenza (resistant), selected for their differential response to Pratylenchus neglectus.

- describe tomato cultivar

Tomato cultivar used is Moneymaker, a widely used susceptible line for Meloidogyne incognita infection studies.

- describe carrot cultivar

Carrot cultivar used is Nantes, a standard variety for Pratylenchus penetrans infection assays.

- germinate seeds

Seeds were surface-sterilized with 70% ethanol and 2% sodium hypochlorite, rinsed with sterile water, and germinated on moist filter paper in Petri dishes at 25°C under a 16-hour photoperiod.

- transfer seedlings to SAP tubes

Seedlings were transferred to Sterile Agar Plates (SAP) containing Hoagland’s nutrient solution and grown for 7–14 days until root systems were sufficiently developed for inoculation.

- water seedlings with Hoagland solution

Seedlings were watered every 48 hours with half-strength Hoagland solution to maintain nutrient availability without promoting excessive growth.

- describe nematode cultures

Nematode cultures of Meloidogyne graminicola, Meloidogyne incognita, Heterodera schachtii, and Pratylenchus penetrans were maintained on host plants under greenhouse conditions and periodically harvested for inoculum preparation.

- extract M. graminicola from infected roots

M. graminicola juveniles were extracted from infected rice roots by maceration in water, sieving through 20- and 50-µm sieves, and centrifugation to concentrate juveniles.

- count nematodes in suspension

Nematode suspensions were counted using a dissecting microscope and a Sedgwick-Rafter counting chamber, with concentrations adjusted to 500–1000 juveniles per milliliter.

- inoculate plants with nematodes

Plants were inoculated by adding 10 mL of nematode suspension to the soil around the base of each seedling, ensuring direct contact with root tips.

- extract M. incognita from infected roots

M. incognita eggs and juveniles were extracted from tomato roots by maceration, sieving, and incubation in water to allow hatching, followed by concentration via centrifugation.

- extract H. schachtii from sugar beets

Cysts of H. schachtii were extracted from sugar beet roots by washing, drying, and crushing, followed by sieving and incubation in water to induce hatching of juveniles.

- hatch H. schachtii cysts

Cysts were placed in distilled water at 20°C for 7 days, during which time juveniles emerged and were collected for inoculation.

- inoculate plants with H. schachtii

Plants were inoculated by placing 50 cysts in the soil adjacent to the root zone of each seedling.

- extract P. penetrans from carrot disks

P. penetrans was extracted from carrot disks by placing infected tissue in water for 24 hours, collecting migrating juveniles, and concentrating via centrifugation.

- multiply P. penetrans on carrot disks

Carrot disks were placed in Petri dishes with a suspension of P. penetrans and incubated for 3 weeks to allow reproduction, after which juveniles were harvested for use.

- inoculate plants with P. penetrans

Plants were inoculated by placing 100 juveniles in the soil around the root zone.

- describe fungal cultures

Fungal cultures of Fusarium oxysporum, Botrytis cinerea, and Pythium splendens were maintained on potato dextrose agar and periodically subcultured to maintain viability.

- obtain fungal isolates

Fungal isolates were obtained from infected plant tissues and purified by single-spore isolation on selective media.

- inoculate fungal spores on agar plates

Spores were suspended in sterile water and plated on agar at concentrations of 10⁴ spores per mL to assess germination and growth inhibition.

- harvest fungal spores

Spores were harvested by scraping the surface of mature cultures, filtering through glass wool, and centrifuging to concentrate.

- describe chemical treatments

Chemical treatments involved preparing aqueous solutions of the C4H inhibitors at concentrations of 10 µM, 50 µM, and 100 µM, with controls containing solvent only.

- prepare foliar spray solutions

Foliar spray solutions were prepared by adding 0.05% Tween 20 to the inhibitor solution to enhance leaf adhesion and penetration.

- apply foliar sprays to plants

Plants were sprayed until runoff using a hand-held atomizer, with applications performed 24 hours prior to pathogen inoculation.

- describe seed coatings

Seed coatings were prepared by mixing the compound with a hydroxypropyl methylcellulose binder and applying a thin layer to seeds using a rotating drum.

- apply seed coatings

Coated seeds were air-dried for 24 hours and then planted in sterilized soil.

- describe nematode infection experiments

Nematode infection experiments involved inoculating plants with nematodes 7 days after compound application and harvesting roots 14–21 days later for gall or cyst counting.

- inoculate plants with nematodes

Plants were inoculated by adding 10 mL of nematode suspension to the soil around the root zone.

- harvest and phenotype plants

Plants were harvested, washed, and examined for disease symptoms. Root systems were stained with acid fuchsin to visualize nematode galls and cysts.

- count nematodes and galls

Galls and cysts were counted under a dissecting microscope, and nematode numbers were estimated by extracting juveniles from root tissue.

- describe fungal infection experiments

Fungal infection experiments involved spraying leaves or inoculating roots with fungal spores 24 hours after compound application.

- inoculate fungal spores on plants

Spores were sprayed onto leaves or mixed into soil around roots at a concentration of 10⁵ spores per mL.

- harvest and phenotype plants

Plants were harvested 7–14 days post-inoculation, and lesions were measured using image analysis software.

- count fungal lesions

Lesion number and area were quantified using digital imaging and thresholding software.

- describe nematode toxicity assays

Nematode toxicity assays involved incubating juveniles in compound solutions for 24–72 hours and assessing motility under a microscope.

- incubate nematodes with C4H inhibitors

Juveniles were placed in 96-well plates containing 100 µL of compound solution at concentrations up to 1000 µM and incubated at 25°C.

- count living nematodes

Nematodes were considered alive if they exhibited movement upon gentle stimulation with a needle.

- describe fungal toxicity assay

Fungal toxicity assays involved placing fungal spores on agar plates containing the compound and measuring radial growth over time.

- spot fungal spores on agar plates

Spore suspensions were spotted in the center of agar plates containing the compound at varying concentrations.

- measure fungal colony growth

Colony diameter was measured daily for 7 days using calipers.

- describe Pseudomonas syringae infection experiment

Pseudomonas syringae infection experiments involved infiltrating leaves with a bacterial suspension of 10⁶ CFU/mL and measuring disease progression over 5 days.

- inoculate plants with P. syringae

Bacterial suspension was infiltrated into leaves using a needleless syringe.

- describe embodiment of treating crops with greatest estimated losses

In one embodiment, the compound is applied to rice, tomato, and sugar beet, crops that suffer the greatest estimated global losses due to nematode infection.

- describe embodiment of applying compound to plant parts

In one embodiment, the compound is applied directly to roots via soil drench to target nematodes, and to leaves via foliar spray to target fungi and bacteria.

- describe embodiment of applying compound to soil

In one embodiment, the compound is incorporated into the soil at planting at a rate of 100 g/ha to provide long-term protection against soil-borne pathogens.

- describe embodiment of using compound in combination

In one embodiment, the compound is applied in combination with a low dose of a conventional fungicide to achieve synergistic disease control.

- describe embodiment of manufacturing composition

In one embodiment, the compound is formulated as a water-dispersible granule containing 50% active ingredient, 30% kaolin, 15% lignosulfonate, and 5% silica.
