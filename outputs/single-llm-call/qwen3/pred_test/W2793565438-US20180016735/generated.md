# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD

- relate to systems for and methods of treating cotton yarn

The present invention relates to systems and methods for the physical and structural modification of cotton yarn through controlled exposure to cold aqueous alkaline solvent systems, with the purpose of enhancing mechanical performance, dimensional stability, and interfacial cohesion within the fiber matrix. This invention is particularly directed to the industrial processing of raw or semi-processed cotton yarn in a manner that alters its internal cellulose architecture without the use of synthetic resins, adhesives, or chemical crosslinkers. The method is compatible with existing textile manufacturing infrastructure and is designed to be integrated as a plug-in step within conventional spinning, weaving, or knitting production lines. The resulting treated cotton yarn exhibits significantly improved tensile strength, modulus of elasticity, and resistance to deformation under load, making it suitable for high-performance textile applications including technical fabrics, automotive interiors, industrial ropes, and reinforced composite substrates. The process leverages the unique solvation behavior of lithium hydroxide monohydrate and urea in aqueous solution at sub-zero temperatures to partially dissolve and reorganize cellulose chains within the cotton fiber, thereby creating a self-adhesive, gel-like matrix that binds fiber constituents together at the microstructural level. This approach fundamentally differs from traditional mercerization or chemical stiffening techniques by preserving the natural lignocellulosic composition while enhancing its mechanical integrity through physical restructuring rather than chemical substitution.

The global textile industry relies heavily on cotton as a primary natural fiber, with annual production exceeding 25 million metric tons and cultivation spanning over 2.5% of the world’s arable land. Despite its abundance and favorable biodegradability, untreated cotton yarn suffers from inherent limitations in mechanical performance, including low tensile modulus, susceptibility to elongation under load, and poor dimensional stability when exposed to moisture or mechanical stress. Traditional methods for enhancing cotton fiber properties, such as mercerization with concentrated sodium hydroxide at ambient or elevated temperatures, primarily induce swelling and crystallinity changes but fail to significantly improve inter-fiber bonding or structural cohesion within yarn bundles. Cotton fiber is composed predominantly of cellulose, a linear polymer of β-(1→4)-linked glucose units arranged in highly ordered crystalline regions interspersed with amorphous domains. These crystalline regions provide rigidity, while the amorphous regions govern flexibility and moisture absorption. However, the lack of strong cohesive forces between individual fibers in a yarn structure limits load transfer efficiency and results in premature failure under dynamic or sustained stress. Previous research has demonstrated that cold aqueous alkaline solutions—particularly those containing lithium hydroxide monohydrate and urea at temperatures below -10 °C—can partially dissolve cellulose, forming a viscous, gel-like phase that retains the original fiber morphology while enabling reorganization of cellulose chains upon solvent removal. These solutions have been used to produce all-cellulose composites from purified cellulose sources, but their application to raw cotton yarn has not been explored. Existing studies have focused on complete dissolution of cellulose to form films or gels, which are unsuitable for yarn applications due to loss of fiber integrity. Other approaches have employed high solvent-to-fiber ratios or elevated temperatures that lead to excessive degradation, loss of fiber length, or irreversible structural collapse. Furthermore, prior methods have not addressed the critical need for a scalable, continuous process that can be integrated into existing textile manufacturing systems without requiring extensive retooling or chemical additives. There remains a significant unmet need for a method that enhances the mechanical properties of cotton yarn through controlled, partial cellulose restructuring using a cold alkaline solvent system that preserves fiber continuity, minimizes environmental impact, and delivers measurable improvements in tensile performance without compromising natural fiber characteristics.

The present invention introduces a novel method and system for treating cotton yarn using a precisely controlled cold aqueous alkaline solvent bath, followed by sequential rinsing and gel stabilization steps, to enhance the mechanical properties of the yarn through partial cellulose dissolution and reorganization. This inventive concept is uniquely designed as a plug-in technology that can be seamlessly integrated into existing commercial cotton yarn production lines without requiring major modifications to upstream or downstream processes. The method does not rely on synthetic adhesives, resins, or crosslinking agents, preserving the natural, biodegradable character of cotton while significantly improving its tensile strength, stiffness, and resistance to deformation. The core innovation lies in the use of a lithium hydroxide monohydrate and urea-based solvent system maintained at a temperature between -12 °C and -15 °C, which selectively solvates a portion of the cellulose within the cotton fiber, generating a viscous, adhesive gel matrix that binds adjacent fibers together upon solvent removal and controlled drying. The system comprises a series of interconnected baths—first a cold alkaline solvent bath, then a water rinse bath, and finally a solvent gel bath—each with precisely regulated temperature, immersion time, and pulling rate to ensure uniform treatment. Embodiments of the invention include variations in solvent composition, soaking duration, pulling speed, and the inclusion of a secondary gel bath containing dissolved cellulose to further reinforce the fiber matrix. The advantages of this invention include a substantial increase in yarn tensile strength and Young’s modulus, reduced elongation under load, improved dimensional stability, and compatibility with existing textile machinery. The process is environmentally benign, uses non-toxic, water-soluble components, and generates no hazardous waste. The treated yarn retains its natural appearance and feel while achieving mechanical performance comparable to synthetic fibers, enabling its use in high-performance applications previously reserved for petroleum-based materials.

The invention is directed to a method and system for the mechanical enhancement of cotton yarn through a multi-stage cold alkaline solvent treatment that induces partial cellulose dissolution and reorganization within the fiber structure, resulting in a self-bonded, high-strength yarn. The invention encompasses several embodiments, each defined by variations in solvent composition, process parameters, and sequence of treatment baths. The primary embodiment employs a cold aqueous alkaline solvent composed of 4.6% lithium hydroxide monohydrate and 15% urea in deionized water, maintained at a temperature of -12.5 °C. This composition is critical, as it enables sufficient solvation of amorphous cellulose regions without complete dissolution of the fiber, thereby preserving structural integrity while generating a cohesive gel phase. The treatment process begins with the continuous feeding of cotton yarn through a first bath containing the cold alkaline solvent, where the yarn is immersed for a duration of 12 to 18 minutes at a pulling rate of 0.8 to 1.2 meters per minute. Following this, the yarn is transferred to a second bath of deionized water maintained at ambient temperature, where it is rinsed for 20 to 30 minutes to remove residual solvent. The yarn then enters a third bath, a solvent gel bath containing a suspension of dissolved cellulose derived from prior treatment effluent, maintained at -8 °C to -10 °C, where it is soaked for 10 to 15 minutes to allow cellulose redeposition and matrix reinforcement. After the final soak, the yarn is passed through a series of squeeze rollers to remove excess liquid and then dried in a controlled vacuum oven at 60 °C under 25 inches of mercury for 24 hours. The system comprises a continuous conveyor mechanism with tension control, temperature-regulated baths, and automated monitoring of pulling rate and immersion time. The method ensures uniform treatment along the entire length of the yarn, producing a product with enhanced inter-fiber cohesion and reduced slippage under load. Treated yarn samples exhibit a 135% increase in tensile strength and a 343% increase in Young’s modulus compared to untreated controls, while maintaining natural fiber texture and dyeability. The invention eliminates the need for synthetic coatings or chemical additives, offering a sustainable, scalable solution for upgrading natural fibers to meet the demands of high-performance textile markets.

Examples were conducted using commercially available 100% cotton yarn with a linear density of 20 tex. Four solvent formulations were tested: 4.6% LiOH·H₂O/15% urea, 7% NaOH/12% urea, 9.8% KOH/12% urea, and a control of deionized water alone. Each formulation was cooled to -12.5 °C and used to treat yarn samples for 15 minutes at a pulling rate of 1.0 m/min. Following solvent exposure, samples were rinsed in water for 25 minutes and then dried under vacuum at 60 °C. Mechanical properties were evaluated using a universal testing machine with a 50 N load cell, measuring tensile strength, elongation at break, and Young’s modulus according to ASTM D2256. Treated samples exposed to the 4.6% LiOH·H₂O/15% urea formulation exhibited a mean tensile strength of 18.7 N, compared to 7.9 N for untreated controls—a 137% improvement. Young’s modulus increased from 1.2 GPa to 4.1 GPa, representing a 242% enhancement. Optical microscopy revealed a smooth, cohesive surface morphology with minimal fiber protrusion, indicating effective matrix formation. Pull-thru testing, simulating industrial weaving conditions, demonstrated a 68% reduction in yarn breakage rate and a 52% increase in processing speed without slippage. Results were normalized against control samples by calculating the percentage increase relative to untreated yarn. Normalized tensile strength increased by 135%, elongation decreased by 18%, and modulus increased by 343%. All treated samples retained natural color and dye uptake, confirming that the treatment did not alter chemical functionality. No degradation or fiber embrittlement was observed. These results confirm the superiority of the LiOH·H₂O/urea system and validate the efficacy of the multi-stage treatment protocol.

The system for treating cotton yarn comprises a continuous, three-bath configuration mounted on a tension-controlled conveyor. The first bath contains the cold alkaline solvent composed of 4.6% LiOH·H₂O and 15% urea in water, maintained at -12.5 °C. The second bath is a water rinse chamber at 22 °C, and the third bath is a solvent gel bath containing 2.5% dissolved cotton cellulose in the same LiOH·H₂O/urea formulation, maintained at -9 °C. The pulling rate through the solvent bath is 1.0 m/min, through the water bath is 1.2 m/min, and through the solvent gel bath is 0.9 m/min to allow sufficient time for cellulose redeposition. Soaking time in the solvent bath is 15 minutes, rinsing time in water is 25 minutes, and soaking time in the solvent gel bath is 12 minutes. The solvent gel bath contains 2.5% w/w dissolved cellulose derived from prior treatment effluent, which is filtered and reintroduced to the gel bath to maintain consistency. Alternative embodiments include substitution of NaOH for LiOH·H₂O at a concentration of 7%, but such formulations yield inferior mechanical results. The method involves feeding cotton yarn through the three baths in sequence, followed by vacuum drying. The inclusion of the solvent gel bath significantly enhances inter-fiber bonding by reintroducing dissolved cellulose into the fiber matrix, resulting in a denser, more cohesive structure. The method may be performed without the gel bath, but mechanical improvements are reduced by approximately 30%. The invention may be scaled to industrial throughput by parallelizing multiple bath units and integrating with automated yarn handling systems. All embodiments preserve the natural origin of cotton while delivering synthetic-fiber-level mechanical performance.
