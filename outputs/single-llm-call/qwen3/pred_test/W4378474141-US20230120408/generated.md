# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

### 1. Field of the Invention

The present invention relates to the field of superconducting thin film materials and devices, specifically to wafer-scale magnesium diboride (MgB₂) films with enhanced superconducting properties suitable for integration into microwave and quantum electronic devices. The invention encompasses the synthesis, characterization, and application of ultra-smooth, high-critical-temperature MgB₂ films deposited on commercially viable silicon substrates, with precise control over stoichiometry, resistivity, surface morphology, and thermal stability. These films are engineered for use in high-frequency resonators, kinetic inductance detectors, parametric amplifiers, and quantum circuits where low dissipation, tunable kinetic inductance, and operational stability above 20 K are critical performance metrics.

### 2. Description of the Related Art

Conventional methods for fabricating magnesium diboride thin films have been hindered by the inherent volatility of magnesium at elevated temperatures, leading to stoichiometric deviations, poor film uniformity, and degraded superconducting transition temperatures. Prior approaches relying on in-situ sputtering at high substrate temperatures resulted in oxidized surfaces, small grain sizes, and transition temperatures below 20 K due to magnesium evaporation and contamination from ambient oxygen. Post-deposition annealing techniques attempted to mitigate these issues but often introduced severe surface roughness exceeding 10 nm root-mean-square, rendering the films unsuitable for nanoscale device patterning. Alternative substrate materials such as sapphire or silicon carbide offered improved lattice matching but introduced new challenges, including oxygen diffusion from the substrate into the MgB₂ layer, interfacial reaction layers, and prohibitive cost for large-area manufacturing. Furthermore, prior art methods lacked the ability to independently tune resistivity and critical temperature over a wide range without introducing impurities or relying on gas-phase reactive sputtering, which suffers from hysteresis and nonuniformity in stoichiometric control. The inability to produce smooth, large-area, high-Tc MgB₂ films on silicon substrates with reproducible electrical properties has prevented the widespread adoption of MgB₂ in practical superconducting electronics, despite its theoretical advantages over conventional materials such as niobium or niobium titanium nitride.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

- introduce wafer with superconducting MgB2 film  
A superconducting wafer is disclosed comprising a silicon substrate, a thin silicon nitride buffer layer deposited thereon, and a uniform magnesium diboride thin film formed atop the buffer layer, wherein the magnesium diboride film exhibits a critical transition temperature exceeding 30 kelvins and a surface roughness of less than 0.5 nanometers root-mean-square across a wafer diameter of at least 100 millimeters.

- specify sheet resistance and thickness uniformity  
The magnesium diboride film demonstrates a sheet resistance uniformity of less than five percent across the entire wafer surface, with thickness variation of no more than three percent, enabling consistent device performance across multiple integrated circuits fabricated on the same substrate.

- specify surface roughness  
The surface of the magnesium diboride film is characterized by an atomic-scale smoothness, with a root-mean-square roughness of less than 0.48 nanometers, as measured by atomic force microscopy over a 10-micrometer by 10-micrometer scan area, permitting high-resolution photolithographic patterning and minimizing parasitic losses in microwave resonators.

- specify resistivity range  
The resistivity of the magnesium diboride film, following thermal annealing, ranges from 25 to 100 microohm-centimeters, allowing for precise tuning of kinetic inductance values suitable for high-frequency quantum devices without the need for compositional doping or alloying.

- introduce wafer with superconducting MgB2 film (alternative)  
An alternative embodiment of the wafer comprises a sapphire substrate with a hexagonal crystal structure, upon which the magnesium diboride film is deposited, wherein the film exhibits a critical transition temperature exceeding 35 kelvins and a grain size exceeding 500 nanometers, resulting in enhanced critical current density despite increased surface roughness.

- specify critical temperature  
The magnesium diboride film exhibits a zero-resistance critical transition temperature of at least 30 kelvins, with certain embodiments achieving a transition temperature as high as 37.2 kelvins, representing the highest reported value for any sputter-deposited MgB₂ film on a silicon-based platform.

- introduce capping layer  
A capping layer of elemental boron is disposed directly atop the magnesium-boron precursor film prior to thermal annealing, wherein the boron layer is sufficiently thin to permit diffusion of magnesium through it during annealing while preventing magnesium evaporation and forming a viscous, crack-free oxide cap that suppresses surface degradation.

- introduce method of making MgB2 film  
A method is disclosed for fabricating the magnesium diboride film by co-sputtering magnesium and boron at room temperature onto a silicon nitride-buffered silicon substrate, followed by thermal annealing under controlled vacuum conditions to induce complete reaction and crystallization into a stoichiometric MgB₂ phase.

- tune Mg to B ratio  
The atomic ratio of magnesium to boron in the as-deposited precursor film is deliberately varied between 0.8:1 and 1.2:1 to linearly tune the post-annealed resistivity of the magnesium diboride film, thereby enabling precise control over kinetic inductance without altering film thickness or deposition geometry.

- select deposition and annealing conditions  
Deposition is performed using radio-frequency magnetron sputtering with a substrate bias of 15 watts to reduce surface roughness, while annealing is conducted in a rapid thermal processor at temperatures between 550 and 650 degrees Celsius for durations ranging from 1 to 10 minutes under a vacuum pressure below 10⁻⁶ torr.

- specify film properties  
The resulting magnesium diboride film exhibits a combination of high critical temperature, low surface roughness, high sheet resistance uniformity, and tunable resistivity, enabling its direct integration into microwave and quantum devices without requiring post-annealing surface polishing or chemical etching.

- specify deposition methods  
The magnesium and boron layers are deposited via co-sputtering using elemental targets, or alternatively, as alternating monolayers of magnesium and boron, with each layer having a thickness between 0.5 and 5 nanometers, to promote interfacial diffusion and grain growth during subsequent annealing.

- specify annealing conditions  
The annealing process employs a ramp rate of one to thirty-three degrees Celsius per second, with a hold time at peak temperature sufficient to ensure complete reaction of magnesium and boron while avoiding magnesium evaporation, and is followed by controlled cooling at a rate not exceeding five degrees Celsius per second to minimize thermal stress and cracking.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

- introduce MgB2 film synthesis  
The synthesis of the magnesium diboride thin film begins with the preparation of a high-resistivity silicon wafer, which is cleaned and loaded into a vacuum deposition chamber. A thin buffer layer of silicon nitride, deposited by low-pressure chemical vapor deposition, is formed on the silicon surface to prevent interdiffusion of silicon into the superconducting layer. The buffer layer is maintained at a thickness between 20 and 150 nanometers to ensure complete coverage without introducing excessive strain or optical absorption.

### Technical Description

- deposit magnesium and boron on a substrate  
Magnesium and boron are simultaneously sputtered onto the silicon nitride buffer layer using separate elemental targets in a single deposition run, with the substrate maintained at room temperature throughout the process to prevent premature reaction or magnesium evaporation. The sputtering is conducted in a low-pressure argon atmosphere at approximately 1 to 2 millitorr, with the boron target powered at a fixed radio-frequency level of 580 watts and the magnesium target power varied between 50 and 250 watts to control the Mg:B stoichiometry.

- describe co-sputtering method and apparatus  
The co-sputtering apparatus employs magnetron sources with independently controlled power supplies and shutter mechanisms to ensure precise temporal and spatial control over the deposition of each element. A substrate bias of 15 watts is applied during deposition to enhance adhesion and reduce surface roughness without inducing excessive ion bombardment that would compromise stoichiometry.

- select amount of magnesium and boron for reaction  
The total thickness of the magnesium-boron precursor film is maintained between 30 and 80 nanometers, with the relative sputtering durations adjusted to achieve the desired atomic ratio, which is confirmed post-deposition using X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy and energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy.

- tune Mg/B ratio for desired resistivity and superconducting transition temperature  
By increasing the boron content relative to magnesium, the resistivity of the as-deposited composite film increases linearly, and this trend is preserved after annealing, enabling the post-annealed MgB₂ film to exhibit resistivity values ranging from 25 to 100 microohm-centimeters, directly correlating with the kinetic inductance of the final device.

- deposit boron and magnesium as alternating layers  
In an alternative embodiment, magnesium and boron are deposited as alternating monolayers, each approximately one to five nanometers thick, to create a multilayered precursor that promotes grain boundary formation and large crystallite growth during annealing, resulting in films with critical temperatures exceeding 35 kelvins.

- describe effect of layer thickness on film properties  
Thinner individual layers enhance interfacial mixing during annealing, promoting uniform grain growth and reducing defect density, while thicker layers lead to larger grain sizes at the expense of increased surface roughness, allowing for trade-offs between transition temperature and surface smoothness depending on device requirements.

- deposit capping layer on film  
Immediately following deposition of the magnesium-boron composite, a thin layer of elemental boron, approximately 10 to 50 nanometers thick, is deposited atop the film to serve as a protective cap during subsequent thermal processing.

- describe purpose of capping layer  
The boron capping layer prevents the evaporation of magnesium during annealing by forming a transient, viscous boron oxide layer upon exposure to residual oxygen, which seals the surface and inhibits magnesium loss while permitting diffusion of magnesium atoms upward to react with underlying boron.

- select capping layer material and thickness  
The capping layer is composed of elemental boron due to its high melting point, chemical inertness at annealing temperatures, and ability to form a non-cracking, self-healing oxide layer. Thicknesses below 10 nanometers are insufficient to prevent magnesium loss, while thicknesses exceeding 100 nanometers impede diffusion kinetics and increase residual stress.

- thermally anneal capped film  
The capped film is transferred to a rapid thermal processor and heated under high vacuum conditions to a peak temperature between 550 and 650 degrees Celsius, held for a duration of one to ten minutes, and then cooled at a controlled rate to preserve crystalline integrity.

- describe reaction of magnesium and boron during annealing  
During annealing, magnesium atoms diffuse through the boron cap and react with boron atoms in the underlying layer to form crystalline MgB₂, with the reaction proceeding via solid-state diffusion and nucleation at grain boundaries, resulting in a polycrystalline film with minimal voids or secondary phases.

- select annealing temperature and hold time  
Annealing temperatures above 650 degrees Celsius induce magnesium evaporation and surface roughening, while temperatures below 550 degrees Celsius result in incomplete reaction and low transition temperatures. Hold times shorter than one minute yield incomplete crystallization, while times exceeding ten minutes lead to excessive grain growth and increased roughness.

- describe effect of annealing conditions on film properties  
Optimized annealing conditions yield films with critical transition temperatures exceeding 30 kelvins, resistivity values within the target range, and surface roughness below 0.5 nanometers, demonstrating a direct correlation between thermal history and superconducting performance.

- cool capped film  
The film is cooled slowly at a rate of no more than five degrees Celsius per second to minimize thermal gradients and prevent cracking or delamination of the capping layer or underlying film.

- describe resulting composition of matter  
The resulting composition of matter is a polycrystalline magnesium diboride thin film with a hexagonal crystal structure, stoichiometric magnesium-to-boron ratio near 1:2, and minimal presence of secondary phases such as MgB₄ or MgB₇, which are identified as negligible due to their dielectric nature and absence in X-ray diffraction spectra.

- illustrate composition of matter with FIGS. 2A-2B  
Figures 2A and 2B illustrate the cross-sectional transmission electron microscopy images of the MgB₂ film, showing a sharp interface with the silicon nitride buffer layer, uniform grain distribution, and absence of interdiffusion or oxidation at the substrate interface.

- describe properties of MgB2 film  
The magnesium diboride film exhibits a zero-resistance transition temperature of up to 37.2 kelvins, a critical current density exceeding 10⁶ amperes per square centimeter, and a kinetic inductance ranging from 5 to 50 picohenries per square, depending on resistivity and film thickness.

- illustrate effect of capping layer and thermal annealing on superconductive properties  
The inclusion of the boron capping layer and optimized annealing protocol increases the critical transition temperature by more than 20 kelvins compared to uncapped films, while simultaneously reducing surface roughness by a factor of five, enabling device-level performance previously unattainable.

- illustrate uniformity of film thickness and sheet resistance  
Measurements across 100-millimeter wafers demonstrate thickness variations of less than three percent and sheet resistance variations of less than five percent, confirming the scalability and reproducibility of the process for industrial manufacturing.

- illustrate tunability of resistivity using Mg:B ratio  
A linear relationship is observed between the Mg:B ratio in the precursor and the resistivity of the final film, with a 10 percent increase in boron content yielding a 20 percent increase in resistivity, enabling precise design of kinetic inductance for circuit applications.

- illustrate surface roughness of film  
Atomic force microscopy measurements confirm a root-mean-square roughness of 0.34 to 0.47 nanometers across multiple samples, enabling sub-micron photolithographic patterning without edge roughness or pattern distortion.

- illustrate superconductive properties of film  
Four-point probe measurements and microwave resonator characterization confirm low surface resistance, high quality factors exceeding 10⁴ at 4.5 kelvins, and consistent performance across multiple devices fabricated on the same wafer.

- describe example films and compositions of matter  
Example 1: A film deposited on silicon nitride-buffered silicon with a Mg:B ratio of 1.0:1.0, annealed at 600°C for 5 minutes, exhibits a Tc of 32 K, sheet resistance of 38 μΩ·cm, and surface roughness of 0.45 nm.  
Example 2: A film with a Mg:B ratio of 0.8:1.0, annealed at 600°C for 3 minutes, yields a resistivity of 110 μΩ·cm and Tc of 28 K.  
Example 3: A film with alternating Mg/B layers, annealed at 630°C for 8 minutes, achieves a Tc of 37.2 K with roughness of 1.2 nm.  
Example 4: A film with Mg:B ratio of 1.0:1.1, annealed at 580°C for 7 minutes, exhibits resistivity of 35 μΩ·cm and Tc of 31 K.  
Example 5: A film with Mg:B ratio of 0.9:1.0, annealed at 620°C for 6 minutes, achieves a Tc of 30.5 K and resistivity of 42 μΩ·cm.

- describe method of making a film  
The method comprises depositing magnesium and boron via co-sputtering at room temperature, depositing a boron capping layer, thermally annealing the capped film under vacuum, and cooling the film at a controlled rate to produce a high-quality MgB₂ film.

- describe depositing magnesium and boron  
Magnesium and boron are co-sputtered using elemental targets with independent power controls, with the substrate biased at 15 W to reduce surface roughness and enhance film density.

- describe depositing capping layer  
A layer of elemental boron, 10 to 50 nanometers thick, is deposited immediately after the magnesium-boron composite to form a protective cap.

- describe thermal annealing  
The capped film is annealed in a rapid thermal processor at 550–650°C for 1–10 minutes under vacuum pressure below 10⁻⁶ torr.

- describe cooling  
The film is cooled at a rate of no more than five degrees Celsius per second to prevent thermal stress-induced cracking.

- describe tuning Mg:B ratio  
The atomic ratio of magnesium to boron is adjusted by varying the sputtering power of the magnesium target while maintaining constant boron sputtering power.

- describe selecting film thickness and surface area  
Film thickness is selected between 30 and 80 nanometers to balance kinetic inductance and fabrication yield, with wafer surface areas up to 150 millimeters in diameter being uniformly processed.

- describe selecting capping layer thickness and material  
The capping layer is composed of elemental boron with a thickness of 10 to 50 nanometers to prevent magnesium evaporation while permitting diffusion.

- describe selecting annealing temperature and hold time  
Annealing temperature is selected between 550 and 650°C, with hold time between 1 and 10 minutes, based on desired transition temperature and surface roughness.

- describe co-depositing boron and magnesium  
Magnesium and boron are co-deposited simultaneously using separate magnetron sources with synchronized shutter control.

- describe depositing alternating layers of boron and magnesium  
Alternating layers of magnesium and boron, each 0.5 to 5 nanometers thick, are deposited sequentially to promote interfacial diffusion and large-grain growth.

- describe controlling hold time at annealing temperature  
Hold time is adjusted to ensure complete reaction without excessive grain growth, with longer times increasing transition temperature at the expense of roughness.

- describe removing capping layer  
The boron capping layer is removed after annealing using an argon ion mill, with the process terminated prior to etching into the MgB₂ layer to preserve film integrity.

- describe manufacturing device using film  
The MgB₂ film is patterned using photolithography and etching to form microwave resonators, kinetic inductance qubits, or transmission lines, with passivation layers of titanium-gold or aluminum nitride applied to protect against oxidation during device fabrication.

## CONCLUSION

- conclude description of preferred embodiment  
The preferred embodiment of the invention comprises a silicon-based wafer with a silicon nitride buffer layer, a magnesium diboride thin film deposited via co-sputtering and annealed under a boron capping layer, exhibiting a critical transition temperature exceeding 30 kelvins, surface roughness below 0.5 nanometers, and resistivity tunable between 25 and 100 microohm-centimeters, enabling the fabrication of high-performance superconducting devices on a commercially scalable platform.
