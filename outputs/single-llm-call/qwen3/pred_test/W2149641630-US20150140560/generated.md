## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates to a microviscometer based on asynchronous magnetic bead rotation for the detection and quantification of analytes through measurement of changes in fluid viscosity. Specifically, the invention pertains to a device and method for determining the physical state of biological macromolecules, including nucleic acids, proteins, carbohydrates, and lipids, by monitoring the rotational dynamics of paramagnetic particles suspended in a liquid medium under the influence of a rotating magnetic field. The device operates at microscopic scales, requiring only minute sample volumes, and provides real-time, label-free readouts of molecular transformations such as polymerization, cleavage, ligation, and hydrolysis. The microviscometer is designed for integration into diagnostic platforms, laboratory automation systems, and point-of-care devices where rapid, cost-effective, and instrument-minimal analysis of molecular reactions is required. The invention encompasses both the hardware configuration and the analytical methodology by which rotational period changes are correlated with solution viscosity and, by extension, analyte concentration or molecular weight.

## BACKGROUND

Sensitive detection of DNA has become a cornerstone of modern diagnostics, enabling early identification of infectious agents, genetic mutations, and cancer biomarkers. Conventional methods rely heavily on fluorescent labeling, enzymatic amplification, or hybridization-based signal generation, all of which require costly reagents, complex instrumentation, and extensive sample preparation. These limitations hinder deployment in resource-limited settings and introduce delays that compromise real-time monitoring capabilities. While alternative approaches such as electrochemical sensing and surface plasmon resonance have been explored, they often lack the broad applicability, simplicity, and quantitative robustness of fluorescence-based techniques. A fundamentally different strategy involves measuring the physical property of solution viscosity, which is inherently sensitive to the size and concentration of macromolecules such as double-stranded DNA. As DNA strands are cleaved or elongated, the solution’s resistance to flow changes predictably, providing a direct, label-free indicator of molecular state. However, prior methods for measuring viscosity in small volumes have been slow, impractical, or incompatible with dynamic reactions. Conventional viscometers such as Ubbelohde or Ostwald tubes require milliliter-scale volumes, lengthy equilibration times, and are unsuitable for continuous monitoring. The present invention overcomes these limitations by introducing a microviscometer based on asynchronous magnetic bead rotation, which enables rapid, sensitive, and real-time viscosity measurements in volumes as small as tens of picoliters. The principle relies on the observation that when a paramagnetic bead is subjected to a rotating magnetic field above a critical frequency, its rotational motion becomes asynchronous with the driving field due to viscous drag. The period of this asynchronous rotation is linearly proportional to the viscosity of the surrounding medium, allowing precise inference of molecular changes without chemical modification or external labels.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The invention introduces a novel method for detecting changes in the physical state of analytes through the measurement of solution viscosity using asynchronous magnetic bead rotation, or AMBR. The core principle of AMBR is that the rotational period of a paramagnetic bead in a rotating magnetic field, when operating in the asynchronous regime, is directly proportional to the viscosity of the surrounding fluid. This relationship enables the detection of molecular transformations such as polymerization, cleavage, ligation, and hydrolysis by monitoring the resulting viscosity changes in real time. A physical change in state occurs when the molecular weight or length of a polymer, such as DNA or protein, is altered, thereby modifying the solution’s resistance to flow. A chemical change in state is inferred when enzymatic or catalytic reactions modify the molecular structure of an analyte, leading to a measurable shift in viscosity. The invention encompasses a wide range of analytes, including nucleic acids, proteins, carbohydrates, lipids, and whole cells, each of which can be monitored through their impact on solution rheology. The method permits continuous, real-time measurement without the need for sample extraction, washing, or labeling, and is compatible with standard laboratory reaction conditions. Embodiments of the invention include systems for monitoring nucleic acid synthesis during polymerase chain reaction, tracking DNA digestion by restriction enzymes, observing protein hydrolysis by proteases, detecting ligation events in nucleic acid repair pathways, and quantifying carbohydrate degradation. The system is capable of operating with sample volumes ranging from 10 picoliters to 10 microliters, making it suitable for integration into microfluidic platforms and high-throughput screening systems.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

The invention provides a method and apparatus for detecting analyte-induced changes in solution viscosity through the asynchronous rotation of paramagnetic beads under a controlled rotating magnetic field. The AMBR method involves suspending a population of paramagnetic microbeads in a liquid sample and exposing them to a planar, rotating magnetic field generated by orthogonal Helmholtz coils. When the driving frequency exceeds a threshold value, the beads rotate asynchronously with respect to the field, and their rotational period becomes a sensitive function of the surrounding fluid’s viscosity. The use of magnetic particles is critical, as their induced magnetic dipole moment allows precise torque control without direct physical contact, enabling non-invasive, high-resolution measurements. The detection of viscosity changes is motivated by the fact that many biological reactions—such as DNA amplification, enzymatic cleavage, and protein folding—produce measurable alterations in solution rheology that are directly correlated with molecular weight or concentration. Applications of the invention include real-time monitoring of PCR, restriction digestion, nucleic acid ligation, protein degradation, and carbohydrate hydrolysis, all without the need for fluorescent or radioactive labels. The advantages of AMBR over prior art include its label-free nature, compatibility with small sample volumes, real-time capability, insensitivity to optical interference, and ability to function in turbid or colored samples where optical methods fail. Terminology used herein, such as “asynchronous rotation,” refers to the condition in which the bead’s rotational frequency is lower than that of the applied magnetic field, and “viscosity-dependent period” denotes the measurable time interval between successive rotations of the bead, which increases linearly with increasing solution viscosity.

### Asynchronous Magnetic Bead Rotation (AMBR)

The AMBR principle is grounded in the force balance between magnetic torque and viscous drag acting on a paramagnetic bead in a rotating field. In the synchronous regime, the bead rotates in lockstep with the field, but above a critical frequency, the bead enters the asynchronous regime, where its rotation lags behind the field due to viscous resistance. This lag is quantified as the rotational period, which increases linearly with viscosity. The system operates in two modes: synchronous, for calibration and low-viscosity samples, and asynchronous, for high-sensitivity detection of molecular changes. Paramagnetic beads with diameters ranging from 1 to 50 micrometers are employed, with magnetic content derived from iron oxide nanoparticles embedded in polymer matrices. Analytes include nucleic acids, proteins, and cellular lysates, each of which alters viscosity upon reaction. Current AMBR systems monitor bead motion using high-speed microscopy and image analysis software, with rotational periods determined via fast Fourier transform of intensity time series. Asynchronous rotation is defined as the condition in which the bead’s angular velocity is less than that of the driving field, resulting in a phase lag that stabilizes over time. Cluster rotation refers to the collective motion of multiple beads under the same field, which enhances signal-to-noise ratio when averaged across a population. Viscosity measurement is achieved by correlating the rotational period with known viscosity standards, yielding a calibration curve that enables quantification of unknown samples. Advantages over previous understanding include the recognition that inter-bead variability is dominated by magnetic properties rather than size, and that averaging across multiple beads mitigates manufacturing inconsistencies. The system operates with sample volumes as small as 10 picoliters, with practical ranges extending from 10 pL to 10 µL, and further extending to 50 µL in non-microfluidic configurations. Additional volume ranges include 100 pL, 500 pL, 1 nL, 5 nL, 10 nL, 50 nL, 100 nL, and 500 nL, each suitable for specific applications. The AMBR method exhibits viscosity detection capabilities spanning from 0.8 centipoise to greater than 100 centipoise, with additional ranges including 1–5 cP, 5–20 cP, 20–50 cP, and 50–100 cP. Analyte concentration detection is enabled through the linear relationship between viscosity and macromolecular concentration, permitting quantification of DNA, protein, or polysaccharide levels. Applications of concentration detection include quantification of PCR products, estimation of enzyme activity, and monitoring of nucleic acid synthesis. Nucleic acid synthesis is monitored by tracking viscosity increases during polymerase-driven elongation. Protein synthesis is detected through viscosity changes accompanying ribosomal translation or in vitro expression. Nucleic acid hydrolysis is observed as viscosity decreases during endonuclease digestion. Nucleic acid ligation is detected by viscosity increases as fragments join into longer chains. Protein hydrolysis is monitored by viscosity reduction during protease cleavage. Other monitoring applications include carbohydrate depolymerization, lipid micelle formation, and cellular aggregation. The AMBR system’s capabilities encompass continuous, real-time, label-free detection of molecular transformations across a broad dynamic range. Advantages include minimal sample consumption, rapid response time, compatibility with complex matrices, and scalability to multiplexed formats. Limitations of prior art include reliance on optical labels, susceptibility to background interference, and inability to perform real-time measurements in opaque samples. Benefits of AMBR over prior art include superior robustness in complex biological fluids, elimination of reagent costs associated with labeling, and the potential for integration into portable diagnostic platforms. The AMBR method represents a paradigm shift in molecular detection by replacing optical signals with rheological ones.

### Analytes

The invention is applicable to a diverse array of analytes whose physical state can be altered by chemical or enzymatic processes. Analytes include nucleic acids, proteins, carbohydrates, lipids, and whole cells, each of which induces a measurable change in solution viscosity upon transformation. A change in state refers to any molecular alteration that modifies the hydrodynamic volume or concentration of the analyte in solution, including cleavage, elongation, aggregation, or conformational transition. Examples of changes in state include DNA fragmentation, protein unfolding, polysaccharide depolymerization, and lipid micellization. Cells, including bacterial, fungal, and mammalian cells, serve as analytes when their lysis, aggregation, or membrane integrity changes the viscosity of the surrounding medium. Nucleic acids, particularly double-stranded DNA, are primary analytes due to their well-characterized viscosity-concentration relationships. Nucleic acid oligomers refer to synthetic or enzymatically generated chains of nucleotides, including primers, amplicons, siRNA, and CRISPR guide RNAs. Examples of nucleic acid oligomers include 20-base primers, 500-base PCR products, 5-kilobase plasmids, and 10-kilobase genomic fragments. Nucleobases include adenine, thymine, cytosine, guanine, and uracil, as well as modified forms such as 5-methylcytosine and pseudouridine. Universal bases, such as inosine or 3-nitropyrrole, are included as analytes when incorporated into oligonucleotides, as they alter hybridization kinetics and thus indirectly affect solution rheology. The length of nucleic acid oligomers ranges from 10 base pairs to over 100 kilobase pairs, with examples including 100 bp, 500 bp, 1 kb, 5 kb, 10 kb, and 50 kb fragments. Proteins are defined as polypeptide chains folded into functional conformations, and include enzymes, antibodies, structural proteins, and signaling molecules. Protein types encompass monomers, dimers, multimers, and post-translationally modified variants. Amino acids are the building blocks of proteins and are classified as essential, nonessential, acidic, basic, polar, or nonpolar. Protein synthesis detection occurs during in vitro translation or cell-free expression systems, where viscosity increases as nascent chains elongate. Non-naturally occurring proteins, such as fusion proteins, engineered enzymes, and synthetic peptides, are detectable if they alter solution rheology. Fragments of proteins, including cleavage products from proteolysis, are monitored as viscosity decreases. Analogs of proteins, such as peptidomimetics or D-amino acid variants, are included if they retain sufficient molecular weight to influence viscosity. Variants of proteins, including splice isoforms and point mutants, are detectable if their hydrodynamic radius differs significantly from the wild-type. Carbohydrates are defined as polyhydroxy aldehydes or ketones, including monosaccharides, oligosaccharides, and polysaccharides. Oligosaccharides such as maltose, lactose, and glycans are analytes whose hydrolysis reduces solution viscosity. Lipids are hydrophobic biomolecules that include fatty acids, phospholipids, and sterols. Lipid-derived hormones such as prostaglandins, leukotrienes, and steroid hormones are analytes whose aggregation or micelle formation can be detected through viscosity shifts. The invention is not limited to these examples and extends to any molecule whose concentration or molecular weight change in solution results in a measurable alteration in viscosity.

## EXAMPLES

### Example 1

Reagents used in the viscosity measurements included glycerol, deionized water, lambda DNA, pUC18 plasmid, restriction enzymes EcoRI and HaeIII, paramagnetic beads of 7.6, 16, and 45 micrometers in diameter, and bovine serum albumin as a blocking agent. Viscosity measurements were performed by suspending beads at a concentration of 0.0075% w/v in sample solutions contained between two glass slides separated by 210-micrometer spacers. The sample volume ranged from 5 to 10 microliters. Image stacks were captured at 10 frames per second using a high-resolution microscope, and rotational periods were determined by fast Fourier transform of intensity profiles from individual beads. Viscosities of glycerol-water mixtures were independently verified using a calibrated Ubbelohde viscometer, with results showing agreement within 5% across the range of 0.89 to 2.8 centipoise.

### Calibration of AMBR Viscometer

A calibration experiment was conducted using glycerol-water solutions of known viscosity, measured both by AMBR and Ubbelohde methods. A linear relationship was established between the rotational period of the magnetic beads and the solution viscosity, with a correlation coefficient exceeding 0.99. The correlation curve was constructed by plotting rotational period against viscosity for 12 distinct concentrations. The theoretical framework was derived from the balance of magnetic torque and viscous drag, yielding the equation T = kη, where T is the rotational period, η is viscosity, and k is a system-dependent constant. Nonlinear magnetic oscillation theory was applied to account for the transition from synchronous to asynchronous rotation. The magnetic torque equation was derived as τ_m = (3/2)χ''Vmµ₀B², and the viscous drag torque as τ_v = κηVω, where χ'' is the imaginary magnetic susceptibility, Vm is the magnetic volume, B is the field strength, κ is the shape factor, V is the bead volume, and ω is the angular velocity. Combining these equations under steady-state conditions yielded the linear relationship T ∝ η. Experimental validation confirmed this relationship across multiple bead sizes and driving frequencies, with deviations observed only near the instability threshold.

### Example 3

The influence of bead properties on rotation periods was investigated by measuring the rotational behavior of 20 individual beads in the same glycerol solution. The relative standard deviation in rotation period was approximately 10%, with no clear correlation to bead diameter. Continuous monitoring of a single bead over 30 minutes revealed a relative standard deviation of only 1%, indicating that inter-bead variation arises primarily from differences in magnetic content and susceptibility rather than size. This finding supports the use of population averaging to mitigate manufacturing variability and confirms that the system’s sensitivity is limited by bead uniformity, not measurement precision.

### Preparation of Digestion Reaction Samples

Digestion reactions were prepared by mixing lambda DNA with EcoRI enzyme and buffer, followed by incubation at 37°C for one hour. Samples were then equilibrated to 25°C before AMBR measurement. Gel electrophoresis confirmed the expected fragment pattern, with bands at 3530, 4878, 5643, 5804, 7421, and 21,226 base pairs. Viscosity measurements showed a progressive decrease in rotational period corresponding to DNA fragmentation. The linear relationship between viscosity and DNA concentration was confirmed, with intrinsic viscosity values consistent with literature reports. The AMBR method detected the progression of digestion in real time, with a resolution sufficient to distinguish between undigested and partially digested samples. The system also detected sequence variation by distinguishing between EcoRI-cut and PvuI-cut DNA, which produced different fragment size distributions and thus different viscosity profiles. The method was further validated by correlating viscosity changes with fluorescent signal intensities from gel bands, demonstrating quantitative agreement within 15%. Real-time detection of PCR product formation was achieved with a temporal resolution of 10 seconds per measurement, enabling observation of amplification kinetics without interruption. The system detected DNA concentrations as low as 0.1 ng/µL, corresponding to approximately 100 copies of a 4.5-kb amplicon.
