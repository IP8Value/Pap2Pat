# DESCRIPTION

- introduce microelectrodes and devices for neural interfacing applications  
Microelectrodes and associated devices have been developed to enable precise bidirectional communication between neural tissue and external electronic systems, facilitating both the recording of endogenous electrical signals and the delivery of controlled electrical stimulation. These devices serve as critical interfaces in neuroengineering applications where the restoration or modulation of nervous system function is required, particularly in cases involving sensory deficits, motor impairments, or neurological disorders. The design and performance of such systems are fundamentally constrained by the physical and electrochemical properties of the materials used at the tissue-electrode interface. Conventional microelectrodes, typically fabricated from metals such as platinum or iridium oxide, have been widely employed in clinical and research settings, yet their long-term stability, charge injection capacity, and signal fidelity remain limited by material degradation, high impedance, and suboptimal biocompatibility. Recent advances in microfabrication and nanomaterial science have expanded the scope of viable electrode architectures, enabling the development of high-density arrays capable of resolving fine-scale neural activity with improved spatial resolution and temporal precision. The integration of three-dimensional nanostructured surfaces has emerged as a promising strategy to enhance the effective surface area of electrodes without increasing their physical footprint, thereby addressing the inherent trade-off between miniaturization and functional performance. These innovations are particularly relevant for applications requiring chronic implantation, where mechanical durability, chemical inertness, and resistance to biofouling are essential for sustained operational integrity.

- describe neurostimulation and recording electric signals  
Neurostimulation involves the application of controlled electrical pulses to neural tissue to elicit or modulate action potentials in targeted neuronal populations, while neural recording entails the detection of spontaneous or evoked extracellular voltage fluctuations arising from the collective activity of nearby neurons. Both processes rely on the efficient transfer of charge across the electrode-tissue interface, a phenomenon governed by the interplay of capacitive and faradaic mechanisms. For effective stimulation, electrodes must deliver sufficient charge density to depolarize neuronal membranes without inducing irreversible electrochemical reactions that could damage surrounding tissue or degrade the electrode itself. Similarly, for high-fidelity recording, electrodes must exhibit low intrinsic noise and high signal-to-noise ratios to resolve small-amplitude neural signals, often in the range of tens of microvolts, against background thermal and electronic interference. The impedance of the electrode at physiologically relevant frequencies—particularly around 1 kHz—directly influences the quality of both stimulation and recording, as higher impedances attenuate signal amplitude and increase noise susceptibility. The development of electrode materials and geometries that simultaneously minimize impedance, maximize charge storage capacity, and maintain electrochemical stability under prolonged use remains a central challenge in the field of neural interfacing.

- list examples of applications (cochlear implants, retinal implants, deep brain stimulation, cortical implants)  
Microelectrode-based neural interfaces are integral to a broad spectrum of clinical and experimental applications, including cochlear implants that restore auditory perception by directly stimulating spiral ganglion neurons, retinal implants designed to bypass damaged photoreceptors and activate remaining retinal circuitry to generate visual percepts, deep brain stimulation systems that modulate pathological oscillatory activity in basal ganglia nuclei to alleviate symptoms of Parkinson’s disease and essential tremor, and cortical implants that decode motor intent from primary motor or premotor areas to drive brain-computer interfaces for individuals with paralysis. Each of these applications demands electrodes capable of chronic implantation, high spatial selectivity, and reliable bidirectional communication with neural tissue. The success of such systems is contingent upon the ability of the electrode material to maintain stable electrical properties over extended periods, resist encapsulation by glial scar tissue, and deliver precise, repeatable stimulation without triggering inflammatory responses or material corrosion. The limitations of conventional materials in meeting these criteria have motivated the exploration of novel alternatives with enhanced electrochemical and biocompatible characteristics.

- describe requirements for electrodes used for neurostimulation  
Electrodes intended for neurostimulation must satisfy a stringent set of functional and safety requirements, including high charge injection capacity, low impedance, electrochemical stability within physiological voltage windows, mechanical robustness, and long-term biocompatibility. The charge injection capacity must be sufficient to depolarize neuronal membranes without exceeding the water electrolysis threshold, which can lead to harmful byproducts such as hydrogen and oxygen gas, or the formation of reactive oxygen species. Electrodes must also exhibit minimal polarization under repeated stimulation cycles to prevent signal drift and ensure consistent therapeutic outcomes. Furthermore, the electrode surface must remain chemically inert in the presence of biological fluids, resist protein adsorption and cellular overgrowth, and maintain structural integrity during implantation and long-term exposure to dynamic mechanical stresses. The geometric configuration of the electrode, including its surface roughness, porosity, and three-dimensional architecture, plays a decisive role in determining its effective surface area and, consequently, its ability to store and transfer charge efficiently.

- explain biocompatibility and charge transfer process  
Biocompatibility in the context of neural electrodes refers to the ability of the material to coexist with neural tissue without eliciting adverse immune responses, inflammation, or progressive degeneration of adjacent neurons. A biocompatible electrode must not only avoid cytotoxicity but also promote favorable interactions with neural cells, such as neurite outgrowth and synaptic integration, while minimizing glial scarring that can electrically insulate the electrode over time. The charge transfer process at the electrode-tissue interface occurs through a combination of double-layer capacitance and faradaic reactions. In capacitive mechanisms, charge is stored electrostatically at the interface without net chemical change, whereas faradaic processes involve electron transfer across the interface, leading to redox reactions. For safe and sustainable stimulation, capacitive mechanisms are preferred due to their reversibility and minimal tissue damage. Materials that enable high double-layer capacitance through increased surface area—such as nanostructured surfaces—can significantly enhance charge transfer efficiency while avoiding the risks associated with faradaic reactions. The surface chemistry, hydrophilicity, and topography of the electrode further influence protein adsorption, cell adhesion, and the formation of the extracellular matrix, all of which modulate the long-term performance of the interface.

- describe electrochemical properties of electrodes  
The electrochemical properties of neural electrodes are characterized by parameters such as impedance, double-layer capacitance, charge storage capacity, potential window, and electrochemical stability. Impedance, typically measured at 1 kHz, reflects the opposition to alternating current flow and inversely correlates with signal fidelity in recording applications. Double-layer capacitance quantifies the electrode’s ability to store charge electrostatically at the interface and is directly proportional to the effective surface area. Charge storage capacity, derived from cyclic voltammetry, indicates the total charge that can be delivered per unit area during stimulation and is a critical determinant of stimulation efficiency. The potential window defines the range of voltages over which the electrode can operate without inducing water decomposition or irreversible chemical reactions. A wide potential window is essential for maximizing the safety margin during stimulation, particularly in small-diameter electrodes where charge densities are elevated. Electrochemical stability ensures that these properties remain consistent over time, even under repeated cycling and prolonged exposure to physiological environments.

- discuss limitations of electrodes used for neurostimulation  
Despite significant advances in materials science, conventional electrode materials such as platinum, iridium oxide, and titanium nitride remain limited by inherent trade-offs between charge injection capacity, impedance, and long-term stability. Platinum, while widely used, suffers from low charge storage capacity and susceptibility to mechanical degradation under repeated use. Iridium oxide offers higher capacitance but is prone to dissolution under high charge densities and exhibits variable performance across fabrication batches. Titanium nitride provides improved stability but still lacks the electrochemical window and surface area necessary for high-density, low-noise recording. Moreover, all metallic electrodes are susceptible to corrosion, passivation, and encapsulation by glial tissue, leading to progressive signal loss and increased impedance over time. Nanostructured coatings such as conductive polymers offer enhanced surface area but suffer from mechanical fragility, limited operational lifetime, and potential leaching of toxic components. These limitations underscore the need for a new class of electrode materials that combine the electrochemical advantages of diamond with the structural benefits of nanostructuring, enabling a durable, high-performance interface suitable for chronic implantation.

- describe electrodes used for electro-physiological applications ex-vivo  
In ex vivo electro-physiological applications, electrodes are employed to record and stimulate neural tissue in controlled laboratory environments, such as acute brain slices, spinal cord preparations, or dissociated neuronal cultures. These applications demand electrodes with high spatial resolution, low noise, and minimal perturbation of the native tissue environment. Conventional electrodes used in such settings are typically fabricated from platinum or gold and are integrated into multi-electrode arrays with micrometer-scale spacing. However, their performance is often compromised by high impedance and limited signal-to-noise ratio, particularly when recording from immature or small-diameter neurons. The use of nanostructured surfaces has been shown to improve signal acquisition by increasing the effective surface area and reducing the impedance of the interface, thereby enabling the detection of low-amplitude action potentials and local field potentials that would otherwise be obscured by background noise. The ability to maintain stable recordings over extended durations is critical for longitudinal studies of neural network dynamics, synaptic plasticity, and pharmacological responses.

- explain electrochemical properties required for electro-physiological applications  
For ex vivo electro-physiological applications, electrodes must exhibit low intrinsic noise, high signal-to-noise ratio, and minimal polarization during recording. The impedance must be sufficiently low to preserve the amplitude and temporal fidelity of neural signals, particularly those in the sub-millivolt range characteristic of single-unit activity. The double-layer capacitance should be maximized to enhance charge transfer efficiency without introducing faradaic artifacts, and the potential window must remain stable across repeated measurements to ensure reproducibility. Electrodes must also resist fouling by proteins and cellular debris, which can accumulate during prolonged recordings and degrade signal quality. The surface chemistry must be compatible with physiological buffers such as artificial cerebrospinal fluid, and the material must not leach ions or generate reactive species that could alter the ionic composition of the medium or induce cellular stress. These requirements collectively necessitate a material platform that combines chemical inertness, structural robustness, and tunable electrochemical behavior.

- describe development of electrodes with increasingly small diameters  
The miniaturization of neural electrodes has been driven by the need to increase spatial resolution and electrode density in multi-electrode arrays, enabling the simultaneous recording and stimulation of discrete neural populations. As electrode diameters shrink below 20 micrometers, the surface-to-volume ratio increases dramatically, leading to a proportional rise in impedance and a corresponding reduction in charge injection capacity. This scaling law imposes a fundamental constraint on the performance of conventional metallic electrodes, as their intrinsic capacitance and conductivity are insufficient to compensate for the reduced geometric area. Consequently, smaller electrodes exhibit higher noise levels, reduced stimulation efficacy, and diminished signal fidelity, limiting their utility in high-resolution neural interfacing. To overcome these limitations, researchers have turned to nanostructuring techniques that introduce three-dimensional topographies—such as nanotubes, nanopillars, or porous scaffolds—to increase the effective surface area without altering the physical dimensions of the electrode. These approaches have demonstrated significant improvements in charge storage and impedance reduction, but their long-term stability and biocompatibility remain unproven in chronic implantation scenarios.

- discuss problem of gradual degradation of electrodes  
A persistent challenge in neural interfacing is the gradual degradation of electrode performance over time, a phenomenon driven by mechanical wear, electrochemical corrosion, biofouling, and glial encapsulation. Metallic electrodes, despite their initial high conductivity, are susceptible to oxidation, dissolution, and delamination under prolonged stimulation, particularly when operated near the limits of their electrochemical stability window. Conductive polymers, while offering high capacitance, suffer from mechanical fatigue, swelling, and leaching of dopants, leading to irreversible loss of function. Even advanced materials such as iridium oxide exhibit long-term instability under repeated charge injection cycles. In addition to material degradation, the formation of a fibrous glial scar around the implant creates an insulating barrier that increases impedance and attenuates signal transmission. This progressive decline in performance undermines the reliability of chronic implants and necessitates frequent recalibration or replacement, which is impractical in clinical settings. A durable, chemically inert material capable of maintaining stable electrochemical properties over years of implantation is therefore essential for the next generation of neural prosthetics.

- introduce synthetic diamond as a potential electrode material  
Synthetic diamond has emerged as a compelling candidate for neural electrode applications due to its exceptional chemical inertness, mechanical hardness, thermal conductivity, and wide electrochemical potential window. Unlike conventional metals and polymers, diamond does not corrode, oxidize, or degrade under physiological conditions, even when subjected to prolonged electrical stimulation. Its ability to withstand extreme potentials without inducing water electrolysis or generating toxic byproducts makes it uniquely suited for safe, long-term neural interfacing. Furthermore, diamond can be doped with boron to render it electrically conductive while preserving its structural and chemical stability, resulting in boron-doped diamond (BDD), a material that combines the robustness of diamond with the electronic properties required for neural stimulation and recording. The intrinsic biocompatibility of diamond has been demonstrated in numerous in vitro and in vivo studies, where it supports neuronal adhesion and growth without eliciting inflammatory responses. These attributes position synthetic diamond as a transformative material for next-generation neural interfaces.

- describe properties of synthetic diamond (chemically and biologically inert, excellent electrochemical properties)  
Synthetic diamond is chemically inert under physiological conditions, resisting reaction with water, oxygen, chloride ions, and other components of biological fluids. It does not dissolve, oxidize, or release toxic ions, even under prolonged exposure to high current densities or aggressive electrochemical environments. Its biological inertness has been confirmed through extensive cell culture studies, where neurons and glial cells exhibit normal morphology, viability, and neurite extension on diamond surfaces. The electrochemical properties of boron-doped diamond are particularly advantageous, featuring a wide potential window exceeding 3 volts in aqueous media, far broader than that of platinum or iridium oxide. This allows for high charge injection without triggering harmful side reactions. Additionally, diamond exhibits low background current, minimal hysteresis, and excellent reversibility in cyclic voltammetry, making it ideal for both stimulation and recording applications. Its high thermal conductivity further enhances stability by dissipating heat generated during electrical stimulation, reducing the risk of localized tissue damage.

- discuss growth of synthetic diamond  
Synthetic diamond is grown using chemical vapor deposition techniques, in which a carbon-containing gas mixture is decomposed under controlled temperature and pressure conditions to deposit crystalline diamond layers onto a substrate. Common methods include microwave plasma-enhanced chemical vapor deposition, radiofrequency chemical vapor deposition, and hot filament chemical vapor deposition. These processes enable precise control over crystal structure, doping concentration, and film thickness, allowing for the fabrication of both single-crystal and polycrystalline diamond films. The incorporation of boron during growth imparts electrical conductivity while maintaining the material’s structural integrity. The growth parameters—such as methane-to-hydrogen ratio, temperature, pressure, and doping concentration—can be tuned to optimize the surface morphology, grain size, and electrochemical performance of the resulting diamond films. This level of control is critical for producing electrodes with consistent and reproducible properties suitable for neural interfacing applications.

- describe poly-crystalline diamond doped with boron (BDD)  
Poly-crystalline diamond doped with boron (BDD) is a conductive form of synthetic diamond composed of interconnected diamond grains with boron atoms incorporated into the crystal lattice during growth. This material retains the chemical inertness and mechanical robustness of diamond while exhibiting sufficient electrical conductivity for use as an electrode. The boron concentration is typically maintained in the range of 10²⁰ to 10²¹ atoms per cubic centimeter to achieve optimal conductivity without compromising structural stability. BDD electrodes demonstrate excellent stability under repeated stimulation cycles, minimal polarization, and low noise levels in physiological media. Their surface can be functionalized through oxygen or hydrogen termination to modulate hydrophilicity and cell adhesion properties. Despite these advantages, conventional BDD electrodes suffer from low double-layer capacitance and high impedance due to their relatively smooth, planar surface geometry, which limits their effectiveness in high-resolution neural interfacing.

- discuss limitations of BDD (small double layer electrochemical capacity, high impedance)  
Although boron-doped diamond offers unparalleled chemical stability and a wide electrochemical window, its planar, polycrystalline structure results in a low effective surface area, leading to a small double-layer capacitance and high interfacial impedance. These limitations restrict its charge injection capacity and signal fidelity, particularly in microscale applications where surface area is inherently constrained. The impedance of conventional BDD electrodes at 1 kHz often exceeds 1 MΩ for small-diameter devices, rendering them unsuitable for high-fidelity neural recording. Similarly, their charge storage capacity remains significantly lower than that of nanostructured alternatives such as iridium oxide or conductive polymers. Without surface modification, BDD electrodes are unable to compete with state-of-the-art materials in terms of performance metrics critical for neural interfacing, despite their superior durability and biocompatibility.

- state technical problem to be solved  
The technical problem to be solved is the inability of existing electrode materials to simultaneously achieve high charge injection capacity, low impedance, low intrinsic noise, mechanical robustness, and long-term biocompatibility in microscale neural interfaces. While boron-doped diamond provides exceptional stability and safety, its planar geometry renders it ineffective for high-resolution applications. Conversely, nanostructured materials such as carbon nanotubes or conductive polymers enhance surface area and reduce impedance but lack the durability and chemical inertness required for chronic implantation. There exists a critical need for an electrode architecture that integrates the electrochemical advantages of nanostructuring with the long-term stability of synthetic diamond, enabling a durable, high-performance interface capable of reliable bidirectional neural communication over extended periods.

- introduce object of the invention (microelectrode for neural interfacing applications)  
The object of the invention is a microelectrode for neural interfacing applications comprising a multilayered structure designed to optimize charge transfer, minimize impedance, and ensure long-term biocompatibility through the integration of a nanostructured synthetic diamond surface. This microelectrode is engineered to overcome the limitations of conventional materials by combining a conductive substrate, an adhesion layer, and a three-dimensional nanostructured layer composed of hollow or solid tubes formed from boron-doped polycrystalline diamond. The architecture enables a dramatic increase in effective surface area while preserving the chemical inertness and electrochemical stability of diamond, resulting in a device capable of high-fidelity neural recording and safe, efficient stimulation under chronic implantation conditions.

- describe structure of microelectrode (stack of layers)  
The microelectrode comprises a stack of three distinct layers, each serving a specific functional role in the overall performance of the device. The first layer is a biocompatible substrate, typically composed of a rigid or flexible material such as fused silica or polyimide, providing mechanical support and electrical insulation. The second layer is an adhesion material, selected from titanium, titanium nitride, or chromium, which ensures strong bonding between the substrate and the subsequent diamond layer. The third layer is a textured material composed of a compact set of hollow or solid tubes formed from boron-doped polycrystalline diamond, arranged in a vertically aligned configuration that projects radially outward from the plane of the substrate. This third layer constitutes the active electrochemical interface and is responsible for charge transfer with neural tissue.

- describe third layer (textured material with hollow or solid tubes)  
The third layer is a textured material comprising a dense array of hollow or solid tubes, each formed from boron-doped polycrystalline diamond and oriented perpendicular to the plane of the substrate. These tubes are characterized by nanometric to micrometric dimensions, with diameters ranging from 50 nanometers to 5 micrometers and heights extending from 1 to 10 micrometers. The tubes are separated from one another by interstitial spaces, creating a porous, three-dimensional architecture that significantly increases the effective surface area of the electrode. The diamond composition of the tubes ensures chemical inertness, electrochemical stability, and resistance to degradation, while their vertical alignment facilitates efficient charge transport along their length and minimizes inter-tube electrical interference.

- describe properties of tubes (radially external peripheral layer, doped poly-crystalline diamond)  
Each tube is composed of a radially external peripheral layer of boron-doped polycrystalline diamond, which forms the outermost electrochemical interface in direct contact with neural tissue. The diamond lattice is doped with boron at a concentration sufficient to render the material conductive, typically between 1 × 10²⁰ and 5 × 10²¹ atoms per cubic centimeter. The polycrystalline nature of the diamond provides a high density of grain boundaries that enhance electron transport along the tube walls, while the crystalline structure ensures mechanical rigidity and resistance to dissolution. The surface of the tubes may be oxygen-terminated to promote hydrophilicity and improve biocompatibility, facilitating neuronal adhesion and reducing glial encapsulation. The combination of diamond’s intrinsic properties with the nanostructured geometry results in a material that exhibits high double-layer capacitance, low impedance, and exceptional charge storage capacity.

- describe arrangement of tubes (separated from each other, projecting away from first and second layers)  
The tubes are arranged in a regular, spaced configuration such that each tube is physically and electrically isolated from its neighbors, preventing cross-talk and ensuring independent signal acquisition or stimulation at each site. The tubes project perpendicularly away from the plane of the first and second layers, forming a vertically oriented, forest-like structure that extends into the surrounding physiological medium. This arrangement maximizes the accessible surface area for charge transfer while minimizing the footprint of the electrode on the substrate. The spacing between adjacent tubes is maintained at a distance sufficient to prevent electrical shorting and to allow for the penetration of extracellular fluid, ensuring uniform electrochemical activity across the entire interface.

- describe particular embodiments of microelectrode  
Particular embodiments of the microelectrode include variations in tube geometry, doping concentration, and surface termination. In one embodiment, the tubes are hollow, with internal diameters ranging from 10 to 500 nanometers, enabling fluid penetration and enhancing surface-to-volume ratio. In another, the tubes are solid, with diameters optimized for mechanical robustness and charge transport efficiency. The diamond may be doped with boron during growth or post-deposition via ion implantation. The surface of the tubes may be hydrogen-terminated to enhance electron emission or oxygen-terminated to improve biocompatibility. The height of the tubes may be adjusted between 1 and 10 micrometers depending on the target application, with taller structures favoring higher capacitance and shorter structures providing greater mechanical stability.

- describe multi-electrode network for neural interfacing applications  
The microelectrode may be integrated into a multi-electrode network comprising an array of hundreds to thousands of individual microelectrodes arranged in a grid pattern on a common substrate. Each electrode in the network is independently addressable, allowing for spatially resolved recording and stimulation across a broad neural territory. The network is designed to conform to the topography of neural tissue, enabling high-density interfacing with cortical, retinal, or spinal structures. The use of diamond-based microelectrodes in such a network ensures long-term stability, low noise, and high signal fidelity, making it suitable for chronic brain-computer interfaces, sensory prostheses, and neurophysiological research applications.

- describe flexible implant for neural interfacing applications  
In another embodiment, the microelectrode is incorporated into a flexible implant designed for conformal integration with curved or dynamic neural surfaces, such as the cerebral cortex or spinal cord. The substrate layer is fabricated from a flexible polymer such as polyimide or parylene, enabling the implant to bend and stretch without compromising the integrity of the diamond tubes or their electrical connections. Metal interconnects are embedded within the flexible substrate to route signals to external recording and stimulation hardware. The flexibility of the implant reduces mechanical mismatch with neural tissue, minimizing chronic inflammation and tissue damage, while the diamond interface ensures sustained electrochemical performance over years of implantation.

- describe method for manufacturing microelectrode  
The method for manufacturing the microelectrode involves a sequence of microfabrication steps that begin with the deposition of a biocompatible substrate, followed by the patterning of an adhesion layer, the growth of carbon nanotubes, the deposition of diamond nanoparticles, and the final growth of a boron-doped polycrystalline diamond layer over the nanotube template. The process is conducted in a cleanroom environment using standard photolithographic, etching, and chemical vapor deposition techniques. The resulting structure is then passivated with a dielectric layer, except at the tips of the diamond tubes, which are exposed to form the active electrode surface.

- describe steps of manufacturing method  
The manufacturing method comprises a series of sequential steps: first, a biocompatible substrate is cleaned and prepared; second, a thin layer of adhesion material is deposited and patterned using photolithography; third, a catalyst layer is deposited and annealed to form nanoparticles; fourth, carbon nanotubes are grown via chemical vapor deposition using the nanoparticles as catalysts; fifth, a layer of non-doped diamond nanoparticles is deposited onto the nanotubes to serve as seeds for subsequent diamond growth; sixth, a boron-doped polycrystalline diamond layer is grown over the nanotube-seed structure using microwave plasma-enhanced chemical vapor deposition; seventh, excess diamond material is removed by reactive ion etching to expose the tops of the tubes; eighth, metal contact pads are deposited and patterned to connect the diamond tubes to external circuitry; ninth, a passivation layer is applied to the substrate and interconnects, leaving only the tips of the diamond tubes exposed; and tenth, the device is cleaned and sterilized for implantation.

- describe particular embodiments of manufacturing method  
Particular embodiments of the manufacturing method include alternative catalyst materials such as nickel, cobalt, or titanium, and alternative growth techniques such as radiofrequency chemical vapor deposition or hot filament chemical vapor deposition. In one embodiment, the carbon nanotubes are grown using a plasma-enhanced process, while in another, they are synthesized via laser ablation and transferred to the substrate. The deposition of diamond nanoparticles may be achieved through inkjet printing, electrospray, or spin coating. The final diamond layer may be grown in multiple stages to control grain size and doping uniformity. In some embodiments, a second material such as titanium nitride is deposited beneath the diamond layer to enhance adhesion or electrical conductivity.

- describe growth of carbon nanotubes  
The growth of carbon nanotubes is performed using chemical vapor deposition in the presence of a catalytic metal nanoparticle, typically nickel or cobalt, deposited on the adhesion layer. Upon heating in a hydrocarbon-containing atmosphere, the catalyst particles facilitate the decomposition of carbon-containing gases, leading to the nucleation and elongation of vertically aligned carbon nanotubes. The length, diameter, and density of the nanotubes are controlled by adjusting the growth temperature, gas composition, and duration. The resulting nanotubes serve as a three-dimensional scaffold for the subsequent deposition of diamond nanoparticles and the growth of the boron-doped diamond layer.

- describe deposition of non-doped diamond nanoparticles  
Non-doped diamond nanoparticles, typically 5 to 10 nanometers in diameter, are deposited onto the carbon nanotube scaffold using an electrostatic grafting method or colloidal suspension. The nanoparticles adhere to the nanotube surfaces, acting as nucleation sites for the subsequent growth of a continuous diamond film. This step is critical for ensuring uniform diamond coverage and strong interfacial bonding between the nanotubes and the overlying diamond layer. The deposition is performed in a controlled environment to prevent agglomeration and to ensure monolayer coverage.

- describe deposition of doped diamond  
The deposition of boron-doped diamond is achieved using microwave plasma-enhanced chemical vapor deposition, wherein a gas mixture of methane, hydrogen, and trimethylboron is introduced into a reaction chamber. The plasma dissociates the gases, allowing carbon and boron atoms to deposit onto the diamond nanoparticle seeds, gradually growing a continuous polycrystalline diamond layer over the nanotube scaffold. The boron concentration is carefully controlled to ensure electrical conductivity without compromising the structural integrity of the diamond lattice. The growth proceeds at temperatures between 600 and 800 degrees Celsius for durations ranging from 1 to 24 hours, depending on the desired film thickness.

- describe formation of fourth layer  
The fourth layer is a passivation layer composed of a dielectric material such as silicon dioxide, silicon nitride, or SU8, which is deposited over the entire substrate and metal interconnects to electrically isolate the device from surrounding tissue. This layer is patterned using photolithography and etching to expose only the tips of the diamond tubes, ensuring that charge transfer occurs exclusively at the active electrode sites. The passivation layer prevents current leakage, reduces biofouling, and enhances the long-term stability of the device.

- describe deposition of metal layer  
The metal layer, typically composed of chromium and gold or titanium and platinum, is deposited onto the substrate to form electrical contacts between the diamond tubes and external circuitry. The metal is deposited using physical vapor deposition techniques such as sputtering or electron-beam evaporation and is patterned using lift-off or etching to create conductive traces that route signals from each microelectrode to bonding pads. The metal layer is designed to be mechanically robust and chemically stable under physiological conditions.

- describe de-wetting step  
The de-wetting step involves the thermal annealing of a thin metal film to induce its breakup into discrete nanoparticles, which serve as catalysts for the growth of carbon nanotubes. This process is performed under controlled temperature and atmosphere to ensure uniform particle size and spacing. The resulting nanoparticles are distributed across the adhesion layer in a pattern corresponding to the desired electrode array geometry, enabling the subsequent growth of vertically aligned nanotubes at precise locations.

- describe growth of carbon nanotubes on metal drops  
Carbon nanotubes grow from the metal nanoparticles through a vapor-liquid-solid mechanism, in which carbon atoms dissolved in the molten metal precipitate at the tip of the nanoparticle, forming a tubular structure that extends vertically away from the substrate. The growth direction is dictated by the orientation of the metal particle and the gas environment, resulting in highly aligned nanotubes that are uniformly distributed across the electrode surface. The length and density of the nanotubes are determined by the growth duration and the concentration of carbon-containing gases.

- describe deposition of poly-electrolyte polymer and diamond nanoparticles  
In an alternative fabrication approach, a poly-electrolyte polymer is deposited onto the substrate to create a charged surface that facilitates the electrostatic binding of diamond nanoparticles. The polymer layer is applied via spin coating or dip coating and is subsequently cross-linked to ensure stability. Diamond nanoparticles are then introduced in an aqueous suspension and adhere to the polymer surface, forming a uniform seed layer for diamond growth. This method enables precise control over nanoparticle density and distribution, particularly on flexible substrates.

- describe deposition by ink jet type printing  
Deposition of diamond nanoparticles may be achieved using inkjet printing, wherein a suspension of nanoparticles is ejected through micro-nozzles onto predefined locations on the substrate. This technique allows for high-resolution patterning of the seed layer, enabling the fabrication of custom electrode geometries and multi-electrode arrays with variable density. The inkjet process is compatible with flexible substrates and permits rapid prototyping of electrode designs.

- describe electrospray of diamond nanoparticles  
Electrospray is employed to generate a fine mist of diamond nanoparticle suspension, which is directed toward the substrate under an electric field. The charged particles are attracted to the surface, resulting in a uniform and controlled deposition. This method is particularly useful for coating complex three-dimensional structures such as carbon nanotubes, ensuring complete coverage and strong adhesion of the seed layer.

- describe chemical vapor deposition assisted by microwave plasma  
Chemical vapor deposition assisted by microwave plasma is the primary method for growing boron-doped polycrystalline diamond. Microwave energy is used to generate a high-density plasma from a gas mixture of methane, hydrogen, and trimethylboron, which dissociates the molecules and enables the deposition of diamond atoms onto the seed layer. The plasma environment promotes high-quality crystal growth with minimal defects, resulting in a dense, conductive diamond film with excellent electrochemical properties.

- describe radiofrequency chemical vapor deposition  
Radiofrequency chemical vapor deposition is an alternative method for diamond growth, wherein radiofrequency energy is applied to a gas mixture to generate a plasma. This technique offers lower substrate temperatures and greater control over the growth rate, making it suitable for temperature-sensitive substrates such as polymers. The resulting diamond films exhibit comparable electrochemical performance to those grown via microwave plasma.

- describe hot filament chemical vapor deposition  
Hot filament chemical vapor deposition involves the use of a heated tungsten filament to thermally decompose a gas mixture, producing reactive carbon and boron species that deposit onto the substrate. This method is simpler and less expensive than plasma-based techniques and is suitable for large-area deposition. While the growth rate is slower and the crystal quality may be slightly lower, the method remains viable for certain applications requiring cost-effective fabrication.

- describe second material (titanium nitride, non-doped poly-crystalline diamond, poly-crystalline diamond doped with bor)  
The second material, serving as the adhesion layer, may be titanium nitride, non-doped polycrystalline diamond, or boron-doped polycrystalline diamond, depending on the substrate and desired electrical properties. Titanium nitride provides excellent adhesion to both silicon and polymer substrates and exhibits high electrical conductivity. Non-doped diamond serves as a buffer layer to enhance mechanical coupling between the substrate and the doped diamond layer. Boron-doped diamond may be used as a conductive interlayer to reduce overall impedance in multi-layered architectures.

- describe second step of manufacturing method  
The second step of the manufacturing method involves the deposition of the second material, which functions as an adhesion layer between the substrate and the carbon nanotube scaffold. This material is deposited using physical vapor deposition or atomic layer deposition to form a uniform, thin film with thicknesses ranging from 5 to 50 nanometers. The material is selected based on its compatibility with the substrate and its ability to bond strongly with both the substrate and the subsequent diamond layer.

- describe tenth step of manufacturing method  
The tenth step of the manufacturing method involves the application of a final passivation layer to electrically isolate the metal interconnects and substrate while exposing only the tips of the diamond tubes. This layer is deposited using plasma-enhanced chemical vapor deposition or spin coating and is patterned using photolithography and reactive ion etching to define the active electrode sites. The passivation layer ensures long-term stability, prevents current leakage, and minimizes biofouling.

- describe microelectrode arrangement  
The microelectrodes are arranged in a two-dimensional array on a common substrate, with each electrode independently addressable. The spacing between adjacent electrodes ranges from 20 to 200 micrometers, depending on the target neural tissue and desired spatial resolution. The arrangement is optimized to match the anatomical layout of the target region, such as the retinal ganglion cell layer, cortical columns, or spinal cord segments.

- illustrate microelectrode arrangement  
The microelectrode arrangement is illustrated as a grid of circular or square electrode sites, each comprising a central diamond tube structure surrounded by a passivation layer. The metal interconnects route signals from each electrode to bonding pads located at the periphery of the substrate. The array may be linear, hexagonal, or irregular, depending on the application, and may be integrated into a flexible substrate for conformal implantation.

- describe tube structure  
The tube structure is cylindrical, with either a solid core or a hollow lumen, formed entirely of boron-doped polycrystalline diamond. The walls of the tubes are composed of interconnected diamond grains, providing mechanical strength and electrical conductivity. The surface of the tubes may be roughened at the nanoscale to further enhance charge transfer. The tubes are vertically aligned and uniformly distributed across the electrode surface.

- describe application of MEA networks  
MEA networks composed of the disclosed microelectrodes are applied in chronic neural implants for sensory restoration, motor control, and neuroscientific research. These networks enable high-density recording of neural activity and precise stimulation of targeted neuronal populations, facilitating the development of brain-computer interfaces, retinal prostheses, cochlear implants, and deep brain stimulation systems. The durability and biocompatibility of the diamond-based electrodes make them suitable for long-term use in both clinical and experimental settings.

- describe flexible implant for neural interfacing  
A flexible implant for neural interfacing comprises an array of the disclosed microelectrodes integrated onto a thin, flexible polymer substrate such as polyimide or parylene. The implant is designed to conform to the curvature of neural tissue, minimizing mechanical stress and promoting long-term integration. The metal interconnects are embedded within the substrate, and the diamond tubes project outward to interface with neural tissue. The flexible design enables implantation in dynamic environments such as the cerebral cortex or spinal cord without inducing chronic inflammation.

- describe covering matrix  
The covering matrix is a biocompatible, insulating polymer layer that encapsulates the metal interconnects and substrate while leaving the tips of the diamond tubes exposed. This matrix prevents electrical shorting, reduces biofouling, and protects the device from mechanical abrasion. The matrix is applied using spin coating, dip coating, or plasma-enhanced deposition and is patterned to expose only the active electrode sites.

- outline method for manufacturing microelectrode  
The method for manufacturing the microelectrode comprises a sequence of steps beginning with substrate preparation, followed by the deposition of an adhesion layer, the growth of carbon nanotubes, the deposition of diamond nanoparticles, the growth of boron-doped diamond, the patterning of the passivation layer, the deposition of metal contacts, and final cleaning and sterilization.

- provide first step of method  
The first step of the method involves the selection and cleaning of a biocompatible substrate, such as fused silica or polyimide, to ensure a contaminant-free surface for subsequent deposition steps.

- describe first material  
The first material is a biocompatible substrate chosen for its electrical insulation, mechanical stability, and compatibility with neural tissue. It may be rigid for acute applications or flexible for chronic implants requiring conformal integration.

- provide second step of method  
The second step of the method involves the deposition of a second material, functioning as an adhesion layer, onto the substrate using physical vapor deposition or atomic layer deposition.

- describe second material  
The second material is selected from titanium, titanium nitride, chromium, or non-doped polycrystalline diamond, chosen for its ability to bond strongly with both the substrate and the subsequent diamond layer.

- provide third step of method  
The third step of the method involves the deposition of a catalyst layer, typically nickel or cobalt, followed by thermal annealing to form discrete nanoparticles that will serve as growth sites for carbon nanotubes.

- describe third material  
The third material is synthetic polycrystalline diamond doped with boron, grown over a carbon nanotube scaffold to form a three-dimensional, high-surface-area electrode interface.

- describe tube structure  
The tube structure consists of vertically aligned, hollow or solid cylinders composed of boron-doped polycrystalline diamond, with diameters ranging from 50 nanometers to 5 micrometers and heights from 1 to 10 micrometers.

- outline fourth step of method  
The fourth step of the method involves the growth of carbon nanotubes on the catalyst nanoparticles using chemical vapor deposition in a hydrocarbon atmosphere.

- describe seventh step of method  
The seventh step of the method involves the reactive ion etching of excess diamond material to expose the tips of the diamond tubes, defining the active electrode surface.

- describe eighth step of method  
The eighth step of the method involves the deposition of a metal contact layer, typically chromium and gold, to electrically connect the diamond tubes to external circuitry.

- describe ninth step of method  
The ninth step of the method involves the deposition and patterning of a passivation layer to electrically isolate the substrate and metal interconnects while leaving the tips of the diamond tubes exposed.

- describe fifth step of method  
The fifth step of the method involves the deposition of non-doped diamond nanoparticles onto the carbon nanotube scaffold to serve as seeds for the growth of the boron-doped diamond layer.

- describe sixth step of method  
The sixth step of the method involves the growth of a boron-doped polycrystalline diamond layer over the nanoparticle-seeded nanotube scaffold using microwave plasma-enhanced chemical vapor deposition.

- describe CNT etching  
CNT etching is performed using oxygen plasma or aqua regia to remove residual catalyst material and to clean the surface of the carbon nanotubes prior to diamond deposition. This step ensures that the diamond grows directly on the nanotube walls, enhancing adhesion and electrical connectivity.

- describe diamond synthesis methods  
Diamond synthesis methods include microwave plasma-enhanced chemical vapor deposition, radiofrequency chemical vapor deposition, and hot filament chemical vapor deposition. Each method enables the controlled growth of boron-doped polycrystalline diamond with tailored grain size, doping concentration, and surface morphology.

- describe alternative of the invention  
An alternative embodiment of the invention involves the direct deposition of a structured diamond layer onto a support substrate without the use of a carbon nanotube template. In this variant, the diamond is grown in a porous, dendritic morphology using modified chemical vapor deposition parameters to achieve high surface area without the need for a nanotube scaffold.

- deposit structured diamond layer on supports  
In this alternative, a structured diamond layer is deposited directly onto a support substrate using chemical vapor deposition with controlled gas flow and temperature gradients to induce the formation of a porous, three-dimensional diamond architecture. The resulting structure mimics the surface area enhancement of nanotube-templated diamond but eliminates the need for intermediate carbon nanotube growth and removal steps.

- motivate structured material  
The structured material is motivated by the need to increase the effective surface area of the electrode without increasing its physical dimensions, thereby enhancing charge storage capacity and reducing impedance. The three-dimensional architecture enables more efficient charge transfer between the electrode and neural tissue, improving both recording sensitivity and stimulation efficacy.

- describe functions of structured material  
The structured material serves to increase the electrochemical surface area, reduce interfacial impedance, enhance charge storage capacity, and promote neuronal adhesion. It also provides mechanical reinforcement to the electrode interface and minimizes the formation of glial scar tissue by presenting a biomimetic topography that is favorable for neural integration.

- describe electric current outlet  
The electric current outlet is formed by the exposed tips of the diamond tubes, which serve as the sole sites of charge transfer between the electrode and neural tissue. These outlets are electrically connected to the underlying metal interconnects and are surrounded by a passivation layer to prevent current leakage.

- describe fourth layer  
The fourth layer is a passivation layer composed of a dielectric material such as silicon dioxide, silicon nitride, or SU8, which covers the substrate and metal interconnects while leaving the tips of the diamond tubes exposed. This layer ensures electrical isolation, prevents biofouling, and enhances the long-term stability of the device.

- describe material of electric outlet  
The material of the electric outlet is boron-doped polycrystalline diamond, which provides a chemically inert, mechanically robust, and electrochemically stable surface for charge transfer with neural tissue.

- describe passivation layer  
The passivation layer is a thin, insulating film deposited over the substrate and metal interconnects to prevent electrical shorting, reduce biofouling, and protect the device from environmental degradation. It is patterned to expose only the active electrode sites, ensuring that current flows exclusively through the diamond tubes.

- describe method for producing current outlet and passivation layer  
The method for producing the current outlet and passivation layer involves depositing a dielectric material over the entire device, followed by photolithographic patterning and reactive ion etching to remove the dielectric from the tips of the diamond tubes. This exposes the active electrode surface while maintaining electrical isolation of all other components.

- describe advantages of the invention  
The advantages of the invention include a dramatic reduction in electrode impedance, a significant increase in charge storage capacity, enhanced signal-to-noise ratio for neural recording, improved stimulation efficiency, long-term electrochemical stability, resistance to biofouling and glial encapsulation, and mechanical durability under chronic implantation. The use of synthetic diamond ensures biocompatibility and chemical inertness, while the three-dimensional nanostructure enables high-resolution neural interfacing without compromising device longevity.

- describe method for manufacturing MEA multi-electrode network  
The method for manufacturing a multi-electrode array involves the parallel fabrication of hundreds to thousands of the disclosed microelectrodes on a single substrate using standard microfabrication techniques. Each electrode is independently addressable, and the entire array is integrated with external electronics for simultaneous recording and stimulation.

- describe deposit of diamond particles  
Diamond particles are deposited onto the substrate using electrospray, inkjet printing, or spin coating to form a uniform seed layer for subsequent diamond growth. The particles are sized between 5 and 10 nanometers to ensure high nucleation density and uniform film growth.

- describe growth of diamond  
Diamond growth is performed using microwave plasma-enhanced chemical vapor deposition, with methane, hydrogen, and trimethylboron as precursor gases. The growth proceeds at temperatures between 600 and 800 degrees Celsius for durations ranging from 1 to 24 hours, resulting in a continuous, boron-doped polycrystalline diamond film.

- describe deposit of nickel  
Nickel is deposited using electron-beam evaporation or sputtering to form a thin layer that is subsequently annealed to form discrete nanoparticles. These nanoparticles serve as catalysts for the growth of carbon nanotubes.

- describe growth of carbon nanotubes  
Carbon nanotubes are grown using chemical vapor deposition in a hydrocarbon atmosphere, with the nickel nanoparticles acting as catalysts. The growth proceeds for 10 to 30 minutes, resulting in vertically aligned nanotubes with lengths of 1 to 5 micrometers.

- describe deposit of nanodiamond layer  
A nanodiamond layer is deposited onto the carbon nanotubes using electrostatic grafting or colloidal suspension to provide nucleation sites for the subsequent growth of boron-doped diamond. The layer is uniform and monolayer-thick to ensure consistent diamond growth.

- describe growth of diamond  
The growth of diamond proceeds via microwave plasma-enhanced chemical vapor deposition, with the nanodiamond layer acting as a seed for the formation of a continuous, boron-doped polycrystalline diamond film over the nanotube scaffold.

- describe protection of diamond structures  
The diamond structures are protected during subsequent processing steps by applying a temporary mask or by using selective etching techniques that preserve the integrity of the diamond tubes while removing excess material.

- describe etching of non-protected diamond layer  
The non-protected diamond layer is removed using reactive ion etching with an oxygen plasma, which selectively etches away diamond material not shielded by the mask, exposing the tips of the diamond tubes.

- describe metal contact outlet  
The metal contact outlet is formed by depositing a stack of chromium and gold onto the substrate, connecting the diamond tubes to external circuitry. The metal is patterned using lift-off or etching to ensure electrical isolation between adjacent electrodes.

- describe passivation layer  
The passivation layer is a dielectric material such as SU8 or silicon dioxide, deposited over the substrate and metal interconnects to prevent current leakage and biofouling. It is patterned to expose only the tips of the diamond tubes.

- describe second embodiment of method for manufacturing MEA multi-electrode network  
In the second embodiment, a titanium nitride layer is deposited as the adhesion layer, followed by the deposition of a nickel layer for nanotube growth. Carbon nanotubes are grown, followed by the deposition of diamond nanoparticles and the growth of a boron-doped diamond layer. The passivation and metal contact steps are identical to the first embodiment.

- describe deposit of TiN layer  
The titanium nitride layer is deposited using reactive sputtering to form a thin, adherent film that enhances the bond between the substrate and the subsequent layers. It is deposited to a thickness of 10 to 50 nanometers.

- describe deposit of nickel layer  
The nickel layer is deposited using electron-beam evaporation to a thickness of 5 to 10 nanometers and is subsequently annealed to form discrete nanoparticles for nanotube growth.

- describe growth of carbon nanotubes  
Carbon nanotubes are grown using plasma-enhanced chemical vapor deposition in a methane and hydrogen atmosphere, with the nickel nanoparticles acting as catalysts.

- describe deposit of diamond nanoparticles  
Diamond nanoparticles are deposited using electrospray or inkjet printing to form a uniform seed layer on the nanotubes.

- describe growth of diamond  
Boron-doped diamond is grown using microwave plasma-enhanced chemical vapor deposition, with the diamond nanoparticles serving as nucleation sites.

- describe metal contact outlet  
Metal contact outlets are formed by depositing chromium and gold and patterning them using lift-off to connect the diamond tubes to external circuitry.

- describe passivation layer  
A passivation layer of SU8 or silicon dioxide is deposited and patterned to expose only the tips of the diamond tubes.

- describe third embodiment of method for manufacturing network of multiple electrodes  
In the third embodiment, the substrate is coated with a non-doped polycrystalline diamond layer as the adhesion material, followed by the deposition of nickel nanoparticles, growth of carbon nanotubes, deposition of diamond nanoparticles, growth of boron-doped diamond, and patterning of passivation and metal contacts.

- describe fourth embodiment of method for manufacturing network of multiple electrodes  
In the fourth embodiment, the carbon nanotubes are replaced with a porous, dendritic diamond structure grown directly from the substrate using modified chemical vapor deposition parameters, eliminating the need for a nanotube template. The diamond is doped with boron during growth to form a high-surface-area, three-dimensional electrode interface.
