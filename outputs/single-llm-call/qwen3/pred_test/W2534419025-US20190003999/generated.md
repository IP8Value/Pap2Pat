## BACKGROUND

- Single particle cryo-electron microscopy has emerged as a powerful technique for determining the three-dimensional structures of biological macromolecules at near-atomic resolution without the need for crystallization. The method relies on the rapid vitrification of purified protein samples embedded in a thin layer of amorphous ice, followed by imaging under low-dose electron conditions to minimize radiation damage. Despite its transformative impact, the success of single particle analysis is critically dependent on the quality of sample preparation, particularly the uniformity, thickness, and molecular orientation of the specimen on the electron microscopy grid. Traditional grid substrates, such as amorphous carbon or lacey carbon films, often suffer from poor particle distribution, non-specific adsorption, and insufficient mechanical stability during cryo-fixation, leading to heterogeneous ice layers and low signal-to-noise ratios in the resulting micrographs. Efforts to improve grid performance have included the development of functionalized surfaces that enable on-grid purification, thereby reducing the need for extensive sample handling and minimizing sample loss during buffer exchange. One promising approach involves the use of lipid monolayers modified with affinity ligands such as nitrilotriacetic acid (NTA) to capture histidine-tagged proteins directly from complex mixtures. However, these lipid-based systems are inherently fragile, prone to collapse under electron beam exposure, and exhibit limited reproducibility due to the dynamic nature of lipid packing and phase transitions at the air-water interface. Moreover, the hydrophobic lipid backbone often promotes non-specific binding of cellular contaminants, which obscures target particles and compromises structural resolution. The rigidity of the underlying support is equally critical: grids that lack sufficient mechanical stability deform under the vacuum and thermal stresses of cryo-EM, resulting in distorted particle orientations and unreliable reconstructions. Low-dose imaging techniques, while essential for preserving sample integrity, further amplify the need for high-contrast, low-background substrates that can efficiently transmit electrons while minimizing noise. The limitations of existing platforms have thus created a pressing need for a novel grid architecture that combines the chemical specificity of affinity capture with the physical robustness of a rigid, electron-transparent support, enabling direct purification and structural analysis of target proteins from crude biological lysates in a single, streamlined workflow.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

- The invention provides a novel electron microscopy grid comprising a rigid, electron-transparent substrate coated with a monolayer of functionalized graphene oxide modified with capture agents and deactivating agents. The capture agents are configured to selectively bind target molecules bearing specific affinity tags, while the deactivating agents suppress non-specific interactions with non-target components present in complex biological samples. This dual-function coating enables on-grid purification and cryo-fixation of target proteins directly from clarified cell lysates, eliminating the need for prior affinity chromatography and significantly reducing sample handling artifacts. The resulting grid supports high-resolution single particle analysis by providing a thin, conductive, and chemically defined surface that enhances image contrast, minimizes background noise, and preserves the native conformation of captured biomolecules.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

- The invention pertains to a specialized electron microscopy grid engineered for on-grid affinity purification and high-resolution structural analysis of biological macromolecules. The grid comprises a rigid, electron-transparent support, typically composed of copper or gold mesh, coated with a monolayer of graphene oxide or graphene oxide derivatives that exhibit controlled elasticity and mechanical resilience under cryogenic conditions. The surface of the graphene oxide is chemically modified with capture agents, specifically nitrilotriacetic acid moieties, which coordinate transition metal ions such as nickel or cobalt to form high-affinity binding sites for histidine-tagged proteins. These capture agents are covalently tethered to the graphene oxide lattice through stable amide or ester linkages, ensuring their stability during washing, incubation, and cryo-fixation steps. To prevent non-specific adsorption of cellular debris, lipids, or endogenous proteins, the graphene oxide surface is further treated with deactivating agents that block reactive functional groups such as epoxides, aldehydes, and carboxylic acids, which are naturally present on oxidized carbon surfaces and are known to engage in indiscriminate covalent or electrostatic interactions with biomolecules. Examples of deactivating agents include 4-aminobenzoic acid, bovine serum albumin, and polyethylene glycol derivatives, each of which effectively passivates the surface without interfering with the affinity binding capacity of the NTA-metal complex. The graphene oxide sheets used in this invention are synthesized via modified Hummers’ methods to achieve optimal oxidation levels, yielding a material with a high density of oxygen-containing functional groups suitable for subsequent derivatization. The sheets are exfoliated by ultrasonication in aqueous or mixed solvent systems to produce monodisperse, single-layer flakes with lateral dimensions ranging from 5 to 20 micrometers, which are then transferred onto the grid substrate using Langmuir-Schaefer deposition at the air-water interface. This technique ensures the formation of a uniform, contiguous monolayer with minimal stacking or aggregation, critical for achieving high-resolution imaging. The surface pressure during compression is carefully controlled to approximately 15 mN/m, a threshold that promotes close packing of the sheets while avoiding irreversible coagulation. The resulting coating is highly conductive, facilitating efficient dissipation of electron charge and reducing beam-induced specimen movement. The NTA groups are introduced via a multi-step chemical modification process involving activation of carboxylic acid groups with thionyl chloride, followed by nucleophilic substitution with a protected lysine-NTA derivative, and subsequent deprotection under acidic conditions to yield the active chelating ligand. The use of multiple deactivating agents in sequence—first a small molecule such as 4-aminobenzoic acid to chemically cap reactive sites, followed by a protein such as bovine serum albumin to sterically shield residual hydrophobic patches—provides synergistic suppression of non-specific binding. The target proteins for this invention include histidine-tagged enzymes, viral particles, chaperonins, and membrane protein complexes expressed in bacterial or eukaryotic systems. The preparation of samples involves the direct application of clarified cell lysate to the functionalized grid, followed by incubation with a metal ion solution to activate the NTA sites, washing to remove unbound material, and cryo-fixation via plunge freezing in ethane. The grids are then imaged using low-dose transmission electron microscopy under cryogenic conditions, yielding micrographs with exceptional particle density and minimal background interference. The resolution achieved by this method routinely exceeds 8 Å for well-behaved targets such as His6-GroEL, with the ability to resolve secondary structural elements and side-chain densities in many cases. Structural analysis is performed using standard single particle reconstruction software, with manual or semi-automated particle picking enabled by the high contrast and uniform distribution of captured particles. The graphene oxide substrate, due to its atomic thinness and high electron transparency, contributes negligible background signal compared to traditional amorphous carbon films, thereby enhancing the signal-to-noise ratio. The use of lipid monolayers is avoided entirely, eliminating the instability and variability associated with lipid phase transitions. Alternative substrates such as single-walled carbon nanotubes or metal-coated graphene may be employed in embodiments where enhanced conductivity or mechanical reinforcement is desired. The graphene oxide sheets may be further modified with fluorescent tags, such as fluorescein-conjugated derivatives, for optical validation of coating integrity prior to electron microscopy. The entire process—from grid functionalization to structural reconstruction—can be completed within a single day, significantly accelerating the pipeline for structural biology studies. The invention enables the direct analysis of proteins from crude lysates, reducing sample consumption, minimizing purification artifacts, and expanding the scope of targets amenable to cryo-EM analysis.

## EXAMPLES

### Example 1

- Graphene oxide sheets were functionalized with nitrilotriacetic acid by first activating the carboxylic acid groups of graphene oxide with thionyl chloride in anhydrous dichloromethane under reflux for three days. The activated intermediate was then reacted with tris(O-t-butyl)-Nα,Nα-dicarboxymethyllysine in acetonitrile and triethylamine at 100°C for three days to yield the protected NTA ester conjugate. The t-butyl protecting groups were subsequently removed by treatment with trifluoroacetic acid in tetrahydrofuran at 60°C for five hours, yielding graphene oxide-NTA. Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy confirmed the presence of characteristic amide and carboxylate stretches at 1650 cm⁻¹ and 1720 cm⁻¹, respectively, and the disappearance of aliphatic C–H stretches at 2930 cm⁻¹ following deprotection. UV-visible spectroscopy revealed a hypsochromic shift in the absorption maximum from 240 nm for pristine graphene oxide to 280 nm for graphene oxide-NTA, consistent with increased electron density in the π-conjugated system due to the introduction of polar nitrogen-containing moieties.

### Example 2

- Graphene oxide was synthesized using an improved Hummers’ method involving the oxidation of graphite flakes with potassium permanganate, sodium nitrate, and concentrated sulfuric acid under controlled temperature conditions. The resulting material was purified by repeated centrifugation and dialysis against deionized water until the supernatant reached neutral pH. UV-visible spectroscopy of the aqueous dispersion exhibited a distinct absorption peak at 230 nm corresponding to π–π* transitions of the aromatic core and a shoulder at 300 nm attributable to n–π* transitions of carbonyl groups, confirming successful oxidation. The concentration of graphene oxide was quantified using a previously determined extinction coefficient of 1200 L·g⁻¹·cm⁻¹ at 270 nm, and the dispersion was stored at 4°C for subsequent functionalization.

### Example 3

- Graphene oxide-NTA was synthesized by reacting the thionyl chloride-activated graphene oxide with tris(O-t-butyl)-Nα,Nα-dicarboxymethyllysine in anhydrous acetonitrile containing triethylamine as a base. The reaction mixture was stirred at 100°C for 72 hours under nitrogen atmosphere. After evaporation of solvents under reduced pressure, the product was washed with tetrahydrofuran and water to remove unreacted reagents and byproducts. The t-butyl ester groups were then cleaved using a 1:3 mixture of trifluoroacetic acid and tetrahydrofuran at 60°C for five hours. The resulting graphene oxide-NTA was isolated by centrifugation, washed ten times with water, and lyophilized to yield a dry powder suitable for dispersion and grid coating.

### Example 4

- The lyophilized graphene oxide-NTA powder was dispersed in a 5:1 mixture of methanol and water at a concentration of 1 mg/mL and subjected to probe sonication using a 150 W output for five cycles, each consisting of 45 seconds of sonication followed by 45 seconds of rest. The resulting dispersion was centrifuged at 1200 g for 10 minutes to remove large aggregates, and the supernatant was collected and subjected to four additional rounds of sonication and centrifugation to ensure complete exfoliation. The final supernatant, containing predominantly single-layer graphene oxide-NTA sheets, was filtered through a 0.22 μm syringe filter and stored under inert atmosphere for Langmuir trough experiments.

### Example 5

- An aqueous dispersion of exfoliated graphene oxide-NTA was introduced dropwise at the air-water interface of a Kibron μTrough using a syringe pump operating at 100 μL/min. The subphase consisted of deionized water maintained at 20°C. As the dispersion spread, surface pressure was monitored using a Wilhelmy plate. Upon reaching a surface pressure of 15 mN/m, the film was allowed to equilibrate for five minutes before compression to the same pressure to ensure uniform packing. The Langmuir trough was then used to transfer the monolayer onto 1500 mesh copper grids via the Langmuir-Schaefer method, with the grids lowered vertically through the interface at a speed of 5 mm/min.

### Example 6

- The surface pressure-area isotherm of the graphene oxide-NTA monolayer was recorded by compressing the film from an initial area of 1000 Å² per molecule to a final area of 200 Å² per molecule. The isotherm displayed a distinct plateau region between 400 and 250 Å² per molecule, indicating the transition from isolated sheets to close-packed monolayer coverage. Further compression beyond 250 Å² per molecule resulted in a steep rise in pressure, indicative of sheet folding and stacking. The area per molecule at the onset of the plateau was consistent with the lateral dimensions of the exfoliated sheets, confirming that the monolayer was composed of individual graphene oxide-NTA flakes rather than multilayer aggregates.

### Example 7

- Graphene oxide-NTA was modified with 4-aminobenzoic acid by incubating a 1 mg/mL dispersion with 30 mg of 4-aminobenzoic acid in phosphate-buffered saline at pH 7.4 for 24 hours on a rotary mixer. The reaction was initiated by probe sonication for 30 seconds at 150 W to enhance mixing. The modified material was then purified by centrifugation and washing with deionized water to remove unbound 4-aminobenzoic acid. UV-visible spectroscopy showed a slight red shift in the absorption maximum, consistent with the formation of amide bonds between the carboxylic acid groups of graphene oxide-NTA and the amine of 4-aminobenzoic acid.

### Example 8

- Fluorescein was conjugated to 4-aminobenzoic acid-modified graphene oxide-NTA by adding 2 mg of aminofluorescein to a 10 mL dispersion of PABA-GO-NTA at 1 mg/mL. The mixture was sonicated for 30 seconds at 150 W and then incubated in the dark for 24 hours on a rotary mixer. The resulting fluorescein-labeled material was purified by ten cycles of centrifugation and resuspension in methanol-water to remove unreacted dye. Fluorescence microscopy confirmed the homogeneous distribution of fluorescent signal across the graphene oxide sheets, validating the successful modification and retention of surface accessibility.

### Example 9

- Bovine serum albumin was diluted to a concentration of 0.1 mg/mL in phosphate-buffered saline and applied to graphene oxide-NTA or PABA-GO-NTA-coated grids that had been dried overnight in a desiccator. Five microliters of the solution were deposited onto each grid, incubated for five minutes, and then washed three times with 20 microliters of deionized water. The grids were stored in a desiccator until use. Subsequent incubation with purified His6-T7 bacteriophage and Ni²⁺ activation demonstrated significantly reduced non-specific binding compared to unblocked controls, confirming the efficacy of bovine serum albumin as a blocking agent. The grids were then used for cryo-electron microscopy, yielding high-contrast images of phage particles with minimal background contamination.
