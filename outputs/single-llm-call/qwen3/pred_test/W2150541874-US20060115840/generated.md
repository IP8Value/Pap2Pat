## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

DNA arrays have emerged as indispensable tools for high-throughput analysis of gene expression, enabling simultaneous interrogation of thousands of genetic targets within a single experimental platform. These arrays rely on the principle of nucleic acid hybridization, where immobilized probes on a solid surface bind complementary nucleic acid sequences from a complex biological sample. The development of macroarrays and microarrays revolutionized molecular biology by increasing the density and throughput of such analyses. Macroarrays, typically consisting of cDNA clones spotted at relatively low density on nylon membranes, were among the first platforms to enable genome-scale expression profiling. However, their limited resolution, low signal-to-noise ratios, and cumbersome handling procedures rendered them increasingly obsolete as technology advanced. Microarrays, in contrast, utilize high-density spotting of oligonucleotides or cDNAs onto glass or silicon substrates, achieving feature densities exceeding ten thousand elements per square centimeter. This dramatic increase in spatial resolution permits the simultaneous monitoring of entire transcriptomes with unprecedented efficiency and scalability.

The microarray experiment process generally involves several sequential steps: extraction of total RNA from biological samples, conversion of RNA into complementary DNA via reverse transcription, labeling of the cDNA with fluorescent dyes such as Cy3 and Cy5, hybridization of the labeled cDNA to the array surface under controlled temperature and buffer conditions, washing to remove non-specifically bound material, and scanning of the array using a laser-based fluorescence detector to quantify signal intensities at each probe feature. The resulting data are then analyzed to determine relative expression levels of genes between experimental conditions. Despite their widespread adoption, current microarray analyses suffer from significant limitations. One major issue is the assumption of global transcript stability across experimental conditions, which underpins many normalization strategies such as global median or mean scaling. This assumption is frequently invalid in toxicological, developmental, or disease-state studies where large-scale transcriptional reprogramming occurs. Additionally, commercial arrays often lack sufficient representation of biologically relevant genes, particularly those involved in xenobiotic metabolism, stress response, or DNA repair pathways, thereby limiting their utility in targeted investigations.

To address these shortcomings, external control targets have been introduced as spike-in standards—exogenous nucleic acids not native to the organism under study—that are added in known quantities to the hybridization mixture. These controls are intended to provide a consistent reference for normalization, independent of endogenous biological variation. However, conventional approaches to external controls involve spiking multiple distinct RNA sequences at varying concentrations into the sample prior to hybridization. This method introduces significant technical complexity, including the need for precise quantification of multiple RNA species, potential cross-hybridization artifacts, and variability in labeling efficiency across different transcripts. Furthermore, because these controls are introduced in solution, their hybridization behavior may not accurately reflect the physical constraints and surface dynamics experienced by endogenous probes immobilized on the array. As a result, normalization based on such external controls often fails to correct for spatially heterogeneous hybridization artifacts, print-tip carryover, or slide-to-slide variability.

WO2004/064482 describes a microarray system that incorporates external control probes printed directly onto the array surface, enabling a more direct comparison between control and sample hybridization kinetics. However, this system lacks a systematic dilution series designed to span the full dynamic range of detection, and does not integrate spatially randomized placement of controls to account for positional artifacts. Moreover, it does not provide a mechanism for quantifying cross-spot contamination or evaluating hybridization uniformity across sub-grids. Consequently, current microarray analysis systems remain inadequate for applications requiring high precision, reproducibility, and robust normalization in the presence of widespread transcriptomic changes.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The object of the invention is to provide an improved microarray system capable of accurate, reproducible, and biologically meaningful normalization in the context of focused gene expression profiling, particularly where global transcript shifts are anticipated. This is achieved through the integration of a novel array architecture that incorporates a precisely calibrated external control probe dilution series printed directly onto the solid support in a spatially randomized pattern across multiple sub-grids. The external control target consists of a single oligonucleotide sequence complementary to a non-endogenous nucleic acid, such as Arabidopsis thaliana chlorophyll synthase, which is absent from the genome of the organism under study. This probe is printed in a series of dilutions ranging from sub-picomolar to micromolar concentrations, thereby generating a continuous gradient of hybridization signal intensities that spans the full dynamic range of the detection system—from background noise to signal saturation.

The addition of this external control target enables normalization that accounts for both technical and biological variability, including differences in hybridization efficiency, dye labeling bias, scanner sensitivity, and spatial artifacts such as edge effects or uneven solution distribution. Unlike conventional spike-in methods that rely on solution-phase control RNAs, the present invention embeds the control probe directly on the array surface, ensuring that it experiences identical physical and chemical conditions as the sample probes during hybridization, washing, and scanning. This design ensures that the hybridization signal intensities of the external control features accurately mirror the behavior of endogenous targets, allowing for direct, intensity-dependent calibration of sample data. The external control probe is printed in duplicate or triplicate within each sub-grid, enabling statistical assessment of spot-to-spot variability and identification of printing or hybridization anomalies.

The array system comprises a solid support, a plurality of sample probes immobilized in a defined spatial arrangement, and the external control probe dilution series interspersed among the sample probes. The hybridizing mixture contains labeled target nucleic acids derived from the biological sample, along with a fixed concentration of the corresponding external control cRNA, ensuring that the external control signal remains constant across all hybridizations. The printing process utilizes robotic arrayers capable of precise deposition of nanoliter volumes, with optimized buffer formulations and humidity-controlled environments to ensure consistent spot morphology and hybridization performance. The resulting array exhibits a grid-based architecture with multiple sub-grids, each containing a complete set of external control dilutions, negative controls, and sample probes, arranged to minimize systematic bias and maximize reproducibility.

The normalization process involves measuring hybridization intensities of the external control features across their dilution series, constructing a calibration curve that relates probe concentration to signal output, and applying this curve to adjust the intensities of sample probes in an intensity-dependent manner. This composite normalization approach, which combines external control calibration with localized smoothing algorithms, significantly improves the accuracy of differential expression detection. The invention further includes a kit comprising the printed array, labeled external control cRNA, hybridization buffer components, washing solutions, and detailed protocols for sample preparation, hybridization, and data analysis. The system enables reliable detection of low-abundance transcripts, minimizes false positives arising from technical artifacts, and provides built-in quality control metrics for cross-spot contamination, hybridization uniformity, and signal linearity.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE PREFERRED EMBODIMENT

### Definitions:

A gene is a functional unit of heredity comprising a sequence of nucleotides that encodes a product, typically a protein or a functional RNA molecule. A probe is a single-stranded nucleic acid molecule, chemically immobilized on a solid support, designed to specifically hybridize with a complementary target sequence. A target is a nucleic acid molecule derived from a biological sample that is labeled and permitted to hybridize with the probe. A complement refers to a nucleic acid sequence that is perfectly or substantially base-paired with another sequence according to Watson-Crick pairing rules. An oligonucleotide is a synthetic nucleic acid polymer consisting of a short sequence of nucleotides, typically between 50 and 100 bases in length. Specifically hybridizing describes the selective binding of a probe to its intended target sequence under defined conditions, with minimal cross-hybridization to non-target sequences. A toxicant is a chemical or physical agent capable of inducing adverse biological effects, including alterations in gene expression. Normalization is the process of adjusting raw hybridization signal intensities to account for systematic technical variations, thereby enabling accurate biological comparisons. External refers to a nucleic acid sequence not native to the organism under investigation. A hybridizing mixture is a solution containing labeled target nucleic acids, buffer components, blocking agents, and salts, prepared for introduction to the microarray surface. Labelled denotes the covalent attachment of a detectable moiety, such as a fluorescent dye, to a nucleic acid molecule. A fluorescent label is a chemical compound that emits light upon excitation by a specific wavelength of light, enabling detection by optical scanners. An array is a two-dimensional arrangement of immobilized probes on a solid substrate, designed for parallel hybridization with multiple target sequences. Expression refers to the process by which information encoded in a gene is transcribed into RNA and, in some cases, translated into protein. A nucleic acid is a biopolymer composed of nucleotide monomers, including DNA and RNA. A polynucleotide is a long chain of nucleotides, typically exceeding fifty bases in length. A kit is a packaged set of components and reagents provided for the performance of a specific assay, including the array, labeled controls, buffers, and instructions. Kit components include the printed microarray, external control cRNA, hybridization buffer, wash solutions, and documentation. Array components consist of the solid support, immobilized sample probes, external control probes, and negative controls. Hybridizing mixture components include labeled target cRNA, hybridization buffer, detergents, blocking agents, and salt solutions.

### General Overview of the Invention

The invention provides a microarray system and method for gene expression analysis that overcomes the limitations of conventional normalization strategies by integrating a precisely calibrated external control dilution series directly onto the array surface. This system enables intensity-dependent normalization that accounts for both technical variability and biological heterogeneity, ensuring accurate detection of differential gene expression even when a substantial proportion of the transcriptome is altered. The invention is particularly suited for toxicogenomic, pharmacogenomic, and disease profiling applications where global transcript shifts are common and traditional normalization methods fail.

### Description of Preferred Embodiments

The array system comprises a glass slide substrate coated with a proprietary matrix that enhances probe immobilization and reduces non-specific binding. The external control probe is a 70-base oligonucleotide complementary to the Arabidopsis thaliana chlorophyll synthase gene, printed in a series of 18 concentrations ranging from 0.000015 μM to 100 μM in ArrayIT spotting solution. Each dilution is printed in duplicate or triplicate within each of 12 sub-grids, resulting in over 2,000 external control features per array. The hybridizing mixture contains a fixed concentration of in vitro transcribed cRNA corresponding to the external control sequence, spiked into each sample prior to hybridization. Sample probes consist of 70-mer oligonucleotides targeting 1,600 genes associated with xenobiotic metabolism, stress response, and DNA repair pathways. The printing process is performed using a Virtek ChipWriterPro robotic arrayer operating at 65% relative humidity, with pins washed and sonicated between loadings to minimize carryover. Each sub-grid is arranged in a 16×12 grid pattern, with external control spots randomly distributed among sample probes to mitigate spatial bias. Buffer spots are interspersed to assess background and carryover, while negative controls include random 70-mer oligonucleotide pools and randomized hexamers. The array exhibits a spatial randomization of features to eliminate systematic artifacts, and duplicate printing of sample probes across four supergrids enhances statistical reliability. The hybridizing mixture includes 2× hybridization buffer, 0.1% SDS, 0.1% BSA, and 5× SSC, with hybridization conducted at 60°C for 17 hours. The system is designed to detect transcripts at a sensitivity of one molecule per 2,000,000 cellular transcripts. Normalization is performed by fitting a LOWESS curve to the external control signal intensities across their dilution series, then applying this calibration to adjust sample probe intensities in an intensity-dependent manner. The kit includes the printed array, lyophilized external control cRNA, hybridization buffer, wash solutions, and a detailed protocol for sample preparation, hybridization, scanning, and data normalization.

## EXAMPLES

### A. Materials and Methods

Genes predictive of toxicant exposure were selected from published toxicogenomic studies and curated into a list of 1,600 murine genes. Custom 70-mer oligonucleotides were designed using proprietary algorithms to ensure optimal melting temperature, GC content, and absence of secondary structure. Oligonucleotides were synthesized with a 5′ C6 amino modifier and purified by HPLC. The external control oligonucleotide was designed against Arabidopsis thaliana chlorophyll synthase (RefSeq NM_115041) and synthesized identically. A dilution series was prepared in ArrayIT spotting solution spanning eight orders of magnitude. Oligonucleotides were printed onto PowerMatrix slides using a ChipWriter Pro robotic arrayer under controlled humidity. Slides were post-processed by baking, blocking, and rinsing prior to hybridization. Labeled cRNA was prepared from mouse liver RNA using Agilent’s Low Input Fluorescent Linear Amplification Kit, with 5 ng of external control cRNA spiked into each sample. Hybridization was performed in an HS 4800 station at 60°C for 17 hours, followed by stringent washing. Arrays were scanned at dual photomultiplier settings to capture both low- and high-intensity signals.

### B. Results

Optimal slide and printing buffer were identified through comparative hybridization and signal-to-noise analysis. Oligonucleotide printing concentration was optimized to yield linear signal responses across the dilution series. The external control feature demonstrated no cross-hybridization with murine RNA, confirming specificity. The dilution series covered the full dynamic range of the scanner, from background to saturation. Spatial arrangement of features eliminated positional artifacts, and replicate spots demonstrated low intra-subgrid variability. Cross-spot contamination was quantified at less than 0.7% per print-tip group. The external control normalization method outperformed standard LOWESS in sensitivity and specificity, as validated by RT-PCR. The ToxArray™ design enabled detection of 35 differentially expressed genes following phenobarbital exposure, with 15 of 19 conflicting results resolved in favor of the external control method. Spot replication increased detection power, with four replicates identifying 35 genes versus 13 with single spots. The system reliably detected transcripts at concentrations as low as 0.0000005 ng of external RNA per ng of murine RNA.
