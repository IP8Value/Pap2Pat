## FIELD

- define chaotic systems and entanglement

Chaotic systems are deterministic dynamical systems characterized by extreme sensitivity to initial conditions, a positive maximal Lyapunov exponent, and the presence of an aperiodic, bounded trajectory that never repeats yet remains confined to a structured region of phase space known as an attractor. These systems exhibit complex, unpredictable long-term behavior despite being governed by precise, non-random equations, and they are ubiquitous across physical, biological, and engineering domains. Entanglement, in the context of classical dynamical systems, refers to a mutually sustaining, information-mediated coupling between two or more such systems, wherein the stabilization of one system’s periodic orbit directly and deterministically enforces the stabilization of another’s, such that the persistence of each orbit is contingent upon the continuous exchange of symbolic control information with its partner. This form of entanglement does not involve quantum superposition or nonlocality but instead emerges from the nonlinear, feedback-driven synchronization of unstable periodic orbits within classical chaotic frameworks, creating a self-reinforcing, closed-loop dynamical relationship that persists absent external intervention.

## BACKGROUND

- motivate entanglement and chaos theory

The study of chaotic systems has long sought to reconcile deterministic laws with emergent unpredictability, revealing that even simple nonlinear equations can generate intricate, seemingly random behavior. Despite the absence of randomness in their governing equations, chaotic systems produce trajectories that explore vast regions of phase space with statistical regularity, making them ideal candidates for modeling real-world phenomena ranging from weather patterns to neural activity. Meanwhile, entanglement has traditionally been regarded as a uniquely quantum phenomenon, arising from the non-separability of composite wave functions and enabling correlations between distant particles that defy classical explanation. Yet recent investigations into the symbolic dynamics of chaotic systems have uncovered that entanglement-like correlations can emerge not from quantum coherence, but from the deterministic, information-exchange-driven stabilization of unstable periodic orbits. This suggests that entanglement, as a phenomenon of mutual dependence and state correlation, may have a broader physical foundation than previously assumed—extending beyond quantum mechanics into the domain of classical nonlinear dynamics. The motivation for this discovery lies in the potential to bridge the conceptual gap between classical and quantum descriptions of nature, offering a new framework for understanding how complex systems synchronize, store information, and respond to measurement without invoking quantum postulates.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE DISCLOSURE

- introduce cupolets

Cupolets are periodic orbits of chaotic systems that have been stabilized through the repeated application of small, binary control perturbations derived from symbolic sequences. Unlike traditional unstable periodic orbits, which exist only as mathematical constructs within the attractor of a chaotic system, cupolets are physically realizable states that persist indefinitely under the influence of their corresponding control codes. Each cupolet is uniquely associated with a specific binary sequence, and its stabilization is independent of initial conditions, meaning that regardless of the system’s starting state, the application of the correct control sequence will invariably drive the system into the same periodic orbit. This property makes cupolets ideal candidates for encoding information, as their dynamical identity is fully determined by their control code, and their stability is maintained solely by the periodic repetition of that code.

- describe stabilization of UPOs

The stabilization of unstable periodic orbits (UPOs) in chaotic systems is typically achieved through methods such as the OGY control scheme or Newton-based iterative techniques, which require continuous monitoring and fine-tuned perturbations to nudge trajectories toward desired orbits. In contrast, the stabilization of cupolets relies on a discrete, symbolic control framework that applies perturbations only at specific intersections with predefined Poincaré surfaces, reducing the need for continuous feedback. These perturbations are minimal and localized, preserving the underlying topology of the attractor while enabling the system to settle into a periodic state. The resulting cupolet is not an artificial construct but a shadowing orbit that closely approximates a true UPO, with deviations arising only from the finite precision of the control bins and the discrete nature of the applied perturbations.

- reference prior art

Prior art in the field of chaotic control has focused on stabilizing individual UPOs using adaptive feedback mechanisms, such as those developed by Ott, Grebogi, and Yorke, or on synchronizing chaotic systems through coupling of their state variables. Other approaches have explored the use of symbolic dynamics for encoding information in chaotic signals, particularly in secure communication. However, none of these methods have demonstrated the emergence of mutually sustaining, self-perpetuating entanglement between two or more stabilized orbits, nor have they utilized the bidirectional exchange of symbolic visitation sequences as the primary mechanism for maintaining periodicity. The present invention departs from these approaches by introducing a control architecture in which entanglement is not imposed externally but arises spontaneously from the internal dynamics of the system when symbolic feedback loops are established.

- define entanglement between cupolets

Entanglement between cupolets is defined as a state of mutual stabilization in which the periodic orbit of one cupolet is maintained by the symbolic output of another, and vice versa, such that the removal of either system’s control input does not disrupt the periodicity of the other. This state is not the result of direct physical coupling or energy exchange but arises from the deterministic transformation of one cupolet’s visitation sequence into the control code required to stabilize its partner. The entanglement is thus an information-theoretic phenomenon, wherein the stability of each orbit is encoded in the symbolic dynamics of its partner, creating a closed-loop system that requires no external intervention to persist.

- describe exchange function

The exchange function is a deterministic, binary transformation that maps a visitation sequence from one cupolet to an emitted sequence that serves as the control code for another cupolet. It operates as a symbolic intermediary between two chaotic systems, converting the lobe-hopping pattern of one system into the precise sequence of micro- and macro-controls needed to stabilize the orbit of the other. The exchange function may be as simple as an identity operator, in which case the visitation sequence is directly used as the control code, or it may involve complex bitwise operations such as thresholding, complementation, or logical filtering. The choice of exchange function determines the nature of the entanglement, including its robustness, sensitivity to perturbation, and compatibility with logic operations.

- explain pure entanglement

Pure entanglement occurs when the exchange function is the identity operator, meaning that the visitation sequence of one cupolet is transmitted without modification to serve as the control code for its partner. In this case, the entanglement requires no external processing or environmental mediation—the stabilization is achieved directly through the symbolic correspondence between the two orbits. Pure entanglement is the simplest and most robust form of cupolet entanglement, as it eliminates any potential for error introduced by nontrivial transformations. It also suggests that entanglement may occur naturally in physical systems where chaotic dynamics are in close proximity and their symbolic outputs are directly accessible to one another.

- discuss physical realizability

The physical realizability of cupolet entanglement is demonstrated through the implementation of Chua’s circuit—a well-established analog chaotic oscillator—equipped with digital control units that monitor trajectory intersections and apply perturbations in real time. The system operates entirely within classical physics, using standard electronic components such as resistors, capacitors, operational amplifiers, and field-programmable gate arrays to generate, detect, and process symbolic sequences. The control perturbations are applied as brief voltage pulses that do not alter the fundamental dynamics of the circuit but are sufficient to guide the trajectory into a desired orbit. The entire system is scalable, manufacturable using integrated circuit technology, and capable of operating at frequencies exceeding megahertz, confirming that cupolet entanglement is not a theoretical abstraction but a physically implementable phenomenon.

- introduce generating cupolets

The generation of cupolets begins with the definition of a Poincaré surface of section on the chaotic attractor, partitioned into a finite number of binary bins. As the system evolves, each intersection with the surface is recorded as a binary digit, producing a visitation sequence that encodes the trajectory’s lobe-hopping behavior. A repeating binary control sequence is then applied to the system, with each bit instructing whether a microcontrol (small perturbation to center the trajectory) or a macrocontrol (larger perturbation to induce a lobe transition) should be applied. When the control sequence is periodic and sufficiently short, the system settles into a stable, repeating orbit—the cupolet—whose period and topology are uniquely determined by the control code. This process can be automated to generate thousands of distinct cupolets from a single chaotic system by systematically testing all possible control sequences up to a given length.

- describe controlling chaotic systems

Controlling chaotic systems for cupolet generation involves the precise timing and magnitude of perturbations applied at each intersection with the Poincaré surface. Microcontrols are small, localized adjustments that reset the trajectory to the center of the bin it has entered, while macrocontrols are larger perturbations that force a transition to a different bin several steps downstream in the symbolic sequence. The combination of these two control types allows for the steering of the system along any desired symbolic path. The control scheme is adaptive, meaning that the perturbations are applied only when necessary, allowing the system to evolve freely between intersections. This minimizes energy consumption and preserves the natural dynamics of the attractor, ensuring that the resulting cupolet is a faithful representation of an underlying UPO.

- define Poincaré surface of section

The Poincaré surface of section is a hyperplane intersecting the chaotic attractor at a location where the trajectory crosses it transversely, enabling the reduction of continuous dynamics to a discrete sequence of points. Each crossing is recorded as a symbol, typically a binary digit indicating which lobe or region of the attractor the trajectory has entered. This symbolic representation captures the essential topological information of the system’s motion, transforming the continuous trajectory into a discrete sequence that can be processed, stored, and manipulated as digital information. The surface is chosen such that the crossings occur frequently enough to provide high-resolution symbolic dynamics but infrequently enough to avoid redundancy, ensuring that each symbol corresponds to a unique phase state.

- describe partitioning Poincaré sections

Partitioning the Poincaré sections involves dividing each surface into a grid of small, non-overlapping bins, each assigned a unique binary identifier. The size of the bins is determined by the desired precision of control and the Lyapunov exponent of the system, with smaller bins allowing for finer control but requiring more computational resources. For the double scroll oscillator, each lobe’s surface is divided into 2000 bins, resulting in a 11-bit resolution for symbolic encoding. The binning is performed offline and remains fixed during operation, ensuring that the control scheme is deterministic and reproducible. The binary labels of the bins are mapped directly to the control bits, enabling the system to translate trajectory position into control instruction.

- define coding function

The coding function is the mapping that assigns a unique binary sequence to each cupolet based on the control code required to stabilize it. This function is injective, meaning that no two distinct control sequences produce the same cupolet, and surjective, meaning that every possible control sequence of a given length generates a valid cupolet. The coding function thus establishes a one-to-one correspondence between symbolic information and dynamical state, enabling the storage, retrieval, and manipulation of chaotic orbits as digital data. It is the foundation upon which all subsequent operations—entanglement, logic, and memory—are built.

- describe macrocontrols

Macrocontrols are larger perturbations applied to the system to induce a deliberate transition between lobes or regions of the attractor, typically occurring at intervals specified by the control sequence. Unlike microcontrols, which only correct the trajectory’s position within a bin, macrocontrols alter the symbolic sequence by forcing a change in the lobe visited, effectively shifting the orbit’s path through the attractor. These perturbations are calculated as the minimal force required to produce a specified change in the visitation sequence several steps ahead, ensuring that the control remains as non-invasive as possible. Macrocontrols are responsible for the long-range steering of the trajectory and are essential for generating cupolets with periods greater than one.

- describe microcontrols

Microcontrols are small, localized perturbations applied each time the trajectory intersects a Poincaré surface, designed to reset the system’s state to the center of the bin it has entered. These perturbations are typically on the order of 10⁻⁶ to 10⁻⁸ of the system’s natural dynamics and are sufficient to counteract the exponential divergence caused by the positive Lyapunov exponent. Microcontrols do not alter the symbolic sequence but ensure that the trajectory remains confined to the intended bin, preserving the integrity of the control code. Their repeated application over time enforces the periodicity of the cupolet, allowing the system to maintain its orbit even in the presence of noise or parameter drift.

- explain control scheme

The control scheme is a hybrid analog-digital framework that combines the continuous dynamics of a chaotic oscillator with discrete, binary control instructions. It operates by monitoring the system’s trajectory as it crosses predefined Poincaré surfaces, recording each intersection as a binary symbol, and applying micro- or macrocontrols according to the current bit in a repeating control sequence. The scheme is self-synchronizing, as the timing of control applications is determined by the system’s intrinsic dynamics rather than an external clock. This allows the system to adapt to variations in oscillation frequency and maintain stability even under non-ideal conditions. The control scheme is the enabling mechanism for cupolet generation, entanglement, and logic operations.
