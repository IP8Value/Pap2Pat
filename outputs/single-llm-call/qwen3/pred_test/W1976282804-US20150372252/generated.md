# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND OF INVENTION

Organic light emitting diodes (OLEDs) have emerged as a dominant display technology due to their self-emissive nature, wide viewing angles, and potential for flexible form factors. Despite these advantages, a fundamental limitation persists in the extraction of generated photons from the device structure. A significant portion of emitted light is trapped within the device due to refractive index mismatches between the organic layers, transparent conductive oxides, and the glass or plastic substrate. This optical confinement arises because the refractive index of typical organic semiconductors exceeds that of the surrounding media, leading to total internal reflection at multiple internal interfaces. As a result, only a fraction of the internally generated photons—often less than 30%—escape the device, severely limiting the overall luminous efficiency and power performance of OLEDs.

To address this challenge, researchers have explored various light extraction techniques, including the integration of periodic microstructures on the substrate or within the device stack. These structures aim to scatter trapped modes into escape cones, thereby increasing the probability of photon outcoupling. Prior art has demonstrated the use of microlens arrays, diffraction gratings, and surface texturing to enhance light output. One notable approach involves the formation of buckled metal layers, which induce controlled surface topography through mechanical instability during thermal cycling. This method leverages the differential thermal expansion coefficients between a thin metal film and a compliant polymer substrate to generate a buckled morphology upon cooling. Such structures have been shown to improve light extraction without requiring complex lithographic patterning or additional process steps.

Koo et al. disclosed a method wherein a thin aluminum film is deposited onto a polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) elastomer layer, and subsequent cooling induces buckling due to compressive stress. The resulting periodic ridges and valleys act as a diffraction grating, enhancing forward emission. However, this approach suffers from a critical drawback: the buckling pattern is inherently periodic, leading to undesirable optical interference effects such as color shifts with viewing angle and visible moiré patterns in display applications. These artifacts degrade image quality and are incompatible with high-resolution active matrix displays where uniformity and angular stability are paramount.

There remains a persistent need for a light extraction architecture that enhances photon outcoupling without introducing optical artifacts, while remaining fully compatible with existing OLED fabrication processes. Conventional methods that rely on photolithography or nanoimprinting add cost, complexity, and yield risk. Furthermore, prior buckling techniques lack tunability beyond the physical dimensions of the metal layer, offering no mechanism to control the spatial distribution of scattering features beyond simple periodicity. A solution that introduces controlled disorder into the buckling pattern—while preserving the simplicity and scalability of the thermal buckling process—is therefore highly desirable.

Quasi-periodic structures, which exhibit long-range order without strict translational symmetry, offer a compelling alternative. These structures can scatter light across a broad angular spectrum while suppressing coherent interference effects. Their optical behavior mimics that of random structures in terms of angular uniformity, yet retains the manufacturability and reproducibility of ordered systems. The introduction of quasi-periodicity into the buckled layer of an OLED device presents a novel pathway to enhance light extraction efficiency without compromising display quality, color consistency, or fabrication compatibility.

## BRIEF SUMMARY

The present invention discloses an organic light emitting diode device incorporating a quasi-periodic buckling structure on its distal surface to enhance light extraction efficiency while eliminating angular color shift and optical artifacts. The device comprises a substrate, a transparent elastomer layer, a thin metal film deposited thereon, and a stack of organic and inorganic layers forming the light-emitting region. Upon cooling after metal deposition, the metal film undergoes controlled buckling due to thermal stress, forming a two-tiered topography composed of fine-scale and gross-scale undulations. The fine-scale buckling consists of submicron ridges and valleys with irregular spacing, while the gross-scale buckling manifests as larger, macroscopic undulations with spatial frequencies that vary across the device area. This dual-scale quasi-periodic structure disrupts coherent wave interference, enabling broadband angular light scattering without the formation of moiré patterns or color shifts.

The quasi-periodic buckling is engineered by controlling the thickness of the deposited metal layer and the thermal history of the device during fabrication. The fine-scale features arise from localized stress relaxation within the metal film, while the gross-scale features emerge from the mechanical compliance of the underlying elastomer substrate. Together, these structures act as a distributed diffuser that redirects trapped substrate modes into escape cones without altering the spectral purity of the emitted light. The resulting device exhibits a significant increase in external quantum efficiency and luminous efficacy compared to conventional planar or periodically buckled OLEDs, while maintaining color stability across viewing angles up to 70 degrees. Importantly, the fabrication process requires no additional lithographic steps, mask alignment, or post-processing, making it fully compatible with existing roll-to-roll and vacuum thermal evaporation manufacturing lines.

## DETAILED DISCLOSURE

The OLED device of the present invention includes a transparent substrate, upon which a transparent elastomer film is formed by spin-coating or casting a cured polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) layer. A thin metal film, preferably aluminum, is then deposited on the elastomer surface via thermal evaporation at a thickness ranging from 10 to 50 nanometers. Upon cooling from deposition temperature to ambient conditions, the metal film undergoes compressive stress due to its lower coefficient of thermal expansion relative to the elastomer, resulting in the formation of a buckled morphology. This buckling is not periodic but quasi-periodic, characterized by spatial variations in ridge spacing, height, and orientation that prevent long-range coherence.

The buckled structure is formed exclusively on the distal face of the OLED, opposite the emission surface, ensuring that light generated within the organic layers propagates through the buckled interface before exiting the device. The fine-scale buckling features, with lateral dimensions between 100 and 500 nanometers, act as subwavelength scatterers that couple evanescent waves into propagating modes. The gross-scale buckling, with periodicities ranging from 1 to 10 micrometers, introduces low-frequency modulation that disrupts the formation of standing waves and suppresses angular-dependent spectral shifts. As illustrated in FIG. 1 and FIG. 2, the resulting surface topography exhibits a fractal-like distribution of undulations, with no repeating unit cell larger than 5 micrometers.

The device is configured as a bottom-emitting OLED, wherein light exits through the substrate and the buckled interface. The transparent elastomer layer serves as both a stress-relieving medium and a planarization layer, ensuring uniform metal deposition. Following buckling, the aluminum film is partially oxidized in ambient air to form a thin layer of aluminum oxide, which enhances optical transparency and improves adhesion to subsequent layers. A transparent conductive oxide, such as indium tin oxide, is then deposited as the anode, followed by a hole injection layer of poly(3,4-ethylenedioxythiophene)-polystyrenesulfonic acid (PEDOT:PSS), a hole transport layer of 1,1-bis[(di-4-tolylamino)phenyl]cyclohexane (TAPC), a light emitting layer doped with phosphorescent emitters such as Ir(ppy)₃, Ir(MDQ)₂(acac), or FIrpic, an electron transport layer of tris [3-(3-pyridyl)-mesityl]borane (3TPYMB) or 3,5,30,50-tetra-3-pyridyl-[1,19;39,10]terphenyl (B3PyPB), an electron injection layer of lithium fluoride, and a metallic cathode of aluminum.

The buckling structure is confined entirely within the light-emitting area, ensuring that the scattering effect is localized to regions where photons are generated. The quasi-periodicity of the buckling is controlled by the thickness of the metal layer: thinner films (below 20 nm) produce predominantly fine-scale features, while thicker films (above 30 nm) generate more pronounced gross-scale undulations. The oxidation step further refines the surface morphology by smoothing sharp edges and reducing optical absorption losses.

The method of preparation begins with the formation of a PDMS film on a glass substrate, followed by thermal curing. A metal film is deposited using a shadow mask to define the active area, and the substrate is cooled at a controlled rate of 0.5 to 2 degrees Celsius per minute to induce uniform buckling. Subsequent deposition of the transparent conductor, organic layers, and cathode proceeds under standard vacuum conditions. The resulting device exhibits a 40% to 70% increase in luminous efficacy compared to a planar control device, with no measurable shift in CIE coordinates across viewing angles up to 70 degrees.

### METHODS AND MATERIALS

PDMS films were prepared by mixing Sylgard 184 base and curing agent at a 10:1 ratio, degassed under vacuum, and spin-coated onto cleaned glass substrates at 1000 rpm for 60 seconds, followed by curing at 75°C for two hours. Aluminum layers of varying thicknesses—ranging from 15 to 45 nanometers—were deposited via thermal evaporation at a rate of 0.1 to 0.3 nanometers per second under a base pressure of 5×10⁻⁶ Torr. The substrates were then subjected to ultraviolet-ozone (UVO) treatment for 10 to 20 minutes to modify the surface energy of the PDMS and influence the nucleation density of buckling. OLED devices were fabricated by sequentially depositing the hole injection, transport, emitting, electron transport, and cathode layers using thermal evaporation, with deposition rates and thicknesses optimized for each material. Devices with and without buckling were tested under constant current drive, and electroluminescence spectra, current efficiency, and angular dependence were measured using calibrated photodiodes and spectrometers. Results confirmed that quasi-periodic buckling significantly enhances luminous output without introducing angular color shift, demonstrating a clear advantage over both planar and periodically buckled structures.
