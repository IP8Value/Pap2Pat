# DESCRIPTION

Prostate cancer is one of the most prevalent malignancies affecting men worldwide, representing a leading cause of cancer-related morbidity and mortality. Its clinical behavior varies significantly among individuals, with some tumors remaining indolent for years while others rapidly progress to invasive and metastatic disease. Accurate stratification of disease aggressiveness remains a critical challenge in clinical oncology, as current diagnostic tools often fail to predict which patients will develop life-threatening complications such as bone metastasis. The progression of prostate cancer is closely linked to its interaction with the surrounding microenvironment, particularly the periprostatic adipose tissue, which serves not merely as a passive structural component but as an active participant in tumor dissemination. The molecular dialogue between cancer cells and adipocytes within this tissue has emerged as a key determinant of local invasion and systemic spread, yet the specific mediators and receptors governing this process have only recently begun to be elucidated.

The TNM classification system provides a standardized framework for staging prostate cancer based on tumor size, lymph node involvement, and distant metastasis. However, this system does not fully capture the biological heterogeneity underlying disease progression, particularly in cases where microscopic infiltration of adjacent adipose tissue precedes overt metastatic spread. To address this gap, prostate cancers have been further classified into four distinct clinical stages based on histopathological features, Gleason scoring, and biochemical recurrence patterns. These stages reflect not only anatomical extent but also underlying molecular drivers that dictate therapeutic response and long-term prognosis. Among the most significant biological factors influencing this progression is the presence and activity of periprostatic adipose tissue, a specialized fat depot that envelops the prostate gland and undergoes profound functional changes in response to metabolic and inflammatory stimuli.

Periprostatic adipose tissue is composed of a heterogeneous population of cells, including mature adipocytes, preadipocytes, immune cells, endothelial cells, and extracellular matrix components. Unlike adipose tissue in other anatomical locations, PPAT exhibits unique secretory properties that directly influence neighboring epithelial cells. Adipocytes within this tissue are metabolically active, dynamically responding to fluctuations in systemic energy balance by releasing a wide array of bioactive molecules known as adipokines. These secretions include cytokines, growth factors, and chemokines that modulate cell proliferation, angiogenesis, immune evasion, and motility. Notably, adipocytes are not inert energy storage units but rather endocrine cells capable of shaping the tumor microenvironment through paracrine signaling. In the context of prostate cancer, this secretory activity becomes pathological when it promotes the migration and invasion of malignant epithelial cells beyond the confines of the prostate capsule.

Obesity is a well-established risk factor for the development of aggressive prostate cancer, with epidemiological studies consistently demonstrating a correlation between elevated body mass index and increased incidence of high-grade tumors, biochemical recurrence, and cancer-specific mortality. This association is not merely coincidental but mechanistically rooted in the altered secretory profile of adipose tissue under conditions of metabolic dysregulation. Obese individuals exhibit hypertrophic adipocytes that secrete higher levels of pro-inflammatory mediators, including chemokines that attract immune and malignant cells alike. Chemokines are a family of small signaling proteins classified into four major subfamilies—C, CC, CXC, and CX3C—based on the arrangement of conserved cysteine residues. These molecules function as chemoattractants, guiding the directional migration of cells expressing specific G protein-coupled receptors. In prostate cancer, certain chemokine-receptor pairs have been implicated in tumor dissemination, with the CXCR4/CXCL12 axis historically considered central to bone homing. However, emerging evidence suggests that other chemokine pathways, particularly those involving CCR3 and its ligands, play a previously underappreciated role in both local and distant metastasis.

The interaction between CCR2 and its ligand CCL2 has been documented in prostate cancer models, primarily in the context of macrophage recruitment and tumor growth promotion. Similarly, CXCR4 and its ligand CXCL12 mediate homing of cancer cells to the bone marrow by mimicking the physiological trafficking of hematopoietic stem cells. Yet, these pathways alone cannot fully account for the observed patterns of metastatic spread, particularly in obese patients. Recent findings reveal that CCR3, a receptor previously associated with eosinophilic inflammation and allergic diseases, is aberrantly expressed in a subset of prostate cancer cells and is functionally linked to their migratory capacity. Preliminary studies in prostate cancer tissues have indicated that CCR3 expression correlates with advanced pathological stage and early biochemical recurrence, suggesting its potential as a biomarker of poor prognosis. However, prior investigations have been limited by small sample sizes, lack of functional validation, and insufficient characterization of the cellular sources of its ligands.

Therapeutic strategies targeting localized prostate cancer have traditionally focused on surgical resection, radiation, or androgen deprivation, yet these approaches often fail to prevent metastatic progression in high-risk patients. The D’Amico classification, which stratifies patients based on PSA levels, Gleason score, and clinical stage, remains the standard for risk assessment but does not guide the selection of targeted therapies. There is a pressing need for novel interventions that disrupt the molecular crosstalk between cancer cells and their adipose microenvironment. The inventors have identified a previously unrecognized role for CCR3 in mediating the directed migration of prostate cancer cells toward adipocyte-derived signals, particularly in the context of obesity and aging. In vitro and in vivo experiments demonstrate that CCR3 expression is not only elevated in aggressive prostate cancer cell lines but also correlates strongly with tumor invasiveness and biological recurrence. Furthermore, medullary adipocytes, a distinct population of bone marrow adipocytes, secrete elevated levels of CCL7, the primary ligand for CCR3, and this secretion is significantly amplified in obese subjects. Inhibition of either CCR3 or CCL7 substantially reduces tumor cell migration in experimental models, establishing this axis as a viable therapeutic target.

The invention encompasses a novel therapeutic strategy for the prevention or treatment of prostate cancer through the targeted inhibition of the CCL7/CCR3 signaling pathway. An inhibitor of CCR3 expression may be defined as any molecule capable of reducing the transcription, translation, or stability of the CCR3 gene product. Preferred embodiments include short hairpin RNA sequences specifically designed to silence the CCR3 gene via RNA interference, as well as antisense oligonucleotides complementary to CCR3 mRNA that induce degradation or translational blockade. Inhibitors of the CCL7/CCR3 interaction encompass competitive and non-competitive antagonists that bind to CCR3 and prevent ligand engagement, including small organic molecules, peptide-based inhibitors, peptidomimetics, and monoclonal antibodies directed against either CCR3 or CCL7. Aptamers selected to bind with high affinity to CCL7 or CCR3 represent another class of inhibitors capable of neutralizing this interaction. The invention further includes methods for identifying novel inhibitors through high-throughput screening assays that measure inhibition of CCL7-induced chemotaxis in prostate cancer cells.

The preferred embodiment of the invention involves the administration of such inhibitors in combination with pharmaceutically acceptable excipients suitable for systemic or localized delivery. Routes of administration include oral, intravenous, subcutaneous, intraperitoneal, or intraprostatic injection, depending on the stage and location of disease. The effective quantity of inhibitor is determined based on pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic parameters established in preclinical models, with dosing regimens tailored to achieve sustained suppression of CCR3 signaling without inducing systemic toxicity. A method for preventing or treating prostate cancer is defined by the administration of an effective quantity of a CCR3 or CCL7 inhibitor to a subject diagnosed with or at risk for prostate cancer, thereby reducing tumor cell migration, delaying local invasion, and inhibiting metastatic dissemination.

An ex vivo method for determining the degree of aggressiveness of a prostate cancer tumor involves obtaining a biopsy sample of prostate tumor cells and a control sample of healthy prostate epithelium from the same patient. The concentration of CCR3 protein or mRNA is measured in both samples using quantitative immunohistochemistry, flow cytometry, or real-time PCR. The measured value is compared to a reference concentration of CCR3 established in normal, non-neoplastic prostate epithelial tissue. Subjects exhibiting a statistically significant overexpression of CCR3 relative to this reference are classified as having a high-aggressiveness tumor phenotype, warranting more aggressive therapeutic intervention. This method provides a clinically actionable biomarker for risk stratification and personalized treatment planning.
