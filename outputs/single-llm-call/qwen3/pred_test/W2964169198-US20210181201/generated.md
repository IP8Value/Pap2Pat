## TECHNICAL FIELD

- define technical field

The present invention relates to the field of extracellular vesicle research and diagnostic biomarker analysis, specifically to the development and use of recombinant extracellular vesicles as calibrated biological reference materials for the standardization, calibration, normalization, and quality control of methods used to isolate, detect, and quantify endogenous extracellular vesicles from biological fluids. The invention provides a reproducible, trackable, and commutable biological reference system that mimics the physical and biochemical properties of naturally occurring extracellular vesicles, enabling accurate inter-laboratory comparisons, instrument calibration, and recovery efficiency assessments in clinical and research settings. The recombinant extracellular vesicles are engineered to contain a self-assembling viral structural protein fused to a heterologous marker, thereby permitting unambiguous discrimination from endogenous vesicles while preserving their native behavior during separation, storage, and analytical procedures. This technology addresses critical limitations in the current extracellular vesicle field, where the absence of standardized reference materials has hindered reproducibility, comparability, and clinical translation of vesicle-based diagnostics.

## BACKGROUND

- motivate liquid biopsies

Liquid biopsies have emerged as a transformative approach for non-invasive disease detection, monitoring, and therapeutic response assessment, particularly in oncology, neurodegenerative disorders, and autoimmune conditions. The analysis of biofluids such as plasma, serum, urine, and cerebrospinal fluid for molecular signatures offers a minimally invasive alternative to tissue biopsies, enabling repeated sampling over time and capturing dynamic changes in disease states. Among the most promising components of liquid biopsies are extracellular vesicles, which carry a molecular payload reflective of their cell of origin, including proteins, nucleic acids, lipids, and metabolites. These vesicles are released by both healthy and diseased cells and are increasingly recognized as key mediators of intercellular communication and disease progression. Their presence in circulation and stability in biofluids make them ideal candidates for biomarker discovery and diagnostic development.

- introduce extracellular vesicles

Extracellular vesicles are nano-sized, membrane-bound particles secreted by virtually all cell types through distinct biogenesis pathways, including the endosomal sorting complex required for transport (ESCRT)-dependent and ESCRT-independent mechanisms. These vesicles range in size from approximately 30 to 1000 nanometers and are classified into subtypes such as exosomes, microvesicles, and apoptotic bodies, although current analytical limitations often prevent precise categorization in clinical samples. Extracellular vesicles are enriched with specific surface markers such as tetraspanins CD9, CD63, and CD81, as well as intraluminal proteins including ALIX, TSG101, and flotillin-1, and they encapsulate a diverse array of nucleic acids and lipids that mirror the molecular profile of their parent cells.

- describe structure of EV

The structural architecture of extracellular vesicles consists of a lipid bilayer membrane that encloses a luminal space containing cytosolic components derived from the parent cell. The membrane is composed of a complex mixture of phospholipids, sphingolipids, cholesterol, and glycolipids, arranged in a non-random, asymmetric fashion that differs from the plasma membrane of the originating cell. This lipid composition contributes to the vesicle’s buoyant density, mechanical stability, and interaction with biological matrices. The surface of extracellular vesicles displays transmembrane and lipid-anchored proteins that mediate cellular uptake, immune recognition, and targeting to recipient cells. The structural integrity of these vesicles is essential for their biological function and analytical detection, as degradation or aggregation can lead to loss of molecular content and misrepresentation of concentration.

- discuss role of EV in cancer

In cancer, extracellular vesicles play a pivotal role in tumor microenvironment remodeling, immune evasion, metastatic niche preparation, and drug resistance. Tumor-derived vesicles carry oncogenic proteins, mutant nucleic acids, and immunosuppressive ligands that facilitate communication between cancer cells and stromal components, promoting angiogenesis, fibroblast activation, and pre-metastatic organ colonization. Their cargo reflects the genetic and phenotypic heterogeneity of the tumor, making them valuable sources of diagnostic and prognostic information. Detection and quantification of tumor-derived extracellular vesicles in patient plasma or serum have shown promise in early cancer diagnosis, monitoring treatment response, and detecting minimal residual disease. However, the clinical utility of these vesicles is currently constrained by technical variability in isolation and detection methods.

- limitations of current EV research

Current extracellular vesicle research is hindered by a lack of standardized protocols for isolation, characterization, and quantification. Numerous methods—including differential ultracentrifugation, density gradient centrifugation, size exclusion chromatography, precipitation kits, and immunoaffinity capture—are employed across laboratories, each with distinct recovery efficiencies, purity profiles, and biases toward specific vesicle subpopulations. Furthermore, the absence of universally accepted reference materials prevents accurate calibration of instruments such as nanoparticle tracking analyzers, flow cytometers, and microplate readers, leading to significant inter-laboratory variability in reported vesicle concentrations. Synthetic particles such as polystyrene beads or liposomes, often used as surrogates, fail to replicate the refractive index, surface chemistry, and biochemical composition of native vesicles, resulting in systematic underestimation of particle size and concentration. Biological reference materials derived from cell culture supernatants or biofluids lack specificity and traceability, rendering them unsuitable for quantitative normalization or method validation.

- need for biological reference material

There is an urgent and unmet need for a biological reference material that possesses the physical and biochemical characteristics of endogenous extracellular vesicles, is genetically or chemically trackable to distinguish it from native vesicles, and behaves identically to sample vesicles under all experimental conditions. Such a material would enable the calibration of optical and non-optical detection systems, the determination of recovery efficiencies across isolation protocols, the normalization of inter-user and inter-assay variability, and the validation of downstream analytical techniques such as proteomics, transcriptomics, and lipidomics. The development of a standardized, stable, and universally applicable reference material is essential to advance extracellular vesicle research from a fragmented, qualitative field into a robust, quantitative, and clinically translatable discipline.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

- introduce recombinant extracellular vesicles (rEV) as biological reference material

The present invention introduces recombinant extracellular vesicles (rEV) as a novel class of biological reference material designed to overcome the limitations of existing calibration standards in extracellular vesicle research. These rEV are produced through the controlled expression of a self-assembling viral structural protein fused to a heterologous marker in mammalian cells, resulting in the formation of vesicle-like particles that faithfully mimic the size, density, morphology, and molecular composition of endogenous extracellular vesicles. Unlike synthetic particles or unmodified biological samples, rEV are derived from a biological system that recapitulates the natural biogenesis pathways of extracellular vesicles, ensuring structural and functional commutability with native vesicles across diverse biofluids and analytical platforms.

- describe rEV composition: self-assembling protein and heterologous marker

The recombinant extracellular vesicles of the invention comprise a self-assembling protein that drives the formation of a lipid bilayer vesicle and a heterologous marker that enables specific detection and quantification without interfering with vesicle integrity or behavior. The self-assembling protein is derived from the gag polyprotein of the human immunodeficiency virus type 1 (HIV-1), which, when expressed in isolation, spontaneously oligomerizes at the plasma membrane and recruits endogenous ESCRT machinery to bud off as membrane-enclosed particles. The heterologous marker is covalently fused to the C-terminus of the gag protein and may be selected from a fluorescent protein, a nucleic acid sequence encoding a detectable transcript, or a protein epitope recognized by a specific antibody. This fusion ensures that the marker is encapsulated within the lumen or embedded in the membrane of the vesicle, thereby providing a stable, non-leaky signal that distinguishes rEV from endogenous vesicles while preserving their native biophysical properties.

- explain physical and biochemical characteristics of rEV

The recombinant extracellular vesicles exhibit physical characteristics including a hydrodynamic diameter of 50 to 180 nanometers, a buoyant density of 1.086 to 1.119 grams per milliliter, a zeta potential of approximately −32 millivolts, and a refractive index of approximately 1.37, all of which are indistinguishable from those of endogenous extracellular vesicles derived from human plasma, urine, or cancer cell lines. Biochemically, rEV are enriched in canonical extracellular vesicle markers such as ALIX, TSG101, syntenin-1, flotillin-1, CD9, CD81, and CD63, as confirmed by Western blot, mass spectrometry, and immune-electron microscopy. Their lipid composition includes phosphatidylcholine, phosphatidylethanolamine, phosphatidylserine, sphingomyelin, and lysophospholipids in proportions comparable to those of native vesicles, and their cholesterol content per particle is statistically equivalent to that of endogenous vesicles. These characteristics ensure that rEV behave as authentic extracellular vesicles during isolation, storage, and detection procedures.

- compare rEV with endogenous sample EV

When subjected to identical isolation protocols—including density gradient centrifugation, size exclusion chromatography, and differential ultracentrifugation—recombinant extracellular vesicles co-fractionate with endogenous extracellular vesicles in identical density ranges and elution volumes. Their size distribution, as measured by nanoparticle tracking analysis, and their morphology, as visualized by transmission electron microscopy, are indistinguishable from those of endogenous vesicles isolated from breast cancer cells, cancer-associated fibroblasts, or patient plasma. Proteomic and lipidomic analyses reveal that rEV share over 89% of their protein content and exhibit a Spearman correlation coefficient of 0.921 with endogenous vesicles, confirming their biochemical fidelity. This high degree of similarity ensures that rEV are commutable, meaning they respond to experimental conditions in the same manner as native vesicles, thereby enabling accurate assessment of recovery efficiency and method performance.

- describe method to determine recovery rate of sample EV

The invention provides a method for determining the recovery rate of endogenous extracellular vesicles from a biofluid sample by spiking a known quantity of recombinant extracellular vesicles into the sample prior to isolation, followed by quantification of the recovered recombinant vesicles and calculation of the percentage of input material that is recovered. This recovery rate is then used to normalize the measured concentration of endogenous vesicles, correcting for losses incurred during isolation and enabling accurate inter-sample and inter-laboratory comparisons. The method eliminates the need for indirect quantification via protein concentration or arbitrary normalization factors, providing a direct, quantitative, and traceable measure of vesicle yield.

- outline method steps: mixing, isolating, detecting, and determining recovery rate

The method comprises the following steps: first, a defined quantity of recombinant extracellular vesicles is mixed with a biofluid sample to form a spiked mixture; second, the spiked mixture is subjected to an isolation procedure selected from density gradient centrifugation, size exclusion chromatography, differential ultracentrifugation, or immunoaffinity capture; third, the recombinant extracellular vesicles remaining in the isolated fraction are detected and quantified using a method specific to the heterologous marker, such as fluorescence measurement, enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay, or reverse transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction; fourth, the recovery rate is calculated as the ratio of the quantity of recombinant extracellular vesicles detected after isolation to the quantity initially spiked, expressed as a percentage. This recovery rate is then applied to the measured concentration of endogenous extracellular vesicles to derive a normalized, accurate count per volume of original biofluid.

- define "sample extracellular vesicle (sample EV)"

A sample extracellular vesicle, or sample EV, refers to any naturally occurring extracellular vesicle that is isolated from a biological fluid or tissue culture supernatant and that is the subject of analysis in a diagnostic, research, or clinical context. Sample EVs are derived from endogenous cellular processes and contain a molecular cargo reflective of their cell of origin, including proteins, nucleic acids, lipids, and metabolites. Sample EVs are distinguished from recombinant extracellular vesicles by the absence of the heterologous marker introduced in the recombinant vesicles of the invention.

- define "endogenous EV"

An endogenous extracellular vesicle, or endogenous EV, is a naturally produced extracellular vesicle that is secreted by a living cell through physiological or pathological mechanisms without genetic or chemical modification. Endogenous EVs are characterized by their native lipid composition, surface protein expression, and molecular cargo, and they are the target analytes in all applications of the present invention. The term encompasses vesicles derived from any cell type, including but not limited to tumor cells, immune cells, endothelial cells, and fibroblasts, and includes vesicles isolated from any biofluid, including plasma, serum, urine, cerebrospinal fluid, saliva, and ascites.

- define "heterologous marker"

A heterologous marker is a molecular entity that is not naturally expressed in the host cell or in endogenous extracellular vesicles and that is introduced into the recombinant extracellular vesicles to enable their specific detection, quantification, and discrimination from endogenous vesicles. The heterologous marker may be a protein, a nucleic acid sequence, or a chemical tag that is genetically fused to the self-assembling protein or chemically conjugated to the vesicle membrane. The marker must be detectable by a method that is sensitive, specific, and compatible with standard laboratory instrumentation, and it must not alter the physical or biochemical properties of the vesicle.

- describe examples of heterologous markers

Examples of heterologous markers include fluorescent proteins such as enhanced green fluorescent protein (EGFP), mCherry, or mNeonGreen; nucleic acid sequences encoding detectable transcripts such as EGFP mRNA, luciferase mRNA, or a synthetic RNA sequence with a unique 3’ untranslated region; epitope tags such as FLAG, HA, or Myc; and enzymatic reporters such as β-galactosidase or alkaline phosphatase. In a preferred embodiment, the heterologous marker is a fusion protein consisting of the HIV-1 gag polyprotein covalently linked to enhanced green fluorescent protein, enabling simultaneous detection via fluorescence, Western blot, and RT-qPCR.

- describe method with heterologous nucleic acid molecule or protein

The invention encompasses methods wherein the heterologous marker is a nucleic acid molecule or a protein that is encapsulated within the lumen of the recombinant extracellular vesicle. When the marker is a nucleic acid molecule, such as a messenger RNA encoding a fluorescent protein, it is transcribed and packaged during vesicle biogenesis and can be quantified by reverse transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction following RNA extraction. When the marker is a protein, such as a fusion of gag and EGFP, it is incorporated into the vesicle membrane or lumen during assembly and can be detected by immunoassay, fluorescence microscopy, or flow cytometry. Both forms of the marker permit quantitative analysis without requiring genetic modification of the sample source.

- describe method with fused heterologous protein

In a preferred embodiment, the heterologous marker is a protein that is genetically fused to the C-terminus of the self-assembling protein, such that the fusion protein is expressed as a single polypeptide chain and incorporated into the recombinant extracellular vesicle during its formation. The fusion protein retains the self-assembling properties of the parent protein and ensures that the marker is stably associated with the vesicle structure. The fusion may be designed to preserve membrane topology, with the heterologous protein oriented toward the lumen or the extravesicular surface, depending on the desired detection strategy. This method ensures that the marker is uniformly distributed across all recombinant vesicles and is not subject to dissociation or degradation during isolation or storage.

- describe method with fused heterologous nucleic acid

In an alternative embodiment, the heterologous marker is a nucleic acid sequence that is co-packaged with the self-assembling protein during vesicle biogenesis. The nucleic acid sequence may be engineered to contain a specific RNA-binding motif, such as the HIV-1 packaging signal (PSI), which directs its encapsulation into the vesicle lumen in association with the gag polyprotein. This method allows for the production of recombinant extracellular vesicles that contain a defined quantity of a detectable RNA transcript, enabling quantification by RT-qPCR with high sensitivity and dynamic range. The nucleic acid may be derived from a reporter gene such as EGFP, luciferase, or a synthetic sequence with no homology to human transcripts.

- outline detecting step: determining amount of heterologous nucleic acid

The detecting step includes the extraction of nucleic acids from the recombinant extracellular vesicles, followed by reverse transcription to generate complementary DNA and quantitative polymerase chain reaction amplification using primers specific to the heterologous nucleic acid sequence. The cycle threshold (Ct) value obtained from the qPCR reaction is correlated with the number of recombinant vesicles present in the sample using a standard curve generated from serial dilutions of known quantities of recombinant vesicles. This allows for the absolute quantification of recombinant vesicles in the sample, which is then used to calculate the recovery rate of endogenous vesicles.

- describe examples of detecting techniques

Examples of detecting techniques include fluorescence-based methods such as fluorescent nanoparticle tracking analysis and fluorescence-triggered high-resolution flow cytometry; protein-based methods such as enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay for gag-derived epitopes or Western blot detection of fusion proteins; nucleic acid-based methods such as reverse transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction for heterologous mRNA; and microplate reader-based quantification of fluorescence intensity. Each method is validated for linear dynamic range, limit of detection, and precision, and may be selected based on the available instrumentation and the nature of the heterologous marker.

- describe preferred embodiment: light-emitting protein or mRNA

The preferred embodiment of the invention utilizes a recombinant extracellular vesicle comprising a gag-EGFP fusion protein and encapsulated EGFP mRNA. The enhanced green fluorescent protein serves as a highly sensitive, non-toxic, and stable fluorescent marker that enables detection by multiple orthogonal methods. The presence of EGFP mRNA allows for independent quantification by RT-qPCR, providing a dual-mode detection system that enhances accuracy and reliability. The combination of fluorescence and RNA-based detection ensures that recombinant vesicles can be tracked across diverse experimental conditions, including those involving protease treatment, lipid extraction, or membrane disruption.

- define "self-assembling protein"

A self-assembling protein is a polypeptide that, when expressed in a host cell, spontaneously oligomerizes and recruits endogenous cellular machinery to form a membrane-bound vesicle structure without requiring additional viral components or exogenous scaffolding. The self-assembling protein is derived from a viral structural protein, such as the HIV-1 gag polyprotein, which possesses intrinsic membrane-binding, oligomerization, and budding capabilities. The protein must be capable of forming vesicles of appropriate size and density that are indistinguishable from endogenous extracellular vesicles in terms of biophysical properties and biochemical composition.

- describe examples of self-assembling proteins

Examples of self-assembling proteins include the HIV-1 gag polyprotein, the human immunodeficiency virus type 2 gag protein, the murine leukemia virus gag protein, the hepatitis B virus core protein, and the influenza virus matrix protein M1. In a preferred embodiment, the self-assembling protein is the HIV-1 gag polyprotein, which contains the matrix (MA), capsid (CA), nucleocapsid (NC), and p6 domains necessary for membrane targeting, oligomerization, and ESCRT-mediated budding. The gag protein may be modified to remove viral enzymatic domains such as protease and reverse transcriptase to ensure biosafety while preserving its structural and budding functions.

- describe method with chosen self-assembling protein

The method of producing recombinant extracellular vesicles involves transfecting a mammalian host cell, such as HEK293T, with a plasmid encoding the chosen self-assembling protein fused to a heterologous marker. The transfected cells are cultured under standard conditions, and the recombinant vesicles are secreted into the conditioned medium over a defined period, typically 24 to 72 hours. The conditioned medium is then harvested, clarified by low-speed centrifugation and filtration, concentrated, and subjected to density gradient centrifugation or size exclusion chromatography to isolate the recombinant vesicles. The resulting preparation is enriched for vesicles containing the self-assembling protein and the heterologous marker, with minimal contamination from cellular debris or non-vesicular proteins.

- define "biofluid sample"

A biofluid sample is any biological fluid derived from a living organism that contains extracellular vesicles and is suitable for diagnostic or research analysis. Biofluid samples include, but are not limited to, plasma, serum, urine, cerebrospinal fluid, saliva, bronchoalveolar lavage fluid, ascites, amniotic fluid, and seminal fluid. The biofluid sample may be collected from healthy individuals or patients with disease and may be stored frozen or processed immediately. The sample may be pre-treated to remove cells, platelets, or large debris prior to spiking with recombinant extracellular vesicles.

- describe examples of biofluid samples

Examples of biofluid samples include plasma derived from venous blood collected in citrate or EDTA tubes, serum obtained from clotted blood, urine collected from healthy volunteers or cancer patients, cerebrospinal fluid obtained by lumbar puncture, and saliva collected via passive drool or oral swab. Each biofluid may contain endogenous extracellular vesicles derived from multiple cell types, and the recombinant extracellular vesicles of the invention are compatible with all of these sample types, enabling their use across diverse clinical and research applications.

- describe method with plasma or serum sample

The method may be applied to plasma or serum samples by spiking a known quantity of recombinant extracellular vesicles into 1 to 5 milliliters of sample, followed by isolation using density gradient centrifugation, size exclusion chromatography, or differential ultracentrifugation. The spiked sample is processed identically to unspiked controls, and the recovery of recombinant vesicles is quantified using fluorescence, ELISA, or RT-qPCR. The recovery rate is then used to normalize the concentration of endogenous vesicles in the plasma or serum, enabling accurate comparison between patient and control samples.

- outline isolation step: any type of isolation method

The isolation step may be performed using any method commonly employed for extracellular vesicle purification, including but not limited to differential ultracentrifugation, density gradient centrifugation, size exclusion chromatography, polymer-based precipitation, immunoaffinity capture, ultrafiltration, microfluidic separation, or acoustic-based isolation. The recombinant extracellular vesicles are compatible with all of these methods and co-isolate with endogenous vesicles according to their physical and biochemical properties, ensuring that the recovery rate accurately reflects the performance of the chosen isolation protocol.

- describe detecting step: quantifying rEV

The detecting step involves the quantification of recombinant extracellular vesicles in the isolated fraction using a method specific to the heterologous marker. This may include measuring fluorescence intensity using a microplate reader or flow cytometer, detecting the gag protein using an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay for p24, quantifying EGFP mRNA via reverse transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction, or visualizing the fusion protein via Western blot. The quantity of recombinant vesicles detected is compared to the known quantity spiked into the sample to determine the recovery rate.

- describe examples of detecting techniques

Examples of detecting techniques include fluorescent nanoparticle tracking analysis for quantification of fluorescent vesicles, high-resolution flow cytometry for detection of single vesicles based on fluorescence and scatter, enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay for quantification of gag-derived epitopes, Western blot for semi-quantitative detection of fusion protein bands, RT-qPCR for quantification of encapsulated mRNA, and spectrophotometric measurement of fluorescence intensity in microplate wells. Each technique is validated for linearity, sensitivity, and reproducibility, and may be selected based on the available instrumentation and the nature of the heterologous marker.

- describe method with separation step

The method may include an additional separation step to physically segregate recombinant extracellular vesicles from endogenous vesicles after recovery quantification. This separation may be achieved by exploiting a unique property of the recombinant vesicles, such as the presence of a chemically conjugated polyethylene glycol moiety or an epitope tag not present on endogenous vesicles. This step enables downstream analysis of endogenous vesicles without interference from the recombinant marker, facilitating proteomic, lipidomic, or transcriptomic profiling.

- describe separation step: density-based separation

In one embodiment, the separation step involves density-based separation using an iodixanol or sucrose density gradient. Recombinant extracellular vesicles modified with polyethylene glycol exhibit a buoyant density identical to endogenous vesicles, whereas unmodified recombinant vesicles spiked in plasma may shift to higher densities due to protein corona formation. This shift can be reversed by protease treatment, allowing for selective isolation of endogenous vesicles from the recombinant population. Density-based separation enables the physical separation of recombinant and endogenous vesicles for independent downstream analysis.

- describe detecting step: detecting rEV in first fraction

After density-based separation, the recombinant extracellular vesicles are detected in the first fraction corresponding to their buoyant density, typically between 1.086 and 1.119 grams per milliliter. Detection is performed using fluorescence, ELISA, or RT-qPCR, and the quantity in this fraction is used to calculate the recovery rate. The presence of recombinant vesicles in this fraction confirms their commutability with endogenous vesicles, while their absence in other fractions confirms the specificity of the separation.

- describe examples of detecting techniques

Examples of detecting techniques include fluorescent nanoparticle tracking analysis to count fluorescent vesicles in each density fraction, anti-p24 enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay to quantify gag protein in pooled fractions, and RT-qPCR to measure EGFP mRNA levels in each fraction. These methods provide quantitative data that can be correlated with the number of endogenous vesicles in the same fraction, enabling accurate normalization.

- describe method with additional biophysical isolation step

The method may include an additional biophysical isolation step to further purify the recombinant extracellular vesicles or to separate them from endogenous vesicles. This step may involve size exclusion chromatography, ultrafiltration, or magnetic bead-based capture using an antibody specific to the heterologous marker. The biophysical step is performed after quantification of recovery and before downstream analysis of endogenous vesicles to ensure that the recombinant marker does not interfere with subsequent assays.

- describe examples of biophysical isolation steps

Examples of biophysical isolation steps include size exclusion chromatography using Sepharose CL-2B or Superose 6 columns, ultrafiltration using 10 kDa molecular weight cutoff filters, and magnetic capture using antibody-coated beads directed against polyethylene glycol or an epitope tag on the recombinant vesicle. These steps are optimized to retain recombinant vesicles while removing endogenous vesicles or vice versa, depending on the experimental objective.

- describe method with size exclusion chromatography

In a preferred embodiment, size exclusion chromatography is used to separate recombinant extracellular vesicles from endogenous vesicles after quantification. The recombinant vesicles are modified with polyethylene glycol, which allows them to be captured by anti-PEG antibodies immobilized on magnetic beads, while endogenous vesicles pass through unaffected. The elution profile of recombinant vesicles is identical to that of endogenous vesicles, confirming their commutability, and the anti-PEG capture provides a clean separation for downstream analysis.

- describe detecting step: detecting rEV in first fraction

Following size exclusion chromatography, the recombinant extracellular vesicles are detected in the first elution fraction corresponding to their hydrodynamic size, typically between 50 and 180 nanometers. Detection is performed using fluorescence, ELISA, or RT-qPCR, and the quantity in this fraction is used to calculate the recovery rate. The presence of recombinant vesicles in this fraction confirms their compatibility with size-based separation methods.

- describe examples of detecting techniques

Examples of detecting techniques include fluorescent nanoparticle tracking analysis to quantify fluorescent vesicles in each elution fraction, anti-p24 enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay to measure gag protein concentration, and RT-qPCR to quantify EGFP mRNA levels. These methods are linear over a wide dynamic range and provide absolute quantification of recombinant vesicles.

- describe method with protease treatment

The method may include a protease treatment step to remove protein corona formed on recombinant extracellular vesicles when spiked into plasma. Treatment with proteinase K at a concentration of 1 mg/mL for 60 minutes at 37°C removes plasma proteins bound to the vesicle surface without affecting the integrity of the vesicle membrane or the heterologous marker. This treatment reverses the density shift induced by corona formation and restores the buoyant density of recombinant vesicles to that of endogenous vesicles, enabling accurate density-based separation.

- describe method with lipid conjugated PEG

The method may include the post-insertion conjugation of recombinant extracellular vesicles with a lipid-conjugated polyethylene glycol molecule, such as DMPE-PEG, to mask the vesicle surface and prevent protein corona formation. This modification increases the hydrodynamic size of the vesicles by approximately 10 nanometers but does not alter their fluorescence, density, or morphology. The PEG moiety provides a unique epitope for specific capture using anti-PEG antibodies, enabling complete segregation of recombinant vesicles from endogenous vesicles after recovery quantification.

- describe method with immune precipitation

The method may include an immune precipitation step using antibodies directed against the heterologous marker, such as anti-PEG, anti-FLAG, or anti-EGFP, immobilized on magnetic beads. The recombinant extracellular vesicles are captured by the antibody-coated beads, while endogenous vesicles remain in the supernatant. The captured vesicles are then quantified to determine recovery, and the supernatant is used for downstream analysis of endogenous vesicles. This method provides a highly specific and efficient means of separating recombinant and endogenous vesicles.

- describe method with incubation with immunoglobulin proteins

The method may include incubation of recombinant extracellular vesicles with physiological concentrations of immunoglobulins, such as IgG or IgM, to induce density shift and enable separation by density gradient centrifugation. This step is useful for validating the commutability of recombinant vesicles under conditions that mimic in vivo protein binding. The density shift is reversible by protease treatment, allowing for subsequent isolation of endogenous vesicles.

- describe method to calibrate device for sample EV analysis

The invention provides a method to calibrate devices used for extracellular vesicle analysis, such as nanoparticle tracking analyzers, flow cytometers, or microplate readers, by spiking a known quantity of recombinant extracellular vesicles into a fluid matrix and analyzing the device’s ability to detect and quantify the recombinant vesicles. The device settings are adjusted until the measured quantity of recombinant vesicles matches the known input quantity, thereby establishing a calibration curve that corrects for instrument-specific biases in size, scatter, and fluorescence detection.

- outline calibration method steps: mixing, introducing, analyzing, and deriving

The calibration method comprises the following steps: first, a defined quantity of recombinant extracellular vesicles is mixed with a fluid matrix such as phosphate-buffered saline or plasma; second, the spiked mixture is introduced into the device for analysis; third, the device measures a property of the recombinant vesicles, such as particle concentration, fluorescence intensity, or scatter signal; fourth, the measured value is compared to the known input quantity, and the device settings are adjusted to derive a calibration factor that corrects for systematic error. This calibration factor is then applied to all subsequent analyses of endogenous vesicles.

- define "to calibrate"

To calibrate means to adjust the output of a measurement device so that its readings correspond accurately to known reference values, thereby minimizing systematic error and ensuring reproducibility across instruments and operators.

- define "fluid"

A fluid refers to any liquid or suspension that can be analyzed by the device and that contains or is compatible with extracellular vesicles. Fluids include phosphate-buffered saline, plasma, serum, urine, cerebrospinal fluid, and cell culture medium.

- define "optionally"

Optionally means that a particular step, component, or condition may be included or excluded depending on the experimental objective, without departing from the scope of the invention.

- define "a property"

A property refers to a measurable characteristic of a recombinant extracellular vesicle that is used for detection or quantification, including but not limited to particle concentration, size distribution, fluorescence intensity, zeta potential, refractive index, or protein content.

- describe examples of analyzing a property

Examples of analyzing a property include measuring particle concentration using nanoparticle tracking analysis, determining size distribution using dynamic light scattering, quantifying fluorescence intensity using a microplate reader, assessing zeta potential using laser Doppler electrophoresis, and evaluating refractive index using Mie theory applied to NTA data.

- define "device settings"

Device settings refer to the operational parameters of an analytical instrument, such as laser power, detector gain, threshold level, flow rate, or integration time, that influence the detection and quantification of extracellular vesicles.

- describe examples of devices to be calibrated

Examples of devices to be calibrated include nanoparticle tracking analyzers, high-resolution flow cytometers, microplate readers, digital PCR systems, mass spectrometers, and ultracentrifuges equipped with automated fraction collectors.

- describe method to evaluate EV isolation

The invention provides a method to evaluate the performance of extracellular vesicle isolation protocols by spiking recombinant extracellular vesicles into a biofluid sample, subjecting the sample to the isolation protocol, and quantifying the recovery of recombinant vesicles. The recovery rate serves as a direct measure of the efficiency and reproducibility of the isolation method.

- outline evaluation method steps: mixing, isolating, and detecting

The evaluation method comprises the following steps: first, a known quantity of recombinant extracellular vesicles is mixed with a biofluid sample; second, the mixture is subjected to an isolation procedure; third, the recombinant extracellular vesicles remaining in the isolated fraction are detected and quantified using a method specific to the heterologous marker. The recovery rate is calculated and compared across different isolation methods to determine the most efficient and reproducible protocol.

### EXAMPLES

- list antibodies used for immunostaining

The following primary antibodies were used for immunostaining: mouse monoclonal anti-ALIX (1:1000, #2171), rabbit monoclonal anti-CD9 clone D3H4P (1:1000, #13403S), mouse monoclonal anti-CD63 clone MEM-259 (1:200, #ab8219), rabbit monoclonal anti-syntenin-1 (1:1000, #ab133267), mouse monoclonal anti-CD81 (1:1000, #SC-166029), mouse monoclonal anti-TSG101 (1:100, #SC-7964), mouse monoclonal anti-flotillin-1 (1:1000, #610820), rat anti-mouse CD9 (1:1000, #553758), mouse monoclonal anti-green fluorescent protein (GFP) (1:1000, #MAB3580), mouse monoclonal anti-α-tubulin (1:4000), sheep anti-mouse horseradish peroxidase-linked antibody (1:3000, #NA931V), and donkey anti-rabbit horseradish peroxidase-linked antibody (1:4000, #NA934V). For immune-electron microscopy, a primary mouse monoclonal anti-CD63 antibody (1:50, clone H5C6) and a rabbit anti-mouse IgG (1:2000) conjugated to 10 nm gold-labeled Protein A were used.

- describe cell culture and transfection conditions

Human HEK293T cells were cultured in high-glucose DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum, 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 µg/mL streptomycin at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 10% CO₂. Cells were passaged at 70–80% confluency and discarded after ten passages. Transfection was performed using linear polyethyleneimine (PEI) at a PEI:DNA ratio of 5:1, with a final DNA concentration of 1 µg/mL. Cells were transfected with pMET7-gag-EGFP or empty pMET7mcs plasmid and incubated for 72 hours prior to conditioned medium collection.

- outline rEV isolation procedure

Conditioned medium was harvested, centrifuged at 200 × g for 10 minutes, filtered through 0.45 µm cellulose acetate, and concentrated 300-fold using a 10 kDa cutoff centrifugal filter. The concentrated medium was filtered through a 0.2 µm filter and loaded onto a discontinuous OptiPrep density gradient composed of 40%, 20%, 10%, and 5% iodixanol solutions. The gradient was centrifuged at 100,000 × g for 18 hours at 4 °C. Fractions 8–10, corresponding to a density of 1.086–1.119 g/mL, were pooled, diluted in PBS, and pelleted at 100,000 × g for 3 hours. The pellet was resuspended in 100 µL PBS and stored in 10 µL aliquots at −80 °C.

- describe cell viability measurement

Cell viability was assessed using a Countess Automatic Cell Counter with 0.1% trypan blue exclusion. Cells were trypsinized at 72 hours post-transfection, diluted 1:10 in trypan blue solution, and counted in duplicate. Viability was consistently greater than 90% across all transfection conditions.

- detail Optiprep density gradient ultracentrifugation

OptiPrep density gradient ultracentrifugation was performed as described above, with gradients prepared using a Biomek 4000 automated workstation. Density of each fraction was determined by measuring absorbance at 340 nm and interpolating against a standard curve of known iodixanol concentrations. Fractions were collected in 1 mL increments and processed as described.

- outline sample collection from patients

Venous blood was collected from healthy volunteers and breast cancer patients using Venosafe-citrate tubes. Blood was centrifuged at 2500 × g for 15 minutes, followed by a second centrifugation at 2500 × g for 15 minutes to obtain platelet-depleted plasma. Plasma was aliquoted and stored at −80 °C until use. Urine samples were collected from healthy volunteers, centrifuged at 1000 × g for 10 minutes, and processed immediately.

- describe EV isolation from plasma

Plasma was diluted 1:1 in PBS and subjected to differential ultracentrifugation (2000 × g for 30 minutes, 12,000 × g for 45 minutes, and 110,000 × g for 70 minutes) or size exclusion chromatography using a 10 mL syringe packed with Sepharose CL-2B. For SEC, 2 mL plasma was loaded, and 1 mL fractions were collected under gravitational flow.

- detail differential ultracentrifugation

Differential ultracentrifugation was performed as described by Thery et al., with two rounds of 110,000 × g centrifugation using SW32.1 Ti and SW55 Ti rotors. The final pellet was resuspended in 100 µL PBS.

- outline size exclusion chromatography

Size exclusion chromatography was performed using a 10 mL syringe packed with 10 mL pre-washed Sepharose CL-2B. Plasma was loaded, and 1 mL fractions were collected under gravitational flow. Fractions 4–6 were pooled for further analysis.

- describe additional purification steps

Following SEC, fractions 4–6 were pooled and concentrated to 1 mL using a 10 kDa cutoff filter. The concentrated sample was layered onto an OptiPrep density gradient and centrifuged as described. Proteinase K treatment was performed at 1 mg/mL for 60 minutes at 37 °C prior to density gradient centrifugation.

- outline ExoQuick-TC protocol

ExoQuick-TC was used according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Plasma was mixed with ExoQuick-TC reagent at a 1:5 ratio, incubated at 4 °C for 30 minutes, and centrifuged at 1500 × g for 30 minutes. The pellet was resuspended in PBS.

- describe immune electron microscopy

Isolated vesicles were deposited on glow-discharged formvar carbon-coated grids, blocked with 1% BSA in PBS, and incubated with primary anti-CD63 antibody for 20 minutes. After washing, grids were incubated with rabbit anti-mouse IgG for 20 minutes, followed by 10 nm gold-conjugated Protein A for 20 minutes. Grids were stained with uranyl acetate and imaged using a Tecnai Spirit transmission electron microscope.

- outline nanoparticle tracking analysis

Nanoparticle tracking analysis was performed using a NanoSight LM10-HS microscope equipped with a 488 nm laser. Three 30-second videos were recorded for each sample at a camera level of 13 and detection threshold of 3. For fluorescent NTA, a 500 nm longpass filter was used, and the camera level was increased to 16. Data were analyzed using NTA software 3.3.

- describe high resolution flow cytometry

High-resolution flow cytometry was performed on a BD Influx flow cytometer using a 200 mW 488 nm laser and a 140 µm nozzle. Samples were triggered on fluorescence, and forward scatter was measured at a reduced wide-angle angle of 15–25°. Fluorescence thresholds were set to exclude background noise, and event rates were maintained below 10,000/s.

- detail zeta potential measurements

Zeta potential was measured using a Zetasizer Nano ZS in disposable folded capillary cells at 22 °C. Vesicles were suspended in distilled water, and five measurements of 10–100 runs were performed using the automated mode. Results were averaged across replicates.

- outline rEV quantification via fluorescence intensity measurement

Fluorescence intensity was measured using a SpectraMax Paradigm microplate reader with a 488 nm excitation and 500 nm emission filter. Samples were diluted in PBS, and relative fluorescence units were calculated by subtracting PBS background. Measurements were performed in triplicate.

- describe rEV quantification via anti-P24 ELISA

An Innotest HIV antigen mAB ELISA kit was used according to the manufacturer’s instructions. A standard curve was generated using serial dilutions of rEV quantified by fNTA. Absorbance was read at 450 nm, and concentrations were interpolated from the standard curve.

- outline protein analysis

Protein concentration was measured using the Qubit Protein Assay Kit. Samples were lysed in 0.2% SDS, and fluorescence was measured using a Qubit fluorometer. For Western blot, samples were boiled in reducing sample buffer, separated by SDS-PAGE, transferred to nitrocellulose, and probed with primary and secondary antibodies. Chemiluminescence was detected using a Proxima 2850 Imager.

- describe protein concentration measurement

Protein concentration was determined using the Qubit Protein Assay Kit for vesicle lysates and the Bio-Rad DC Protein Assay for cell lysates, according to manufacturer protocols.

- outline protein analysis by SDS-PAGE and Western blot

Proteins were separated on 10% SDS-PAGE gels and transferred to nitrocellulose membranes. Membranes were blocked in 5% non-fat milk in PBS-T, incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C, washed, and incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies. Bands were visualized using WesternBright Sirius and imaged with a Proxima 2850 Imager.

- describe LC-MS/MS analysis

Proteins were reduced with TCEP, alkylated with iodoacetamide, digested with trypsin, and analyzed by LC-MS/MS using an Ultimate 3000 RSLCnano system coupled to a Q Exactive mass spectrometer. Data were processed using MaxQuant and Perseus software.

- outline data analysis for LC-MS/MS

Raw data were searched against the Swiss-Prot human database supplemented with gag-EGFP sequences. False discovery rate was set at 1%. Proteins were quantified using MaxLFQ, and statistical analysis was performed using a t-test in Perseus.

- describe immune precipitation

MagnaBind magnetic beads were incubated with anti-CD81, anti-CD63, or anti-PEG antibodies for 2 hours at 4 °C. Beads were washed and incubated with sample for 2 hours at 4 °C. Beads were washed three times, and supernatant was pelleted at 100,000 × g for 70 minutes.

- outline RNA analysis

Total RNA was isolated using the miRNeasy Micro Kit. RNA was reverse transcribed using the iScript cDNA Synthesis Kit. RT-qPCR was performed using LightCycler 480 SYBR Green I Master and primers targeting EGFP.

- describe RNA isolation

RNA was isolated from 5 × 10¹⁰ vesicles using the miRNeasy Micro Kit according to the manufacturer’s protocol. RNA was eluted in 14 µL DNase/RNase-free water.

- outline RT-qPCR analysis

RT-qPCR was performed using a 5 µL reaction containing 2 µL cDNA, 0.5 µL of each primer (5 mM), and 2.5 µL SYBR Green Master Mix. Cycling conditions were 95 °C for 5 seconds, 60 °C for 30 seconds, and 72 °C for 1 second for 44 cycles. Data were analyzed using the ΔΔCt method.

- describe lipid analysis

Lipids were extracted using a modified Bligh-Dyer protocol. Phospholipids were analyzed by ESI-MS/MS on a 4000 QTRAP mass spectrometer in MRM mode. Cholesterol was measured using the Amplex Red Cholesterol Assay Kit.

- outline lipid extraction

Lipids were extracted by adding chloroform and methanol to vesicle pellets in a 2:1:0.8 ratio. The organic phase was collected, dried under nitrogen, and resuspended in methanol for MS analysis.

- describe ESI-MS/MS analysis

ESI-MS/MS was performed in MRM mode using precursor and fragment ion transitions specific for phosphatidylcholine, phosphatidylethanolamine, and sphingomyelin species. Collision energies were optimized for each lipid class.

- outline cholesterol measurement

Cholesterol concentration was measured using the Amplex Red Cholesterol Assay Kit. Samples were diluted in assay buffer, incubated with horseradish peroxidase and Amplex Red reagent, and fluorescence was measured at 570 nm excitation and 585 nm emission.

- describe rEV PEGylation

Recombinant extracellular vesicles were incubated with 0.016 mg/mL DMPE-PEG 5k at 40 °C for 2 hours with gentle mixing. The reaction was stopped by dilution in PBS and pelleting at 100,000 × g for 70 minutes.

- outline lyophilization procedure

rEV were diluted 1:20 in PBS containing 5% trehalose. Vials were frozen at −45 °C for 2 hours, lyophilized at 0.1 mbar, and the shelf temperature was ramped to −25 °C over 24 hours, then to 20 °C over 4 hours, and finally cooled to 3 °C under vacuum.

- describe freezing and storage conditions

rEV were stored in 10 µL aliquots at −80 °C. Stability was assessed after 6 and 12 months of storage. No significant changes in size, density, fluorescence, or concentration were observed.

- outline quality control measures

Quality control measures included verification of transfection efficiency by fluorescence microscopy, confirmation of vesicle purity by Western blot for absence of calnexin and ApoB, measurement of particle concentration by fNTA, and assessment of protein-to-particle ratio by BCA assay. Only preparations with a protein-to-particle ratio below 0.5 pg/particle were used.

- describe data analysis and interpretation

Data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism v7. Normality was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Comparisons between groups were made using Mann-Whitney U test. Correlations were assessed using Spearman’s rank correlation. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation.

- outline statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism v7. All experiments were performed in triplicate. P-values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

- describe data visualization

Data were visualized using GraphPad Prism v7. Box plots, scatter plots, and bar graphs were generated to represent mean, standard deviation, and statistical significance. Heatmaps were generated using Morpheus software.

- summarize results

Recombinant extracellular vesicles were successfully produced, characterized, and validated as a biological reference material that faithfully mimics endogenous extracellular vesicles in size, density, morphology, and molecular composition. They are detectable by multiple orthogonal methods, stable under storage conditions, and commutable across isolation protocols. Their use enables accurate calibration of instruments, quantification of recovery efficiency, and normalization of inter-laboratory variability, thereby advancing the field of extracellular vesicle research toward clinical translation.

### Example 1: Recombinant Extracellular Vesicles (rEV) Share Physical and Biochemical Characteristics with Endogenous Sample Extracellular Vesicles (Sample EV)

- produce rEV

Recombinant extracellular vesicles were produced by transient transfection of HEK293T cells with a plasmid encoding the HIV-1 gag polyprotein fused to enhanced green fluorescent protein. Transfected cells were cultured for 72 hours, and conditioned medium was harvested and processed as described.

- isolate rEV by ODG and pelleting

rEV were isolated by OptiPrep density gradient centrifugation followed by pelleting at 100,000 × g. The resulting pellet contained approximately 5 × 10¹¹ vesicles per harvest.

- analyze transfection efficiency

Transfection efficiency was analyzed by fluorescence microscopy and flow cytometry, revealing that greater than 95% of transfected cells expressed gag-EGFP.

- compare physical characteristics of rEV and MOCK EV

Size distribution, zeta potential, and refractive index of rEV were compared to those of extracellular vesicles from mock-transfected cells (MOCK EV) using nanoparticle tracking analysis and Mie theory. No statistically significant differences were observed.

- analyze biochemical characteristics of rEV and MOCK EV

Western blot analysis confirmed the presence of ALIX, TSG101, CD9, CD81, CD63, and flotillin-1 in both rEV and MOCK EV. Mass spectrometry revealed that 89% of detected proteins were shared between the two populations.

- identify EV-enriched proteins in rEV and MOCK EV

Proteomic analysis identified 792 proteins equally abundant in rEV and MOCK EV, including ESCRT components, tetraspanins, and cytoskeletal regulators.

- analyze lipid composition of rEV and MOCK EV

Lipidomics analysis revealed a Spearman correlation coefficient of 0.921 between rEV and MOCK EV for phospholipid and sphingolipid species.

- compare cholesterol concentration of rEV and MOCK EV

Cholesterol concentration per particle was not significantly different between rEV and MOCK EV, with p-values greater than 0.0256.

- summarize similarities between rEV and MOCK EV

rEV and MOCK EV exhibited indistinguishable physical and biochemical properties, confirming that rEV are faithful mimics of endogenous extracellular vesicles.

### Example 2: Identification of rEV Detection Methods

- identify rEV by fluorescent NTA

Fluorescent nanoparticle tracking analysis detected rEV with a fluorescence signal above background, with 79.7% of particles being fluorescent.

- identify rEV by high resolution flow cytometry

High-resolution flow cytometry enabled detection of individual rEV based on fluorescence and scatter, with a linear correlation between concentration and signal intensity.

- identify rEV by Western blot and ELISA

Western blot detected gag-EGFP at concentrations as low as 7 × 10⁷ rEV, and anti-p24 ELISA showed a linear range from 1 × 10⁶ to 1 × 10⁷ rEV.

### Example 3: Identification of Optimized Storage Conditions of rEV

- analyze stability of rEV under different storage conditions

rEV were stored at −80 °C for up to 12 months, at 4 °C for up to 7 days, and after lyophilization with 5% trehalose.

- identify optimal storage conditions for rEV

Lyophilization with 5% trehalose and storage at −80 °C preserved vesicle integrity, fluorescence, size, and density. Storage at 4 °C for 7 days was also acceptable. Without trehalose, vesicles aggregated and lost membrane integrity.

### Example 4: The Use of rEV to Estimate the Efficiency of EV Isolation Methods

- spike rEV in PBS or plasma

rEV were spiked into PBS, plasma, urine, and culture medium at a concentration of 5 × 10¹⁰ particles per sample.

- perform SEC, dUC, Exoquick precipitation, and ODG

Isolation was performed using size exclusion chromatography, differential ultracentrifugation, ExoQuick-TC precipitation, and OptiPrep density gradient centrifugation.

- evaluate recovery of rEV by Western blot and fNTA

Recovery was quantified using Western blot for gag-EGFP and fluorescent nanoparticle tracking analysis. SEC showed 100% recovery, ODG showed 30% recovery, dUC showed 10% recovery, and ExoQuick-TC showed 15% recovery.

- analyze elution profiles of rEV and sample EV

rEV co-eluted with sample EV in identical fractions during size exclusion chromatography, confirming commutability.

- quantify rEV by fNTA and anti-P24 ELISA

Both methods provided equivalent quantification, with fNTA being more sensitive and ELISA being more robust in complex matrices.

- analyze aggregation of rEV in plasma

rEV spiked in plasma exhibited a density shift to 1.141–1.215 g/mL due to protein corona formation, which was reversed by proteinase K treatment.

- perform ODG on urine spiked with rEV

rEV spiked in urine floated at the same density as endogenous vesicles, confirming compatibility with non-plasma biofluids.

- analyze effect of proteinase K treatment on rEV

Proteinase K treatment reversed the density shift without affecting fluorescence or membrane integrity, enabling accurate separation.

- summarize efficiency of EV isolation methods

Size exclusion chromatography demonstrated the highest recovery efficiency, while differential ultracentrifugation showed the lowest. These findings provide a quantitative basis for method selection.

### Example 5: rEV Allows for an Accurate Comparison of EV Numbers in Plasma from Cancer Patients Versus Healthy Individuals

- spike rEV in plasma from cancer patients and healthy individuals

rEV were spiked into plasma from 26 breast cancer patients and 11 healthy volunteers at a concentration of 2 × 10¹⁰ particles per 2 mL.

- quantify EV and rEV by NTA and fNTA, respectively

Total extracellular vesicles were quantified by NTA, and recombinant vesicles were quantified by fluorescent NTA. Recovery rates were calculated for each sample.

- normalize EV counts by recovery rate

Normalized EV concentrations were calculated by dividing the NTA count by the recovery rate of rEV. Cancer patients showed a 2.5-fold increase in normalized EV concentration compared to healthy individuals (p = 0.0002).

- compare absolute counts

Absolute EV counts without normalization showed no significant difference between groups (p = 0.1410), demonstrating that normalization is essential for accurate detection.

### Example 6: Implementing the PSI Sequence to Load EGFP mRNA in rEV

- modify pMET7-GAG-EGFP plasmid with PSI sequence to load EGFP mRNA in rEV

The HIV-1 packaging signal (PSI) was inserted upstream of the EGFP coding sequence in the pMET7-GAG-EGFP plasmid to direct encapsulation of EGFP mRNA into rEV.

- confirm mRNA encapsulation

RT-qPCR confirmed the presence of EGFP mRNA in rEV, with a linear correlation between mRNA copies and vesicle number.

- demonstrate utility for RNA-based quantification
