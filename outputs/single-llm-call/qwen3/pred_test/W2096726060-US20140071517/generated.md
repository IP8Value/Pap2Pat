## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

- The present invention relates to optical upconverting nanomaterials capable of converting infrared radiation into visible light through a non-linear photophysical process, and more specifically to composite nanocrystals doped with transition metals and functionalized with organic dye antennas that enable efficient, wavelength-selective upconversion for applications in photovoltaics, infrared detection, laser threat warning systems, and covert marking.

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

- Optical upconversion is a process in which two or more low-energy photons are sequentially absorbed and converted into a single higher-energy photon, resulting in emission at a shorter wavelength than the excitation light. This phenomenon has been observed in lanthanide-doped crystalline materials, particularly in hexagonal sodium yttrium fluoride hosts, where energy transfer between sensitizer and activator ions enables the generation of visible luminescence under near-infrared excitation. However, conventional upconversion materials are fundamentally limited by the narrow absorption bandwidth and low molar absorptivity of f-f electronic transitions in rare earth ions, which restrict their utility to monochromatic laser sources and result in inefficient light harvesting under broadband or ambient infrared illumination. The intrinsic weakness of these atomic transitions, coupled with the stringent requirement for precise spectral matching between excitation source and dopant absorption line, has hindered the practical deployment of upconversion in real-world environments where light sources are broadband, variable, or unknown. Furthermore, the low quantum efficiency of direct atomic upconversion, typically below 0.1%, combined with the difficulty of integrating these materials into flexible or transparent solid-state devices, has limited their adoption beyond specialized laboratory settings.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

- The present invention discloses a novel class of optical upconverting nanomaterials comprising a crystalline nanocrystal doped with transition metal ions that function as energy transfer facilitators and emitters, surface-functionalized with organic dye molecules acting as optical antennas to capture incident infrared radiation with high efficiency and broad spectral bandwidth. The nanocrystal is composed of a low-phonon-energy host lattice, preferably sodium yttrium fluoride, doped with at least one transition metal ion selected from the group consisting of ytterbium, erbium, holmium, and thulium, wherein the dopant serves either as an energy transfer facilitator, an emitter, or both. The dye antenna, covalently bound to the nanocrystal surface via an amide linkage formed between a carboxylic acid, isothiocyanate, or NHS ester group on the dye and an amine-containing siloxane layer on the nanocrystal, absorbs infrared photons over a spectral width exceeding 100 nanometers and transfers the absorbed energy to the nanocrystal via Förster resonance energy transfer. The invention enables simultaneous upconversion from multiple distinct infrared input wavelengths into multiple distinct visible output wavelengths within a single nanomaterial, thereby permitting spectral discrimination of otherwise invisible infrared radiation. The composite nanomaterial exhibits a greater than tenfold increase in effective absorption bandwidth and a tenfold enhancement in upconversion quantum efficiency compared to undoped or dye-free nanocrystals. The material is applicable in solar cells to extend the spectral response of silicon photodiodes into the near-infrared, in silicon-based infrared detectors to provide visible readouts of infrared signals, in laser threat warning systems to enable real-time visual identification of infrared laser sources, and as a covert marker for authentication and tracking under infrared illumination. The optical upconverting nanomaterial is defined as a nanoscale particle with dimensions between 10 and 100 nanometers, comprising a crystalline core, an encapsulating silica shell, and surface-bound organic dyes that collectively facilitate non-radiative energy transfer from infrared to visible wavelengths. The transition metal-doped material comprises at least one rare earth element selected from the group consisting of ytterbium, erbium, holmium, and thulium, wherein the dopant concentration is optimized to balance energy transfer efficiency and minimize quenching. Energy transfer facilitating transition metal dopants are those ions that absorb infrared photons and transfer energy to emitter ions, while emitter transition metal dopants are those that emit visible photons following energy accumulation. Förster resonance energy transfer occurs over distances of less than ten nanometers and proceeds via a non-radiative dipole-dipole coupling mechanism between the dye’s excited state and the lanthanide ion’s acceptor state. Possible transition metal dopants include ytterbium, erbium, holmium, thulium, neodymium, and praseodymium. In certain embodiments, a single transition metal ion serves as both energy transfer facilitator and emitter, while in others, distinct transition metal ions perform these roles separately. The nanomaterial may be configured to accept multiple distinct infrared input wavelengths and emit multiple distinct visible output wavelengths through parallel energy pathways within the same nanocrystal. The optical antenna is composed of an organic dye molecule possessing strong absorption in the near-infrared region, high fluorescence quantum yield at the sensitizer’s gateway wavelength, and minimal absorption in the visible spectrum. The optical antenna is covalently bonded to the nanocrystal via a siloxane-based linker layer containing primary, secondary, or tertiary amine functionality, and the entire structure is further encapsulated by a thin silica layer that protects the dye from environmental degradation and enables dispersion in polymeric matrices. Additional ligands, including polyethylene glycol derivatives, carboxylates, or phosphonates, may be appended to the outer surface to enhance colloidal stability and biocompatibility.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

- Optical means refers to any system or device that manipulates light through reflection, refraction, absorption, or emission, including lenses, filters, waveguides, and photodetectors. Infrared radiation is electromagnetic radiation with wavelengths ranging from approximately 700 nanometers to 1 millimeter, encompassing near-infrared, mid-infrared, and far-infrared bands. Visible light refers to electromagnetic radiation detectable by the human eye, typically spanning wavelengths from 400 to 700 nanometers. Ultraviolet radiation encompasses wavelengths below 400 nanometers, extending down to approximately 10 nanometers. Near-infrared radiation denotes the portion of the infrared spectrum from 700 to 1400 nanometers, which is most relevant for biological and photonic applications. An optical antenna is a molecular or nanostructured component designed to capture incident electromagnetic radiation and efficiently transfer the absorbed energy to a neighboring acceptor, typically through non-radiative mechanisms such as Förster resonance energy transfer. Upconversion is a nonlinear optical process in which multiple low-energy photons are absorbed and converted into a single higher-energy photon, resulting in emission at a shorter wavelength than the excitation source. Förster resonance energy transfer is a non-radiative mechanism of energy transfer between a donor chromophore and an acceptor chromophore, mediated by dipole-dipole coupling and dependent on the inverse sixth power of the separation distance. Transition elements or metals refer to chemical elements in the d-block of the periodic table, including but not limited to ytterbium, erbium, holmium, and thulium, which exhibit partially filled f-orbitals and characteristic electronic transitions suitable for upconversion. Rare earth elements or metals are the 15 lanthanide elements plus scandium and yttrium, known for their sharp emission lines and long-lived excited states. An organic dye is a synthetic or naturally derived molecule capable of absorbing and emitting light in the visible or near-infrared spectrum, often containing conjugated π-electron systems. A sensitizer is a species that absorbs incident radiation and transfers energy to another species, typically an emitter, via non-radiative processes. An energy transfer facilitating material is a substance that accepts energy from an optical antenna and transfers it to an emitter ion, thereby enabling upconversion. The optical upconverting nanomaterial is a composite particle comprising a crystalline nanocrystal doped with transition metal ions and functionalized with an organic dye antenna, wherein the dye captures infrared photons and transfers energy to the nanocrystal via Förster resonance energy transfer, resulting in visible emission. The nanocrystal is composed of a low-phonon-energy host lattice such as NaYF₄, doped with transition metal ions at concentrations optimized to maximize energy transfer efficiency and minimize concentration quenching. The nanocrystal exhibits a hexagonal crystal structure, with dimensions ranging from 15 to 70 nanometers, and is synthesized via a high-temperature organometallic reaction involving lanthanide acetates, sodium acetate, oleic acid, octadecene, and ammonium fluoride. The nanocrystal is subsequently functionalized with an amine-containing siloxane monomer such as 3-aminopropyltrimethoxysilane, forming a thin silica shell that enables covalent attachment of dyes bearing NHS ester, carboxylic acid, or isothiocyanate groups. The dye structure is selected from the group consisting of heptamethine cyanines, dithiones, squaryliums, tris azos, and the Dyomics series of dyes, including DY-831, DyLight 800, DLS-776A, and NIR-797, each possessing a distinct absorption band in the near-infrared region and a fluorescence emission spectrum overlapping with the sensitizer’s excitation transition. The metal ions M include H₂, Cu, Co, CoR₃, Ni, SiR₃R₄, AlR₃, Zn, Fe, FeR₃, and Mn, which may be incorporated into the siloxane matrix or used as catalysts in functionalization reactions. The R₄ groups include —R₃, —Cl, —CH₃, —H, —Ph, —OCH₃, and —OCH₂CH₃, which modulate the steric and electronic properties of the siloxane linker. The M′ ions include K, Li, Na, and H, which serve as counterions in carboxylate-containing dyes. The heptamethines are cyanine dyes characterized by a polymethine chain of seven carbon atoms, exhibiting strong absorption in the 750–850 nm range. The dithiones are sulfur-containing chromophores with extended conjugation and high extinction coefficients. The squaryliums are four-membered ring dyes with intense near-infrared absorption and high photostability. The tris azos are azo-based dyes with multiple azo linkages that extend absorption into the infrared. The Dyomics series of dyes are proprietary cyanine derivatives engineered for biological labeling and possessing NHS ester functionality for covalent conjugation. Specific dyes include DY-831, DyLight 800, DLS-776A, NIR-797, and fluorescein-free acid. The energy transfer facilitating transition metal dopants include ytterbium and neodymium, which absorb infrared photons and transfer energy to emitter ions. The emitter transition metal dopants include erbium, holmium, and thulium, which accumulate energy and emit visible photons. The operation of the nanomaterial involves the sequential absorption of infrared photons by the dye antenna, non-radiative energy transfer to the sensitizer ion via Förster resonance energy transfer, followed by sequential energy transfer to the emitter ion through cross-relaxation or excited-state absorption, culminating in visible emission. The energy pathway proceeds from the dye’s excited singlet state to the sensitizer’s ground state, then through a ladder of intermediate energy levels in the activator ion until a radiative transition to the ground state occurs. The Dexter mechanism is an alternative energy transfer pathway involving orbital overlap and electron exchange, which may contribute to energy transfer at very short distances. Composite materials are formed by embedding the upconverting nanomaterials in transparent polymeric matrices such as poly(methyl methacrylate), polycarbonate, or silicone elastomers. Integrated parallel optical pathways allow a single nanocrystal to simultaneously respond to multiple infrared wavelengths and emit multiple visible colors through distinct dopant configurations. Distinct elements serve as energy transfer facilitators and emitters when, for example, ytterbium acts as the sensitizer and erbium as the emitter, or when erbium serves both roles. The operation of the nanomaterial with distinct elements involves the absorption of infrared photons by the sensitizer, followed by energy migration to the emitter, which then emits visible light. The energy transfer facilitating transition metal dopants are selected for their high absorption cross-section and long excited-state lifetime, while the emitter transition metal dopants are selected for their high quantum yield and distinct emission spectrum. Multiple distinct input wavelengths are enabled by pairing different dye antennas with different nanocrystal compositions, each responsive to a unique infrared band. Multiple distinct output wavelengths arise from the use of different emitter dopants, such as erbium emitting green light and thulium emitting blue light. A single infrared input wavelength may be used with a single dye and a single emitter to produce a single visible output. Multiple distinct dyes may be attached to the same nanocrystal to broaden the absorption bandwidth or to enable multi-color emission. A common emission spectrum may be achieved by using multiple dyes that transfer energy to the same emitter ion. The fabrication of optical nanomaterials involves the synthesis of lanthanide-doped NaYF₄ nanocrystals via a hot-injection method, followed by surface functionalization with amine-containing siloxanes, attachment of infrared dyes via amide bond formation, and optional encapsulation with additional silica layers. The synthesis of lanthanide-doped NaYF₄ nanocrystals involves mixing stoichiometric quantities of lanthanide acetates and sodium acetate with oleic acid and octadecene under vacuum at 120°C to form a clear solution, cooling to room temperature, adding ammonium fluoride in methanol to precipitate the fluoride precursor, heating to 324°C for 20 minutes to crystallize the hexagonal phase, and purifying the nanocrystals by ethanol precipitation and centrifugation. The upconversion efficiency in nanocrystalline materials is strongly dependent on crystalline phase purity, particle size, and surface defect density, with the hexagonal phase exhibiting superior performance over the cubic phase. The synthetic method for lanthanide-doped nanoparticle material involves the conversion of acetate salts to oleate complexes, followed by fluoride incorporation and thermal crystallization. The preparation of lanthanide salts and sodium acetate involves weighing stoichiometric amounts of anhydrous acetates under inert atmosphere and dissolving them in a mixture of oleic acid and octadecene. The formation of a clear solution is achieved by heating under vacuum to remove water and oxygen, followed by cooling to room temperature to prevent premature nucleation. The addition of ammonium fluoride results in immediate formation of a white precipitate, indicating the formation of amorphous sodium yttrium fluoride. The purification of nanocrystals through precipitation and centrifugation involves dispersing the reaction mixture in ethanol, centrifuging at 4000 RPM, washing with toluene or cyclohexane, and repeating the cycle to remove unreacted precursors and ligands. The characterization of nanocrystals using transmission electron microscope and X-ray diffraction confirms the hexagonal morphology and crystalline structure, with particle dimensions ranging from 15 to 25 nm in the long axis and 10 to 15 nm in the short axis. Modifications to produce nanorods or nanosheets involve altering the reaction temperature, time, or ligand-to-metal ratio to control anisotropic growth. The dispersion of NaYF₄:Ln³⁺ nanoparticles in cyclohexane is performed to facilitate surface functionalization in a non-polar medium. The attachment of dye to amine-containing siloxane monomer involves mixing the nanoparticles with 3-aminopropyltrimethoxysilane under reverse microemulsion conditions to form a thin silica shell with surface-exposed amine groups. The formation of an inner silica shell encapsulating layer with attached dye is achieved by subsequent reaction of the amine-functionalized particles with NHS ester- or carboxylic acid-functionalized dyes under mild conditions to preserve dye integrity. The attachment of dye to encapsulated nanoparticles involves dispersing silica-coated nanoparticles in ethanol, adding dye solution, and stirring for 20 minutes at room temperature to form covalent amide bonds. The fabrication of films involves suspending the upconverting nanoparticles in a monomeric solution of methyl methacrylate, curing under UV light or heat to form transparent poly(methyl methacrylate) films, and embedding the nanoparticles in a solid matrix suitable for optical devices.

## Synthesis of Lanthanide-Doped NaYF₄ Nanocrystals

- Upconversion in nanocrystalline materials is highly dependent on the crystalline phase, with the hexagonal phase exhibiting significantly higher efficiency than the cubic phase due to reduced non-radiative relaxation pathways. The synthetic method for lanthanide-doped nanoparticle material involves the preparation of lanthanide salts and sodium acetate in stoichiometric ratios, typically 1 mmol total lanthanides and 1 mmol sodium acetate, dissolved in a mixture of 4 mL oleic acid and 11 mL octadecene. The solution is heated under vacuum at 120°C to form a clear, homogeneous mixture, then cooled to room temperature to prevent premature nucleation. Four mmol of ammonium fluoride, dissolved in 10 mL methanol, is added to the mixture, resulting in the immediate formation of a white precipitate corresponding to amorphous sodium yttrium fluoride. The solution is stirred for 30 minutes to ensure complete mixing, then slowly heated to evaporate the methanol under nitrogen purge at 100°C. The mixture is then raised to 324°C and held for 20 minutes to induce crystallization into the hexagonal phase. After natural cooling, the nanocrystals are precipitated with ethanol, centrifuged at 4000 RPM, washed with toluene or cyclohexane, and reprecipitated twice to remove excess ligands and unreacted precursors. The nanocrystals are characterized using transmission electron microscopy, revealing hexagonal rods with dimensions of 25 ± 5 nm in length and 15 ± 5 nm in width, and X-ray diffraction confirming the hexagonal NaYF₄ structure. Modifications to the synthesis, such as increasing the reaction temperature or extending the heating time, result in larger particles, while altering the oleic acid-to-lanthanide ratio influences the aspect ratio of the nanorods, enabling the production of nanosheets or elongated nanorods.

### Encapsulation of Attached Dye

- The dispersion of NaYF₄:Ln³⁺ nanoparticles in cyclohexane is performed to create a non-polar environment compatible with the hydrophobic oleic acid ligands on the nanoparticle surface. The attachment of dye to amine-containing siloxane monomer is accomplished by introducing 3-aminopropyltrimethoxysilane into a reverse microemulsion formed by Igepal CO-520 surfactant in cyclohexane, followed by addition of the nanoparticle suspension and sonication to ensure uniform dispersion. The amine-functionalized siloxane monomer undergoes hydrolysis and condensation around each nanoparticle, forming a thin silica shell with surface-exposed amine groups. The formation of the inner silica shell encapsulating layer with attached dye is achieved by reacting the amine-functionalized nanoparticles with NHS ester- or carboxylic acid-functionalized dyes under mild conditions in ethanol, allowing the formation of stable amide bonds without degrading the dye structure. The resulting nanocomposite is purified by centrifugation and washing to remove unbound dye.

### Attachment of Dye to Encapsulated Nanoparticle

- The dispersion of NaYF₄:Ln³⁺ nanoparticles in cyclohexane is carried out to maintain colloidal stability prior to surface functionalization. The formation of a silica shell encapsulating layer with amine functionality is achieved by introducing 3-aminopropyltrimethoxysilane into a reverse microemulsion containing the nanoparticles, followed by stirring for two hours to allow controlled condensation of the siloxane monomer around each particle. The attachment of dye to the silica-coated nanoparticles is performed by adding a solution of NHS ester- or isothiocyanate-functionalized dye in ethanol or dimethyl sulfoxide, stirring for 20 minutes at room temperature, and allowing covalent amide bond formation between the dye and the surface amine groups. The nanoparticle-dye nanocomposite is then purified by repeated centrifugation and washing with ethanol until the supernatant is free of unbound dye.

### Fabrication of Films

- Various techniques for fabricating transparent films include solution casting, spin coating, and in-situ polymerization. The suspension of nanoparticles in a monomeric solution of methyl methacrylate is prepared by dispersing the upconverting nanomaterials in a solution containing the monomer, a photoinitiator, and a crosslinking agent. The mixture is poured into a mold and cured under ultraviolet light or elevated temperature to form a transparent poly(methyl methacrylate) film. The fabrication of clear PMMA films using a modified procedure of Boyer et al. involves the use of a degassed monomer solution, controlled curing conditions, and post-cure annealing to eliminate residual stress and optical scattering, resulting in optically transparent films that retain the upconversion properties of the embedded nanoparticles.
