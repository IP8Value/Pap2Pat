# DESCRIPTION

- introduce invention

The present invention relates to a novel therapeutic approach for mitigating cognitive impairments induced by sleep deprivation through the targeted modulation of α-amino-3-hydroxy-5-methyl-4-isoxazolepropionic acid (AMPA) receptors. This invention discloses the unexpected and clinically significant discovery that positive allosteric modulators of AMPA receptors, when administered to subjects experiencing prolonged wakefulness, restore cognitive performance to levels exceeding those observed under normal alert conditions. Unlike conventional stimulants that act through dopaminergic, noradrenergic, or adenosinergic pathways, the compounds of this invention enhance synaptic transmission by selectively increasing the amplitude and duration of glutamate-mediated currents at AMPA receptor channels without inducing hyperarousal, agitation, or dependence. The invention further demonstrates that such modulation not only reverses deficits in attention, working memory, and response latency caused by sleep loss but also elevates performance beyond baseline levels in nonhuman primates engaged in complex cognitive tasks. This effect is accompanied by a normalization of task-related cerebral glucose metabolism in key neural circuits, including the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex, medial temporal lobe, and dorsal striatum, indicating a restoration of functional network dynamics disrupted by sleep deprivation. The invention provides a pharmacological strategy that addresses the root neurobiological consequences of sleep loss—namely, the downregulation of glutamatergic synaptic efficacy—rather than merely masking symptoms through central nervous system stimulation. This represents a paradigm shift in the treatment of cognitive impairment due to sleep loss, offering a safer, more physiologically coherent alternative to existing agents such as modafinil, caffeine, or amphetamines.

## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

- relate to methods of use for compounds and pharmaceutical compositions

The present invention pertains to methods of using specific compounds that act as positive allosteric modulators of AMPA receptors for the treatment, prevention, or alleviation of cognitive dysfunction resulting from sleep deprivation in humans and other mammals. These methods encompass the administration of pharmaceutically active compounds that enhance the function of glutamate-gated ion channels by reducing receptor desensitization and prolonging channel open time, thereby amplifying postsynaptic excitatory currents without directly activating the receptor. The invention further relates to pharmaceutical compositions comprising such compounds in combination with pharmaceutically acceptable carriers, excipients, or diluents, formulated for oral, intravenous, transdermal, buccal, or other systemic routes of delivery. The invention also encompasses the use of such compounds in the manufacture of medicaments for the treatment of conditions characterized by impaired cognitive performance due to extended wakefulness, including shift work disorder, jet lag, prolonged caregiving duties, critical illness-related sleep disruption, and military or emergency response scenarios involving sustained operational demands. The compounds of the invention are not classified as stimulants, do not induce euphoria or dependence, and do not alter sleep architecture when administered acutely, making them uniquely suited for chronic or repeated use in populations vulnerable to circadian disruption.

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

- describe sleep deprivation in humans

Sleep deprivation, whether acute or chronic, constitutes a pervasive and growing public health concern affecting millions of individuals worldwide. It arises from voluntary or involuntary reductions in sleep duration below physiological requirements, commonly observed in shift workers, healthcare professionals, military personnel, caregivers of infants or the critically ill, and individuals with sleep disorders or psychiatric conditions. Prolonged wakefulness leads to a progressive decline in cognitive functions, including attention, working memory, decision-making, and response inhibition, which are critical for safe and effective performance in high-stakes environments. The consequences of such impairments extend beyond individual performance to include increased risk of accidents, medical errors, and long-term neurocognitive decline. Despite the availability of stimulant medications such as caffeine, modafinil, and amphetamines, these agents often produce side effects including anxiety, tachycardia, insomnia rebound, and potential for abuse, limiting their utility in sustained use scenarios. Moreover, these compounds do not address the underlying neurobiological alterations caused by sleep loss, instead acting as compensatory agents that artificially elevate arousal without restoring the synaptic and metabolic integrity of neural circuits essential for higher-order cognition.

### Electrophysiology of Sleep and Sleep-Deprivation

- discuss nonlinear analysis of EEG during sleep deprivation

Sleep is a dynamically regulated state characterized by distinct electrophysiological signatures, including synchronized slow-wave activity during non-rapid eye movement (NREM) sleep and desynchronized, high-frequency oscillations during rapid eye movement (REM) sleep. During wakefulness, the electroencephalogram (EEG) exhibits low-amplitude, high-frequency activity dominated by beta and gamma bands, reflecting the tonic firing of cortical neurons engaged in sensory processing and executive function. Upon sleep deprivation, this baseline pattern deteriorates, with the emergence of slow-wave delta oscillations (0.5–5 Hz) and transient bursts of beta activity, indicative of localized microsleep episodes and cortical instability. Event-related potentials, particularly the P300 component associated with attentional allocation and stimulus evaluation, show marked attenuation in amplitude and prolonged latency during sleep deprivation, signaling impaired information processing. Nonlinear analyses of EEG signals reveal reduced complexity and increased predictability in brain dynamics, suggesting a loss of adaptive flexibility in neural networks. These electrophysiological changes are not unique to humans; similar patterns of delta power elevation, beta band intrusion, and diminished event-related potential amplitude have been consistently documented in rodents and nonhuman primates subjected to prolonged wakefulness, validating these species as translational models for studying the neurophysiological consequences of sleep loss.

Neuroimaging studies using positron emission tomography (PET) and functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) have revealed that sleep deprivation induces a profound reorganization of brain metabolism and functional connectivity. Under normal conditions, tasks requiring working memory and sustained attention engage a distributed network involving the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex, anterior cingulate, thalamus, and medial temporal lobe. Following prolonged wakefulness, there is a marked reduction in glucose metabolism in the thalamus, basal ganglia, and cerebellum, regions critical for sensory gating and motor coordination, while compensatory increases in metabolic demand occur in the prefrontal cortex and parietal association areas. This pattern reflects a failure of subcortical regulatory systems to maintain optimal cortical activation, forcing higher-order regions to operate under increased energetic strain. Functional MRI studies further demonstrate that sleep-deprived individuals recruit additional brain regions not typically engaged during task performance, including the insula and orbitofrontal cortex, suggesting a less efficient, more effortful mode of processing. These neural adaptations correlate with declines in cognitive performance and are accompanied by mood disturbances, including irritability, emotional lability, and reduced motivation, which are linked to diminished dopaminergic tone in mesolimbic pathways. Nonhuman primates provide a uniquely relevant model for these phenomena due to their anatomical and functional homology with humans in prefrontal circuitry, making them indispensable for evaluating the efficacy of pharmacological interventions aimed at restoring cognitive resilience.

### Role of Stress in Sleep Deprivation and Cognitive Performance

- discuss effects of chronic stress on hippocampal-dependent cognition
- describe effects of chronic stress on hippocampal electrophysiology and anatomy
- discuss view that extended sleep deprivation is a form of chronic stress

Extended sleep deprivation activates physiological stress pathways, including the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis and sympathetic nervous system, leading to elevated cortisol levels and sustained noradrenergic activation. These stress responses induce structural and functional alterations in the hippocampus, a region essential for memory consolidation and contextual processing, including dendritic atrophy, reduced neurogenesis, and impaired long-term potentiation. Electrophysiological recordings demonstrate decreased synaptic efficacy and altered theta-gamma coupling in hippocampal circuits during prolonged wakefulness, mirroring changes observed in models of chronic stress. The convergence of metabolic, neurochemical, and structural changes induced by sleep deprivation supports the conceptualization of extended wakefulness as a form of chronic physiological stress, which disrupts the synaptic plasticity mechanisms underlying learning and memory. This perspective underscores the need for therapeutic strategies that target synaptic resilience rather than merely counteracting fatigue, as conventional stimulants fail to reverse the neuroadaptive changes that impair cognitive function at the circuit level.

Sleep plays a critical role in memory consolidation through the reactivation of task-engaged neuronal ensembles during slow-wave sleep, facilitating the transfer of information from hippocampal to neocortical storage sites. Disruption of this process through sleep deprivation leads to profound deficits in both declarative and procedural memory, as evidenced by impaired performance in spatial navigation tasks such as the water maze and radial arm maze in rodents, and in learned avoidance paradigms across species. In humans, sleep deprivation impairs the ability to form and retrieve episodic memories, particularly those dependent on the medial temporal lobe. Neurochemical studies reveal that sleep loss alters the balance of monoaminergic systems, including reductions in serotonin and norepinephrine availability, and increases in prostaglandin D2 synthesis, which promote sleep pressure and suppress synaptic plasticity. The hippocampus, in particular, is highly vulnerable to sleep deprivation, as its neurons exhibit reduced firing coherence and diminished phase-locking to theta oscillations during memory encoding. Lesions to the hippocampus and adjacent cortical regions produce memory deficits strikingly similar to those observed after sleep deprivation, including impaired spatial working memory and an inability to form relational representations between stimuli. Single-unit recordings in primates have identified specific hippocampal neurons whose activity encodes behavioral events during memory tasks, and these neural correlates are significantly attenuated following prolonged wakefulness, providing a direct electrophysiological link between sleep loss and memory failure.

The delayed-match-to-sample (DMTS) task is a cognitive paradigm designed to assess working memory and attentional control in nonhuman primates. In this task, a monkey is seated before a computer video display and trained to manipulate a cursor using a fluorescent marker attached to its hand, which is tracked in two dimensions via an infrared camera system. During the Sample phase, a visual image is presented at one of nine possible locations on the screen, and the animal must fixate on the image for a brief period. This is followed by a delay interval ranging from 1 to 30 seconds, during which no stimuli are presented. In the Match phase, multiple images are displayed simultaneously, one of which corresponds to the sample image, and the animal must move the cursor to select the matching image. Correct responses are rewarded with a liquid reinforcer, and performance is scored as the percentage of trials in which the correct image is selected. The task is systematically varied by increasing the length of the delay and the number of distractor images in the match phase to increase cognitive load. The task is validated as a model of human working memory by its sensitivity to pharmacological and physiological manipulations known to affect prefrontal and hippocampal function. Neural activity in the hippocampus and related regions is monitored via implanted electrodes during task performance to correlate behavioral outcomes with underlying electrophysiological changes. Eye and limb movements are tracked continuously to ensure task compliance and to rule out motor deficits as confounding factors. Animals are trained over several months to achieve stable baseline performance, defined as consistent accuracy above 70% across all trial types. Sleep deprivation is induced by continuous supervision for 30 to 36 hours, during which animals are kept awake through environmental enrichment, intermittent interaction, and controlled lighting, with EEG monitoring to confirm the absence of sleep episodes. Test compounds are administered intravenously 10 minutes prior to task initiation, and cognitive performance is assessed under four conditions: normal vehicle, normal + compound, sleep-deprived vehicle, and sleep-deprived + compound. The effect of the compound is evaluated by comparing accuracy, response latency, and neural metabolic activity across conditions, with statistical significance determined by repeated-measures ANOVA and multivariate discriminant analysis.

Pharmaceutical formulations are prepared by admixing the active compound with one or more pharmaceutically acceptable carriers, diluents, stabilizers, or excipients to facilitate delivery, absorption, and stability. Unit-dose formulations are designed for precise administration, such as tablets, capsules, or injectable vials containing a single therapeutic dose. Intravenous administration is achieved through sterile solutions in isotonic saline or dextrose, suitable for rapid systemic delivery in clinical emergencies. Oral formulations include immediate-release tablets, extended-release capsules, suspensions, and syrups, optionally coated for gastric protection. Buccal administration employs films or tablets designed for dissolution in the oral mucosa, enabling rapid absorption without first-pass metabolism. Topical administration is applicable for transdermal delivery via patches or gels, particularly for sustained release over extended periods. Parenteral administration includes subcutaneous, intramuscular, and intrathecal routes, depending on the pharmacokinetic profile required. Transdermal administration utilizes patches containing permeation enhancers to facilitate skin penetration. Isotonic preparations are adjusted to match physiological osmolarity to minimize tissue irritation. Aqueous suspensions are employed for poorly soluble compounds, stabilized with surfactants or suspending agents, while non-aqueous suspensions utilize oils or alcohols for lipophilic derivatives. Lyophilized formulations are prepared for compounds sensitive to aqueous degradation, reconstituted immediately prior to use. Emulsifying agents such as lecithin or polysorbates are incorporated to stabilize oil-in-water or water-in-oil dispersions. Rectal administration is achieved via suppositories formulated with cocoa butter or polyethylene glycol bases. Ointments, creams, lotions, and pastes are prepared with hydrophilic or hydrophobic bases for localized or systemic delivery. Transdermal patches consist of a reservoir or matrix containing the active compound, bonded to an adhesive layer and protective backing, enabling controlled release over hours to days. Liposomal formulations encapsulate the compound within phospholipid bilayers to enhance blood-brain barrier penetration and reduce systemic toxicity.

### Dosage

- introduce therapeutically effective dosage
- detail dosage ranges for different routes of administration

A therapeutically effective dosage is that amount sufficient to produce a clinically meaningful improvement in cognitive performance during sleep deprivation, as measured by increased accuracy, reduced response latency, or normalized neural metabolic activity, without inducing adverse effects. For intravenous administration, the dosage range is from 0.1 to 2.0 mg/kg, with preferred doses between 0.3 and 1.0 mg/kg. For oral administration, the dosage range is from 0.5 to 10 mg/kg, with preferred doses between 1.0 and 5.0 mg/kg, administered once or twice daily. For transdermal delivery, the dosage range is from 0.05 to 1.0 mg/kg/day, delivered via patch over 12 to 24 hours. For buccal administration, doses range from 0.2 to 5.0 mg per administration, delivered as a fast-dissolving film. For intramuscular injection, doses range from 0.2 to 1.5 mg/kg, administered as a single dose prior to anticipated sleep deprivation. Dosage regimens are adjusted based on patient weight, age, renal and hepatic function, and the severity and duration of sleep deprivation. In chronic use scenarios, dosing is titrated to maintain cognitive performance above baseline levels while minimizing the potential for tolerance or cumulative effects.
