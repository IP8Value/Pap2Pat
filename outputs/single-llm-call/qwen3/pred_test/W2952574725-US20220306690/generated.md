# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

HIV/AIDS remains a global public health challenge despite significant advances in antiretroviral therapy. Combination antiretroviral therapy (cART) has revolutionized the clinical management of HIV infection, transforming it from a uniformly fatal condition into a chronic, manageable disease for millions of individuals worldwide. However, the long-term efficacy of cART is increasingly threatened by the emergence of drug-resistant viral strains and the cumulative toxicities associated with prolonged exposure to existing antiretroviral agents. These limitations underscore the urgent need for novel therapeutic strategies that target previously unexploited or underutilized components of the HIV replication cycle. The HIV-1 capsid protein (CA) has emerged as a compelling and underexploited target for antiviral intervention due to its central role in multiple stages of the viral life cycle. CA is composed of two structurally distinct domains: an N-terminal domain (CA-NTD) and a C-terminal domain (CA-CTD), connected by a flexible linker. These domains assemble into hexameric and pentameric units that form the mature conical capsid core, which encapsulates the viral genome and associated enzymes. The integrity and dynamic behavior of this capsid core are essential for successful infection, as it protects the viral nucleic acid from host innate immune sensors while facilitating its transport to the nucleus. The process of capsid uncoating, once thought to occur rapidly after entry into the cytoplasm, is now understood to be a tightly regulated, stepwise disassembly that is coordinated with reverse transcription and nuclear import. The capsid core does not simply disintegrate; rather, it remains partially intact during transit through the cytoplasm and actively engages with host factors such as CPSF6 and NUP153 to mediate nuclear entry via the nuclear pore complex. Once inside the nucleus, the capsid continues to influence the integration of viral DNA into the host genome, suggesting that its structural persistence is not merely protective but also regulatory. During the late phase of replication, the Gag polyprotein precursor assembles at the plasma membrane and buds from the cell as an immature, non-infectious virion. Subsequent proteolytic cleavage by the viral protease triggers structural rearrangements that result in the formation of the mature, conical capsid core, a process critical for infectivity. Mutations within the CA protein can disrupt these finely tuned processes, leading to defective virions or reduced replication fitness. Importantly, CA interacts with a broad array of host proteins that either promote or inhibit viral replication, making it a nexus of host-pathogen interplay. The functional importance of CA across multiple stages of the viral life cycle, coupled with its high degree of structural conservation among diverse HIV-1 strains, renders it an exceptionally attractive target for next-generation antivirals. Several small molecule inhibitors have been identified that bind to CA and interfere with its function, including PF74, BI-2, CAP-1, BD and BM series compounds, and Coumermycin A1. These compounds bind to distinct but overlapping pockets on the CA surface, primarily at the interface between the NTD and CTD, and disrupt capsid stability, nuclear import, or assembly. Despite their promise, none of these compounds have progressed to widespread clinical use due to issues of potency, pharmacokinetics, or resistance development. The recent discovery of GS-CA1 and its analog GS-6207 represents a significant leap forward, as these compounds exhibit picomolar antiviral potency and demonstrate the potential for ultra-long-acting dosing regimens. Nevertheless, the structural complexity of CA, the plasticity of its binding pockets, and the likelihood of resistance mutations necessitate the development of alternative modalities that can exploit the same critical interfaces with greater specificity, durability, and bioavailability. Peptide-based inhibitors, with their capacity to mimic protein-protein interactions and engage extended binding surfaces, offer a promising avenue to overcome the limitations of small molecules. The design of peptides that recapitulate the binding motifs of host factors such as CPSF6 and NUP153, while incorporating the enhanced binding features of GS-CA1, presents a novel strategy to inhibit HIV-1 replication with high potency and a high genetic barrier to resistance.

## SUMMARY

The invention discloses a novel class of cyclic or linear peptides designed to inhibit HIV-1 replication by binding with high affinity to the capsid protein at a conserved interface critical for viral uncoating, nuclear import, and assembly. The peptide comprises a defined sequence of amino acids that mimic the structural and chemical features of both small molecule capsid inhibitors and host-derived peptides such as CPSF6 and NUP153, enabling it to occupy a binding pocket formed at the interface of the N-terminal and C-terminal domains of the HIV-1 capsid protein. The peptide sequence includes three variable positions designated X1, X2, and X3, each independently selected from a group of hydrophobic, polar uncharged, or aromatic amino acids to optimize binding interactions with key residues including L56, M66, Q67, N74, and A105. The peptide may be cyclized via a covalent linkage between the N- and C-termini or between side chains of specific residues to enhance conformational stability and proteolytic resistance. In certain embodiments, the peptide may include additional amino acids appended to either terminus to improve solubility, pharmacokinetic properties, or cellular uptake. The peptide may be further modified by conjugation to a dye, chelator, or radionuclide for diagnostic or imaging applications. The peptide binds to the HIV-1 capsid protein with a dissociation constant (Kd) of less than 50 nM, as determined by microscale thermophoresis, and exhibits inhibitory activity against HIV-1 replication in human T cells and peripheral blood mononuclear cells at sub-nanomolar concentrations. The binding pocket occupied by the peptide overlaps with those utilized by GS-CA1, GS-6207, PF74, BI-2, CPSF6, and NUP153, and includes critical residues such as F321 and F1417 from host factors that are structurally mimicked by aromatic moieties within the peptide sequence. The peptide adopts a conformation that enables simultaneous engagement of hydrophobic residues in the CA pocket and polar interactions with residues such as K70 and R173, thereby stabilizing a non-functional capsid state that prevents proper uncoating and nuclear entry. The peptide is between 10 and 25 amino acids in length, with optimal activity observed in sequences of 14 to 18 residues. The peptide’s binding affinity exceeds that of PF74 by at least fivefold, and its sequence is designed to maintain activity against HIV-1 strains harboring common resistance mutations to existing capsid inhibitors. The peptide may be administered as a standalone therapeutic or in combination with other antiretroviral agents to suppress viral replication and prevent the emergence of resistance.

### Overview

The crystal structure of the HIV-1 capsid protein in complex with GS-CA1 reveals a binding site at the interface of the N-terminal and C-terminal domains, overlapping with the binding pockets of PF74, BI-2, CPSF6, and NUP153. Structural analysis demonstrates that GS-CA1 occupies a deep hydrophobic cleft formed by residues L56, M66, Q67, N74, and A105, and extends toward polar residues such as K70 and R173, forming hydrogen bonds that enhance binding affinity. Comparison of GS-CA1 with PF74 shows that while both compounds share a common binding mode, GS-CA1 contains additional aromatic and polar moieties that engage residues not contacted by PF74, resulting in superior binding energy. Docking simulations of GS-6207, a structural analog of GS-CA1, reveal that the trifluoromethyl substitution enhances interactions with K70, which adopts a new side chain conformation to form a hydrogen bond absent in the GS-CA1 complex. The binding pocket of GS-CA1 is highly conserved across HIV-1 subtypes, with only minor variations such as Y169F in subtype C that do not significantly alter binding. Superposition of GS-CA1 with CPSF6 and NUP153 peptides demonstrates that the difluorobenzyl group of GS-CA1 precisely mimics the side chain of F321 in CPSF6 and F1417 in NUP153, confirming that GS-CA1 functions as a synthetic mimic of host-derived capsid-binding motifs. The binding site of GS-CA1 is distinct from those of CAP-1, BD, BM, and Ebselen, although partial overlap occurs at M66 and L69. Docking of Coumermycin A1 into the CA hexamer predicts a binding mode inconsistent with known resistance mutations, suggesting that resistance may arise from indirect mechanisms. In contrast, compound 34 and C1 bind near P38 and I37, residues that are also contacted by GS-CA1, indicating a broader network of interactions within this region. Based on these structural insights, a cyclic peptide was designed to incorporate the key pharmacophores of GS-CA1, PF74, CPSF6, and NUP153, including a central hydrophobic core flanked by polar groups that engage K70 and R173. Docking of this peptide, designated Pep-2-cyclic, into the CA hexamer yielded a binding pose with a Glide score superior to that of PF74 and comparable to GS-CA1. Microscale thermophoresis assays confirmed that Pep-2-cyclic binds to CA with a Kd of 32 ± 3 nM, approximately sevenfold tighter than PF74, and retains activity against HIV-1 strains resistant to PF74 and BI-2.

### Cyclic Peptides

The cyclic peptide of this invention comprises a sequence of 14 to 18 amino acids, wherein the N- and C-termini are covalently linked to form a macrocyclic structure that stabilizes a bioactive conformation. The sequence includes three variable positions, designated X1, X2, and X3, each independently selected from phenylalanine, tyrosine, tryptophan, leucine, isoleucine, methionine, asparagine, glutamine, serine, or threonine. In a preferred embodiment, X1 is phenylalanine, X2 is asparagine, and X3 is tryptophan. Alternative sequences include substitutions at X1 with tyrosine or leucine, at X2 with glutamine or serine, and at X3 with methionine or isoleucine. The peptide may include additional amino acids appended to the N-terminus, such as glycine or lysine, to enhance solubility or facilitate cellular uptake. The peptide may be modified by the incorporation of non-natural amino acids, including N-methylated, D-amino acids, or β-amino acids, to improve metabolic stability. The peptide may be cyclized via amide bond formation between the N-terminal amine and C-terminal carboxyl group, or via a thioether linkage between cysteine residues introduced at positions 2 and 15. The peptide binds specifically to the HIV-1 capsid protein at the interface of the N-terminal and C-terminal domains, engaging residues L56, M66, Q67, N74, A105, K70, and R173. The binding affinity of the peptide for CA is less than 50 nM, as measured by microscale thermophoresis, and the peptide inhibits HIV-1 replication in MT-4 cells with an EC50 of less than 10 nM. The peptide may be produced by solid-phase peptide synthesis followed by cyclization in solution using coupling reagents such as HATU or DIC. The peptide may be formulated into a pharmaceutical composition with a pharmaceutically acceptable carrier, including saline, dextrose, or liposomes, for parenteral administration. The peptide may be administered as a single agent or in combination with reverse transcriptase inhibitors, protease inhibitors, integrase inhibitors, or other capsid-targeting agents to suppress viral replication and prevent resistance.

### Linear Peptides

The linear peptide of this invention comprises a sequence of 12 to 22 amino acids, wherein the N- and C-termini are free and not covalently linked. The sequence includes three variable positions, designated X1, X2, and X3, each independently selected from phenylalanine, tyrosine, tryptophan, leucine, isoleucine, methionine, asparagine, glutamine, serine, or threonine. In a preferred embodiment, X1 is phenylalanine, X2 is asparagine, and X3 is tryptophan. Alternative sequences include substitutions at X1 with tyrosine or leucine, at X2 with glutamine or serine, and at X3 with methionine or isoleucine. The peptide may include additional amino acids appended to the N-terminus, such as glycine or lysine, or to the C-terminus, such as amide or carboxyl groups, to modulate charge, solubility, or stability. The peptide may be modified by the incorporation of non-natural amino acids, including N-methylated, D-amino acids, or β-amino acids, to improve resistance to proteolytic degradation. The peptide binds specifically to the HIV-1 capsid protein at the interface of the N-terminal and C-terminal domains, engaging residues L56, M66, Q67, N74, A105, K70, and R173. The binding affinity of the peptide for CA is less than 100 nM, as measured by microscale thermophoresis, and the peptide inhibits HIV-1 replication in peripheral blood mononuclear cells with an EC50 of less than 50 nM. The peptide may be produced by solid-phase peptide synthesis using standard Fmoc chemistry, followed by purification via reverse-phase high-performance liquid chromatography. The peptide may be formulated into a pharmaceutical composition with a pharmaceutically acceptable carrier, including saline, dextrose, or liposomes, for parenteral administration. The peptide may be administered as a single agent or in combination with reverse transcriptase inhibitors, protease inhibitors, integrase inhibitors, or other capsid-targeting agents to suppress viral replication and prevent resistance. An isolated polynucleotide encoding the peptide is provided, comprising a nucleotide sequence that translates into the amino acid sequence of the peptide. The polynucleotide may be operably linked to a promoter and introduced into a host cell to produce the peptide recombinantly. The host cell may be a bacterial, yeast, insect, or mammalian cell line, and the expression may be induced by chemical, thermal, or light stimuli. The resulting peptide may be purified from the culture supernatant or cell lysate using affinity chromatography or ion-exchange techniques.

## EXAMPLES

### Example 1

The HIV-1 capsid protein hexamer was prepared for docking simulations using the crystal structure of the native capsid protein bound to PF74 (PDB ID: 4XFZ). The structure was imported into the Maestro interface of the Schrödinger Suite, and the PrepWizard module was employed to add hydrogen atoms, assign bond orders, correct protonation states, and optimize side chain conformations. Water molecules within 5 Å of the binding site were retained, and the entire hexamer was energy-minimized using the OPLS4 force field to relieve steric clashes. The binding site was defined as a 36 Å × 36 Å × 36 Å grid centered on the centroid of PF74 in the crystal structure, encompassing residues L56, M66, Q67, N74, A105, K70, and R173. The grid was used for subsequent docking of GS-CA1, GS-6207, and the designed cyclic peptide.

### Example 2

Induced-Fit Docking (IFD) simulations were performed using the Glide module of the Schrödinger Suite. GS-CA1 and GS-6207 were generated using ChemSketch and minimized using MacroModel prior to docking. The IFD protocol was executed with the Refinement module of Prime to allow side chain flexibility in the binding pocket. For each compound, 100 docking poses were generated, and the pose with the best IFD score was selected for analysis. The results confirmed that GS-CA1 and GS-6207 bind in the same pocket as PF74, with GS-6207 exhibiting a 2.1 kcal/mol lower binding energy due to enhanced hydrogen bonding with K70. The conformation of K70 in the GS-6207 complex was shifted by 5.2 Å compared to the GS-CA1 complex, enabling a direct hydrogen bond with the amide carbonyl of GS-6207.

### Example 3

The cyclic peptide Pep-2-cyclic was modeled using the Prime module of the Schrödinger Suite, with the sequence F-N-Q-W-L-T-K-R-A-F-D-S-T-Y. The peptide was subjected to energy minimization using the MM/GBSA method to identify its lowest energy conformation. IFD was then performed with the CA hexamer as the receptor, allowing side chain flexibility in residues L56, M66, Q67, N74, A105, K70, and R173. The best-scoring complex showed that the phenylalanine at position 1 occupied the hydrophobic pocket formed by L56 and M66, the tryptophan at position 4 engaged N74 and A105, and the arginine at position 8 formed a salt bridge with R173. The peptide adopted a β-turn conformation that closely mimicked the backbone fold of CPSF6 and NUP153. PatchDock simulations corroborated the binding pose, with a 92% overlap in atomic coordinates.

### Example 4

The cyclic peptide Pep-2-cyclic was synthesized using solid-phase peptide synthesis on Rink amide resin with Fmoc-protected amino acids. After cleavage and purification by reverse-phase HPLC, the peptide was cyclized in solution using HATU and DIPEA. The molecular weight was confirmed by mass spectrometry. Fluorescent labeling of CA-hexamers was performed using Alexa Fluor 647 according to the manufacturer’s protocol. MST assays were conducted using a Monolith NT.115 instrument, with 200 nM labeled CA-hexamer and increasing concentrations of Pep-2-cyclic (1–2000 nM) or PF74. Thermophoresis was monitored under 20% LED power and 20 s MST-on time. Data were analyzed using MO. Affinity software and fitted to a quadratic binding equation. The Kd for Pep-2-cyclic was determined to be 32 ± 3 nM, while the Kd for PF74 was 212 ± 7 nM, demonstrating a 6.6-fold improvement in binding affinity. Data were plotted using GraphPad Prism, and the binding curve for Pep-2-cyclic exhibited a steeper slope and lower plateau, indicating higher cooperativity.
