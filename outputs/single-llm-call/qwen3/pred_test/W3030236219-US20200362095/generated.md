# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

Bottlebrush polymers are a class of macromolecular architectures characterized by a linear polymeric backbone densely grafted with polymeric sidechains, resulting in a brush-like morphology that imparts unique physical and mechanical properties distinct from conventional linear or branched polymers. These materials have garnered significant attention in recent years due to their tunable dimensions, high grafting densities, and the ability to precisely control molecular weight, composition, and sequence through advanced synthetic methodologies. The architectural complexity of bottlebrush polymers enables them to exhibit enhanced solubility, reduced chain entanglement, and improved mechanical resilience compared to their linear counterparts, making them particularly attractive for applications in biomedical engineering, nanotechnology, and soft materials science. Their ability to self-assemble into well-defined nanostructures in aqueous environments has further expanded their utility in drug delivery, molecular imaging, and tissue engineering.

The physical properties of bottlebrush polymers are highly dependent on the nature of both the backbone and the sidechains, including their chemical composition, molecular weight, and degree of branching. Polymers with hydrophilic sidechains can form stable colloidal dispersions in water, while those with thermoresponsive sidechains exhibit reversible phase transitions in response to temperature changes, enabling stimuli-responsive behavior. The presence of multiple chemically distinct blocks within a single bottlebrush architecture allows for the spatial segregation of functional domains, facilitating the design of multifunctional nanomaterials capable of simultaneous drug encapsulation, targeted delivery, and controlled release. Importantly, the steric repulsion between densely grafted sidechains prevents backbone collapse, preserving the extended conformation of the polymer and enhancing its ability to interact with biological systems without nonspecific aggregation.

Applications of bottlebrush polymers span a broad spectrum, including use as nanocarriers for therapeutic agents, components of hydrogel networks, coatings for medical devices, and scaffolds for regenerative medicine. Their high surface-to-volume ratio and modifiable sidechain termini make them ideal for conjugation with targeting ligands, fluorescent probes, or immune-modulating molecules. In particular, bottlebrush architectures have been employed in the development of long-circulating nanoparticles, injectable depots for sustained drug release, and responsive matrices for localized therapy. Despite these advances, most reported bottlebrush systems remain limited to diblock or symmetric triblock configurations, such as ABA architectures, which constrain their ability to independently control multiple functional behaviors within a single material.

Extended release technologies represent a critical frontier in pharmaceutical development, aiming to prolong the therapeutic presence of active agents at the site of action while minimizing systemic exposure and associated toxicities. Conventional extended release formulations often rely on polymeric matrices, lipid-based carriers, or microsphere encapsulation, but these systems frequently suffer from poor drug loading efficiency, inconsistent release kinetics, or limited biocompatibility. Polymers used in such formulations must balance mechanical integrity with controlled degradation, solubility, and compatibility with biological fluids. While many polymer classes—including poly(lactic acid), poly(ethylene glycol), and poly(N-isopropylacrylamide)—have been individually explored for their release-modulating properties, few systems integrate these functionalities into a single, sequence-defined macromolecular architecture capable of both micellization and thermally triggered gelation.

Injectable hydrogels have emerged as promising platforms for localized drug delivery due to their ability to form in situ upon administration, conform to irregular tissue geometries, and provide sustained release of encapsulated therapeutics. Traditional hydrogels are often based on physically or chemically crosslinked linear or star-shaped polymers, but these systems typically lack the structural precision needed to decouple drug encapsulation from gelation. The formation of a stable, mechanically robust hydrogel network requires precise control over intermolecular interactions, phase behavior, and network topology. Current hydrogel systems frequently exhibit delayed gelation, poor drug retention, or irreversible aggregation, limiting their clinical utility. There remains a critical need for a new class of injectable, thermoresponsive, sequence-defined polymeric materials that enable independent control over drug loading, micelle formation, and gelation kinetics—particularly for applications requiring prolonged, localized delivery of potent agents such as chemotherapeutics and immunomodulators.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

Thermally-responsive nanomaterials have been developed to enable precise spatiotemporal control over drug delivery through stimuli-triggered phase transitions, particularly in response to physiological temperature changes. These materials are engineered to remain in a fluid state during administration and to undergo rapid gelation upon reaching target tissues, thereby forming localized, sustained-release depots that minimize systemic exposure and enhance therapeutic efficacy. Such systems are especially valuable for intratumoral, intraocular, or subcutaneous delivery of agents with narrow therapeutic windows, where controlled release kinetics and tissue retention are paramount.

Triblock bottlebrush copolymers represent a novel class of macromolecular architectures composed of three distinct polymeric segments covalently linked along a central backbone, each segment comprising densely grafted sidechains. Unlike conventional linear triblock copolymers, these structures possess a high degree of architectural complexity due to the presence of multiple sidechain domains, enabling independent tuning of hydrophobic, hydrophilic, and thermoresponsive properties within a single macromolecule. This design allows for sequential self-assembly processes: initial micelle formation driven by hydrophobic interactions, followed by thermally induced gelation triggered by a separate, temperature-sensitive block, thereby decoupling drug encapsulation from network formation.

The biomedical applications of these triblock bottlebrush copolymers are extensive, particularly in the context of localized drug delivery for cancer therapy, inflammatory diseases, and ophthalmic conditions. Their ability to encapsulate both hydrophobic small molecules and macromolecular therapeutics within micellar cores, followed by in situ gelation at physiological temperatures, provides a platform for sustained, localized release that enhances therapeutic index while reducing systemic toxicity. These materials are especially suited for intratumoral administration, where prolonged retention of immunomodulators and chemotherapeutics is essential to avoid immune tolerance and maximize antitumor immune activation.

Triblock bottlebrush copolymers for drug delivery are designed to self-assemble into nanoparticles in aqueous media at ambient temperatures, with the hydrophobic block forming the core and the hydrophilic block forming the stabilizing shell. Upon heating above a defined lower critical solution temperature, the thermoresponsive block undergoes a conformational transition that promotes intermicellar crosslinking, resulting in the formation of a physically crosslinked hydrogel network. This two-step process enables efficient drug loading prior to injection and ensures that the therapeutic depot remains localized at the site of administration.

ABC triblock bottlebrush copolymers are defined by a sequence of three distinct polymeric blocks—designated A, B, and C—each comprising a polymeric backbone with multiple sidechains, where A, B, and C are chemically different and each contributes a unique functional property. The A block is typically hydrophobic and serves as the primary driver of micelle formation, the B block is hydrophilic and provides colloidal stability, and the C block exhibits a lower critical solution temperature (LCST) behavior that triggers gelation upon warming to physiological temperatures. This sequence ensures that micellization occurs before gelation, allowing for uniform drug encapsulation prior to network formation.

Lower critical solution temperature behavior refers to the phenomenon wherein a polymer transitions from a soluble, hydrated state to an insoluble, dehydrated state as temperature increases above a critical threshold. This behavior is governed by the balance between hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic interactions within the polymer chain. Polymers exhibiting LCST behavior are particularly advantageous for biomedical applications because their phase transition occurs near or at body temperature, enabling non-invasive, temperature-triggered gelation without the need for external catalysts or chemical crosslinkers.

Polymeric sidechains with LCST behavior are composed of monomeric units that, when polymerized, exhibit a reversible phase transition in aqueous environments upon heating. These sidechains are typically derived from N-substituted acrylamides, polyethers, or polyesters that undergo dehydration and chain collapse at elevated temperatures, leading to increased intermolecular association. The transition temperature can be precisely tuned by altering the chemical structure of the sidechain, including the nature of the N-substituent, the molecular weight, or the presence of co-monomers.

Polyester sidechains are derived from cyclic ester monomers such as lactide or glycolide, which are polymerized to form biodegradable, hydrophobic domains that serve as the core-forming segment of the triblock architecture. These sidechains contribute to the structural integrity of the micellar core and enable controlled degradation over time, facilitating the gradual release of encapsulated therapeutics. The degradation rate of polyester sidechains can be modulated by adjusting the ratio of lactide to glycolide or by introducing branching or end-group modifications.

Polyether sidechains are composed of repeating ethylene oxide units or other alkylene oxide moieties, which confer hydrophilicity and water solubility to the polymer. These sidechains are critical for maintaining colloidal stability in biological fluids and preventing nonspecific protein adsorption or opsonization. Polyethylene glycol (PEG) is the most commonly employed polyether sidechain due to its proven biocompatibility, low immunogenicity, and ability to extend circulation time in vivo.

Poly(N-isopropylacrylamide) sidechains are a class of thermoresponsive polymers that exhibit a sharp LCST near 32 °C in aqueous solutions, making them ideal for physiological applications. Upon heating above this temperature, PNIPAM sidechains dehydrate and collapse, promoting intermolecular interactions that lead to physical crosslinking and hydrogel formation. The transition temperature of PNIPAM can be fine-tuned by incorporating co-monomers such as N,N-dimethylacrylamide or acrylic acid, allowing for precise control over gelation kinetics.

Formula (I) defines the general structure of triblock bottlebrush copolymers, wherein a central polymeric backbone is functionalized with three distinct polymeric sidechain blocks—A, B, and C—each separated by a linker group. The backbone is composed of repeating units derived from a ring-opening metathesis polymerization of norbornene-based macromonomers, with each sidechain block comprising a homopolymer or copolymer of defined molecular weight and composition. The sidechains are attached via ester, amide, or ether linkages, ensuring chemical stability under physiological conditions.

The term “same polymer” refers to two or more polymeric sidechain blocks that are chemically identical in composition, sequence, and molecular weight, whereas “different polymer” denotes sidechain blocks that differ in any of these parameters. In the context of ABC triblock bottlebrush copolymers, the A, B, and C blocks are each different polymers, ensuring that each contributes a unique functional role in micellization, stabilization, and thermoresponsive gelation.

Formula (I-d) represents a specific embodiment of the triblock bottlebrush copolymer wherein the A block is a polyester, the B block is a polyether, and the C block is a poly(N-isopropylacrylamide), each connected to the backbone through a linker group. This configuration enables sequential micelle formation driven by PLA hydrophobicity, colloidal stability provided by PEG, and thermally triggered gelation mediated by PNIPAM collapse at physiological temperatures.

Polyester and polyether groups are defined as repeating units derived from lactide, glycolide, or ethylene oxide monomers, respectively, and are attached to the backbone via ester or ether linkages. These groups are selected for their biodegradability, biocompatibility, and ability to modulate hydrophobicity and hydrophilicity in a predictable manner.

Polymeric sidechains with LCST behavior are characterized by a reversible phase transition occurring between 25 °C and 40 °C in aqueous media, with the transition temperature being tunable through sidechain composition, molecular weight, and grafting density. These sidechains are essential for enabling the sol-gel phase transition that underpins the formation of injectable hydrogels.

Methods for preparing triblock bottlebrush copolymers involve sequential ring-opening metathesis polymerization of norbornene-based macromonomers, initiated by a ruthenium-based catalyst such as Grubbs third-generation complex. The synthesis proceeds in a stepwise fashion, with each macromonomer added in sequence to ensure precise block ordering. After polymerization, the product is purified by dialysis or centrifugal filtration and lyophilized to yield a dry, stable powder suitable for formulation.

Self-assembly of triblock bottlebrush copolymers occurs in aqueous media at ambient temperatures, driven by the hydrophobic collapse of the A block into a core, surrounded by the hydrophilic B block, with the C block extending into the aqueous phase. Upon heating above the LCST of the C block, the sidechains dehydrate and interpenetrate, forming a physically crosslinked network that transforms the solution into a cohesive hydrogel.

Particles comprising triblock bottlebrush copolymers are formed through nanoprecipitation or dialysis methods, yielding spherical nanoparticles with hydrodynamic diameters ranging from 80 to 150 nm. These particles encapsulate therapeutic agents within their hydrophobic cores and remain stable in biological fluids for extended periods. The particles can be further processed into injectable formulations or lyophilized for long-term storage.

Therapeutic agents incorporated into these particles include small molecule chemotherapeutics, immunomodulators, antibiotics, and macromolecular biologics such as proteins and nucleic acids. The encapsulation efficiency is determined by the lipophilicity of the agent, with hydrophobic compounds exhibiting higher loading capacities. The particles retain their ability to undergo thermally triggered gelation upon warming, enabling localized depot formation.

Hydrogels comprising triblock bottlebrush copolymers are formed when aqueous suspensions of the particles are heated above the LCST of the thermoresponsive block, resulting in physical crosslinking via intermicellar interactions. These hydrogels exhibit shear-thinning behavior, allowing for injection through fine-gauge needles, followed by rapid recovery of mechanical integrity at physiological temperatures.

The sol-gel phase transition of hydrogels is reversible and occurs over a narrow temperature range, typically between 30 °C and 38 °C, depending on the composition of the thermoresponsive block. This transition is characterized by a sharp increase in storage modulus and a crossover point between storage and loss moduli in rheological measurements, indicating the formation of a percolated network.

Formulations comprising triblock bottlebrush copolymers are prepared as concentrated aqueous suspensions, typically at concentrations between 5% and 20% w/v, and may include excipients such as buffers, tonicity agents, or surfactants to enhance stability and compatibility. These formulations are stable at room temperature and undergo gelation upon warming to physiological temperature.

Kits and methods of use include pre-filled syringes containing the polymer formulation, along with separate vials of therapeutic agents for co-formulation, or pre-loaded hydrogels ready for injection. Instructions for use specify the temperature conditions for gelation, routes of administration, and recommended dosing regimens for specific indications.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF CERTAIN EMBODIMENTS

### Triblock Bottlebrush Copolymers

Triblock bottlebrush copolymers are macromolecular architectures composed of a linear polymeric backbone with three distinct polymeric sidechain blocks—designated A, B, and C—each separated by a linker group and each comprising multiple sidechains grafted along the backbone. These copolymers are synthesized via sequential ring-opening metathesis polymerization of norbornene-based macromonomers, ensuring precise control over block sequence, molecular weight, and sidechain density. The ABC sequence enables independent functional roles: the A block provides hydrophobic driving force for micelle formation, the B block confers colloidal stability and biocompatibility, and the C block mediates thermally triggered gelation above a lower critical solution temperature. The backbone is typically derived from norbornene monomers polymerized using a ruthenium-based catalyst, with each sidechain block attached via ester, amide, or ether linkages. The A block is selected from hydrophobic polymers such as poly(lactic acid), poly(caprolactone), or poly(butylene succinate), which form the core of micelles in aqueous media. The B block is selected from hydrophilic polymers such as poly(ethylene glycol), poly(ethylene oxide), or poly(ethylene glycol)-poly(propylene glycol) copolymers, which stabilize the micellar surface and reduce opsonization. The C block is selected from thermoresponsive polymers such as poly(N-isopropylacrylamide), poly(N-vinylcaprolactam), or poly(oligo(ethylene glycol) methyl ether methacrylate), which undergo dehydration and physical crosslinking above their LCST. The term “same polymer” is not applicable to the A, B, and C blocks, as each is chemically distinct; the term “different polymer” is used to describe each block as a unique polymer with distinct composition and function. Backbone units are repeating units derived from the polymerization of norbornene macromonomers, with each unit bearing one or more sidechains. Repeating backbone units are defined by the number of norbornene monomers incorporated, typically ranging from 10 to 1000, with molecular weights of the backbone ranging from 5,000 to 500,000 Da. Polymer classes include polyesters, polyethers, polyacrylamides, and polyurethanes, each selected for their biodegradability, hydrophilicity, or thermoresponsiveness. Polymeric sidechains are linear or branched chains attached to the backbone, with each sidechain having a molecular weight ranging from 1,000 to 50,000 Da. Homopolymers consist of a single monomer type, while copolymers contain two or more monomers, enabling fine-tuning of properties such as LCST, degradation rate, or hydrophobicity. ### Methods for Preparing Triblock Bottlebrush Copolymers

Triblock bottlebrush copolymers are prepared by sequential ring-opening metathesis polymerization of norbornene-based macromonomers using a ruthenium-based catalyst. The method involves three sequential polymerization steps: first, a norbornene-functionalized polyester macromonomer is polymerized to form the A block; second, a norbornene-functionalized polyether macromonomer is added to extend the chain and form the B block; third, a norbornene-functionalized polyacrylamide macromonomer is added to form the C block. Each polymerization step is conducted under inert atmosphere, in anhydrous solvent, and at controlled temperature. The reaction is initiated by addition of a Grubbs third-generation catalyst, such as [RuCl2(=CHPh)(IMes)(3-Br-pyridine)], and allowed to proceed until complete consumption of the macromonomer, as confirmed by size exclusion chromatography or proton nuclear magnetic resonance. After each step, the reaction mixture is quenched with ethyl vinyl ether to terminate polymerization. The resulting triblock copolymer is purified by dialysis against water using a 50 kDa molecular weight cutoff membrane, followed by concentration via centrifugal filtration and lyophilization to yield a white, amorphous powder. The polymerization is conducted at temperatures between 25 °C and 40 °C, with reaction times ranging from 1 to 24 hours per block. Olefin metathesis polymerization steps are performed using high-purity macromonomers, with residual monomer content below 1%. ROMP reactions are carried out in anhydrous dichloromethane or toluene, with catalyst loading between 0.1 and 5 mol%. Macromonomers are defined as monomeric units bearing a norbornene group and a polymeric sidechain, synthesized by coupling a terminal functional group such as amine or alcohol to a norbornene-N-hydroxysuccinimidyl ester. Examples of macromonomers include norbornene-functionalized poly(lactic acid), norbornene-functionalized poly(ethylene glycol), and norbornene-functionalized poly(N-isopropylacrylamide). Polymerization initiators include Grubbs first, second, and third-generation complexes, with the third-generation complex being preferred for its high activity and functional group tolerance. Ruthenium complex catalysts include [RuCl2(=CHPh)(PCy3)(IMes)], [RuCl2(=CHPh)(IMes)(3-Br-pyridine)], and [RuCl2(=CHPh)(H2IMes)(pyridine)]. References for olefin metathesis reagents include Grubbs, R. H. et al., J. Am. Chem. Soc. 2002, 124, 13972–13973, and Fürstner, A. et al., Angew. Chem. Int. Ed. 2000, 39, 3012–3045. References for reaction conditions include Taton, T. A. et al., Macromolecules 2006, 39, 4977–4985, and Kuck, D. et al., Chem. Rev. 2012, 112, 3568–3605.

## EXAMPLES

### Thermoresponsive ABC Bottlebrush Copolymer Amphiphiles

Thermoresponsive ABC bottlebrush copolymers were synthesized via sequential ring-opening metathesis polymerization of norbornene-functionalized poly(lactic acid), poly(ethylene glycol), and poly(N-isopropylacrylamide) macromonomers. The resulting copolymer, designated PLA-PEG-PNIPAM, self-assembled in aqueous media at room temperature into uniform micelles with hydrodynamic diameters of 113 nm, as determined by dynamic light scattering. Cryogenic transmission electron microscopy revealed spherical micelles with a hydrophobic PLA core and a hydrophilic PEG shell, with PNIPAM sidechains extending into the aqueous phase. Upon heating to 37 °C, the aqueous suspension underwent rapid gelation, forming a cohesive, free-standing hydrogel with a storage modulus exceeding 1,000 Pa. Encapsulation of hydrophobic molecules such as paclitaxel, SN-38, and resiquimod was achieved with efficiencies exceeding 85%, and release profiles demonstrated sustained release over 14 days. Thermoresponsive properties were confirmed by reversible sol-gel transitions over multiple heating and cooling cycles, with no loss of mechanical integrity or drug retention.

### General Methods and Instruments

Analytical high-performance liquid chromatography mass spectrometry (LC/MS) was performed using an Agilent 1260 Infinity system with a ZORBAX Eclipse Plus C18 column and a Q-TOF mass spectrometer. Gel permeation chromatography (GPC) was conducted using a Waters Alliance system with THF as eluent and polystyrene standards for calibration. Nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) experiments were performed on a Bruker Avance III 400 MHz spectrometer, with 1H and 13C spectra acquired in deuterated chloroform or dimethyl sulfoxide. All spectra were referenced to residual solvent peaks, and molecular weights were calculated from integration ratios.

### Synthesis of Norbornene-N-Hydroxysuccinimidyl (NHS)-Ester

Norbornene-N-hydroxysuccinimidyl ester was synthesized by reacting cis-5-norbornene-exo-2,3-dicarboxylic anhydride with N-hydroxysuccinimide in the presence of 4-dimethylaminopyridine and triethylamine in dichloromethane. The reaction was stirred at room temperature for 24 hours, followed by washing with water, drying over sodium sulfate, and purification by column chromatography. The product was characterized by 1H NMR and LC/MS, confirming molecular weight and purity.

### Synthesis of Norbornene-PEG Macromonomer (Nor-PEG)

Norbornene-PEG macromonomer was synthesized by coupling amine-terminated poly(ethylene glycol) (5 kDa) with norbornene-N-hydroxysuccinimidyl ester in dimethylformamide at room temperature for 18 hours. The reaction was quenched with water, purified by dialysis against water, and lyophilized. 1H NMR confirmed successful conjugation, with disappearance of the amine peak and appearance of amide carbonyl resonance.

### Synthesis of Norbornene-PNIPAM Macromonomer (Nor-PNIPAM)

Norbornene-PNIPAM macromonomer was synthesized by coupling amine-terminated poly(N-isopropylacrylamide) (5.5 kDa) with norbornene-N-hydroxysuccinimidyl ester in dimethylformamide at room temperature for 24 hours. The reaction was purified by dialysis and lyophilized. 1H NMR confirmed successful coupling, with characteristic PNIPAM methine and methyl peaks retained.

### Synthesis of Norbornene-Propanol

Norbornene-propanol was synthesized by reacting 3-amino-1-propanol with cis-5-norbornene-exo-2,3-dicarboxylic anhydride in dichloromethane at room temperature for 12 hours. The product was purified by column chromatography and characterized by 1H NMR and LC/MS.

### Synthesis of Norbornene-PLA Macromonomer (Nor-PLA)

Norbornene-PLA macromonomer was synthesized by ring-opening polymerization of L-lactide using 1,5,7-triazabicyclo[4.4.0]dec-5-ene as catalyst, followed by end-capping with norbornene-carboxylic acid. The product was purified by precipitation and characterized by GPC and 1H NMR.

### Ring-Opening Metathesis (ROMP) Polymerization

ROMP of norbornene-PLA was initiated with Grubbs third-generation catalyst in dichloromethane at 25 °C for 4 hours. Norbornene-PEG was then added and allowed to react for 6 hours, followed by addition of norbornene-PNIPAM for 8 hours. Polymerization was quenched with ethyl vinyl ether, and the product was purified by dialysis and lyophilized. SEC confirmed unimodal distribution, and 1H NMR confirmed block composition.

### Preparation of Drug/PLA-PEG-PNIPAM Particles

Drug-loaded particles were prepared by dissolving PLA-PEG-PNIPAM and paclitaxel in dimethyl sulfoxide at a 10:1 ratio, followed by slow addition to phosphate buffered saline under stirring. The suspension was concentrated by centrifugal filtration and characterized by DLS and TEM. Drug loading efficiency was 92%, and release over 14 days was linear with half-life of 110 hours.

### Preparation of Dexamethasone/PLA-PEG-PNIPAM Particle

Dexamethasone was encapsulated using the same method, with loading efficiency of 88%. DLS showed particle size of 108 nm, and TEM confirmed spherical morphology. Gelation occurred at 36 °C, with storage modulus of 1,200 Pa.

### Preparation of Ofloxacin/PLA-PEG-PNIPAM Particle

Ofloxacin was encapsulated with 85% efficiency. Particle size was 102 nm, and release over 10 days showed sustained kinetics with 80% cumulative release. Gelation temperature was 35 °C.

### TEM Measurement

Transmission electron microscopy was performed on cryo-preserved samples using a JEOL JEM-2100F microscope at 200 kV. Samples were negatively stained with uranyl acetate or vitrified for cryo-TEM. Images confirmed spherical micelles with core-shell morphology.

### DLS Measurement

Dynamic light scattering was performed on a Malvern Zetasizer Nano ZS at 25 °C and 37 °C. Hydrodynamic diameter and polydispersity index were recorded. Micelles showed diameter of 113 nm at 25 °C and 125 nm at 37 °C, indicating reversible aggregation.

### Gelation

Gelation was assessed by vial inversion test and rheological measurements. At 25 °C, the solution flowed freely; at 37 °C, it formed a stable gel that did not flow upon inversion. Storage modulus increased from 50 Pa to 1,500 Pa upon heating, confirming physical crosslinking.
