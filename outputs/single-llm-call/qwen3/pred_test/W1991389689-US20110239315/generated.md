# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNICAL FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The invention relates to the field of molecular biology and genetic engineering, specifically to the design, construction, and application of synthetic polypeptides capable of binding to predetermined DNA sequences with high specificity and affinity. These polypeptides are derived from transcription activator-like (TAL) effectors of phytopathogenic bacteria in the genus Xanthomonas and are engineered for targeted modulation of gene expression in eukaryotic cells, including plant and human cells. The invention further encompasses methods for assembling such polypeptides using modular cloning techniques, as well as their use in genome editing, transcriptional activation or repression, and the generation of genetically modified organisms with altered phenotypic traits.

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

Phytopathogenic bacteria of the genus Xanthomonas secrete a class of effector proteins known as transcription activator-like (TAL) effectors, which play a central role in pathogenesis by entering host plant cells and activating the expression of specific host genes that facilitate bacterial colonization. These effectors contain a central domain composed of tandem repeats, each approximately 34 amino acids in length, which collectively recognize and bind to a specific DNA sequence within the promoter region of target genes. Each repeat unit within this domain corresponds to a single nucleotide base pair in the target DNA sequence, and the identity of the bound base is determined by two hypervariable amino acids located at positions 12 and 13 of each repeat, commonly referred to as the repeat-variable diresidue (RVD). This one-to-one correspondence between repeat unit and DNA base pair constitutes a predictable and modular code for DNA recognition, enabling the rational design of custom DNA-binding domains. 

The DNA-binding domain of TAL effectors is distinct from other known DNA-binding motifs such as zinc fingers or helix-turn-helix domains, as it does not rely on complex tertiary folding or cooperative interactions between multiple domains. Instead, the linear arrangement of repeat units directly encodes the target sequence, allowing for straightforward prediction and engineering of binding specificity. This feature has motivated efforts to repurpose TAL effectors as programmable tools for genome manipulation in non-native hosts, including mammalian cells. However, prior methods for generating custom DNA-binding proteins, such as zinc finger nucleases, required labor-intensive protein engineering, limited modularity, and often suffered from context-dependent binding affinities and off-target effects. 

Current approaches to constructing TAL-based DNA-binding proteins have been hindered by the highly repetitive nature of the repeat domain, which renders conventional cloning methods prone to recombination errors, misassembly, and low efficiency. PCR-based assembly techniques have been employed but introduce sequence artifacts due to amplification bias and require extensive codon optimization across repeats, which may alter translational efficiency or protein folding. Furthermore, existing methods lack flexibility in accommodating variable numbers of repeats, diverse terminal domains, or fusion partners such as transcriptional activators or nucleases. There remains a critical need for a rapid, scalable, and error-resistant method to assemble TAL effectors with user-defined repeat architectures, enabling precise control over gene expression in diverse eukaryotic systems without reliance on species-specific cofactors.

## BRIEF SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The invention provides a novel method for producing synthetic polypeptides comprising a repeat domain derived from TAL effectors, wherein the repeat domain is assembled from modular repeat units that each recognize a specific DNA base pair through a defined recognition code. Each repeat unit is encoded by a polynucleotide sequence that includes a conserved core flanked by type IIs restriction enzyme recognition sites, enabling seamless, directional, and scarless assembly via Golden Gate cloning. The repeat domain is composed of a series of repeat units arranged in a linear order that corresponds precisely to the nucleotide sequence of a target DNA region, thereby conferring programmable DNA-binding specificity. The polypeptide produced by this method may further include an N-terminal tag, a nuclear localization signal, and a C-terminal regulatory domain, such as the VP16 transcriptional activation domain, to enhance functionality in heterologous hosts. 

The invention further encompasses polynucleotide molecules encoding such polypeptides, host organisms and cells transformed with these molecules, and methods for constructing polypeptides by modular assembly of repeat units in a single-step ligation reaction. The invention also provides a method for targeted modulation of gene expression by introducing the polypeptide into a cell, where it binds to a specific endogenous or exogenous DNA sequence and alters transcriptional activity through recruitment of transcriptional machinery or fusion to effector domains. Additionally, the invention enables directed modification of target DNA sequences by fusing the repeat domain to a nuclease domain, such as FokI or I-SceI, to induce double-strand breaks at predetermined genomic loci, thereby facilitating homologous recombination or non-homologous end joining for precise genome editing. The modular architecture of the invention permits the construction of polypeptides with any number of repeat units ranging from one to over twenty-four, allowing for recognition of DNA sequences of variable length and complexity.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

The invention is described with reference to the accompanying figures, which illustrate the modular architecture of synthetic TAL effectors, the cloning strategy for their assembly, and their functional activity in plant and human cells. Embodiments of the invention include polypeptides comprising a repeat domain composed of tandem repeat units, each containing a conserved 34-amino acid scaffold and a hypervariable region at positions 12 and 13 that determines DNA base-pair specificity. The recognition code is defined by the following amino acid pairs: NI for adenine, HD for cytosine, NN for guanine or adenine, and NG for thymine, with additional variants such as NK, NS, and HN shown to recognize alternative bases. Each repeat unit is encoded by a synthetic polynucleotide sequence that includes flanking BpiI restriction sites, enabling precise, directional insertion into an assembly vector without altering the amino acid sequence. 

The term “repeat domain” refers to a contiguous array of repeat units, each contributing to the recognition of a single DNA base pair, such that the linear order of the repeat units dictates the sequence specificity of the entire domain. A “repeat unit” is a discrete structural and functional module corresponding to one base pair, comprising the conserved core and the hypervariable diresidue. The “recognition code” is the empirically validated correspondence between the amino acid residues at positions 12 and 13 and the target DNA base. The “effector” is a polypeptide comprising a repeat domain and optionally fused to a regulatory domain, such as VP16, GAL4, or a nuclease domain, that modulates gene expression or cleaves DNA upon binding. The term “naturally occurring” refers to polypeptides derived from wild-type Xanthomonas strains without artificial modification of the repeat sequence or fusion partners. 

“Modulating expression” encompasses both the activation and inhibition of transcription of a target gene, achieved by fusing the repeat domain to transcriptional activators or repressors, respectively. “Activating expression” refers to the enhancement of transcriptional output through recruitment of RNA polymerase and co-activators, while “inhibiting expression” refers to suppression of transcription via recruitment of histone deacetylases, methyltransferases, or other chromatin-modifying repressors. Assays for modulation of gene expression include reporter gene systems such as luciferase or β-glucuronidase, quantitative RT-PCR for endogenous mRNA levels, and chromatin immunoprecipitation to confirm binding at the target locus. 

The “regulatory domain” is a polypeptide segment that confers transcriptional activity upon binding to DNA, and may be derived from viral, fungal, or mammalian transcription factors, such as VP16, GAL4, p65, or the Krüppel-associated box (KRAB). Sources of regulatory domains include herpes simplex virus, Saccharomyces cerevisiae, Homo sapiens, and other eukaryotes. Examples of transcription factors and their associated modifiers include histone acetyltransferases such as p300 and CBP, which enhance transcription, and histone deacetylases such as HDAC1 and SIRT1, which repress it. Kinases and phosphatases that modify transcription factors, such as MAPK and PP2A, may also be fused to modulate activity dynamically. Oncogenes such as MYC and RAS, DNA repair enzymes such as PARP1 and DNA-PK, and chromatin remodelers such as SWI/SNF complexes are further examples of proteins whose activity can be harnessed through fusion to the repeat domain. 

The term “gene” refers to a nucleic acid sequence encoding a functional product, including coding and non-coding regions such as promoters, enhancers, introns, and untranslated regions. The “target gene” is the specific gene whose expression is intended to be modulated by the polypeptide. The term “plant” encompasses all members of the kingdom Plantae, including monocots, dicots, gymnosperms, and bryophytes, as well as their tissues, cells, and protoplasts. “Modulating the expression of a target gene in plant cells” refers to the alteration of transcriptional activity of an endogenous or transgene promoter through targeted binding of the polypeptide to its regulatory region. 

The invention encompasses polynucleotide molecules encoding the synthetic polypeptides, including analogs with modified sugar-phosphate backbones such as phosphorothioates, 2′-O-methyl RNA, or locked nucleic acids, and forms such as linear, circular, or viral vector-integrated sequences. A polypeptide is said to “specifically bind to a target DNA sequence” when it binds with a dissociation constant lower than 10⁻⁸ M and exhibits negligible binding to non-cognate sequences under physiological conditions. The “target DNA sequence” is the specific nucleotide sequence recognized by the repeat domain, typically 14 to 30 base pairs in length and preceded by a 5′ thymine residue. “Endogenous” refers to sequences naturally present in the host genome, while “exogenous” refers to sequences introduced exogenously. A “genetically modified plant” is one whose genome has been altered by the stable integration of a polynucleotide encoding the synthetic polypeptide. 

The “minimal promoter” is a truncated promoter region sufficient to support basal transcription, typically lacking enhancer elements and containing only the TATA box and transcription start site. A “repressor protein” is a polypeptide that reduces transcriptional output, and “repression” is the functional outcome of its binding. An “activator protein” enhances transcription, and “activation” is the resulting increase in mRNA synthesis. A “derivative” is a polypeptide with amino acid substitutions, deletions, or insertions that retain the ability to bind the target sequence. A “repeat unit derived from TAL effector” is a polypeptide segment with conserved structural features and a hypervariable diresidue that confers base-specific recognition. 

The TAL effector family includes AvrBs3, Hax2, Hax3, Hax4, and other AvrBs3-like proteins, all of which share a conserved N-terminal domain, a central tandem repeat domain, and a C-terminal activation domain. The structure of TAL effectors comprises an N-terminal secretion signal, a nuclear localization signal, the repeat domain, and a C-terminal region with acidic residues that function as an activation domain. The DNA-binding specificity of TAL effectors is determined solely by the sequence of the repeat domain, with each repeat unit recognizing one base pair in a 5′ to 3′ direction. The hypervariable region within each repeat unit is located at positions 12 and 13 and is responsible for base-specific recognition. The recognition code is established by empirical correlation between RVDs and nucleotides, and is applied to design polypeptides for any desired DNA sequence. 

The modular architecture of the repeat domain permits the combination of repeat units in any order to generate polypeptides with novel DNA-binding specificities. Prior art has demonstrated mutagenesis and polynucleotide alterations in zinc finger proteins, but the TAL system offers superior predictability and ease of assembly. Examples of constructing new repeat units include the synthesis of plasmids encoding NI, HD, NN, and NG repeats with flanking BpiI sites, followed by Golden Gate assembly into intermediate vectors. Specific binding activities are tested using reporter assays, DNase I footprinting, and electrophoretic mobility shift assays. The number of repeat units in a repeat domain ranges from 1 to 24, with a minimum of 10.5 required for robust activity. Consensus sequences for repeat units are derived from natural TAL effectors and optimized for expression in eukaryotic hosts. 

Modifications to repeat unit sequences may include codon optimization, elimination of cryptic splice sites, and adjustment of GC content to enhance stability and translation. Identity with consensus sequences is maintained at greater than 85% to preserve structural integrity. The hypervariable region is specified to recognize a single base pair, and methods for making a repeat unit include PCR amplification with BpiI-flanked primers, subcloning into pUC57 derivatives, and verification by sequencing. The N-terminal domain of AvrBs3-like proteins includes a conserved 1–20 amino acid segment preceding the first repeat unit, which is essential for nuclear import and stability. The AvrBs3 protein family is defined by sequence homology in the repeat core and C-terminal region, and the hypervariable region is deduced by comparative analysis of natural effectors and their target promoters. 

The correlation between repeat units and base pairs is summarized in a recognition code table, enabling the design of polypeptides to bind any target sequence. Polypeptides are designed by aligning the target DNA sequence with the recognition code and selecting appropriate repeat units. Modularly designed repeat units are combined with endonuclease domains such as FokI to generate site-specific nucleases. The FokI endonuclease domain requires dimerization for cleavage, and thus two TAL-FokI fusions are designed to bind adjacent sites with a defined spacer. The I-SceI endonuclease, which recognizes an 18-bp sequence, may be fused to a repeat domain to enhance homologous recombination frequency. Other endonuclease domains, such as Cas9 or meganucleases, may also be adapted for fusion. 

The invention further includes methods for generating modified plants by introducing the synthetic polypeptide into plant cells via Agrobacterium-mediated transformation, biolistic delivery, or viral vectors. The polypeptide is expressed under the control of a constitutive, tissue-specific, or inducible promoter, and the transformed cells are regenerated into whole plants. The resulting genetically modified plants exhibit altered gene expression patterns, such as enhanced disease resistance, improved yield, or modified metabolic profiles. The invention also encompasses methods for delivering the polypeptide directly into cells using liposomes, cell-penetrating peptides, or toxin-mediated transport systems. 

In human cells, the synthetic polypeptides are expressed from vectors containing the CMV or EF1α promoter and are shown to activate endogenous genes such as PUMA, IFNα1, and IFNβ1. The polypeptides localize to the nucleus and induce transcript levels by 1.5- to 3.5-fold, with greater activation observed when fused to the VP16 activation domain. The invention enables targeted modulation of gene expression in any eukaryotic cell type, including mammalian, insect, and fungal cells, by adapting the expression cassette to the host’s transcriptional machinery. The polypeptide may be synthesized in situ from a delivered DNA construct or delivered as a purified protein. 

The invention further includes the use of the repeat domain for labeling, isolation, or purification of genomic regions via fusion to affinity tags such as FLAG, HA, or biotin ligase. The repeat domain may be inserted into any polypeptide scaffold to confer DNA-binding specificity, including fluorescent proteins, recombinases, or epigenetic modifiers. The term “isolated or substantially purified polynucleotide or protein” refers to molecules removed from their natural context and free of contaminating cellular components. Isolated polynucleotides are characterized by sequence identity, length, and absence of introns, while isolated proteins are characterized by molecular weight, purity, and functional activity. Fragments and variants of the polynucleotides and proteins are included if they retain the ability to bind the target sequence or modulate gene expression. 

Variants may include deletions, insertions, or substitutions that maintain at least 80% sequence identity to the reference sequence, as determined by BLAST or Clustal W alignment. Mutagenic and recombinogenic procedures such as DNA shuffling, PCR-based mutagenesis, and site-directed mutagenesis may be employed to optimize binding affinity or stability. Expression cassettes comprise operably linked transcriptional and translational control elements, including promoters, enhancers, 5′ and 3′ UTRs, and polyadenylation signals. Heterologous sequences such as picornavirus IRES elements, potyvirus leaders, and human immunoglobulin binding protein leaders are included to enhance translation efficiency. 

The invention encompasses plant transformation vectors containing selectable markers such as antibiotic or herbicide resistance genes, and methods for transformation include Agrobacterium tumefaciens-mediated delivery, particle bombardment, electroporation, and viral transduction. Stable transformation results in genomic integration and heritable transmission, while transient transformation allows for rapid screening. The polynucleotide constructs may be delivered into non-plant host cells, including yeast, mammalian, and insect cells, using appropriate expression vectors and transfection methods. The invention further includes the use of synthetic polypeptides for therapeutic and prophylactic applications, including correction of genetic disorders, suppression of oncogenes, and activation of tumor suppressors. 

## EXAMPLES

### Example 1

The basis for DNA specificity of TAL effectors was identified through analysis of AvrBs3 repeat units and their corresponding hypervariable amino acids. Correlation between repeat types and specific base pairs in the target DNA sequence was established by comparing promoter sequences of AvrBs3-induced genes in pepper plants. A model of recognition specificity was proposed based on the RVD code and supported by deletion analysis of AvrBs3 derivatives. UPA-boxes from induced genes were analyzed to predict target DNA sequences for other Xanthomonas TAL effectors. Promoters of known TAL target genes were inspected for conserved motifs matching predicted specificities, leading to the deduction of a definitive code for DNA target specificity of key repeat types. Experimental validation was performed with Hax3 and Hax4 effectors, whose target DNA boxes were derived and tested in reporter assays. Permutation of the Hax4-box demonstrated that the first nucleotide must be a thymine for optimal activation. Analysis of NI-, HD-, and NG-repeat units in Hax4 confirmed their respective specificities for adenine, cytosine, and thymine. An artificial effector, ArtX1, was generated and its corresponding DNA recognition sequence was deduced and validated. Derivatives of the ArtX1-box were analyzed to confirm specificity. The Hax2-box and its IG repeat unit were examined, and permutation of the third base demonstrated strict specificity for thymine. The AvrHah1 and Bs3 promoters were analyzed to identify Hax2 target genes in Arabidopsis thaliana. PAP1 transcript levels and promoter regions were assessed, and putative target DNA sequences were predicted for additional TAL effectors. The influence of repeat unit number on target gene expression was evaluated by constructing artificial effectors with 0.5 to 15.5 HD-repeat units, and promoter activation was analyzed across this range. Artificial effectors with novel DNA-binding specificities were generated, and further embodiments of the invention were introduced, including the use of AvrBs3-family effectors as transcriptional repressors, labels, and endonucleolytic cleavage agents. The Golden Gate cloning method was employed to assemble repeat domains with custom-designed repeat order, and the invention was summarized to include isolated nucleic acid molecules, transformed plants and seeds, and human and non-human host cells.

### Example 2

The TAL repeat unit that binds to guanine nucleotides was identified through functional analysis of HVR motifs. Bioinformatic analysis of HVRs from natural TAL effectors revealed that NN and NK residues were consistently associated with guanine recognition. Target specificity of HVRs with unknown specificity was tested using reporter gene activation assays in plant and human cells, confirming that NN and NK repeat units bind guanine with high fidelity. These findings expanded the recognition code and enabled the design of polypeptides targeting sequences containing guanine residues with improved accuracy.

### Example 3

The DNA binding domain of TAL effectors was shown to be amenable to modular assembly using Golden Gate cloning. Limitations of chemical synthesis for generating full-length effectors were addressed by introducing a two-stage ligation process using type IIS restriction enzymes, specifically BsaI and BpiI. Insert plasmids containing single repeats flanked by BpiI sites were ligated into intermediate vectors, and the assembled 10-mers were released using BpiI and ligated into an acceptor vector containing the N- and C-terminal domains. The final assembly of the repeat unit array was achieved in a single reaction, enabling the rapid construction of effectors with specific base sequence recognition. A schematic of the method is provided in FIG. 19A-D, demonstrating the efficiency and scalability of the approach.

### Example 4

Target DNA-specific nucleases were produced by in vitro generation of repeat domain-FokI nuclease fusion proteins. The fusion constructs were expressed in E. coli, purified, and analyzed by agarose gel electrophoresis following incubation with target DNA substrates. Cleavage products were observed at the expected positions, confirming that the engineered nucleases induced double-strand breaks specifically at the intended genomic loci.

### Example 5

Recognition specificities of additional amino acid pairs, including NS, NK, and HN, were determined through systematic construction of DNA-binding domains using Golden Gate cloning. Experiments were conducted to test binding and activation of reporter genes containing target sequences with these base pairs. Experimental results are provided in FIGS. 20–27, demonstrating that these novel RVDs confer predictable and specific recognition of adenine, cytosine, and guanine, respectively. The use of these recognition specificities in methods and compositions enables the design of polypeptides capable of binding any DNA sequence with near-complete coverage of the nucleotide alphabet.
