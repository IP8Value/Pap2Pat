## BACKGROUND

Pattern recognition receptors serve as critical sentinels of the innate immune system, enabling cells to detect conserved molecular signatures associated with microbial pathogens and cellular damage. These receptors initiate signaling cascades that culminate in the production of cytokines, chemokines, and effector molecules essential for restricting pathogen replication and coordinating adaptive immune responses. Among the most significant outcomes of pattern recognition receptor activation is the induction of interferon proteins, particularly type I and type III interferons, which establish an antiviral state in neighboring and distant cells through the upregulation of hundreds of interferon-stimulated genes. The transcription factor interferon regulatory factor 3 plays a central role in this process, as its phosphorylation by the kinases TBK1 and IKKε triggers its dimerization, nuclear translocation, and binding to interferon-stimulated response elements in the promoters of interferon genes. This pathway is commonly engaged by multiple upstream pattern recognition receptors, each utilizing distinct adaptor proteins to relay signals to the shared terminal kinases. For instance, Toll-like receptor 3 and 4 signal through TRIF, while RIG-I and MDA5, which detect cytoplasmic viral RNA, utilize IPS-1 as their essential adaptor. The stimulator of interferon genes, or STING, functions both as a direct sensor of cyclic dinucleotides and as a critical adaptor molecule for multiple cytosolic DNA-sensing receptors, integrating signals from diverse pathogen-associated molecular patterns into a unified interferon response. The ability to pharmacologically activate this pathway has long been recognized as a promising strategy for broad-spectrum antiviral therapy, given the potent and non-specific antiviral effects conferred by interferon-induced gene expression. Synthetic agonists of Toll-like receptors have demonstrated efficacy against chronic viral infections and enhanced vaccine immunogenicity, while nucleic acid analogs that stimulate RIG-I and MDA5 have shown promise in acute viral settings. Two small molecules, 10-carboxymethyl-9-acridanone and 5,6-dimethylxanthenone-4-acetic acid, were previously identified as activators of the STING pathway; however, their biological activity is restricted to murine cells due to species-specific structural differences in the STING protein, rendering them ineffective in human systems and leading to their failure in clinical trials. The Alphavirus genus includes several mosquito-borne pathogens of global public health concern, such as Chikungunya virus and Venezuelan equine encephalitis virus, which have caused widespread epidemics with significant morbidity and mortality, yet no FDA-approved antiviral drugs or vaccines currently exist for their prevention or treatment. These viruses are exquisitely sensitive to type I interferon, and their replication triggers innate immune recognition through the IPS-1 pathway, suggesting that alternative activation of the interferon response via a distinct mechanism may circumvent viral evasion strategies. Pharmacologic stimulation of the STING pathway in human cells, therefore, represents a compelling therapeutic opportunity. Recent advances in genome editing technologies, particularly CRISPR/Cas9, have enabled precise functional interrogation of innate immune signaling components, allowing for the definitive identification of molecular dependencies in complex cellular responses. In vivo activation of the STING pathway has been demonstrated to confer protection against a variety of viral infections in murine models, but the lack of cross-species active compounds has hindered translation to human applications.

## SUMMARY

The stimulator of interferon genes functions as both a pattern recognition receptor and an essential adaptor protein that integrates signals from multiple cytosolic sensors to activate the transcription factor interferon regulatory factor 3, leading to the synthesis of type I and type III interferons and the establishment of a broad antiviral state. Signaling through STING is dependent on the phosphorylation and activation of TBK1 and IKKε, which in turn phosphorylate IRF3, enabling its nuclear translocation and transcriptional activity. Small molecule agonists capable of activating this pathway have been sought as therapeutic agents for their potential to induce potent antiviral and antitumor immunity. The first synthetic small molecule identified as a human-specific STING activator is disclosed herein, designated as G10, which elicits robust interferon and interferon-stimulated gene expression in human cells without activating the NF-κB pathway in fibroblasts. This compound effectively prevents the replication of multiple Alphaviruses, including Chikungunya virus and Venezuelan equine encephalitis virus, by triggering a STING-dependent cascade that results in IRF3 phosphorylation and subsequent interferon secretion. In vivo administration of a murine-specific STING agonist demonstrates that pharmacologic activation of this pathway can completely suppress viremia when administered early after infection, validating the therapeutic potential of STING stimulation. Methods for identifying novel STING agonists are disclosed, including high-throughput screening platforms utilizing human cell lines engineered to report interferon and IRF3-dependent transcriptional activity. The compound G10 represents the first synthetic molecule definitively shown to activate the human STING pathway, and its discovery enables the development of therapeutics for the prevention and treatment of viral infections, particularly those caused by interferon-sensitive RNA viruses.

### Compounds:

The compound disclosed herein is 4-(2-chloro-6-fluorobenzyl)-N-(furan-2-ylmethyl)-3-oxo-3,4-dihydro-2H-benzo[b][1,4]thiazine-6-carboxamide, designated as G10, having the chemical structure as depicted in Figure 1B and the molecular formula C₂₀H₁₆ClFN₂O₃S. This compound is synthesized through a multi-step organic reaction sequence beginning with the condensation of 2-chloro-6-fluorobenzaldehyde with 2-mercaptobenzoic acid to form the corresponding thioester, followed by cyclization under acidic conditions to yield the 3,4-dihydro-2H-benzo[b][1,4]thiazine core. Subsequent N-alkylation with furan-2-ylmethyl bromide and final carboxamide formation using an activated ester intermediate under mild coupling conditions yields the final compound. Analogues of G10 are prepared by substitution of the chloro and fluoro substituents on the benzyl ring with other halogens, alkyl groups, or electron-withdrawing moieties, by modification of the furan ring to other heterocycles such as thiophene or pyrrole, or by replacement of the carboxamide with bioisosteres such as sulfonamide or urea. These analogues are screened for STING-dependent interferon induction and antiviral activity using the methods described herein. The compound G10 is obtained in a purity of greater than 98% as determined by HPLC and characterized by nuclear magnetic resonance spectroscopy and mass spectrometry.

### Treatment

The pharmaceutical compositions disclosed herein are employed for the treatment of alphavirus infections, including but not limited to Chikungunya virus, Venezuelan equine encephalitis virus, Sindbis virus, Ross River virus, and O’nyong-nyong virus. Administration may be performed by any route suitable for systemic or localized delivery, including intravenous, intramuscular, subcutaneous, intranasal, oral, or inhaled routes. The treatment may be administered for prophylactic purposes to individuals at high risk of exposure, such as travelers to endemic regions, military personnel, or laboratory workers, or for therapeutic purposes in individuals with confirmed or suspected infection. Treatments may be delivered as a single bolus or as repeated administrations at intervals ranging from hourly to weekly, depending on the pharmacokinetic profile of the formulation. The therapeutically effective dosage is determined based on the severity of infection, the age and weight of the subject, the route of administration, and the formulation used, and may range from 0.1 mg/kg to 100 mg/kg body weight. The treatment reduces or inhibits symptoms associated with alphavirus infection, including fever, arthralgia, myalgia, rash, and prolonged joint inflammation. Dosage regimens are adjusted to optimize clinical response, minimize toxicity, and account for individual variability in drug metabolism and immune response. Effective amounts are determined empirically using animal models of alphavirus infection, where reduction in viral load, improvement in clinical score, and prevention of tissue damage are used as endpoints. Administration protocols are guided by the reduction of disease symptoms, including decreased viremia, diminished cytokine levels, and improved mobility or neurological function. The concentration and dose of the compound are calculated to achieve a steady-state plasma concentration sufficient to activate the STING pathway in target tissues, as determined by in vitro assays of interferon induction and IRF3 phosphorylation.

## EXAMPLES

### Example 1 Identification of a Novel IFN/IRF3-Inducing Molecule by High Throughput In Vitro Screening

A library of 51,632 structurally diverse small molecules was screened for the ability to induce interferon-stimulated gene expression in human foreskin fibroblasts stably transfected with a luciferase reporter construct under the control of an interferon-stimulated response element. Cells were plated in 384-well plates and treated with compounds dissolved in 1% dimethyl sulfoxide at a concentration of 10 μM for 7 hours. Luciferase activity was measured using a luminescence plate reader, and signals were normalized to negative controls (1% DMSO) and positive controls (1,000 U/mL interferon-beta). Compounds exhibiting a signal greater than 50% of the maximum positive control response were considered hits. Of the initial hits, 16 compounds were selected for confirmation in duplicate. The compound designated G10 exhibited the third highest signal intensity across all screened compounds and was selected for further characterization based on its potency, low cytotoxicity, and structural novelty. Dose-response curves confirmed that G10 induced luciferase expression with an EC50 of 3.2 μM, demonstrating its efficacy as a potent inducer of interferon-responsive transcription in human cells.

### Example 2—G10 Induces IFN/IRF3- but not NF-κB-Dependent Transcription in Human Fibroblasts

To determine whether G10 specifically activates the interferon regulatory factor 3 pathway without engaging the nuclear factor kappa B pathway, human fibroblasts stably expressing an NF-κB-dependent luciferase reporter were treated with increasing concentrations of G10. In contrast to known NF-κB activators such as tumor necrosis factor-alpha, lipopolysaccharide, and Sendai virus, G10 failed to induce luciferase expression even at concentrations up to 100 μM. Similarly, quantitative PCR analysis of endogenous NF-κB target genes, including IL8, IL1β, and MIP1α, revealed no significant upregulation following G10 treatment. In contrast, G10 induced robust transcription of IRF3-dependent genes including ISG54, ISG56, ISG15, and Viperin, with kinetics and magnitude comparable to those induced by UV-inactivated cytomegalovirus. These results demonstrate that G10 selectively activates the IRF3-dependent transcriptional program without triggering canonical NF-κB signaling in human fibroblasts.

### Example 3—G10 Elicits Antiviral Activity Against New and Old World Alphaviruses

Human fibroblasts were pre-treated with G10 at concentrations ranging from 0.1 to 100 μM for six hours prior to infection with West Nile virus, Vaccinia virus, Chikungunya virus, or Venezuelan equine encephalitis virus. Viral replication was quantified by plaque assay at 48 hours post-infection. G10 reduced Chikungunya virus replication by more than three logs at 10 μM, with an IC90 of 8.01 μM, and reduced Venezuelan equine encephalitis virus replication by over three logs at 25 μM, with an IC90 of 24.57 μM. In contrast, G10 reduced West Nile virus replication by less than one log and Vaccinia virus replication by approximately one log at the highest non-toxic concentration tested. These findings indicate that G10 confers a highly potent and selective antiviral state against Alphaviruses, with minimal effect on other unrelated virus families.

### Example 4—IRF3 is Essential to G10-Induced Transcription and Anti-Alphaviral Activity

CRISPR/Cas9-mediated gene editing was used to generate human fibroblast lines deficient in either IRF3 or STAT1. Immunoblot analysis confirmed the absence of IRF3 and STAT1 proteins in the respective knockout lines. In IRF3-deficient cells, G10 failed to induce luciferase expression from the interferon-stimulated response element reporter, whereas in STAT1-deficient cells, G10 retained full activity. Phosphorylation of IRF3 at serine 386 was detected in wild-type cells following G10 treatment but was absent in IRF3-deficient cells. When challenged with Chikungunya virus, Venezuelan equine encephalitis virus, or Sindbis virus, G10 lost all antiviral activity in IRF3-deficient cells, while interferon-beta retained full efficacy. These results demonstrate that IRF3 is indispensable for both the transcriptional and antiviral effects of G10.

### Example 5—G10-Mediated IRF3 Activation and Anti-Alphaviral Activity Occur Independently of IPS-1/MAVS-Dependent Signaling

Human fibroblasts lacking the IPS-1 adaptor protein were generated using CRISPR/Cas9 and confirmed by immunoblot. In these cells, G10 induced robust phosphorylation of IRF3 and transcription of interferon-stimulated genes, whereas stimulation with poly(I:C) or Sendai virus, which require IPS-1, were completely abrogated. Antiviral activity against Chikungunya virus and Venezuelan equine encephalitis virus was fully preserved in IPS-1-deficient cells following G10 treatment, demonstrating that the molecule’s mechanism of action does not require the RIG-I/MDA5/IPS-1 pathway.

### Example 6—STING is Required for G10-Mediated IRF3 Activation, Gene Expression, and Anti-Alphaviral Activity

STING-deficient human fibroblasts were generated using CRISPR/Cas9 and confirmed by immunoblot. In these cells, G10 failed to induce phosphorylation of IRF3, luciferase reporter expression, or transcription of interferon-stimulated genes. In contrast, stimulation with Sendai virus or poly(I:C), which signal through IPS-1, remained fully active. G10 lost all antiviral activity against Chikungunya virus and Venezuelan equine encephalitis virus in STING-deficient cells, while interferon-beta retained full efficacy. Thermal shift assays using purified human STING C-terminal domain showed no increase in melting temperature upon incubation with G10, indicating a lack of direct binding. These results establish STING as an essential mediator of G10’s biological activity, despite the absence of direct ligand binding.

### Example 7—G10 Induces STING-Dependent Synthesis and Secretion of Bioactive Interferon

G10 treatment induced transcription of both IFNβ and IFNλ1 mRNAs in human fibroblasts. After 18 hours of treatment, supernatants from G10-treated cells induced interferon-responsive luciferase expression in IRF3-deficient reporter cells, confirming the secretion of bioactive interferon. This secretion was abolished in STING-deficient cells but preserved in IPS-1-deficient cells. The magnitude of interferon secretion was significantly reduced in IPS-1-deficient cells compared to wild-type, suggesting a modulatory role for IPS-1 in STING-dependent interferon production, though not an essential one.

### Example 8—STAT1 is Required for G10-Mediated Anti-Alphaviral Activity and ISG Expression

STAT1-deficient fibroblasts were infected with Chikungunya virus, Venezuelan equine encephalitis virus, or Sindbis virus following G10 pretreatment. G10 completely lost its antiviral activity in STAT1-deficient cells, while interferon-beta retained partial efficacy. Expression of interferon-stimulated genes Mx2 and ISG56 was induced by interferon-beta and other stimuli in STAT1-deficient cells but not by G10, indicating that G10-mediated antiviral activity requires STAT1-dependent signaling, likely through autocrine interferon feedback.

### Example 9—G10 Triggers IRF3-Dependent Activity with Less Potency than 2′3′-cGAMP

G10 induced IRF3 phosphorylation with slightly faster kinetics than transfected 2′3′-cGAMP, but at higher concentrations was less potent in inducing transcription of IRF3- and interferon-dependent genes. A 10-fold higher concentration of G10 was required to achieve equivalent levels of ISG15, IFIT1, and Viperin mRNA induction compared to 2′3′-cGAMP. However, IFNβ transcription was induced with similar potency by both compounds, suggesting differential regulation of interferon subtypes. These findings indicate that while G10 activates the same pathway as 2′3′-cGAMP, it does so with lower overall potency, likely due to indirect activation.

### Example 10—G10 Induces Innate Antiviral mRNA Expression in Primary Human Cells

Primary human peripheral blood mononuclear cells and umbilical microvascular endothelial cells were treated with G10 and analyzed for gene expression. G10 induced transcription of IFIT1, IFIT2, IFNβ, ISG15, OAS, and Viperin in a dose-dependent manner. In contrast to fibroblasts, G10 also induced NF-κB-dependent genes such as MIP1α and IL1β in primary cells, indicating cell-type-specific modulation of signaling outputs.

### Example 11—STING Agonism Inhibits CHIKV Replication In Vivo

C57BL/6 mice were administered 25 mg/kg DMXAA intraperitoneally three hours prior to footpad infection with Chikungunya virus. At 72 hours post-infection, serum viral titers were undetectable in DMXAA-treated mice, whereas DMSO-treated controls exhibited titers of 1.47 × 10⁴ PFU/mL. When DMXAA was administered six hours post-infection, viral titers remained undetectable, but administration at 24 hours post-infection resulted in a non-significant reduction in viral load. These results demonstrate that pharmacologic activation of the STING pathway can completely suppress alphavirus viremia in vivo when administered early during infection.
