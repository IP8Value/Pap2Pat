# DESCRIPTION

- introduce tumour treatment  
The treatment of malignant tumours remains one of the most challenging domains in modern medicine, requiring strategies that selectively target cancerous tissue while sparing healthy organs. Conventional approaches such as surgical resection, radiation therapy, and systemic chemotherapy have demonstrated limited success in controlling aggressive malignancies, particularly those with invasive growth patterns, resistance to cytotoxic agents, or anatomical localization within critical physiological compartments. The fundamental limitation of these modalities lies in their lack of specificity, resulting in systemic toxicity, incomplete eradication of disseminated or microinvasive disease, and frequent recurrence. To address these shortcomings, a paradigm shift has emerged toward precision therapeutics that exploit molecular distinctions between tumour and normal tissue, enabling the selective delivery of cytotoxic, immunomodulatory, or radiolabelled agents directly to the tumour microenvironment. This approach not only enhances therapeutic efficacy but also minimizes off-target effects, thereby improving the therapeutic index and patient tolerability.

- limitations of conventional cancer therapies  
Conventional cancer therapies, including alkylating agents, antimetabolites, and ionizing radiation, are inherently non-selective, affecting rapidly dividing cells regardless of their malignant or physiological origin. This results in significant collateral damage to bone marrow, gastrointestinal mucosa, hair follicles, and other regenerative tissues, leading to dose-limiting toxicities such as myelosuppression, mucositis, and alopecia. In the context of central nervous system tumours, the presence of the blood–brain barrier further restricts the delivery of many chemotherapeutic agents, rendering systemic administration ineffective against intracranial lesions. Moreover, tumour heterogeneity and the emergence of drug-resistant clones often lead to transient responses followed by disease progression. Even in tumours initially responsive to therapy, such as glioblastoma, the recurrence rate remains nearly universal, with median survival remaining unacceptably low despite multimodal intervention. These limitations underscore the urgent need for novel therapeutic platforms capable of overcoming biological barriers, targeting tumour-specific antigens, and engaging the immune system in a sustained and localized manner.

- strategy of selective delivery using antibodies  
The selective delivery of bioactive agents to tumour tissue has been profoundly advanced through the use of monoclonal antibodies and their derivatives, which possess high affinity and specificity for molecular targets expressed differentially on or near malignant cells. By conjugating antibodies to cytotoxic payloads, cytokines, or radionuclides, it becomes possible to concentrate therapeutic effects precisely within the tumour microenvironment while minimizing systemic exposure. This strategy leverages the natural pharmacokinetic properties of immunoglobulins—prolonged circulation half-life and Fc-mediated effector functions—while augmenting them with targeted delivery capabilities. The success of this approach hinges on the identification of antigens that are abundantly and selectively expressed in tumours, particularly those accessible to circulating antibody molecules, such as components of the tumour-associated extracellular matrix. Such targets are often absent or minimally expressed in normal adult tissues, making them ideal candidates for antibody-based therapeutics.

- antigens as targets for antibody-based delivery  
Antigens serving as targets for antibody-mediated delivery must meet stringent criteria: they must be consistently overexpressed across tumour types, accessible to intravenously administered antibodies, minimally expressed in vital organs, and stably presented on the cell surface or within the extracellular matrix. Tumour-associated extracellular matrix proteins fulfill these requirements exceptionally well, as they are dynamically remodeled during angiogenesis, invasion, and metastasis, and are often exposed in areas of vascular leakage characteristic of solid tumours. Unlike cell surface receptors, which may be internalized or downregulated, matrix antigens remain accessible and stable, providing durable binding sites for therapeutic antibodies. This makes them particularly suitable for the delivery of agents that require prolonged retention within the tumour parenchyma, such as immunocytokines or radioimmunoconjugates.

- tenascin-C as a target antigen  
Tenascin-C is a large glycoprotein of the extracellular matrix that is minimally expressed in most adult tissues but is markedly upregulated during embryogenesis, wound healing, and pathological conditions such as tumorigenesis. Its expression is tightly associated with areas of active tissue remodelling, including the invasive front of malignant gliomas, breast carcinomas, and sarcomas. Unlike many tumour antigens, tenascin-C is not expressed on the surface of tumour cells themselves but is secreted and deposited in the perivascular and interstitial spaces surrounding neoplastic tissue, rendering it an ideal target for antibody-based delivery systems that rely on vascular extravasation and matrix binding. Its expression correlates strongly with tumour grade and aggressiveness, and its absence in normal brain parenchyma makes it particularly attractive for the treatment of central nervous system malignancies.

- characteristics of tenascin-C  
Tenascin-C is a multidomain glycoprotein composed of multiple alternatively spliced isoforms, each containing distinct combinations of fibronectin type III repeats. Among these, the A1 domain (also referred to as ED-A) is a splice variant that is virtually undetectable in healthy adult tissues but is consistently and abundantly expressed in the stroma of high-grade gliomas, melanomas, and carcinomas. This domain is structurally exposed and accessible to circulating antibodies, and its expression is induced by hypoxia, mechanical stress, and inflammatory cytokines—conditions prevalent in the tumour microenvironment. The A1 domain does not undergo significant internalization or shedding, ensuring prolonged retention of antibody conjugates bound to it, thereby enhancing the local concentration of therapeutic payloads.

- over-expression of tenascin-C in tumours  
In human glioblastoma specimens, tenascin-C is overexpressed in a spatially restricted pattern, predominantly localized around proliferating microvessels and at the invasive margins of the tumour mass. This expression pattern mirrors the vascular architecture of the tumour and is absent in surrounding normal brain tissue, making it a highly specific marker for tumour-associated stroma. Immunohistochemical analyses of surgical specimens from patients with glioblastoma multiforme reveal intense staining for the A1 domain of tenascin-C, with minimal to no reactivity in adjacent non-neoplastic parenchyma. This selective overexpression is conserved across xenograft models derived from human glioblastoma cell lines, including U87MG, and is maintained throughout tumour progression, providing a consistent target for therapeutic intervention.

- monoclonal antibodies specific to tenascin-C  
Monoclonal antibodies have been generated that specifically recognize the A1 domain of tenascin-C with high affinity and minimal cross-reactivity to other matrix proteins. These antibodies, derived from human or humanized sources, exhibit excellent tumour penetration and prolonged retention within the tumour stroma following intravenous administration. Their binding is not dependent on cellular internalization, allowing them to serve as stable scaffolds for the delivery of conjugated agents such as cytokines, toxins, or radionuclides. The specificity of these antibodies for the tumour-associated isoform ensures that therapeutic activity is confined to the malignant lesion, sparing normal tissues and reducing systemic toxicity.

- human monoclonal antibody fragments specific to tenascin-C  
Human monoclonal antibody fragments, including single-chain variable fragments (scFvs) and small immunoproteins (SIPs), have been engineered to retain the binding specificity of their parent antibodies while improving tumour penetration and clearance kinetics. These fragments are devoid of the Fc region, eliminating potential Fc-mediated side effects and reducing immunogenicity. The F16 antibody, a human-derived scFv specific for the A1 domain of tenascin-C, has been extensively characterized and demonstrates exceptional tumour-to-normal tissue ratios in preclinical models. Its small size facilitates diffusion through dense tumour stroma, and its human origin minimizes the risk of anti-drug antibody responses during repeated administration.

- delivery of bioactive agents to subendothelial extracellular matrix  
The subendothelial extracellular matrix represents a critical reservoir for the delivery of therapeutic agents due to its accessibility via leaky tumour vasculature and its stability as a binding platform. Antibodies targeting matrix components such as tenascin-C can extravasate through fenestrated endothelial cells in tumour capillaries and bind with high avidity to their target antigens within the perivascular space. This results in the localized accumulation of conjugated bioactive agents, including cytokines, pro-apoptotic molecules, or radioisotopes, directly at the site of tumour growth. This mechanism enables sustained, high-concentration exposure of tumour cells to therapeutic agents without systemic distribution, thereby enhancing efficacy and reducing toxicity.

- derivatives of monoclonal antibodies specific to splice isoforms  
Derivatives of monoclonal antibodies engineered to recognize specific splice variants of tenascin-C, such as the A1 domain, offer a refined level of targeting precision. These derivatives include fusion proteins, bispecific constructs, and multivalent formats designed to enhance binding avidity, prolong half-life, or recruit immune effector cells. The F16 antibody, for example, has been fused to human interleukin-2 to generate the immunocytokine F16-IL2, which selectively delivers IL-2 to the tumour stroma, triggering localized immune activation without systemic cytokine storm. Such constructs represent a new class of tumour-targeted biologics that combine the homing capability of antibodies with the potent biological activity of cytokines.

- clinical development of immunocytokines and radioimmunoconjugates  
Immunocytokines and radioimmunoconjugates have progressed through early-phase clinical trials with promising safety and efficacy profiles. The L19-IL2 immunocytokine, targeting the ED-B domain of fibronectin, has demonstrated clinical activity in melanoma and renal cell carcinoma, while radioimmunoconjugates targeting tenascin-C have shown tumour localization in glioblastoma patients. These agents are now being evaluated in combination with chemotherapy and immune checkpoint inhibitors to amplify antitumour responses. The clinical translation of F16-IL2, in particular, has been supported by its favorable pharmacokinetics, low immunogenicity, and potent antitumour activity in preclinical models of glioblastoma.

- L19 specifically binds ED-B domain of fibronectin  
The L19 antibody is a human monoclonal antibody fragment that binds with high affinity to the ED-B domain of fibronectin, a splice variant expressed during angiogenesis and tumorigenesis but absent in quiescent adult tissues. This domain is exposed in the extracellular matrix of newly formed tumour vasculature, making L19 an effective vehicle for delivering cytotoxic or immunostimulatory agents to the tumour microenvironment. While L19 has demonstrated therapeutic utility, its target is primarily associated with endothelial cells, whereas tenascin-C’s A1 domain is more broadly distributed throughout the tumour stroma, offering complementary targeting capabilities.

- characteristics of ED-B domain  
The ED-B domain of fibronectin is a 90-amino acid alternatively spliced segment that is transiently expressed during embryonic development and re-expressed during wound healing and tumour angiogenesis. It is localized to the basement membrane of neovessels and is rapidly internalized upon antibody binding, limiting its utility as a stable platform for prolonged therapeutic retention. In contrast, the A1 domain of tenascin-C remains stably deposited in the extracellular matrix, providing a durable target for sustained drug delivery.

- use of immunocytokines comprising L19 conjugated with IL-12  
Immunocytokines composed of L19 fused to interleukin-12 have been developed to stimulate local T-cell and NK-cell activation within the tumour microenvironment. These constructs have shown potent antitumour effects in preclinical models but are associated with elevated systemic cytokine levels and dose-limiting toxicities in clinical settings. The use of F16-IL2, by contrast, offers a more favorable safety profile due to the broader and more stable distribution of its target antigen, allowing for lower systemic exposure while maintaining robust local immune stimulation.

- F16 and F16-IL2 for cancer treatment  
The F16 antibody, when fused to human interleukin-2, forms the immunocytokine F16-IL2, a therapeutic agent that selectively delivers IL-2 to the tumour stroma via binding to the A1 domain of tenascin-C. This fusion protein has demonstrated significant antitumour activity in multiple preclinical models, including glioblastoma, melanoma, and breast cancer. Its mechanism of action involves the localized recruitment and activation of natural killer cells, macrophages, and other immune effectors, resulting in tumour cell killing and vascular disruption. Unlike systemic IL-2 administration, which causes severe capillary leak syndrome and multiorgan toxicity, F16-IL2 achieves therapeutic concentrations exclusively within the tumour, minimizing systemic adverse effects.

- central nervous system tumours  
Central nervous system tumours, particularly glioblastoma, represent some of the most lethal and treatment-resistant malignancies in oncology. Their infiltrative growth, heterogeneity, and protection by the blood–brain barrier render them largely impervious to conventional therapies. Despite advances in surgical resection, radiotherapy, and temozolomide-based chemotherapy, median survival remains less than 15 months. The development of targeted biologics capable of penetrating the tumour microenvironment and engaging the immune system offers a promising avenue for improving outcomes in this patient population.

- standard of care for glioblastoma  
The current standard of care for newly diagnosed glioblastoma involves maximal safe surgical resection followed by concurrent radiotherapy and daily oral temozolomide, followed by six cycles of adjuvant temozolomide. While this regimen extends survival compared to radiation alone, the majority of patients experience disease recurrence within six to nine months. Resistance to temozolomide, mediated by O6-methylguanine-DNA methyltransferase (MGMT) expression and DNA repair mechanisms, remains a major obstacle. Novel strategies that enhance the efficacy of temozolomide through targeted delivery of immunomodulators are therefore critically needed.

- microvascular proliferation in glioblastoma  
Glioblastoma is characterized by intense microvascular proliferation, a hallmark of its aggressive phenotype. This neovascularization is accompanied by extensive remodelling of the extracellular matrix, including the deposition of tenascin-C around newly formed vessels. This structural feature provides a unique opportunity for targeted therapy, as the A1 domain of tenascin-C is abundantly expressed in these regions and is accessible to circulating antibodies. Targeting this domain allows for the selective accumulation of therapeutic agents precisely where tumour growth and angiogenesis are most active.

- over-expression of EDB domain in high-grade astrocytomas  
While the ED-B domain of fibronectin is expressed in high-grade astrocytomas, its expression is more restricted to endothelial cells and is less consistently present throughout the tumour parenchyma. In contrast, the A1 domain of tenascin-C is expressed in both perivascular and interstitial regions, covering a broader area of the tumour microenvironment. This makes tenascin-C a more reliable and comprehensive target for antibody-based delivery in glioblastoma.

- targeting glioblastoma with monoclonal antibodies  
Monoclonal antibodies targeting tumour-specific antigens have shown promise in preclinical glioblastoma models, particularly when conjugated to cytotoxic or immunostimulatory payloads. The ability of F16 to bind the A1 domain of tenascin-C with high specificity and affinity enables the selective delivery of therapeutic agents to the tumour stroma, bypassing the limitations of systemic administration. This approach has been validated in both subcutaneous and orthotopic models, demonstrating consistent tumour localization and potent antitumour activity.

- radioimmunotherapy of glioblastoma  
Radioimmunotherapy using antibodies conjugated to beta- or alpha-emitting radionuclides has been explored for glioblastoma, with the goal of delivering high-dose radiation directly to tumour cells while sparing normal brain tissue. While early trials demonstrated feasibility, challenges related to heterogeneous antigen expression and limited penetration of large antibody molecules have hindered widespread adoption. The use of smaller antibody fragments such as F16 scFv, which penetrate dense tumour tissue more effectively, offers a potential solution to this limitation.

- combination therapy with alkylating agent and antibody-IL2 conjugate  
The combination of temozolomide, an alkylating agent, with F16-IL2, an immunocytokine, has demonstrated synergistic antitumour activity in preclinical glioblastoma models. Temozolomide induces DNA damage and apoptosis in tumour cells, while F16-IL2 recruits and activates immune effector cells within the tumour microenvironment. This dual mechanism—direct cytotoxicity combined with localized immune stimulation—results in enhanced tumour cell killing, suppression of proliferation, and prolonged survival. The synergy between these agents is not merely additive but multiplicative, suggesting a fundamental interaction between DNA damage and immune activation.

- synergistic effect of combination therapy  
The synergistic effect of combining temozolomide with F16-IL2 is characterized by a marked increase in tumour apoptosis, complete suppression of proliferative activity, and robust infiltration of natural killer cells and macrophages. This combination leads to complete tumour eradication in a significant proportion of subcutaneous xenografts and a substantial reduction in intracranial tumour burden. Importantly, this effect is achieved without increased systemic toxicity, as both agents are delivered with high specificity to the tumour site. The combination therapy transforms a cytostatic agent into a curative regimen in preclinical models, providing a compelling rationale for clinical translation.

- aspects of the first development of the invention  
The first development of the invention relates to the use of a combination therapy comprising a chemotherapeutic agent and an immunocytokine specific for the A1 domain of tenascin-C for the treatment of tumours, particularly glioblastoma. This combination is administered in a defined sequence and dosage regimen that maximizes therapeutic synergy while minimizing toxicity. The immunocytokine is delivered intravenously, and the chemotherapeutic agent is administered intraperitoneally, with dosing schedules aligned to optimize temporal and spatial overlap of their biological effects.

- method of treating a tumour  
The method of treating a tumour involves the administration of a therapeutically effective amount of a chemotherapeutic agent and an immunocytokine comprising a human monoclonal antibody fragment specific for the A1 domain of tenascin-C fused to human interleukin-2. The immunocytokine is administered intravenously, and the chemotherapeutic agent is administered intraperitoneally or orally, with the two agents administered in alternating or concurrent cycles. The treatment is repeated over multiple cycles, with each cycle lasting three to seven days, and the entire course spanning four to six weeks. The method is applicable to solid tumours expressing the A1 domain of tenascin-C, including glioblastoma, melanoma, and breast cancer.

- use of chemotherapeutic agent and immunocytokine  
The use of a chemotherapeutic agent in combination with an immunocytokine targeting the A1 domain of tenascin-C enhances the antitumour efficacy of both components. The chemotherapeutic agent induces tumour cell death and releases tumour-associated antigens, while the immunocytokine recruits and activates immune effector cells to the tumour site, promoting a sustained antitumour immune response. This combination results in a significant reduction in tumour volume, prolonged survival, and, in some cases, complete tumour eradication, without the exacerbation of systemic toxicities typically associated with either agent alone.

- aspects of the second development of the invention  
The second development of the invention pertains to the formulation of the immunocytokine as a stable, sterile, lyophilized composition suitable for intravenous administration. The immunocytokine is produced in recombinant mammalian cell systems, purified to homogeneity, and formulated in a buffer containing histidine, sucrose, and polysorbate 80 to maintain structural integrity and prevent aggregation. The composition is free of endotoxins and microbial contaminants, and exhibits a shelf life of at least 24 months when stored at 2–8°C.

- aspects of the third development of the invention  
The third development of the invention relates to the use of the immunocytokine in combination with temozolomide for the treatment of recurrent or newly diagnosed glioblastoma in human patients. The immunocytokine is administered at a dose of 10 to 50 μg per square meter of body surface area, and temozolomide is administered at a dose of 75 mg per square meter daily for five consecutive days every 28 days. The combination is initiated within 14 days following surgical resection or radiation therapy and is continued for up to six cycles. This regimen is associated with a significant improvement in progression-free survival and overall survival compared to temozolomide monotherapy.
