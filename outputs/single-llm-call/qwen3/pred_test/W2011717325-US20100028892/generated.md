## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

The field of invention relates to novel genetic systems and high-throughput screening platforms for the identification of modulators of intracellular proteolytic activity, particularly those involved in the activation of caspases and other cysteine proteases within complex signaling networks. Proteolytic processing of proteins is a fundamental and irreversible post-translational modification that governs critical biological processes such as programmed cell death, inflammatory responses, cellular differentiation, and homeostatic protein turnover. Among the most extensively studied families of intracellular proteases are the caspases, a conserved group of cysteine-dependent aspartate-specific endopeptidases that orchestrate apoptosis and inflammation through sequential proteolytic cascades. These enzymes are synthesized as inactive zymogens and require precise spatial and temporal activation through multi-protein complex assembly, often involving adapter proteins, oligomerization domains, and regulatory scaffolds. Despite their therapeutic relevance in cancer, neurodegenerative disorders, autoimmune diseases, and ischemic injury, the development of high-throughput screening assays for caspase modulators has been severely constrained by the inherent challenges of reconstituting physiologically relevant activation mechanisms in vitro. Purification of full-length, functional caspases remains technically demanding due to their propensity for autoactivation, aggregation, and instability outside native cellular environments. Furthermore, conventional biochemical assays employing isolated catalytic domains fail to capture the context-dependent regulation conferred by upstream activators, co-factors, and subcellular localization, thereby limiting the discovery of allosteric modulators or compounds that target regulatory proteins rather than the protease active site itself. The need for alternative screening methodologies that preserve the integrity of protease activation pathways—particularly those requiring multiple interacting components—has become increasingly urgent as the complexity of proteolytic networks in human disease becomes more apparent. Existing cell-based systems, while informative, have been largely confined to single-component readouts or lethality-based endpoints that lack the sensitivity, scalability, and mechanistic resolution required for robust drug discovery. There exists, therefore, a compelling and unmet need for genetically tractable, scalable, and highly specific cellular platforms capable of reconstituting multi-component mammalian protease activation networks in a simple eukaryotic host, enabling the systematic identification of both inhibitors and activators with high fidelity and throughput.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The invention introduces a suite of genetically engineered yeast-based systems that enable the reconstitution of mammalian protease activation pathways through the use of cleavable transcription factor reporters, allowing for the sensitive, quantitative, and high-throughput detection of proteolytic activity in living cells. The core innovation lies in the design of a single-component system wherein a chimeric transcription factor, composed of a membrane-tethered extracellular domain fused to a DNA-binding domain and a transcriptional activation domain, is interrupted by a protease-specific cleavage sequence. Upon proteolytic cleavage by an active protease, the transcription factor is liberated from membrane tethering, translocates to the nucleus, and activates reporter genes under the control of cognate operator sequences, thereby converting protease activity into a measurable phenotypic output. This system permits the direct assessment of protease activity without requiring exogenous substrate addition or cell lysis, and enables the titration of protease expression levels to avoid cytotoxicity while maintaining detectable signal. A two-component system is further disclosed, wherein an inactive zymogen of a protease is co-expressed with its cognate upstream activator protein, such that protease activation is strictly dependent on the presence of both components. Expression levels of the zymogen and activator are independently engineered using promoters of varying strengths and plasmid copy numbers to ensure that activation occurs only upon proper complex formation, thereby eliminating background noise from spontaneous autoactivation. A plural-component system is also described, wherein three or more mammalian proteins—such as a death receptor, an adapter protein, and a procaspase—are simultaneously expressed in yeast to reconstitute entire signaling cascades like the extrinsic apoptotic pathway or the NLRP1 inflammasome. In each system, the reporter readout is mediated by the activation of selectable marker genes such as LEU2 and lacZ, enabling both visual and quantitative detection via growth selection on nutrient-deficient media and colorimetric assays using X-gal substrate. These systems are employed in drug screening assays to identify small molecules that either inhibit or enhance protease activation, with the ability to distinguish between direct protease inhibitors and compounds targeting upstream regulators. The two-component and plural-component systems are further adapted for the identification of novel protease activators through functional cDNA library screening, wherein libraries of human cDNAs are expressed in yeast strains engineered to require protease activation for reporter gene expression, thereby enabling the discovery of previously unknown regulators of proteolytic networks. The systems are optimized for high-throughput screening by adapting the yeast culture conditions to 384-well microplate formats, with signal-to-noise ratios enhanced through promoter engineering, operator copy number modulation, and controlled expression of protease components. Automated liquid handling systems are integrated to enable reproducible assembly of screening plates, and the Z’ factor is validated to exceed 0.6, confirming assay robustness and suitability for large-scale compound library interrogation. Data analysis is performed using absorbance measurements at 620 nm following X-gal hydrolysis, with hit compounds subjected to secondary counter-screens to eliminate false positives arising from direct inhibition of β-galactosidase or non-specific effects on yeast viability. The invention further encompasses the application of these systems to other protease families, including autophagins, by substituting the cleavage sequence with substrates specific to non-caspase proteases, thereby establishing a broadly applicable platform for the functional characterization of intracellular proteolytic networks.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

Proteolytic processing of proteins is an irreversible and highly regulated biochemical event that serves as a molecular switch for initiating and terminating a vast array of cellular processes. Proteases function as key regulators of apoptosis, inflammation, cell cycle progression, and innate immune responses, making them compelling targets for therapeutic intervention. The cleavage of specific peptide bonds by proteases often triggers conformational changes, subcellular relocalization, or the activation of downstream effectors, thereby propagating signaling cascades with high fidelity. Among the most clinically relevant protease families are the caspases, a group of evolutionarily conserved cysteine endopeptidases that exhibit strict specificity for aspartic acid residues at the P1 position of their substrates. These enzymes are classified into initiator caspases, which are activated through proximity-induced dimerization within multi-protein complexes, and effector caspases, which execute the proteolytic dismantling of cellular structures during apoptosis. The irreversible cleavage of key substrates by effector caspases leads to the systematic dismantling of the cell, including nuclear fragmentation, cytoskeletal collapse, and membrane blebbing. The active sites of most human caspases share a conserved catalytic core structure, yet subtle differences in the geometry and side-chain composition of amino acids surrounding the substrate-binding pockets confer distinct substrate preferences, enabling functional specialization within the family. For example, caspase-3 and -7 prefer DEVD motifs, caspase-1 and -4 favor WEHD, and caspase-8 and -10 recognize LETD sequences. These specificity profiles are conserved across species and have been validated through peptide library screens and structural analyses. The known cleavage sites of endogenous substrates—such as PARP, ICAD, and procaspases themselves—further underscore the precision with which caspases orchestrate cell death and inflammatory signaling. The activation of initiator caspases, however, is not a simple auto-processing event but requires the assembly of large macromolecular platforms. The extrinsic apoptotic pathway is initiated by the binding of death ligands such as FasL or TRAIL to their cognate receptors, which contain cytoplasmic death domains that recruit adapter proteins like FADD via homotypic interactions. FADD, in turn, contains death effector domains that bind the prodomains of caspase-8 and -10, facilitating their dimerization and subsequent autocatalytic activation. In parallel, the intrinsic mitochondrial pathway involves the release of cytochrome c, which binds Apaf-1 to form the apoptosome, a wheel-like complex that recruits and activates caspase-9. This process is regulated by anti-apoptotic proteins such as c-FLIP, which competes with caspase-8 for binding to FADD, and by nucleotide-binding oligomerization domains found in proteins such as CED-4 in C. elegans and its human homolog Apaf-1. The activation of caspase-1, central to inflammasome formation, is triggered by the oligomerization of NLR family proteins such as NLRP1 or NLRP3, which recruit the adapter protein ASC via PYD-PYD interactions, leading to the clustering and activation of procaspase-1 through its CARD domain. The phenotypes observed when animal caspases are expressed in yeast—including growth inhibition, cell death, and reporter gene activation—have provided critical insights into the mechanisms of protease regulation and have inspired the development of cleavable reporter systems that exploit these phenotypes for functional screening. The ability of yeast to tolerate the expression of mammalian proteases and their regulators, combined with its genetic tractability and amenability to high-throughput manipulation, provides a uniquely powerful platform for dissecting proteolytic networks that are otherwise intractable in mammalian systems.

### Caspases

Caspases constitute a family of intracellular cysteine proteases that are essential for the execution of apoptosis and the regulation of inflammation in multicellular organisms. These enzymes are synthesized as inactive zymogens, or procaspases, characterized by an N-terminal prodomain that varies in length and domain composition among different family members. The prodomain may contain death effector domains (DEDs), caspase recruitment domains (CARDs), or pyrin domains (PYDs), which mediate interactions with upstream activators and scaffold proteins. Upon activation, procaspases undergo proteolytic cleavage at conserved aspartic acid residues, resulting in the formation of a heterotetramer composed of two large and two small subunits that together constitute the active enzyme. The catalytic site of each caspase contains a conserved cysteine residue that acts as a nucleophile to attack the carbonyl carbon of the scissile peptide bond, with absolute specificity for aspartic acid at the P1 position. The human genome encodes at least ten caspases, which are broadly categorized into initiator caspases (caspase-2, -8, -9, -10) and effector caspases (caspase-3, -6, -7). Initiator caspases are activated through proximity-induced dimerization within large signaling complexes, whereas effector caspases are activated by cleavage by initiator caspases. The three-dimensional structure of the active site in most human caspases reveals a conserved fold with a substrate-binding groove that accommodates four amino acid residues upstream of the cleavage site (P4–P1). Subtle variations in the amino acid side chains lining this groove account for the distinct substrate preferences observed among caspases. For instance, caspase-3 and -7 possess a relatively narrow and deep S4 pocket that favors bulky hydrophobic residues such as valine or aspartate at the P4 position, whereas caspase-1 and -4 have a more open S4 pocket that accommodates larger residues like tryptophan. These structural differences are reflected in the optimal tetrapeptide substrate sequences determined through combinatorial peptide library screens, which have been validated in vivo and in vitro. The known endogenous substrates of caspases include structural proteins such as lamin A, cytoskeletal regulators such as gelsolin, and DNA repair enzymes such as PARP and ICAD. The cleavage of these substrates leads to the coordinated dismantling of the cell during apoptosis, while the cleavage of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as pro-IL-1β and pro-IL-18 by caspase-1 drives the maturation and secretion of these potent mediators of inflammation. The exquisite specificity of caspases for their substrates ensures that proteolytic signaling is tightly controlled and spatially restricted, preventing unintended cellular damage. The conservation of these cleavage motifs across species and the well-characterized structural basis for substrate recognition make caspases ideal candidates for the development of genetically encoded reporter systems that can be deployed in heterologous cellular environments such as yeast.

### Substrate Specificity of Caspases

The substrate specificity of caspases is determined by the precise architecture of their active site cleft, which accommodates four amino acid residues immediately preceding the scissile bond (P4–P1), with an absolute requirement for aspartic acid at the P1 position. Structural studies of human caspases have revealed that while the catalytic core is highly conserved across the family, the residues lining the substrate-binding pockets exhibit significant variation, leading to distinct preferences for amino acids at the P2, P3, and P4 positions. Caspase-3 and -7, the primary effector caspases, exhibit a strong preference for aspartic acid at P4 and valine at P2, resulting in the optimal recognition of the DEVD motif. In contrast, caspase-1 and -4, which function in inflammatory pathways, favor tryptophan at P4 and histidine at P2, leading to the cleavage of WEHD sequences. Caspase-8 and -10, which initiate the extrinsic apoptotic pathway, show a preference for leucine at P4 and aspartic acid at P2, consistent with the LETD motif found in their physiological substrates. Caspase-9, the central initiator of the intrinsic pathway, recognizes the LEHD sequence, while caspase-6 prefers TEVD. These specificity profiles are not merely theoretical constructs but have been rigorously validated through extensive peptide library screens, mass spectrometry-based substrate profiling, and mutational analyses of endogenous cleavage sites. The P2 position is particularly flexible for caspase-3 and -7, allowing for substitution with histidine, glutamine, or even glycine with only modest reductions in cleavage efficiency, whereas caspase-1 and -4 exhibit a more stringent requirement for histidine at P2. The P3 and P4 positions contribute significantly to binding affinity and selectivity, with hydrophobic residues such as leucine, valine, and tryptophan generally favored. These subtle differences in substrate recognition are exploited in the invention through the design of cleavable transcription factor reporters containing tetrapeptide linkers that match the optimal cleavage sequences of individual caspases. By incorporating these sequences into a membrane-tethered chimeric transcription factor, the system ensures that reporter activation occurs only upon cleavage by the intended caspase, thereby enabling the discrimination of closely related proteases with high fidelity. The use of non-cleavable controls, in which the critical aspartic acid residue is replaced with glycine, further confirms the specificity of the readout, as these constructs fail to activate reporter genes even in the presence of active caspases. The ability to engineer these specificity determinants into a modular reporter system allows for the parallel interrogation of multiple caspases within the same cellular context, providing a comprehensive map of proteolytic activity and enabling the identification of compounds that selectively modulate individual members of the caspase family.

### Mechanisms of Activating Upstream Initiator Caspases

The activation of upstream initiator caspases is a tightly regulated process that occurs exclusively within the context of large, multi-protein signaling complexes assembled in response to specific cellular stimuli. These complexes serve as molecular platforms that bring procaspases into close proximity, facilitating their dimerization and subsequent autocatalytic cleavage. The extrinsic apoptotic pathway is initiated by the binding of death ligands such as Fas ligand, TRAIL, or TNF-α to their respective cell surface receptors, which contain a conserved cytoplasmic region known as the death domain. Upon ligand binding, these receptors undergo conformational changes that promote trimerization and recruitment of the adapter protein FADD, which contains both a death domain and a death effector domain. The death domain of FADD interacts with the death domain of the receptor, while its death effector domain binds to the corresponding death effector domains present in the prodomains of caspase-8 and caspase-10. This recruitment leads to the oligomerization of procaspase-8 and -10 molecules, inducing a conformational change that promotes their dimerization and autoactivation. The resulting active caspase-8 or -10 then cleaves and activates downstream effector caspases such as caspase-3 and -7, initiating the execution phase of apoptosis. The formation of this complex, known as the death-inducing signaling complex, is subject to regulation by anti-apoptotic proteins such as c-FLIP, which contains death effector domains similar to those of caspase-8 and can compete for binding to FADD, thereby inhibiting caspase-8 activation. In the intrinsic pathway, mitochondrial outer membrane permeabilization leads to the release of cytochrome c into the cytosol, where it binds to the adaptor protein Apaf-1. Apaf-1, a homolog of the C. elegans protein CED-4, contains a nucleotide-binding oligomerization domain and a CARD domain. Upon binding cytochrome c and ATP, Apaf-1 undergoes a conformational change that promotes its oligomerization into a heptameric wheel-like structure called the apoptosome. This platform recruits procaspase-9 via CARD-CARD interactions, bringing multiple molecules into close proximity and enabling their autoactivation. The activation of caspase-9 then triggers the cleavage of effector caspases, leading to cell death. The regulation of these pathways is further modulated by the presence of inhibitory proteins that interfere with complex assembly. For example, certain viral proteins mimic the structure of death effector domains to sequester FADD or caspase-8, while cellular inhibitors such as XIAP bind directly to active caspases and block their catalytic activity. The reconstitution of these intricate signaling networks in yeast, which lacks endogenous caspases and their regulators, provides a powerful means to dissect the molecular requirements for protease activation in a controlled and simplified environment. By expressing the individual components of these pathways—receptors, adapters, and procaspases—in defined combinations, the invention enables the systematic identification of the minimal set of proteins necessary for activation, the discovery of novel regulatory factors, and the screening of compounds that modulate complex assembly rather than protease activity itself.

### Phenotypes of Animal Caspases Expressed in Yeast

The expression of animal caspases in the yeast Saccharomyces cerevisiae has revealed a range of phenotypic outcomes that reflect both the intrinsic cytotoxicity of these proteases and their capacity to be regulated by conserved molecular mechanisms. When overexpressed at high levels, many caspases induce rapid growth inhibition or cell death in yeast, a phenomenon that mirrors their pro-apoptotic function in mammalian cells. This cytotoxicity is dependent on the catalytic activity of the caspase, as mutations in the active site cysteine abolish the lethal phenotype. The observation that caspase expression in yeast results in cell death has been exploited in prior studies to identify inhibitors of apoptosis through genetic screens for suppressors of lethality. However, such lethality-based assays are inherently limited in their ability to provide quantitative, dynamic, or mechanistic insights into protease regulation, as they rely on binary endpoints of survival or death. More importantly, not all caspases induce strong growth inhibition in yeast, and the severity of the phenotype can vary depending on expression levels, promoter strength, and the specific caspase isoform. These limitations motivated the development of a cleavable reporter gene system that converts protease activity into a graded, easily quantifiable output. The key insight derived from these studies was that even when caspase expression does not lead to overt cell death, the proteolytic activity of the enzyme can still be harnessed to cleave engineered substrates that liberate transcription factors. This principle enabled the design of a system in which the presence of active caspase results in the activation of reporter genes such as LEU2 and lacZ, allowing for the detection of low-level proteolytic activity that would otherwise be masked by cellular toxicity. The ability to titrate caspase expression using promoters of varying strengths and plasmid copy numbers further refined this approach, enabling the maintenance of protease activity at levels sufficient for reporter activation without compromising yeast viability. These studies demonstrated that the fundamental mechanisms of caspase activation—dimerization, auto-processing, and substrate recognition—are conserved in yeast, despite the absence of endogenous caspase homologs or apoptotic machinery. This conservation underpins the utility of yeast as a heterologous platform for the functional characterization of mammalian proteases and their regulators, providing a genetically tractable system in which complex signaling pathways can be reconstituted, dissected, and screened with unprecedented precision.

## EXAMPLE 1

### Development and Testing of a Cleavable Reporter Gene System for Assaying Protease Activity in Living Yeast

A cleavable reporter gene system was developed to monitor protease activity in living yeast cells by engineering a chimeric transcription factor that is sequestered at the plasma membrane until cleaved by a specific protease. This system is based on the fusion of the extracellular and transmembrane domains of the human Fas receptor (CD95) to a chimeric transcription factor composed of the DNA-binding domain of LexA and the transcriptional activation domain of B42. Between the Fas transmembrane domain and the LexA-B42 transcription factor, a tetrapeptide linker sequence recognized by a specific caspase was inserted. The linker sequences included WEHD for caspase-1, DEVD for caspase-3, LETD for caspase-8, and LEHD for caspase-9, among others. A non-cleavable control variant was generated by substituting the critical aspartic acid residue at the P1 position with glycine, resulting in sequences such as WEHG or DEVG. These constructs were expressed in yeast strain EGY48, which carries a stably integrated LEU2 reporter gene under the control of six LexA operator sequences and an episomal lacZ reporter gene under the control of two LexA operators. The expression of the Fas-LexA-B42 fusion protein was driven by the TEF promoter, chosen for its moderate strength to ensure appropriate expression levels without inducing cytotoxicity. Co-expression of active caspases—either as full-length zymogens or as constitutively active mutants—resulted in the cleavage of the tetrapeptide linker, releasing the LexA-B42 transcription factor from membrane tethering. The liberated transcription factor translocated to the nucleus and activated transcription of the LEU2 and lacZ reporter genes, enabling yeast growth on leucine-deficient medium and the development of a blue color in the presence of X-gal substrate. Yeast expressing the non-cleavable control constructs failed to grow on selective media or produce blue color, regardless of caspase co-expression. The system was validated across all ten human caspases, with each caspase activating only its cognate substrate sequence, demonstrating exquisite specificity. For example, caspase-1 activated the WEHD linker but not DEVD, while caspase-3 activated DEVD but not LETD. The system was further refined by testing different numbers of cleavage sites (1–6) and varying the strength of the promoter driving the fusion protein, with a single cleavage site proving optimal for signal-to-noise ratio. The use of low-copy plasmids for caspase expression and high-copy plasmids for reporter constructs ensured that background activation was minimized while maintaining a robust, detectable signal. This single-component system provided a reliable, quantitative, and scalable method for monitoring caspase activity in real time within living cells.

### A Genetic System for Monitoring Caspase 1 Activity in Yeast Cells.

To establish a genetically encoded system for monitoring caspase-1 activity in yeast, a fusion protein was engineered consisting of the N-terminal transmembrane domain of Fas, a cleavable linker containing the sequence GWEHDG, and the LexA-B42 transcription factor. This construct, designated Fas-d-S1-TA, was expressed in yeast strain EGY48, which contains a LEU2 reporter gene under the control of six LexA operator sequences and a lacZ reporter gene under the control of two LexA operators. The Fas-d-S1-TA construct was cloned into a low-copy plasmid under the control of the TEF promoter to ensure moderate expression levels. Active caspase-1 was co-expressed from a separate plasmid under the ADH promoter, which provides strong constitutive expression. As a negative control, a catalytically inactive mutant of caspase-1 was generated by substituting the active site cysteine with alanine (C285A). Yeast transformants were plated on complete medium, leucine-deficient medium, and medium supplemented with X-gal to assess growth and β-galactosidase activity. Yeast expressing Fas-d-S1-TA alone or Fas-d-S1-TA with the inactive caspase-1 mutant exhibited no growth on leucine-deficient medium and remained white on X-gal plates, indicating no reporter activation. In contrast, yeast co-expressing Fas-d-S1-TA and active caspase-1 exhibited robust growth on leucine-deficient medium and developed a distinct blue color within 48 hours, confirming cleavage of the WEHD linker and liberation of the transcription factor. The signal-to-noise ratio was optimized by adjusting the number of LexA operators in the reporter constructs and by testing alternative promoters for caspase-1 expression, with the ADH promoter yielding the highest signal without inducing cytotoxicity. β-galactosidase activity was quantified using a colorimetric assay, with active caspase-1 producing a signal approximately 20-fold above background. These results demonstrated that the system is highly specific, sensitive, and capable of detecting caspase-1 activity in a living cell context without the need for cell lysis or exogenous substrate addition.

### Substrate Specifities of the Caspases Expressed in Yeast.

To systematically characterize the substrate specificity of all ten human caspases, a series of Fas-LexA-B42 fusion proteins were constructed, each containing a different tetrapeptide linker sequence known to be cleaved by specific caspases. These included WEHD for caspase-1, DEVD for caspase-3, LETD for caspase-8, LEHD for caspase-9, TEVD for caspase-6, and DEHD for caspase-2, along with non-cleavable controls containing glycine substitutions at the P1 position. Each construct was co-expressed in yeast with individual caspases expressed from plasmids under the ADH or TEF promoter. Yeast transformants were plated on leucine-deficient medium and X-gal-containing agar to assess reporter gene activation. Caspase-1 activated only the WEHD-containing substrate, while caspase-4 and -5 also showed activity with WEHD, consistent with their classification as inflammatory caspases. Caspase-3 and -7 activated the DEVD substrate but not WEHD or LETD, demonstrating their specificity for effector caspase motifs. Caspase-2 activated both DEVD and DEHD substrates, consistent with prior biochemical data indicating its ability to cleave substrates with histidine at the P2 position. Caspase-6 activated TEVD, while caspase-8 and -10 activated LETD. Caspase-9 activated LEHD, and no cross-reactivity was observed between initiator and effector caspase substrates. The non-cleavable controls, in which aspartic acid was replaced with glycine, showed no reporter activation under any condition. The results were consistent with known substrate preferences derived from peptide library screens and structural analyses, validating the yeast-based system as a faithful reporter of caspase specificity. The system was further confirmed by testing catalytically inactive caspase mutants, which failed to activate any substrate, and by demonstrating that the cleavage event was protease-dependent and not due to endogenous yeast proteases. This comprehensive mapping of substrate specificity established the foundation for the development of multiplexed screening platforms capable of distinguishing between individual caspases within complex proteolytic networks.

### Development of Two-component Systems Demonstrating Function of Caspase Activators in Yeast

A two-component system was developed to reconstitute the physiological activation of initiator caspases by their upstream regulatory proteins in yeast. This system separates the expression of the inactive zymogen form of a caspase from the expression of its cognate activator, ensuring that protease activation occurs only upon their physical interaction. For example, pro-caspase-1 was expressed from a low-copy plasmid under the weak CYC1 promoter, while the adapter protein ASC was expressed from a high-copy plasmid under the strong TEF promoter. The cleavable Fas-LexA-B42 reporter containing the WEHD linker was co-expressed in the same cell. In the absence of ASC, pro-caspase-1 remained inactive and failed to activate the reporter. Similarly, expression of ASC alone did not trigger reporter activation. Only when both components were co-expressed did the yeast grow on leucine-deficient medium and produce a blue color on X-gal plates, indicating that ASC induced the dimerization and autoactivation of pro-caspase-1. This system was extended to other initiator caspases, including pro-caspase-8 and FADD, pro-caspase-9 and Apaf-1, and pro-caspase-2 and RAIDD. In each case, reporter activation was strictly dependent on the co-expression of both the zymogen and its activator. The system was validated by introducing mutations into the catalytic site of the caspase or into the interaction domain of the activator, both of which abolished reporter activation. The use of promoters of varying strengths allowed for precise titration of expression levels to prevent spontaneous activation while maintaining sensitivity to activator-induced cleavage. This two-component system enabled the detection of protease activation events that require complex assembly, providing a powerful tool for identifying compounds that disrupt or enhance these interactions.

### Schematic Representation of a Two-component System for Caspase1 Activators in Yeast Cells.

The two-component system for caspase-1 activation in yeast is schematically represented as a modular genetic circuit comprising three essential elements: a zymogen form of caspase-1, its upstream activator ASC, and a cleavable transcription factor reporter. The pro-caspase-1 protein is expressed from a low-copy plasmid under the control of the CYC1 promoter, ensuring that its expression level is insufficient to induce autoactivation. The adapter protein ASC is expressed from a high-copy plasmid under the strong TEF promoter, ensuring its abundance relative to pro-caspase-1. The cleavable reporter consists of the Fas transmembrane domain fused to a LexA-B42 transcription factor, with a WEHD tetrapeptide linker inserted between them. In the absence of ASC, pro-caspase-1 remains monomeric and inactive, and the transcription factor remains tethered to the plasma membrane, preventing reporter gene activation. Upon co-expression of ASC, the CARD domain of ASC interacts with the CARD domain of pro-caspase-1, inducing oligomerization and proximity-induced autocatalytic cleavage. This cleavage event severs the WEHD linker, releasing the LexA-B42 transcription factor from membrane tethering. The liberated transcription factor translocates to the nucleus and binds to LexA operator sequences upstream of the LEU2 and lacZ reporter genes, activating their transcription. Activation of LEU2 permits yeast growth on leucine-deficient medium, while activation of lacZ produces β-galactosidase, which cleaves X-gal to generate a blue pigment. This system is highly specific, as neither component alone is sufficient to activate the reporter, and mutations in either the caspase active site or the ASC interaction domain abolish activation. The system was validated across multiple independent yeast strains and under varying expression conditions, confirming its robustness and suitability for high-throughput screening applications.

### Development of a Plural-component System—Mammalian Protease Activating Pathways Reconstituted in Yeast

A plural-component system was developed to reconstitute entire mammalian protease activation pathways in yeast, enabling the study of multi-protein signaling complexes that are too complex for two-component systems. The extrinsic apoptotic pathway was reconstituted by co-expressing three components: the death receptor Fas, the adapter protein FADD, and the zymogen form of caspase-8 or caspase-10. Fas was expressed from a high-copy plasmid under the strong ADH promoter, FADD was expressed from a low-copy plasmid under the weak CYC1 promoter, and pro-caspase-8 or pro-caspase-10 was expressed from a low-copy plasmid under the CYC1 promoter. A cleavable reporter containing the LETD linker was co-expressed to monitor caspase activity. In the absence of Fas, FADD alone was insufficient to activate caspase-8, and no reporter signal was detected. In the absence of FADD, Fas expression alone did not activate caspase-8. Only when all three components were co-expressed did the yeast grow on leucine-deficient medium and produce a blue color, indicating that Fas oligomerization recruited FADD, which in turn recruited and activated caspase-8. This system was similarly applied to the NLRP1 inflammasome, where pro-caspase-1, ASC, and a gain-of-function mutant of NLRP1 (NLRP1ΔLRR) were co-expressed. Reporter activation occurred only when all three components were present, demonstrating that the reconstituted pathway faithfully recapitulated the physiological activation mechanism. The system was further validated by omitting individual components, which abolished reporter activation, and by introducing catalytically inactive mutants, which also failed to activate the reporter. This plural-component system provides a powerful platform for studying the assembly and regulation of complex proteolytic networks in a genetically tractable host.

### Schematic Representation of Plural-component Systems for Caspase-8 Activation in Yeast.

The plural-component system for caspase-8 activation in yeast is schematically represented as a three-part genetic circuit that reconstitutes the extrinsic apoptotic pathway. The first component is the death receptor Fas, expressed from a high-copy plasmid under the strong ADH promoter, which promotes its oligomerization in the absence of ligand. The second component is the adapter protein FADD, expressed from a low-copy plasmid under the weak CYC1 promoter, ensuring that its concentration is limiting and sufficient only to bridge Fas and caspase-8 upon receptor clustering. The third component is the zymogen form of caspase-8, expressed from a low-copy plasmid under the CYC1 promoter, ensuring that its expression level is insufficient for autoactivation. The cleavable reporter consists of the Fas transmembrane domain fused to a LexA-B42 transcription factor with a LETD linker inserted between them. In the absence of Fas, FADD and caspase-8 remain unassociated, and the reporter is inactive. In the absence of FADD, Fas oligomerizes but cannot recruit caspase-8, and the reporter remains silent. Only when all three components are co-expressed does Fas oligomerize, recruit FADD via death domain interactions, and recruit caspase-8 via death effector domain interactions, leading to caspase-8 dimerization, autoactivation, and cleavage of the LETD linker. This releases the LexA-B42 transcription factor, which translocates to the nucleus and activates the LEU2 and lacZ reporter genes, resulting in growth on leucine-deficient medium and blue color development on X-gal plates. The system is highly specific, as deletion of any single component abolishes reporter activation, and mutations in the catalytic site of caspase-8 or the interaction domains of Fas or FADD prevent activation. This schematic provides a clear, modular framework for the reconstitution of complex signaling pathways in yeast.

### Testing of Yeast-based, Plural-component Systems for Activating Caspases-8 or -10.

Yeast-based plural-component systems for caspase-8 and caspase-10 activation were tested by transforming strain EGY48 with a combination of plasmids expressing Fas, FADD, and either pro-caspase-8 or pro-caspase-10, along with a cleavable reporter containing the LETD linker. In the first configuration, Fas was expressed from a high-copy plasmid under the ADH promoter, FADD was expressed from a low-copy plasmid under the CYC1 promoter, and pro-caspase-8 or -10 was expressed from a low-copy plasmid under the CYC1 promoter. Yeast transformed with all three components exhibited robust growth on leucine-deficient medium and blue color development on X-gal plates, confirming activation of the caspase. When Fas was omitted, no growth or color development occurred, even in the presence of FADD and caspase-8. When FADD was omitted, no activation occurred, even with Fas and caspase-8 present. When the catalytically inactive caspase-8(C285A) mutant was used, no reporter activation was observed. The same results were obtained with caspase-10, demonstrating that the system is broadly applicable to DED-containing caspases. To further validate the system, cDNA libraries were screened by omitting the Fas plasmid and expressing only FADD and caspase-8, then transforming with a human cDNA library under the GAL1 promoter. Positive clones were selected based on growth on leucine-deficient medium and blue color development. Sequencing of these clones revealed that many encoded known death receptors such as DR4 and DR5, which activate caspase-8 in a FADD-dependent manner. Other clones encoded c-FLIP and caspase-2, which modulate caspase-8 activity. These results confirmed that the plural-component system is not only capable of reconstituting known pathways but also of identifying novel regulators of protease activation through functional screening.

### Development of a Plural-component System for the NALP1 Inflammasome in Yeast

A plural-component system was developed to reconstitute the NLRP1 inflammasome in yeast, enabling the study of innate immune signaling pathways that activate caspase-1. The system consists of three components: pro-caspase-1, the adapter protein ASC, and a gain-of-function mutant of NLRP1 lacking its auto-inhibitory leucine-rich repeat domain (NLRP1ΔLRR). Pro-caspase-1 was expressed from a low-copy plasmid under the weak CYC1 promoter, ASC was expressed from a high-copy plasmid under the TEF promoter, and NLRP1ΔLRR was expressed from a high-copy plasmid under the strong ADH promoter. A cleavable reporter containing the WEHD linker was co-expressed to monitor caspase-1 activity. In the absence of any one component, no reporter activation occurred. When all three components were co-expressed, yeast exhibited robust growth on leucine-deficient medium and developed a blue color on X-gal plates, indicating that NLRP1ΔLRR induced the oligomerization of ASC, which in turn recruited and activated pro-caspase-1. The system was validated by substituting NLRP1ΔLRR with wild-type NLRP1, which failed to activate the reporter, confirming that the deletion of the LRR domain is necessary for constitutive activation. The catalytically inactive caspase-1(C285A) mutant also failed to activate the reporter, confirming the protease dependence of the signal. The system was further tested by replacing NLRP1ΔLRR with NLRP3ΔLRR, which also activated the reporter, demonstrating the versatility of the platform for studying different inflammasome components. This plural-component system provides a genetically tractable model for studying inflammasome assembly and for screening compounds that modulate this critical pathway in inflammation and autoimmunity.

The plural-component system for caspase-1 activation in yeast is schematically represented as a three-part genetic circuit that reconstitutes the NLRP1 inflammasome. Only when all three components are co-expressed does NLRP1ΔLRR oligomerize, recruit ASC via PYD-PYD interactions, and recruit pro-caspase-1 via CARD-CARD interactions, leading to caspase-1 dimerization, autoactivation, and cleavage of the WEHD linker. This releases the LexA-B42 transcription factor, which translocates to the nucleus and activates the LEU2 and lacZ reporter genes, resulting in growth on leucine-deficient medium and blue color development on X-gal plates. The system is highly specific, as deletion of any single component abolishes reporter activation, and mutations in the catalytic site of caspase-1 or the interaction domains of ASC or NLRP1ΔLRR prevent activation. This schematic provides a clear, modular framework for the reconstitution of inflammasome signaling in yeast.

### HTS Implementation of Yeast-based Protease Network Reconstitution Assays

High-throughput screening (HTS) implementations of the yeast-based protease network reconstitution assays were achieved by adapting the culture conditions to 384-well microtiter plate formats. Yeast strains expressing the appropriate combination of protease components and cleavable reporters were cultured in liquid medium containing X-gal substrate, and β-galactosidase activity was measured by absorbance at 620 nm after 48 hours of incubation at 30°C. The initial yeast cell density, incubation time, and X-gal concentration were optimized to maximize signal-to-noise ratio while minimizing variability between wells. For the Fas/FADD/caspase-8 system, a starting cell density of 2×10⁵ cells per well, a 48-hour incubation, and a 400 µg/mL concentration of X-gal yielded optimal results. For the NLRP1ΔLRR/ASC/caspase-1 system, identical conditions were used, demonstrating the scalability and reproducibility of the platform. The Z’ factor was calculated for both systems and exceeded 0.6, confirming that the assays are robust and suitable for HTS. Automated liquid handling systems were employed to dispense compounds, yeast suspensions, and X-gal into 384-well plates, enabling the screening of large compound libraries with minimal manual intervention. The system was validated using the broad-spectrum caspase inhibitor zVAD-fmk, which dose-dependently inhibited reporter activation with an IC50 of approximately 2–5 µM, while a calpain inhibitor had no effect, confirming the specificity of the readout. These results demonstrate that the yeast-based protease network reconstitution assays are fully compatible with HTS workflows and can be used to screen thousands of compounds per day with high fidelity.

### Optimization of Signal:Noise Ratio in Microtiter Plates

The signal-to-noise ratio in the 384-well microtiter plate assays was optimized through systematic variation of multiple parameters, including yeast cell density, incubation time, X-gal concentration, and promoter strength. Initial experiments revealed that cell densities below 1×10⁵ cells per well resulted in weak signals, while densities above 5×10⁵ cells per well led to increased background due to non-specific protease activity and cell lysis. A density of 2×10⁵ cells per well was determined to be optimal, providing a strong, reproducible signal with minimal variability. Incubation times shorter than 48 hours resulted in incomplete reporter activation, while longer incubations led to increased background due to spontaneous reporter activation and yeast senescence. A 48-hour incubation period was selected as the standard. The concentration of X-gal was varied from 50 to 800 µg/mL, with 400 µg/mL providing the highest signal-to-noise ratio without saturation. The strength of the promoter driving the cleavable reporter was also optimized, with the TEF promoter yielding the best balance between expression and background. The number of LexA operator copies in the reporter genes was adjusted to ensure that the signal was sufficiently amplified without inducing toxicity. For the LEU2 reporter, six operators were optimal, while for the lacZ reporter, two operators provided the best signal-to-noise ratio. These optimizations collectively resulted in a Z’ factor greater than 0.6 for both the Fas/FADD/caspase-8 and NLRP1ΔLRR/ASC/caspase-1 systems, confirming their suitability for high-throughput screening.

### Validation of Yeast-based Caspase Assay Using Pharmacological Inhibitor of Caspases

The yeast-based caspase assay was validated using the broad-spectrum caspase inhibitor zVAD-fmk, a cell-permeable peptidyl fluoromethylketone that irreversibly inhibits the catalytic cysteine of caspases. Serial dilutions of zVAD-fmk were added to yeast cultures expressing the Fas/FADD/caspase-8 or NLRP1ΔLRR/ASC/caspase-1 systems in 384-well plates, and β-galactosidase activity was measured after 48 hours. A dose-dependent inhibition of reporter activation was observed, with an IC50 value of approximately 2–5 µM, consistent with previously reported values in mammalian systems. The inhibition was specific to caspase activity, as the calpain inhibitor ALLN had no effect on reporter activation, even at concentrations up to 100 µM. The assay was further validated by testing the effect of zVAD-fmk on yeast expressing only the lacZ reporter gene without any caspase components, which showed no inhibition, confirming that the compound does not directly inhibit β-galactosidase. These results demonstrate that the yeast-based assay faithfully recapitulates the pharmacological profile of caspase inhibitors and is suitable for identifying compounds with known mechanisms of action as well as novel modulators.

### Introduction of Yeast-based Systems for Activating Caspases

The yeast-based systems for activating caspases represent a novel and broadly applicable platform for the functional characterization of proteolytic networks in a genetically tractable eukaryotic host. These systems leverage the conserved mechanisms of caspase activation that are shared between mammals and yeast, despite the absence of endogenous caspase homologs in the latter. By expressing human caspases and their upstream regulators in defined combinations, the invention enables the reconstitution of physiological signaling pathways that are otherwise inaccessible in vitro. The use of cleavable transcription factor reporters converts protease activity into a quantifiable, selectable, and screenable phenotype, allowing for the detection of both inhibitors and activators of proteolytic activation. The systems are modular, scalable, and adaptable to other protease families, making them ideal for high-throughput screening of chemical and cDNA libraries. The ability to titrate expression levels using promoters of varying strengths and plasmid copy numbers ensures that protease activity is maintained at levels sufficient for detection without inducing cytotoxicity. The systems have been validated across all ten human caspases and have been successfully applied to the study of autophagins, demonstrating their versatility and broad utility in protease biology.

### Describe Specificity of Upstream Activators of Initiator Caspases

The specificity of upstream activators of initiator caspases is determined by the homotypic interactions between their respective oligomerization domains, which ensure that only cognate pairs can assemble into functional complexes. For example, the death effector domains (DEDs) of FADD bind exclusively to the DEDs of caspase-8 and caspase-10, while the caspase recruitment domains (CARDs) of ASC bind specifically to the CARDs of caspase-1 and caspase-4. These interactions are highly selective, as heterologous pairings—such as FADD with caspase-9 or ASC with caspase-8—fail to induce activation. This specificity is preserved in the yeast system, where co-expression of non-cognate pairs results in no reporter activation, even when both components are abundantly expressed. The system was used to test the specificity of numerous known and putative activators, including RAIDD, Apaf-1, and c-FLIP, and confirmed that each activator activates only its cognate caspase. This high degree of specificity enables the use of the yeast system to identify novel regulators of caspase activation through functional screening, as only true interactors will rescue reporter activation in a multi-component system. The ability to distinguish between specific and non-specific interactions provides a powerful tool for mapping proteolytic networks and for identifying therapeutic targets that modulate specific pathways without affecting others.

### Validate 3-component Yeast-based Caspase Assay

The three-component yeast-based caspase assay was validated through a series of rigorous controls designed to confirm the specificity, sensitivity, and reproducibility of the system. In each case, the assay required the co-expression of a death receptor, an adapter protein, and a procaspase to produce a detectable signal. Omission of any single component resulted in complete loss of reporter activation, confirming that all three components are necessary for pathway function. The use of catalytically inactive caspase mutants (e.g., C285A) also abolished reporter activation, confirming that proteolytic activity is required for the readout. The system was tested with multiple independent yeast strains and plasmid constructs, and the results were consistently reproducible across multiple experiments. The Z’ factor for the Fas/FADD/caspase-8 system was determined to be 0.72, and for the NLRP1ΔLRR/ASC/caspase-1 system, it was 0.68, both exceeding the threshold of 0.5 required for high-throughput screening. The assay was further validated by screening known modulators of the pathways, including zVAD-fmk, which inhibited both systems with IC50 values consistent with published data, and c-FLIP, which suppressed caspase-8 activation in a dose-dependent manner. These results confirm that the three-component assay is a robust, specific, and reliable platform for the identification of novel modulators of proteolytic networks.

### Describe Flow Chart for cDNA Library Screening Using Reporter Gene Strategy

The flow chart for cDNA library screening using the reporter gene strategy begins with the construction of a human cDNA library in a yeast expression plasmid under the control of a strong promoter such as GAL1 or ADH. The library is transformed into a yeast strain that contains a cleavable reporter system in which reporter gene activation requires the activity of a specific caspase. For example, to screen for activators of caspase-8, yeast are engineered to express FADD and pro-caspase-8 at low levels and a LETD-containing reporter, but lack the death receptor Fas. Only cDNA clones that encode a protein capable of replacing Fas and activating caspase-8 will produce a signal. Transformants are plated on leucine-deficient medium containing X-gal, and blue colonies are selected. Positive clones are recovered, re-transformed into fresh yeast to confirm the phenotype, and sequenced to identify the encoded protein. To eliminate false positives, clones are tested in counter-screens using yeast expressing only the reporter gene or a different caspase system. This strategy enables the functional cloning of novel regulators of protease activation, including receptors, adapters, and enzymes that modulate pathway assembly. The system has been successfully used to identify DR4, DR5, c-FLIP, and caspase-2 as activators of caspase-8, demonstrating its power for discovery-driven biology.

### Describe Use of One-component Yeast-based Caspase Activity Assay

The one-component yeast-based caspase activity assay is used to directly measure the proteolytic activity of a caspase without requiring upstream activators. This assay is ideal for characterizing the substrate specificity of caspases, testing the effect of mutations on catalytic activity, and screening for direct inhibitors of the protease. The assay is highly sensitive and specific, as it does not rely on complex assembly and is not confounded by the expression of upstream regulators. ### Motivate Validation of Yeast-based Assays for Effector Caspase Activators

The validation of yeast-based assays for effector caspase activators is motivated by the need to identify novel regulators of the execution phase of apoptosis, which is mediated by caspases-3, -6, and -7. While initiator caspases are activated by complex assembly, effector caspases are primarily activated by cleavage by upstream initiators, making them less amenable to screening for direct activators. However, the yeast system enables the reconstitution of these pathways by expressing an upstream initiator caspase (e.g., caspase-9) and a low-level zymogen of an effector caspase (e.g., caspase-3) in the same cell. Only when the upstream caspase is active and cleaves the effector will the reporter be activated. This system allows for the screening of cDNA libraries for proteins that enhance this cleavage event, either by promoting the assembly of the apoptosome or by inhibiting endogenous inhibitors. The ability to detect such modulators provides a powerful tool for understanding the regulation of apoptosis and for identifying novel therapeutic targets in diseases characterized by excessive or insufficient cell death.

### Describe Two and Three-component Systems for Effector Caspase Activators

Two-component systems for effector caspase activators involve the co-expression of an upstream initiator caspase and a low-level zymogen of an effector caspase, with a cleavable reporter containing the effector’s substrate sequence. For example, caspase-9 and pro-caspase-3 are co-expressed with a DEVD-containing reporter. Reporter activation occurs only when caspase-9 cleaves and activates caspase-3. Three-component systems extend this concept by including a scaffold protein that facilitates the interaction between the initiator and effector. For example, Apaf-1, caspase-9, and pro-caspase-3 are co-expressed with a DEVD reporter. In this system, reporter activation requires the assembly of the apoptosome, providing a more physiologically relevant readout. These systems enable the screening of compounds that modulate the efficiency of effector caspase activation, either by enhancing complex assembly or by inhibiting negative regulators.

### Describe Flow Chart for cDNA Library Screening

The flow chart for cDNA library screening begins with the construction of a human cDNA library in a yeast expression plasmid under the control of a strong promoter such as GAL1 or ADH. The library is transformed into a yeast strain that contains a cleavable reporter system in which reporter gene activation requires the activity of a specific caspase. For example, to screen for activators of caspase-8, yeast are engineered to express FADD and pro-caspase-8 at low levels and a LETD-containing reporter, but lack the death receptor Fas. Only cDNA clones that encode a protein capable of replacing Fas and activating caspase-8 will produce a signal. Transformants are plated on leucine-deficient medium containing X-gal, and blue colonies are selected. Positive clones are recovered, re-transformed into fresh yeast to confirm the phenotype, and sequenced to identify the encoded protein. To eliminate false positives, clones are tested in counter-screens using yeast expressing only the reporter gene or a different caspase system. This strategy enables the functional cloning of novel regulators of protease activation, including receptors, adapters, and enzymes that modulate pathway assembly. The system has been successfully used to identify DR4, DR5, c-FLIP, and caspase-2 as activators of caspase-8, demonstrating its power for discovery-driven biology.

### Describe Examples of cDNA Cloning Results

Examples of cDNA cloning results include the identification of DR4 and DR5 as activators of caspase-8 in a screen of a HepG2 liver cDNA library, where 28 out of 40 positive clones encoded these death receptors. In a screen of a HeLa cervical carcinoma library, 7 out of 19 positive clones encoded a splice variant of caspase-8 that activated the reporter independently of FADD. In a screen of a HEK293 kidney cell library, 24 out of 55 positive clones encoded FADD, confirming its role as the essential adapter for caspase-10 activation. In a screen of a human placental library, 10 out of 18 positive clones encoded c-FLIP, a known modulator of caspase-8 activity. These results demonstrate the high success rate of the system in identifying known regulators and its potential for discovering novel components of proteolytic networks.

### Optimize Signal:Noise Ratio in Microtiter Plates

The signal-to-noise ratio in the 384-well microtiter plate assays was optimized through systematic variation of multiple parameters, including yeast cell density, incubation time, X-gal concentration, and promoter strength. Initial experiments revealed that cell densities below 1×10⁵ cells per well resulted in weak signals, while densities above 5×10⁵ cells per well led to increased background due to non-specific protease activity and cell lysis. A density of 2×10⁵ cells per well was determined to be optimal, providing a strong, reproducible signal with minimal variability. Incubation times shorter than 48 hours resulted in incomplete reporter activation, while longer incubations led to increased background due to spontaneous reporter activation and yeast senescence. A 48-hour incubation period was selected as the standard. The concentration of X-gal was varied from 50 to 800 µg/mL, with 400 µg/mL providing the highest signal-to-noise ratio without saturation. The strength of the promoter driving the cleavable reporter was also optimized, with the TEF promoter yielding the best balance between expression and background. The number of LexA operator copies in the reporter genes was adjusted to ensure that the signal was sufficiently amplified without inducing toxicity. For the LEU2 reporter, six operators were optimal, while for the lacZ reporter, two operators provided the best signal-to-noise ratio. These optimizations collectively resulted in a Z’ factor greater than 0.6 for both the Fas/FADD/caspase-8 and NLRP1ΔLRR/ASC/caspase-1 systems, confirming their suitability for high-throughput screening.

### Describe Plasmids for Co-expression of Caspases and Substrate Cleavable Transcription Factors

Plasmids for the co-expression of caspases and substrate-cleavable transcription factors were constructed using the pRS series of yeast expression vectors, which provide selectable markers such as HIS3, TRP1, URA3, and LEU2. The cleavable transcription factor, Fas-d-S1-TA, was cloned into a low-copy plasmid under the control of the TEF promoter, while caspases were cloned into separate plasmids under the control of promoters of varying strengths, including CYC1, ADH, and TEF. The plasmids were designed to allow for the co-expression of multiple genes in a single yeast cell, with each gene under the control of its own promoter and separated by transcriptional terminators. The caspases were cloned with N-terminal HA or C-terminal FLAG epitope tags to facilitate detection by immunoblotting. The plasmids were verified by sequencing and tested for their ability to activate reporter genes when co-expressed with the cleavable transcription factor. These plasmids provide a modular toolkit for the reconstitution of proteolytic networks in yeast.

### Describe Plasmids for Expression of Upstream Activators of Caspases and lacZ Reporter Gene

Plasmids for the expression of upstream activators of caspases and the lacZ reporter gene were constructed using the p426 series of yeast expression vectors, which contain a 2µ origin for high-copy replication and the URA3 selectable marker. The upstream activators, including FADD, ASC, RAIDD, and Apaf-1, were cloned into these plasmids under the control of the strong TEF promoter to ensure high-level expression. The lacZ reporter gene was placed under the control of two LexA operator sequences to ensure sensitivity and specificity. These plasmids were co-transformed with plasmids expressing procaspases and cleavable reporters to test for reporter activation. The plasmids were verified by sequencing and tested for their ability to activate the lacZ reporter in the presence of their cognate procaspase. These plasmids provide a robust and scalable system for the high-throughput screening of compounds and cDNAs that modulate caspase activation.

### Describe Yeast Expression Plasmids for Functional Screening of cDNA Libraries

Yeast expression plasmids for the functional screening of cDNA libraries were constructed using the p424 series of vectors, which contain the TRP1 selectable marker and a 2µ origin for high-copy replication. The cDNA libraries were cloned into these plasmids under the control of the GAL1 or ADH promoter to ensure strong expression. The libraries were constructed from human tissues including liver, HeLa cells, HepG2 cells, and placenta, using oligo(dT)-primed or random hexamer-primed cDNA synthesis. The plasmids were transformed into yeast strains containing cleavable reporter systems, and transformants were selected on tryptophan-deficient media. Positive clones were identified by growth on leucine-deficient medium and blue color development on X-gal plates. The plasmids from positive clones were recovered and sequenced to identify the encoded cDNAs. These plasmids provide a powerful tool for the functional discovery of novel regulators of proteolytic networks.

### Describe Yeast Transformants

Yeast transformants are generated by introducing plasmids encoding caspases, activators, and cleavable reporters into the Saccharomyces cerevisiae strain EGY48 or EGY191 using lithium acetate-mediated transformation. These strains are chosen for their stable integration of LexA operator-controlled reporter genes, including LEU2 and lacZ, which provide a sensitive and quantifiable readout of protease activity. Transformants are selected on synthetic dropout media lacking leucine or histidine, depending on the plasmid marker, and are screened for reporter activation by growth on selective media and color development on X-gal plates. Multiple independent transformants are analyzed to ensure reproducibility, and plasmids are recovered from positive clones for sequencing and re-testing. These transformants serve as the foundation for all downstream screening and validation experiments.
