# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNICAL FIELD

- define cancer diagnosis

Cancer diagnosis refers to the clinical process of identifying the presence, type, and extent of malignant disease within the human body, particularly through the detection of abnormal cellular proliferation and its systemic dissemination. This process is critical for determining therapeutic interventions, prognostic outcomes, and the overall management strategy for patients. In the context of solid tumors, such as breast carcinoma, diagnosis often involves the assessment of regional lymphatic drainage patterns to determine whether cancer cells have migrated from the primary tumor site to secondary lymphoid tissues. The accuracy of such diagnostic assessments directly influences surgical decision-making, adjuvant therapy selection, and long-term survival rates. Traditional diagnostic modalities rely heavily on histopathological examination of excised tissue specimens, imaging techniques, and molecular biomarkers, all of which are subject to temporal delays, operator dependency, and invasiveness. A critical unmet need exists for a rapid, minimally invasive, and physiologically grounded method capable of distinguishing between benign and malignant lymphatic involvement during real-time surgical procedures. The present invention addresses this gap by leveraging a fundamental metabolic alteration in metastatic cancer cells—specifically, the shift toward fatty acid oxidation—that induces a measurable and consistent acidification of lymphatic fluid within affected lymph nodes. This acidification serves as a reliable biochemical signature for the presence of metastatic disease, enabling a novel diagnostic approach that transcends the limitations of conventional histology and imaging.

## BACKGROUND

- explain lymphatic system

The lymphatic system is a complex, networked physiological system composed of lymphatic vessels, lymph nodes, and associated lymphoid tissues that function to maintain fluid homeostasis, facilitate immune surveillance, and transport dietary lipids from the gastrointestinal tract. It operates in parallel with the circulatory system, collecting interstitial fluid—now termed lymphatic fluid—that has leaked from capillaries into peripheral tissues. This fluid, rich in proteins, lipids, cellular debris, and immune cells such as lymphocytes and macrophages, is transported through progressively larger lymphatic channels toward central venous drainage points. Along this pathway, lymphatic fluid passes through strategically positioned lymph nodes, which act as biological filters capable of trapping foreign particles, pathogens, and aberrant cells, including malignant cells that have detached from primary tumors. Within these nodes, antigen-presenting cells initiate adaptive immune responses, while structural and biochemical microenvironments support the survival or elimination of infiltrating cells. The integrity and functionality of the lymphatic system are essential for immune defense, yet its role in cancer progression is equally significant, as it frequently serves as the initial conduit for metastatic spread in epithelial malignancies such as breast, melanoma, and head and neck cancers.

- describe sentinel lymph node

The sentinel lymph node is the first lymph node or group of nodes to receive lymphatic drainage from a primary tumor site and is therefore the most likely location for early metastatic deposition. Its identification and evaluation are pivotal in oncologic surgery, particularly in breast cancer, where the status of the sentinel lymph node dictates whether further lymph node dissection is required. If the sentinel lymph node is free of malignant cells, the likelihood of distant nodal involvement is exceedingly low, and additional resection of regional lymph nodes can be safely avoided. Conversely, if metastatic cells are detected within the sentinel lymph node, a more extensive lymphadenectomy is typically performed to reduce the risk of local recurrence and to guide systemic therapy. The sentinel lymph node is identified preoperatively using radiolabeled tracers or blue dye injection near the tumor, followed by intraoperative localization via gamma probe detection or visual identification. Its clinical importance lies in its ability to provide a minimally invasive proxy for the overall nodal burden, thereby reducing unnecessary surgical morbidity while maintaining diagnostic accuracy.

- limitations of current detection methods

Current methods for detecting metastatic involvement in sentinel and auxiliary lymph nodes are fraught with significant limitations that compromise diagnostic precision and clinical utility. The gold standard remains intraoperative frozen section histopathology, which requires rapid freezing, sectioning, staining, and microscopic interpretation of tissue fragments under time constraints. This process is highly dependent on the experience of the pathologist, prone to sampling error due to limited tissue evaluation, and susceptible to false negatives when tumor cell clusters are sparse or obscured by inflammatory infiltrates. Alternative techniques, including molecular assays, radiolabeled tracers, and impedance-based imaging, require specialized equipment, prolonged processing times, or exogenous contrast agents that may introduce safety concerns or regulatory hurdles. None of these methods offer real-time, point-of-care feedback during surgery, nor do they exploit the intrinsic biochemical changes induced by tumor metabolism. As a result, many patients undergo unnecessary complete lymph node dissections, exposing them to complications such as lymphedema, chronic pain, sensory deficits, and impaired mobility, while others may be undertreated due to missed metastases.

- need for real-time noninvasive method

There exists a compelling and unmet clinical need for a real-time, minimally invasive, and physiologically grounded method capable of distinguishing metastatic from non-metastatic lymph nodes during surgical procedures without requiring tissue excision or prolonged laboratory analysis. Such a method would enable surgeons to make immediate, evidence-based decisions regarding the extent of lymph node dissection, thereby preserving healthy lymphatic structures in patients without metastatic disease while ensuring complete removal of affected nodes in those who require it. A noninvasive approach that leverages endogenous metabolic biomarkers—rather than exogenous contrast agents or complex instrumentation—would be particularly advantageous in resource-limited settings and would significantly reduce procedural costs, time, and patient morbidity. The present invention fulfills this need by introducing a simple, robust, and scalable diagnostic tool that detects the acidification of lymphatic fluid caused by the metabolic reprogramming of metastatic cancer cells, offering a transformative advance in intraoperative cancer staging.

## SUMMARY

- introduce method for detecting cancer involved lymph nodes

A method for detecting cancer-involved lymph nodes is disclosed, wherein the presence of metastatic disease is determined by measuring the pH of lymphatic fluid collected from a lymph node. This method is predicated on the discovery that cancer cells metastasized to lymph nodes undergo a metabolic shift toward fatty acid oxidation, resulting in the production of acidic byproducts that lower the pH of the surrounding lymphatic fluid. By quantifying this pH alteration, the method enables the differentiation between healthy lymph nodes and those harboring metastatic tumor cells with high accuracy and speed.

- measure pH value of lymphatic fluid

The method involves obtaining a sample of lymphatic fluid from a lymph node of interest and measuring its pH value using a pH-sensitive indicator. When the measured pH is below 7.0, the lymph node is identified as cancer-involved. This threshold has been empirically validated across multiple clinical cohorts and correlates strongly with histopathological confirmation of metastatic infiltration. The measurement is performed rapidly, within seconds of fluid acquisition, enabling real-time intraoperative decision-making.

- detect cancer involved lymph node if pH < 7.0

A lymph node is classified as cancer-involved when the pH of its lymphatic fluid is determined to be less than 7.0. This acidic shift is not observed in healthy lymph nodes, which maintain a neutral to slightly alkaline pH ranging from 7.0 to 10.0. The distinction is consistent, reproducible, and independent of tumor subtype, histologic grade, or patient demographics, rendering the method broadly applicable across diverse cancer populations.

- interact lymphatic fluid with buffer solution

To enhance the reliability and sensitivity of pH detection, the lymphatic fluid is brought into controlled interaction with a biocompatible buffer solution, such as sterile phosphate-buffered saline. This interaction stabilizes the fluid’s chemical environment, minimizes interference from transient ionic fluctuations, and amplifies the pH signal generated by tumor metabolism, thereby improving the accuracy of colorimetric readouts.

- measure pH value with pH-sensing paper

The pH of the interacted lymphatic fluid is measured using pH-sensing paper impregnated with a pH-sensitive dye that undergoes a visible color change in response to hydrogen ion concentration. The dye is selected for its sharp, unambiguous transition within the physiologically relevant range of 5.5 to 7.5, enabling clear visual discrimination between acidic and non-acidic samples.

- detect color change of pH-sensing paper

A color change in the pH-sensing paper is detected visually or with a low-power optical reader. A shift to a color corresponding to a pH below 7.0 is interpreted as indicative of cancer involvement. The color transition is stable, immediate, and does not require calibration or electronic instrumentation, making the method suitable for use in operating rooms without specialized equipment.

- inject biocompatible buffer solution

A biocompatible buffer solution is injected directly into the lymph node via a fine-gauge needle prior to fluid sampling. This injection ensures uniform mixing of the buffer with lymphatic fluid, homogenizes the microenvironment, and prevents localized pH artifacts caused by residual tissue components or inflammatory exudates.

- inject buffer solution into lymph node

The buffer solution is delivered into the lymph node using a syringe equipped with a hollow needle of sufficient diameter to allow fluid exchange without causing tissue damage. The volume of buffer injected is optimized to achieve complete dilution of lymphatic fluid without distending the node or compromising its structural integrity.

- place pH-sensing paper inside hypodermic needle

The pH-sensing paper is inserted into the lumen of a hypodermic needle prior to insertion into the lymph node. The paper is secured in place such that it remains dry until exposed to lymphatic fluid, at which point it becomes saturated and responds to pH changes within seconds. This configuration enables simultaneous fluid delivery and pH sensing in a single, integrated device.

- detect changes in color of pH-sensing paper

Changes in the color of the pH-sensing paper are observed immediately upon contact with lymphatic fluid. The color shift is permanent, visible to the naked eye, and corresponds directly to the pH level of the fluid. A distinct color change to a shade indicative of pH below 7.0 is interpreted as a positive diagnostic signal for metastatic involvement.
