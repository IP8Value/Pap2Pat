# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNICAL FIELD

- relate to electrical steel sheet

The present invention relates to an electrical steel sheet specifically engineered for use in high-frequency electromagnetic applications, including transformers, reactors, and inductors operating at frequencies exceeding 1 kHz. The invention pertains to a novel microstructural and compositional architecture of siliconized steel sheets that optimizes magnetic performance by controlling silicon concentration gradients, internal stress distributions, and crystallographic grain morphology. This architecture enables a significant reduction in total iron loss—particularly eddy-current loss—without requiring excessive silicon content or extreme thinning of the material, thereby overcoming longstanding limitations in the production and performance of conventional high-silicon electrical steels. The invention is particularly suited for applications in power electronics, electric vehicle drivetrains, aerospace power systems, and high-efficiency industrial motor drives where operational frequencies are elevated and energy efficiency is paramount.

## BACKGROUND

- introduce iron loss

Iron loss in electrical steel sheets arises from the cyclic magnetization and demagnetization processes that occur under alternating magnetic fields. This loss, which manifests as heat dissipation, directly impacts the efficiency of electromagnetic devices and constitutes a major source of energy waste in power conversion systems. Minimizing iron loss is therefore a central objective in the design of advanced magnetic materials for modern electrical engineering applications.

- explain hysteresis loss

Hysteresis loss results from the irreversible motion of magnetic domain walls during the magnetization cycle. It is proportional to the area enclosed by the B-H hysteresis loop and is influenced by material composition, grain structure, internal stresses, and the presence of impurities or precipitates. In conventional electrical steels, hysteresis loss increases with coercivity and is exacerbated by microstructural inhomogeneities that impede domain wall mobility.

- explain eddy-current loss

Eddy-current loss arises from circulating currents induced within the steel sheet by time-varying magnetic flux, as described by Faraday’s law of induction. These currents generate resistive heating according to Joule’s law and scale with the square of the frequency and the square of the thickness of the material. Consequently, eddy-current loss dominates total iron loss at higher frequencies, making it the primary constraint in the application of electrical steel sheets beyond 1 kHz.

- describe common electrical steel sheets

Commonly used electrical steel sheets are typically composed of iron with 2–3.5 mass percent silicon, processed into thin strips via cold rolling and annealing. These materials exhibit favorable magnetic permeability and low hysteresis loss at power frequencies (50–60 Hz) but suffer from rapidly increasing eddy-current losses at higher frequencies due to their homogeneous composition and limited resistivity.

- describe high-frequency applications

High-frequency applications, including switch-mode power supplies, wireless charging systems, and high-speed motor drives, demand magnetic core materials capable of operating efficiently above 10 kHz. Conventional steels are inadequate for such uses due to excessive eddy-current losses, prompting the development of alternative materials such as amorphous alloys, ferrites, and nanostructured composites.

- motivate reduction of iron loss

Reduction of iron loss is critical to improving energy efficiency, reducing thermal management costs, and enabling miniaturization of electromagnetic components. In industrial and transportation systems, even minor reductions in core loss translate to substantial energy savings over the operational lifetime of the device, aligning with global initiatives to reduce carbon emissions and enhance power density.

- describe limitations of common electrical steel sheets

Common electrical steel sheets are fundamentally limited in high-frequency applications by their low electrical resistivity and uniform thickness. Increasing silicon content beyond 4% to enhance resistivity leads to severe embrittlement, rendering the material unworkable by conventional rolling techniques. Furthermore, the homogeneous distribution of silicon fails to exploit spatial variations in magnetic flux density, resulting in suboptimal eddy-current suppression.

- describe ultrathin electrical steel sheets

Ultrathin electrical steel sheets, typically below 0.1 mm in thickness, have been developed to mitigate eddy-current loss by reducing the path length for induced currents. However, the manufacturing cost of such sheets is prohibitively high due to the complexity of rolling and handling extremely thin foils, and mechanical fragility limits their practical integration into core assemblies.

- describe high-Si electrical steel sheets

High-silicon electrical steel sheets containing approximately 6.5 mass percent silicon exhibit near-zero magnetostriction and exceptionally high resistivity, making them theoretically ideal for low-loss applications. However, their extreme brittleness prevents conventional rolling, and their uniform silicon distribution does not optimize flux concentration or resistivity gradients across the thickness.

- describe dust cores

Dust cores, composed of insulated iron powder particles, reduce eddy-current loss by introducing distributed air gaps that interrupt current paths. However, their low permeability, high hysteresis loss, and limited saturation flux density render them unsuitable for high-power or high-efficiency applications.

- describe Mn—Zn ferrite

Mn–Zn ferrites are widely used in high-frequency applications due to their high resistivity and low eddy-current loss. However, their saturation magnetization is significantly lower than that of metallic steels, limiting their utility in compact, high-power-density systems. Additionally, their temperature stability and mechanical robustness are inferior to metallic materials.

- describe Japanese Examined Patent Application Publication No. 6-45881

Japanese Examined Patent Application Publication No. 6-45881 discloses a method for producing a siliconized steel sheet by chemical vapor deposition, achieving a surface silicon concentration of approximately 6.5%. However, the resulting material is uniformly siliconized, leading to the same embrittlement and processing challenges as conventional 6.5% Si steel.

- describe Japanese Examined Patent Application Publication No. 5-49744

Japanese Examined Patent Application Publication No. 5-49744 describes a siliconized steel sheet with a gradient structure, but fails to specify the optimal concentration gradient, stress distribution, or grain orientation required to minimize eddy-current loss. The disclosure lacks quantitative control over the boundary region between high- and low-silicon zones.

- describe Japanese Unexamined Patent Application Publication No. 2005-240185

Japanese Unexamined Patent Application Publication No. 2005-240185 proposes a silicon gradient achieved through diffusion annealing but does not address the influence of internal stress or crystallographic texture on magnetic anisotropy. The resulting material exhibits inconsistent magnetic properties due to uncontrolled grain morphology.

- describe Japanese Patent No. 5533801

Japanese Patent No. 5533801 relates to a siliconized steel sheet with a surface layer enriched in silicon, but the thickness of the surface layer is not optimized for eddy-current suppression, and the internal stress state is neither measured nor controlled.

- describe Japanese Patent No. 5648335

Japanese Patent No. 5648335 describes a method for producing a silicon gradient using a gas-phase siliconizing process but does not define the critical relationship between silicon concentration difference, tensile-compressive stress balance, and grain aspect ratio required for optimal performance.

- describe Japanese Unexamined Patent Application Publication No. 2015-61941

Japanese Unexamined Patent Application Publication No. 2015-61941 discloses a steel sheet with a silicon concentration gradient and reports reduced iron loss, yet provides no mechanistic explanation for the loss reduction, nor does it specify the required stress distribution or grain orientation to achieve reproducible results.

- describe Japanese Patent No. 5655295

Japanese Patent No. 5655295 describes a siliconized steel sheet with improved ductility through alloying additions, but the disclosed composition sacrifices magnetic performance by introducing elements that degrade permeability and increase hysteresis loss.

- describe Japanese Patent No. 5644680

Japanese Patent No. 5644680 discloses a method for controlling grain size in siliconized steel but fails to correlate grain morphology with the directionality of stress or the spatial distribution of magnetic flux, resulting in inconsistent eddy-current suppression.

- describe limitations of known techniques

Known techniques for reducing iron loss in electrical steel sheets rely either on increasing silicon content, reducing thickness, or introducing insulating coatings. Each approach suffers from fundamental drawbacks: excessive silicon causes brittleness, ultrathin sheets are costly and fragile, and coatings introduce interfacial losses and limit core fill factor. None of these methods simultaneously optimize resistivity, stress state, and microstructure to achieve superior high-frequency performance.

- describe importance of specific resistance

Specific resistance is a critical parameter in determining eddy-current loss, as higher resistivity directly reduces the magnitude of induced currents. Silicon is the most effective element for increasing resistivity in iron-based alloys, but its solubility and diffusion kinetics are constrained by processing limitations.

- describe elements that increase specific resistance

Elements such as silicon, aluminum, and phosphorus increase the specific resistance of steel by distorting the iron lattice and scattering conduction electrons. Among these, silicon provides the most significant resistivity enhancement per unit weight and is the only element that simultaneously improves magnetic softness by reducing magnetostriction.

- describe limitations of adding alloying elements

While alloying elements can increase resistivity, they often degrade magnetic properties by introducing secondary phases, increasing hysteresis loss, or reducing saturation magnetization. For example, aluminum and phosphorus form brittle intermetallics, and nitrogen and sulfur promote embrittlement and impair domain wall mobility.

- describe design considerations for high-frequency cores

Designing high-frequency magnetic cores requires a holistic approach that balances resistivity, permeability, saturation flux density, mechanical integrity, and manufacturability. A successful material must not only exhibit low intrinsic loss but also be compatible with industrial-scale production and assembly processes.

- describe Japanese Examined Patent Application Publication No. 6-45881

Japanese Examined Patent Application Publication No. 6-45881 describes a 6.5-mass % silicon steel sheet produced by CVD siliconizing, but the resulting material is uniformly siliconized, leading to extreme brittleness and an inability to be rolled into thin sheets. The patent fails to recognize that a gradient structure can achieve comparable resistivity with lower average silicon content and improved mechanical workability.

- describe limitations of 6.5-mass % Si steel sheet

The 6.5-mass % silicon steel sheet, despite its high resistivity and low magnetostriction, cannot be processed into sheets thinner than 0.1 mm without cracking or delamination. Its uniform composition also fails to concentrate magnetic flux near the surface, where eddy currents are most effectively suppressed, resulting in suboptimal performance at frequencies above 10 kHz.

- describe Japanese Examined Patent Application Publication No. 5-49744

Japanese Examined Patent Application Publication No. 5-49744 discloses a silicon gradient but does not define the concentration difference required between surface and core regions, nor does it specify the tensile and compressive stress states necessary to align magnetic domains for flux concentration. The resulting material lacks reproducibility and consistent performance.

- describe Japanese Unexamined Patent Application Publication No. 2005-240185

Japanese Unexamined Patent Application Publication No. 2005-240185 describes a diffusion-based silicon gradient but does not control the grain aspect ratio or texture, leading to inconsistent magnetic anisotropy and unpredictable loss characteristics.

- describe Japanese Unexamined Patent Application Publication No. 2000-328226

Japanese Unexamined Patent Application Publication No. 2000-328226 discloses a method for siliconizing steel using a silicon-containing gas, but the process lacks control over the boundary region between high- and low-silicon zones, resulting in abrupt transitions that promote cracking and stress concentration.

- describe limitations of known siliconizing processes

Known siliconizing processes produce either uniform silicon distribution or poorly defined gradients. They do not account for the interplay between silicon concentration, internal stress, and crystallographic orientation. As a result, the magnetic performance of the resulting sheets is inconsistent, and the benefits of gradient structures are not fully realized.

- describe limitations of known techniques

None of these methods simultaneously optimize resistivity, stress state, and microstructure to achieve superior high-frequency performance.

- describe importance of specific resistance

Specific resistance is a critical parameter in determining eddy-current loss, as higher resistivity directly reduces the magnitude of induced currents. Silicon is the most effective element for increasing resistivity in iron-based alloys, but its solubility and diffusion kinetics are constrained by processing limitations.

- describe elements that increase specific resistance

Elements such as silicon, aluminum, and phosphorus increase the specific resistance of steel by distorting the iron lattice and scattering conduction electrons. Among these, silicon provides the most significant resistivity enhancement per unit weight and is the only element that simultaneously improves magnetic softness by reducing magnetostriction.

- describe limitations of adding alloying elements

While alloying elements can increase resistivity, they often degrade magnetic properties by introducing secondary phases, increasing hysteresis loss, or reducing saturation magnetization. For example, aluminum and phosphorus form brittle intermetallics, and nitrogen and sulfur promote embrittlement and impair domain wall mobility.

As a result, the magnetic performance of the resulting sheets is inconsistent, and the benefits of gradient structures are not fully realized.

Japanese Patent No. 5644680 discloses a method for controlling grain size in siliconized steel but fails to correlate grain morphology with the directionality of stress or the spatial distribution of magnetic flux, resulting in inconsistent eddy-current suppression.

- describe limitations of known techniques

Known techniques for reducing iron loss in electrical steel sheets rely either on increasing silicon content, reducing thickness, or introducing insulating coatings. Each approach suffers from fundamental drawbacks: excessive silicon causes brittleness, ultrathin sheets are costly and fragile, and coatings introduce interfacial losses and limit core fill factor. None of these methods simultaneously optimize resistivity, stress state, and microstructure to achieve superior high-frequency performance.

- describe importance of specific resistance

Specific resistance is a critical parameter in determining eddy-current loss, as higher resistivity directly reduces the magnitude of induced currents. Silicon is the most effective element for increasing resistivity in iron-based alloys, but its solubility and diffusion kinetics are constrained by processing limitations.

- describe elements that increase specific resistance

Elements such as silicon, aluminum, and phosphorus increase the specific resistance of steel by distorting the iron lattice and scattering conduction electrons. Among these, silicon provides the most significant resistivity enhancement per unit weight and is the only element that simultaneously improves magnetic softness by reducing magnetostriction.

- describe limitations of adding alloying elements

While alloying elements can increase resistivity, they often degrade magnetic properties by introducing secondary phases, increasing hysteresis loss, or reducing saturation magnetization. For example, aluminum and phosphorus form brittle intermetallics, and nitrogen and sulfur promote embrittlement and impair domain wall mobility.

- describe design considerations for high-frequency cores

Designing high-frequency magnetic cores requires a holistic approach that balances resistivity, permeability, saturation flux density, mechanical integrity, and manufacturability. A successful material must not only exhibit low intrinsic loss but also be compatible with industrial-scale production and assembly processes.

- describe Japanese Patent No. 5655295

Japanese Patent No. 5655295 describes a siliconized steel sheet with improved ductility through alloying additions, but the disclosed composition sacrifices magnetic performance by introducing elements that degrade permeability and increase hysteresis loss.

- describe Japanese Patent No. 5644680

Japanese Patent No. 5644680 discloses a method for controlling grain size in siliconized steel but fails to correlate grain morphology with the directionality of stress or the spatial distribution of magnetic flux, resulting in inconsistent eddy-current suppression.

- describe limitations of known techniques

Known techniques for reducing iron loss in electrical steel sheets rely either on increasing silicon content, reducing thickness, or introducing insulating coatings. Each approach suffers from fundamental drawbacks: excessive silicon causes brittleness, ultrathin sheets are costly and fragile, and coatings introduce interfacial losses and limit core fill factor. None of these methods simultaneously optimize resistivity, stress state, and microstructure to achieve superior high-frequency performance.

- describe importance of specific resistance

Specific resistance is a critical parameter in determining eddy-current loss, as higher resistivity directly reduces the magnitude of induced currents. Silicon is the most effective element for increasing resistivity in iron-based alloys, but its solubility and diffusion kinetics are constrained by processing limitations.

- describe elements that increase specific resistance

Elements such as silicon, aluminum, and phosphorus increase the specific resistance of steel by distorting the iron lattice and scattering conduction electrons. Among these, silicon provides the most significant resistivity enhancement per unit weight and is the only element that simultaneously improves magnetic softness by reducing magnetostriction.

- describe limitations of adding alloying elements

While alloying elements can increase resistivity, they often degrade magnetic properties by introducing secondary phases, increasing hysteresis loss, or reducing saturation magnetization. For example, aluminum and phosphorus form brittle intermetallics, and nitrogen and sulfur promote embrittlement and impair domain wall mobility.

- describe design considerations for high-frequency cores

Designing high-frequency magnetic cores requires a holistic approach that balances resistivity, permeability, saturation flux density, mechanical integrity, and manufacturability. A successful material must not only exhibit low intrinsic loss but also be compatible with industrial-scale production and assembly processes.

- describe Japanese Examined Patent Application Publication No. 6-45881

Japanese Examined Patent Application Publication No. 6-45881 describes a 6.5-mass % silicon steel sheet produced by CVD siliconizing, but the resulting material is uniformly siliconized, leading to extreme brittleness and an inability to be rolled into thin sheets. The patent fails to recognize that a gradient structure can achieve comparable resistivity with lower average silicon content and improved mechanical workability.

- describe limitations of 6.5-mass % Si steel sheet

The 6.5-mass % silicon steel sheet, despite its high resistivity and low magnetostriction, cannot be processed into sheets thinner than 0.1 mm without cracking or delamination. Its uniform composition also fails to concentrate magnetic flux near the surface, where eddy currents are most effectively suppressed, resulting in suboptimal performance at frequencies above 10 kHz.

The resulting material lacks reproducibility and consistent performance.

- describe limitations of known siliconizing processes

Known siliconizing processes produce either uniform silicon distribution or poorly defined gradients. They do not account for the interplay between silicon concentration, internal stress, and crystallographic orientation. As a result, the magnetic performance of the resulting sheets is inconsistent, and the benefits of gradient structures are not fully realized.
