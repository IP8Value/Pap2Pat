# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

- introduce blood brain barrier  
The blood-brain barrier is a highly specialized physiological structure that selectively regulates the passage of molecules, ions, and cells between the systemic circulation and the central nervous system. Composed of a continuous monolayer of brain microvascular endothelial cells connected by tight junctions, supported by pericytes embedded in the basement membrane and surrounded by astrocytic end-feet, the barrier ensures the maintenance of a stable neural microenvironment essential for proper neuronal function. It prevents the uncontrolled entry of pathogens, toxins, and large or hydrophilic substances while permitting the selective transport of nutrients, gases, and metabolic waste products through specialized transporters and receptor-mediated mechanisms. The integrity of this barrier is dynamically maintained through reciprocal signaling between endothelial cells, pericytes, astrocytes, microglia, oligodendrocytes, and neurons, collectively forming the neurovascular unit. Disruption of this unit is implicated in a wide spectrum of neurological disorders, including stroke, Alzheimer’s disease, multiple sclerosis, epilepsy, and brain tumors, underscoring the critical importance of preserving its functional architecture.

- limitations of current models  
Existing in vitro models of the blood-brain barrier have historically relied on two-dimensional monolayer cultures of endothelial cells grown on porous membranes, often lacking the full complement of cellular and extracellular components necessary to recapitulate the complexity of the native neurovascular unit. Many of these systems utilize immortalized cell lines that exhibit altered gene expression profiles, reduced tight junction formation, and diminished transport protein activity compared to primary human cells. Even advanced three-dimensional models frequently omit key cell types such as microglia, oligodendrocytes, and electrically active neurons, thereby failing to capture the bidirectional signaling that governs barrier maturation, immune surveillance, and metabolic coupling. Furthermore, these models often do not replicate the mechanical forces of physiological blood flow, lack appropriate extracellular matrix composition, or fail to maintain long-term viability and functional stability beyond a few days, limiting their utility for chronic toxicity screening, drug absorption studies, or disease modeling over extended timeframes.

- need for improved in vitro systems  
There exists a critical and unmet need for a physiologically accurate, reproducible, and scalable in vitro model of the human blood-brain barrier that integrates all major cell types of the neurovascular unit in a three-dimensional architecture that mimics in vivo tissue organization and functional dynamics. Such a system must support the formation of mature tight and adherens junctions, express functional efflux and nutrient transporters, respond appropriately to pathophysiological stimuli such as hypoxia or inflammatory mediators, and permit long-term culture without loss of phenotype. A model capable of incorporating patient-derived cells would further enable personalized medicine applications, including the prediction of individual drug responses, the assessment of genetic mutations affecting barrier integrity, and the evaluation of neurotoxic compounds in a human-relevant context. The development of such a system would significantly enhance the predictive power of preclinical screening, reduce the high attrition rate of central nervous system therapeutics, and accelerate the discovery of effective treatments for neurological diseases.

## SUMMARY

- provide in vitro blood brain barrier model  
An in vitro blood-brain barrier model is provided that comprises a three-dimensional multicellular structure wherein human brain microvascular endothelial cells form a continuous or semi-continuous outer layer surrounding a core of parenchymal brain cells, including astrocytes, microglia, oligodendrocytes, and electrically active neurons, thereby recapitulating the spatial organization and functional interplay of the human neurovascular unit. The model exhibits structural and molecular characteristics of a functional blood-brain barrier, including the expression of tight junction proteins such as ZO-1 and claudin-5, adherens junction proteins such as VE-cadherin and β-catenin, and transport proteins including P-glycoprotein and glucose transporter 1. The model demonstrates selective permeability consistent with the physiological barrier, restricting the passage of charged or hydrophilic molecules while permitting the translocation of lipophilic compounds, and responds dynamically to pharmacological or pathological perturbations such as histamine-induced barrier opening or hypoxia-induced junctional disruption.

- model includes endothelial cell layer and brain tissue layer  
The model comprises a distinct endothelial cell layer that encases a central brain tissue layer composed of astrocytes, microglia, oligodendrocytes, and neurons, arranged in a manner that mirrors the in vivo architecture of the cerebral cortex. The endothelial cells are positioned at the periphery of the structure, forming a contiguous or near-contiguous boundary that interfaces with an external medium, while the parenchymal cells are sequestered internally, establishing a physiologically relevant compartmentalization. This spatial arrangement enables the endothelial layer to function as a selective barrier, while the internal parenchymal cells provide metabolic, trophic, and signaling support necessary for barrier maturation and maintenance. The model preserves the cellular heterogeneity and cell-cell communication networks observed in human brain tissue, allowing for the study of intercellular crosstalk between vascular and neural compartments under controlled conditions.

- optional porous membrane between layers  
In certain embodiments, the model may be supported by a porous membrane positioned between the endothelial cell layer and the brain tissue layer, facilitating the separation of compartments while permitting the diffusion of soluble factors, nutrients, and signaling molecules. The membrane may be composed of materials such as polyethylene terephthalate, polycarbonate, or polydimethylsiloxane, and may be coated with extracellular matrix proteins including collagen, laminin, or fibronectin to enhance cell adhesion, polarization, and barrier function. The presence of the membrane allows for the independent manipulation of the luminal and abluminal environments, enabling studies of transcellular transport, directional signaling, and the effects of apical versus basolateral compound exposure.

- microfluidic device comprising the model  
The in vitro blood-brain barrier model may be integrated into a microfluidic device designed to simulate physiological shear stress, continuous perfusion, and controlled gradients of oxygen, nutrients, and pharmacological agents. The microfluidic system comprises one or more channels lined with the endothelial cell layer, connected to reservoirs that allow for the controlled delivery of media, drugs, or inflammatory mediators. The device may incorporate multiple parallel units to enable high-throughput screening, and may be coupled with real-time imaging, electrical recording, or biosensor technologies to monitor barrier integrity, cellular viability, metabolic activity, and electrophysiological responses. The integration of the model into a microfluidic platform enhances its physiological relevance and enables long-term, dynamic studies under conditions that closely mimic in vivo cerebral circulation.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF EMBODIMENTS

- terminology and definitions  
For the purposes of this disclosure, the term “neurovascular unit” refers to the functional and structural ensemble of brain microvascular endothelial cells, pericytes, astrocytes, microglia, oligodendrocytes, and neurons that collectively regulate blood-brain barrier integrity, cerebral blood flow, metabolic support, and immune surveillance in the central nervous system. The term “organoid” refers to a three-dimensional self-organized multicellular structure derived from primary human cells or stem cells that recapitulates key architectural and functional features of native tissue. The term “functional blood-brain barrier” denotes a structure capable of restricting paracellular permeability to hydrophilic or charged molecules, expressing appropriate transporters and junctional proteins, and responding dynamically to physiological and pathological stimuli. The term “electrically active neurons” refers to neurons capable of generating action potentials and synaptic activity, as confirmed by electrophysiological recording or expression of voltage-gated ion channels and synaptic markers such as MAP2 and synaptophysin.

- in vitro blood brain barrier model with endothelial cell layer  
The in vitro blood-brain barrier model comprises a monolayer or semi-monolayer of human brain microvascular endothelial cells arranged in a three-dimensional configuration, wherein the cells exhibit polarized morphology, express tight junction proteins including ZO-1, claudin-5, and occludin, and form a continuous barrier that limits the passive diffusion of molecules larger than 500 daltons. The endothelial cells are derived from primary human tissue or induced pluripotent stem cells and are cultured under conditions that promote barrier maturation, including the presence of astrocyte-derived factors, pericyte contact, and physiological shear stress. The endothelial layer is capable of expressing functional efflux transporters such as P-glycoprotein and breast cancer resistance protein, and nutrient transporters such as GLUT1, thereby replicating the selective transport properties of the native blood-brain barrier.

- model with neuronal cell layer and porous membrane  
The model further includes a core region composed of human neurons, astrocytes, microglia, and oligodendrocytes, arranged in a three-dimensional architecture that mimics the cellular density and spatial organization of the cerebral cortex. This core is separated from the endothelial layer by a porous membrane that permits the diffusion of small molecules and signaling factors while maintaining physical separation of the vascular and parenchymal compartments. The membrane is coated with extracellular matrix proteins to facilitate cell adhesion and promote the formation of basement membrane-like structures. Neurons within the core are electrically active, as evidenced by the expression of sodium channels, action potential firing, and synaptic marker expression, and are capable of modulating endothelial barrier properties through secreted factors such as sonic hedgehog and angiopoietin-1.

- making the model by depositing cells on a porous membrane  
The model may be fabricated by sequentially depositing human brain microvascular endothelial cells and pericytes onto one side of a porous membrane, followed by the addition of astrocytes, microglia, oligodendrocytes, and neurons onto the opposing side. The cells are allowed to adhere, spread, and self-organize over a period of several days under controlled culture conditions, resulting in the formation of a polarized endothelial barrier on one side and a mature neural parenchyma on the other. The membrane serves as a scaffold that supports the structural integrity of both layers while enabling the exchange of soluble mediators necessary for mutual maturation and functional coordination.

- making the model by adding cells to organoids  
Alternatively, the model may be generated by first forming a multicellular organoid containing astrocytes, microglia, oligodendrocytes, and neurons using a hanging drop or spinner flask method, and subsequently overlaying the organoid with a suspension of endothelial cells and pericytes. The endothelial cells migrate to the surface of the organoid, adhere to its outer layer, and spontaneously form a continuous barrier that encapsulates the neural core. This method recapitulates the natural process of vascularization observed during brain development and results in a structurally and functionally mature neurovascular unit without the need for artificial scaffolds.

- model in a tubular shape to mimic a blood vessel  
In certain embodiments, the model is configured in a tubular geometry to simulate the structure of a cerebral microvessel or arteriole. The tubular structure is formed by embedding endothelial cells and pericytes within a hydrogel matrix that is subsequently molded into a cylindrical shape, with the neural cell types positioned concentrically within the lumen. The tubular model may be integrated into a microfluidic circuit to allow for continuous perfusion, enabling the study of hemodynamic forces, leukocyte adhesion, and transendothelial migration under flow conditions that mimic cerebral circulation.

- cells may be obtained from donors or stem cells  
The cells used in the model may be derived from primary human tissues obtained from surgical resections or post-mortem donations, or may be generated from induced pluripotent stem cells derived from somatic cells of healthy individuals or patients with neurological disorders. The use of patient-derived cells enables the creation of personalized models that reflect individual genetic backgrounds, disease mutations, or drug response profiles. Induced pluripotent stem cells may be differentiated into each of the required cell types using established protocols involving growth factors, small molecules, and extracellular matrix cues, ensuring a scalable and reproducible source of human-relevant cells for model generation.

### 1. Blood, Brain Barrier Models and Methods of Making the Same.

- provide in vitro blood brain barrier model  
The in vitro blood-brain barrier model comprises a three-dimensional, multicellular structure wherein human brain microvascular endothelial cells form a continuous outer layer that encloses a core of parenchymal cells including astrocytes, microglia, oligodendrocytes, and electrically active neurons. The model exhibits structural and functional characteristics of the native human neurovascular unit, including the expression of tight junction proteins, adherens junction proteins, and transmembrane transporters, and demonstrates selective permeability consistent with the physiological barrier.

- model has endothelial cell layer and neuronal cell layer  
The model is organized such that the endothelial cell layer is positioned at the periphery, interfacing with an external fluid environment, while the neuronal cell layer is contained within the interior, surrounded by astrocytes, microglia, and oligodendrocytes. This spatial arrangement enables the endothelial layer to act as a selective barrier, while the internal neuronal population provides trophic and signaling support that enhances barrier maturation and functional stability.

- model has porous membrane between layers  
A porous membrane is optionally interposed between the endothelial cell layer and the neuronal cell layer to facilitate the diffusion of soluble mediators while maintaining physical compartmentalization. The membrane is coated with extracellular matrix proteins to promote cell adhesion and polarization, and may be fabricated from materials such as polyethylene terephthalate, polycarbonate, or polydimethylsiloxane.

- endothelial cells, astrocytes, and pericytes in the model  
The model includes human brain microvascular endothelial cells, human astrocytes, and human pericytes as essential components, each contributing to barrier integrity through direct cell-cell contact and paracrine signaling. Endothelial cells form the primary barrier, pericytes regulate capillary tone and basement membrane deposition, and astrocytes induce tight junction formation and modulate transporter expression.

- neuronal cells are electrically active  
The neuronal cells within the model are electrically active, exhibiting spontaneous action potentials, synaptic activity, and expression of neuronal markers such as βIII-tubulin, MAP2, and synaptophysin. Their presence enhances barrier function through the secretion of neurovascular coupling factors and contributes to the model’s utility in studying neurotoxicity and seizure-related barrier disruption.

- making the model by depositing cells on a porous membrane  
Cells are deposited in sequential layers on a porous membrane, with endothelial cells and pericytes plated on one side and astrocytes, microglia, oligodendrocytes, and neurons on the opposite side. The construct is cultured under conditions that promote differentiation, polarization, and junctional maturation over a period of seven to twenty-one days.

- making the model by adding cells to organoids  
A neural organoid is first formed by aggregating astrocytes, microglia, oligodendrocytes, and neurons in a hanging drop or low-adhesion culture system. Endothelial cells and pericytes are then introduced to the surface of the organoid, where they migrate, adhere, and form a continuous barrier around the neural core.

- model in a tubular shape to mimic a blood vessel  
The model is molded into a tubular configuration using bioprinting or microfluidic casting techniques, with endothelial cells lining the lumen and parenchymal cells arranged concentrically in the surrounding matrix. The tubular geometry permits perfusion and simulates the hemodynamic environment of cerebral microvessels.

- endothelial cells may be perfused into the lumen  
Endothelial cells may be introduced into a pre-formed hollow channel within a hydrogel scaffold via perfusion, allowing for uniform cell coverage and lumen formation under controlled flow conditions.

- sacrificial hydrogel for endothelial cells  
A sacrificial hydrogel, such as gelatin or alginate, is used to create a temporary lumen within a supporting matrix. After cell seeding and matrix stabilization, the sacrificial component is dissolved, leaving behind a hollow channel lined with endothelial cells.

- cells may be obtained from donors or stem cells  
Cells may be sourced from primary human tissues or generated from induced pluripotent stem cells derived from patient-specific somatic cells, enabling the creation of genetically accurate, personalized neurovascular models.

- cells may be bioprinted in a carrier  
Cells are suspended in a biocompatible hydrogel carrier, such as fibrin, collagen, or Matrigel, and deposited using extrusion-based or laser-assisted bioprinting systems to create spatially defined architectures.

- hydrogel carrier for cells  
The hydrogel carrier provides mechanical support, retains cell viability, and facilitates nutrient diffusion during culture. It may be crosslinked using enzymatic, ionic, or photochemical methods to stabilize the structure.

- sacrificial hydrogel for forming a hollow space  
A sacrificial hydrogel is printed in a defined geometry and subsequently removed to generate a hollow lumen, which is then seeded with endothelial cells to form a vascular channel.

- porous membrane may be coated with collagen or laminin  
The porous membrane is functionalized with extracellular matrix proteins such as collagen type IV or laminin to enhance endothelial cell adhesion, promote barrier maturation, and support the formation of basement membrane-like structures.

- microfluidic device comprising the model  
The model is integrated into a microfluidic chip comprising perfusion channels, culture chambers, and sensor arrays, enabling real-time monitoring of barrier integrity, transendothelial transport, and cellular responses under controlled flow and environmental conditions.

### 2. Methods of Use.

- use the model for compound or treatment screening  
The model is employed to screen libraries of small molecules, biologics, or nanoparticle formulations for their ability to cross the blood-brain barrier, modulate barrier function, or exert neurotoxic or neuroprotective effects.

- testing for efficacy, toxicity, or other metabolic activity  
The model is used to evaluate the efficacy of therapeutic agents in treating neurological disorders, assess their neurotoxic potential, and determine their metabolic fate within the neurovascular unit, including enzymatic conversion, efflux, and cellular uptake.

- detecting penetration of a compound through the endothelial layer  
The penetration of test compounds through the endothelial layer is quantified using fluorescent tracers, radiolabeled molecules, or mass spectrometry-based methods to determine permeability coefficients and transcellular transport rates.

- determining immunological activity  
The model is utilized to assess the recruitment, activation, and transmigration of immune cells across the endothelial barrier in response to inflammatory stimuli, cytokines, or autoimmune triggers.

- testing methods of crossing the blood brain barrier  
The model is employed to evaluate novel delivery strategies such as receptor-mediated transcytosis, cell-penetrating peptides, or focused ultrasound-mediated barrier opening for their ability to enhance drug delivery to the brain parenchyma.

- personalized testing with cells from a subject  
The model is generated using cells derived from a specific human subject, enabling the prediction of individual drug responses, the identification of genetic variants affecting barrier function, and the development of patient-specific therapeutic regimens.

- model with cells having a known genetic mutation  
The model incorporates cells harboring known pathogenic mutations associated with neurological diseases, such as mutations in P-glycoprotein, claudin-5, or ABC transporters, to study the mechanistic impact of these mutations on barrier integrity and drug disposition.

## EXAMPLES

### Example 1

- bioprint vessel model of blood brain barrier  
A microfluidic device was fabricated with a central channel lined with human brain microvascular endothelial cells and pericytes, surrounded by a concentric ring of astrocytes, microglia, oligodendrocytes, and electrically active neurons. The structure was bioprinted using a fibrin-based bioink and crosslinked with thrombin to form a stable, perfusable vessel-like construct.

- print electrically active neuronal cells  
Neural progenitor cells were differentiated into mature neurons prior to bioprinting and were suspended in a hydrogel carrier containing neurotrophic factors. The printed neurons exhibited spontaneous calcium transients and expressed βIII-tubulin and synaptophysin, confirming their functional maturity.

- culture and differentiate printed constructs  
The bioprinted constructs were cultured for up to 21 days under perfusion conditions, during which time tight junctions matured, efflux transporters were upregulated, and neuronal activity persisted, demonstrating long-term viability and functional stability.

### Example 2. Human Cortex Model with Spheroid Culture System

- create 3D spheroid model of blood brain barrier  
A spheroid model was generated by aggregating human brain microvascular endothelial cells, pericytes, astrocytes, microglia, oligodendrocytes, and neurons in a hanging drop culture system, resulting in a self-organized structure with endothelial cells forming an outer shell and parenchymal cells forming an internal core.

- source and expand primary human cells  
Primary human brain microvascular endothelial cells, pericytes, and astrocytes were isolated from surgical resections and expanded in culture. Microglia, oligodendrocytes, and neurons were derived from induced pluripotent stem cells using established differentiation protocols.

- manufacture spheroids using hanging drop method  
Spheroids were formed using a hanging drop technique with a cell ratio of 30% endothelial cells, 15% pericytes, 15% astrocytes, 5% microglia, 15% oligodendrocytes, and 20% neurons, yielding uniform spheroids of 300–500 μm in diameter.

- characterize spheroid viability and structure  
Viability exceeded 90% at day 21, as confirmed by live/dead staining. Confocal imaging revealed spatial organization with endothelial cells at the periphery and neurons in the core, with expression of ZO-1 and claudin-5 at cell-cell interfaces.

- perform immunohistochemistry for cell specific markers  
Immunofluorescence confirmed expression of CD31 in endothelial cells, PDGFRβ in pericytes, GFAP in astrocytes, Iba1 in microglia, MBP in oligodendrocytes, and MAP2 in neurons, demonstrating retention of cell-type identity.

- analyze spheroid results and significance  
The spheroid model demonstrated charge-selective barrier function, restricting permeability to mercury ions while permitting passage of MPTP, validating its utility for neurotoxicity screening and drug permeability assessment.

- discuss applications in drug discovery and neurotoxicity testing  
This model provides a physiologically relevant platform for high-throughput screening of CNS therapeutics, predicting blood-brain barrier penetration, and evaluating neurotoxic side effects prior to clinical trials.

### Example 3: Bioprint Functional Cortical Tissue

- bioprint cortical tissue with neurons  
Neurons derived from induced pluripotent stem cells were suspended in a fibrin hydrogel and bioprinted into a three-dimensional cortical-like structure using a multi-nozzle printer.

- suspend neurons in fibrin hydrogel  
Fibrin hydrogel was prepared with thrombin and calcium chloride to enable rapid gelation and cell encapsulation, preserving neuronal viability and promoting neurite outgrowth.

- print and crosslink structures  
The bioprinted constructs were crosslinked with factor XIII and cultured under perfusion for 14 days, during which time neurons extended processes and formed synaptic networks.

- culture and differentiate printed constructs  
The constructs were maintained in a medium containing BDNF, GDNF, and ascorbic acid, resulting in the maturation of dopaminergic and glutamatergic neuronal subtypes.

- perform viability assays  
Live/dead staining and ATP quantification confirmed sustained viability over 21 days, with no significant cell death observed in any layer.

- analyze Beta III tubulin expression  
Immunohistochemistry revealed robust expression of βIII-tubulin throughout the neuronal layer, confirming neuronal differentiation and maturation.

- bioprint microvessel with all cell types  
A microvessel was printed with endothelial cells and pericytes lining the lumen, surrounded by astrocytes, microglia, oligodendrocytes, and neurons in concentric layers, creating a complete neurovascular unit.

- design and print human neurovascular unit  
The entire neurovascular unit was printed in a single process, with each cell type deposited in its anatomically correct position, resulting in a structurally and functionally integrated model.

- discuss applications in drug discovery and neurotoxicity testing  
This fully integrated model enables the simultaneous assessment of drug permeability, neurotoxicity, glial activation, and vascular dysfunction, providing a comprehensive platform for CNS drug development.

### Example 4. Three-Dimensional Bioprinting of the Human Neurovascular Unit

- design and bioprint NVU constructs  
A computational model of the human neurovascular unit was developed to guide the spatial deposition of six cell types using a multi-material bioprinter, ensuring precise cellular organization.

- utilize primary human cells or iPSC-derived cells  
Both primary human cells and induced pluripotent stem cell-derived cells were used interchangeably, with no significant differences observed in barrier function or cell viability.

- expand cells in 2D culture  
All cell types were expanded in two-dimensional culture prior to bioprinting to ensure sufficient cell numbers and uniform differentiation status.

- bioprint cortical tissue with mature neurons  
Neurons were differentiated for 21 days prior to printing and were confirmed to be electrically active via patch-clamp electrophysiology.

- bioprint capillary NVU and micro-arteriole NVU  
Two distinct geometries were printed: a capillary-like structure (diameter 15 μm) and a micro-arteriole-like structure (diameter 50 μm), both exhibiting appropriate cell layering and barrier properties.

- perform immunohistochemistry for cell specific markers  
Immunostaining confirmed the presence of all six cell types with appropriate marker expression and junctional protein localization.

- analyze viability and structure of printed constructs  
Cell viability remained above 85% for 28 days, and structural integrity was maintained under continuous perfusion, demonstrating long-term stability.

- discuss applications in drug discovery and neurotoxicity testing  
This bioprinted neurovascular unit represents a transformative platform for predicting human-specific drug behavior, modeling neurological diseases, and accelerating the development of safe and effective central nervous system therapeutics.
