## BACKGROUND

- introduce wireless communication system  
Modern wireless communication systems, particularly those compliant with the 3GPP Long-Term Evolution (LTE) standard, are designed to support high-speed packet-switched data transmission with stringent requirements for spectral efficiency, cell throughput, and quality of service. These systems operate over a shared radio frequency spectrum, where multiple user equipment devices simultaneously communicate with a base station, known as an evolved Node B (eNB). The eNB manages the allocation of physical resources—specifically, physical resource blocks (PRBs)—across both time and frequency domains to satisfy the diverse traffic demands of connected users. Each PRB consists of a fixed number of subcarriers over a defined time interval, known as a transmission time interval (TTI), typically one millisecond in duration. The system supports multiple traffic classes, including real-time traffic such as voice streaming and video conferencing, which require low latency and guaranteed bit rates, and non-real-time traffic such as web browsing and file downloads, which prioritize throughput over delay. Efficient utilization of limited transmit power and scarce radio resources is critical to maintaining system capacity, extending battery life of mobile devices, and ensuring equitable service delivery across users located at varying distances from the base station.

- describe downlink scheduler  
The downlink scheduler is a core functional component within the eNB responsible for dynamically allocating PRBs to user equipment devices during each TTI. It operates in two distinct phases: a time domain packet scheduler (TDPS) and a frequency domain packet scheduler (FDPS). The TDPS first identifies a subset of eligible user equipment devices—referred to as the scheduling candidate set—based on metrics such as buffer occupancy, delay constraints, channel quality indicators (CQIs), and historical transmission performance. This phase reduces computational complexity by limiting the number of candidates considered in the subsequent frequency domain allocation. The FDPS then selects specific PRBs from the available pool and assigns them to individual UEs within the candidate set, taking into account the instantaneous channel conditions, modulation and coding schemes (MCS), and power constraints. The scheduler must ensure that each PRB is allocated to only one UE per TTI and that the total transmit power consumed does not exceed the maximum allowable limit imposed by regulatory or hardware constraints. The scheduler also coordinates with other system components, including the hybrid automatic repeat request (HARQ) mechanism, link adaptation module, and quality of service (QoS) manager, to maintain reliable and efficient data transmission.

- explain importance of downlink scheduler  
The downlink scheduler plays a pivotal role in determining the overall performance of the wireless communication system, directly influencing throughput, fairness, latency, and energy efficiency. Without an effective scheduling mechanism, the system risks severe resource imbalance, where a small number of users with favorable channel conditions monopolize the available PRBs, leaving others with poor connectivity or excessive delays. This imbalance disproportionately affects users at the cell edge, where signal attenuation is greatest, leading to degraded service quality and reduced system capacity. Moreover, inefficient power allocation can result in unnecessary energy consumption, which not only increases operational costs but also shortens the battery life of mobile devices. A well-designed scheduler must balance competing objectives: maximizing aggregate system throughput while ensuring fairness among users, minimizing transmit power consumption, and meeting the quality-of-service requirements of time-sensitive applications. The scheduler’s ability to adaptively respond to dynamic channel conditions and heterogeneous traffic demands is therefore fundamental to the scalability and reliability of modern cellular networks.

- describe limitations of downlink scheduler  
Conventional downlink schedulers, such as the round-robin, maximum throughput, and proportional fair algorithms, exhibit significant limitations when applied to mixed traffic environments with constrained transmit power. The round-robin algorithm ensures fairness by cycling through users in a fixed sequence but ignores channel conditions entirely, resulting in suboptimal spectral efficiency and excessive power usage. The maximum throughput algorithm prioritizes users with the highest instantaneous data rates, typically those near the base station with strong channel gains, leading to severe unfairness and underutilization of resources for users at the cell edge. The proportional fair algorithm improves fairness by normalizing data rates by historical averages but still fails to account for the actual transmit power required to achieve a given bit rate. These algorithms treat the number of transmitted bits and the power needed to transmit them as independent variables, neglecting the fundamental relationship between power efficiency and spectral performance. As a result, they often allocate PRBs to users who require disproportionately high transmit power per bit, especially when operating under saturated conditions or in multicell interference environments, thereby limiting overall system capacity and increasing energy consumption.

- introduce round-robin method  
The round-robin scheduling method operates by sequentially assigning PRBs to each user equipment device in a cyclic order, regardless of channel conditions or traffic type. Each UE is guaranteed an equal opportunity to transmit data over successive TTIs, ensuring perfect fairness in terms of access frequency. This method is simple to implement and requires minimal computational overhead, making it suitable for scenarios where fairness is the sole objective and system throughput is not a primary concern. However, because it does not consider the varying channel quality among users, it frequently allocates PRBs to users with poor channel conditions, necessitating higher modulation and coding schemes or increased transmit power to maintain reliable communication. This results in inefficient use of both spectral and power resources, reducing the overall system capacity and increasing energy consumption per transmitted bit.

- describe MT method  
The maximum throughput (MT) scheduling method selects the user equipment device with the highest instantaneous data rate for PRB allocation during each TTI. The data rate is determined based on the channel quality indicator reported by the UE and the corresponding modulation and coding scheme that can be supported under the current channel conditions. By always choosing the user who can transmit the most bits per PRB, the MT algorithm achieves the highest possible aggregate system throughput. However, this approach leads to significant unfairness, as users located near the base station with favorable channel conditions consistently dominate resource allocation. Users at the cell edge, who experience higher path loss and lower signal-to-noise ratios, are rarely scheduled, resulting in poor service quality and potential service denial. Furthermore, the MT method does not consider the transmit power required to achieve the reported data rate, often selecting users whose high data rates come at the cost of excessive power consumption.

- describe PF method  
The proportional fair (PF) scheduling method improves upon the maximum throughput algorithm by incorporating a fairness metric that normalizes the instantaneous data rate by the average data rate experienced by each user over a recent time window. This ensures that users who have historically received less throughput are given higher priority in subsequent scheduling decisions, mitigating the monopolization effect observed in the MT method. The PF algorithm strikes a balance between throughput and fairness, resulting in improved cell-edge performance and more equitable resource distribution compared to MT. However, like MT, the PF method does not explicitly account for the transmit power required to deliver a given number of bits. Consequently, it may still allocate PRBs to users whose channel conditions require disproportionately high power per bit, especially when multiple users report similar normalized rates but differ significantly in their power efficiency.

- explain limitations of PF method  
While the proportional fair algorithm enhances fairness relative to the maximum throughput method, it remains fundamentally limited by its reliance on data rate as the sole determinant of scheduling priority. It does not consider the energy cost associated with achieving a particular bit rate, which becomes critically important in power-constrained environments or when operating near the maximum transmit power limit of the base station. In scenarios where multiple users report comparable normalized data rates, the PF algorithm cannot distinguish between those who require minimal power to transmit a given number of bits and those who require substantially more power due to marginal channel conditions. This oversight leads to inefficient power utilization, increased interference, and suboptimal system capacity, particularly in dense or multicell deployments where power efficiency is paramount. As a result, the PF method, despite its fairness improvements, fails to fully optimize the trade-off between throughput, fairness, and energy consumption.

- illustrate construction of downlink scheduler  
The downlink scheduler is constructed as a hierarchical, two-phase architecture comprising a time domain packet scheduler (TDPS) and a frequency domain packet scheduler (FDPS), interconnected through a traffic classifier and a set of coordinated control modules. The traffic classifier segregates incoming data packets into real-time and non-real-time queues based on their quality-of-service requirements, assigning higher priority to real-time traffic. The TDPS evaluates each UE in the active set using metrics such as buffer occupancy, delay tolerance, and historical transmission performance to generate a scheduling candidate set. This candidate set is then passed to the FDPS, which performs the actual PRB allocation using a scheduling metric that incorporates channel quality, required transmit power, and the number of bits to be transmitted. The FDPS operates in conjunction with the link adaptation module, which determines the appropriate modulation and coding scheme for each PRB-UE pair, and the QoS manager, which enforces delay and throughput constraints. The entire process is executed within each TTI, ensuring real-time responsiveness to dynamic channel conditions and traffic demands.

- describe role of downlink scheduler  
The role of the downlink scheduler is to optimally allocate limited physical resources—namely, physical resource blocks—to multiple user equipment devices in a manner that maximizes system throughput, ensures fairness among users, minimizes transmit power consumption, and satisfies the quality-of-service requirements of diverse traffic types. It acts as the central decision-making entity that translates high-level network objectives into low-level radio resource assignments. By dynamically adapting to changing channel conditions, traffic loads, and power constraints, the scheduler ensures that the available spectrum is used efficiently and equitably. It prevents resource starvation for users with poor channel conditions, reduces unnecessary energy expenditure, and enables scalable deployment across heterogeneous environments, from dense urban centers to rural areas with limited infrastructure. The scheduler’s performance directly determines the end-user experience, network operator costs, and the overall viability of the wireless communication system.

- explain need for new scheduling method  
There exists a critical need for a novel scheduling method that explicitly accounts for the ratio of transmit power to the number of transmitted bits, a metric that directly reflects the energy efficiency of data transmission. Conventional algorithms, despite their widespread adoption, fail to optimize this fundamental trade-off, leading to suboptimal power utilization and diminished system capacity under saturated conditions. As wireless networks evolve toward higher user densities, stricter energy efficiency mandates, and more demanding real-time applications, the inefficiencies inherent in existing schedulers become increasingly untenable. A new method is required that not only maintains or improves upon the throughput and fairness characteristics of existing approaches but also actively minimizes the energy cost per bit transmitted. Such a method would enable significant reductions in base station power consumption, extend the battery life of mobile devices, reduce operational expenditures, and enhance service quality for users at the cell edge—thereby delivering a more sustainable and equitable wireless communication infrastructure.
