## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

- relate to method for modulating insulin-independent glucose transport

The present invention relates to methods for modulating insulin-independent glucose transport in skeletal muscle cells, particularly through the administration of the teneurin C-terminal associated peptide (TCAP) or biologically active variants thereof. This invention encompasses therapeutic and performance-enhancing applications directed toward increasing cellular energy availability in skeletal muscle by stimulating glucose uptake via mechanisms that do not require insulin signaling. The methods described herein are applicable to the treatment of metabolic disorders characterized by impaired glucose utilization, including but not limited to type 1 and type 2 diabetes, glycogen storage diseases, and conditions of muscle wasting or fatigue. The invention further extends to the enhancement of physical endurance, recovery from muscular trauma, and the mitigation of hypoglycemic episodes through the targeted activation of glucose transport pathways in skeletal muscle tissue. The discovery that TCAP mediates glucose transport independently of insulin represents a paradigm shift in the understanding of skeletal muscle metabolism and opens new avenues for the development of non-insulin-based therapeutics for metabolic and neuromuscular conditions.

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

- introduce teneurins

Teneurins are a conserved family of transmembrane proteins originally identified in the nervous system, where they play critical roles in cell adhesion, axon guidance, and synaptic organization. These proteins are characterized by a large extracellular domain containing multiple functional motifs, including a unique β-barrel structure at the C-terminus that shares homology with bacterial toxins, and a highly conserved intracellular tail. Teneurins are expressed across diverse metazoan species and are believed to have originated from horizontal gene transfer events between prokaryotes and early eukaryotic ancestors. In vertebrates, four paralogous teneurin genes—TENM1 through TENM4—encode structurally related proteins that are expressed in a tissue-specific manner, with prominent expression observed in the central nervous system, skeletal muscle, and certain endocrine tissues.

- describe structure of teneurins

The teneurin proteins are type II transmembrane proteins, meaning their N-terminus resides intracellularly while their C-terminus extends into the extracellular space. The extracellular region contains a series of epidermal growth factor-like repeats, a YD-repeat domain, and a conserved C-terminal β-barrel fold. At the extreme C-terminal end of this extracellular domain lies a short, highly conserved peptide sequence known as the teneurin C-terminal associated peptide (TCAP), which is generated either through proteolytic cleavage of the full-length teneurin protein or via independent transcription and translation from an alternative promoter. TCAP is approximately 40 amino acids in length and is post-translationally modified at its N-terminus by pyroglutamate formation and at its C-terminus by amidation, both of which confer resistance to proteolytic degradation and enhance biological stability.

- discuss expression of teneurins

Expression of teneurins is not restricted to neuronal tissues. In skeletal muscle, mRNA transcripts for multiple teneurin paralogues, particularly TENM3 and TENM4, are readily detectable in both rodent and human muscle biopsies. Immunohistochemical studies reveal that teneurin proteins are localized to discrete nodal regions along the sarcolemma, often in close proximity to components of the dystrophin-glycoprotein complex. This spatial organization suggests a functional role in maintaining membrane integrity and facilitating intercellular signaling. The presence of teneurins in skeletal muscle, coupled with their evolutionary antiquity and structural conservation, implies that their function extends beyond neural development to include fundamental metabolic regulation in contractile tissues.

- introduce TCAP

TCAP, the C-terminal peptide derived from teneurins, was initially characterized for its neuromodulatory effects, including regulation of stress responses, reproductive behavior, and neuronal glucose metabolism. Synthetic TCAP peptides, particularly TCAP-1, have been shown to cross the blood-brain barrier and exert potent effects on central nervous system activity. However, recent findings demonstrate that TCAP is not confined to the brain and is expressed in peripheral tissues, including skeletal muscle, where it functions as an independent bioactive peptide. Unlike many neuropeptides, TCAP is not merely a cleavage byproduct but appears to be a distinct signaling molecule with its own receptor interactions and downstream signaling cascades.

- describe activity of TCAP

TCAP exerts its biological effects by binding to latrophilins, a family of adhesion G protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs) that are widely expressed in the nervous system and skeletal muscle. Upon binding, TCAP activates phospholipase C (PLC), leading to the generation of inositol trisphosphate (IP3) and diacylglycerol (DAG). This triggers the release of calcium from intracellular stores, particularly the sarcoplasmic reticulum in muscle cells, resulting in a transient elevation of cytosolic calcium levels. This calcium flux initiates a cascade of metabolic events, including the translocation of glucose transporter 4 (GLUT4) to the plasma membrane, increased glycolytic flux, enhanced mitochondrial respiration, and elevated production of ATP and NADH. Critically, these effects occur independently of insulin receptor signaling, making TCAP a novel mediator of glucose uptake in insulin-resistant states.

- discuss skeletal muscle and glucose homeostasis

Skeletal muscle is the primary site of postprandial glucose disposal in humans, accounting for approximately 80% of insulin-stimulated glucose uptake and 40% of total body energy expenditure under basal conditions. The regulation of glucose transport into muscle cells is tightly controlled by a network of signaling pathways, with insulin being the dominant physiological regulator. However, in conditions such as obesity, aging, and type 2 diabetes, insulin signaling becomes impaired, leading to reduced GLUT4 translocation and diminished glucose uptake. This insulin resistance contributes to systemic hyperglycemia and metabolic dysfunction. The discovery that TCAP can stimulate glucose transport through an insulin-independent mechanism offers a promising alternative pathway to restore glucose homeostasis in these pathological states.

- describe insulin resistance and type 2 diabetes

Insulin resistance is a hallmark of type 2 diabetes, characterized by diminished responsiveness of skeletal muscle, adipose tissue, and liver to insulin. In skeletal muscle, this manifests as reduced phosphorylation of insulin receptor substrate-1, impaired activation of PI3K-AKT signaling, and failure of GLUT4 vesicles to translocate to the cell membrane. As a result, glucose accumulates in the bloodstream, leading to chronic hyperglycemia, oxidative stress, and progressive β-cell dysfunction. Current therapeutic strategies for type 2 diabetes rely heavily on insulin sensitizers, insulin secretagogues, or exogenous insulin administration, all of which carry risks of hypoglycemia, weight gain, or loss of efficacy over time. There remains a critical unmet need for therapies that can bypass insulin resistance and directly enhance glucose uptake in muscle tissue.

- discuss type 1 diabetes

Type 1 diabetes results from autoimmune destruction of pancreatic β-cells, leading to absolute insulin deficiency. Patients with type 1 diabetes are entirely dependent on exogenous insulin for survival, yet even with meticulous insulin replacement, many struggle to maintain normoglycemia due to delayed insulin kinetics, variable absorption, and the inability to precisely match insulin delivery to metabolic demand. The absence of endogenous insulin also impairs the natural feedback mechanisms that regulate glucose uptake in muscle during exercise or stress. TCAP-based therapies offer a novel approach to augment glucose utilization in type 1 diabetes without requiring insulin, potentially reducing glycemic variability and the risk of hypoglycemia.

- describe glycogen storage diseases

Glycogen storage diseases (GSDs) are inherited metabolic disorders characterized by defects in glycogen synthesis or breakdown, leading to abnormal accumulation or depletion of glycogen in muscle and liver. In several forms of GSD, such as McArdle disease (GSD-V) and phosphofructokinase deficiency (GSD-VII), the inability to mobilize glucose for energy results in exercise intolerance, muscle cramps, and premature fatigue. While dietary management and avoidance of strenuous activity are current mainstays of therapy, there are no pharmacological agents that directly enhance glucose transport or mitochondrial energy production in these patients. TCAP’s ability to stimulate glucose uptake and mitochondrial respiration independently of glycogenolytic pathways presents a potential therapeutic strategy to improve energy availability in GSD patients.

- discuss need for new therapies

Despite decades of research, therapies for metabolic and neuromuscular disorders remain limited in efficacy and scope. The reliance on insulin-based interventions for diabetes, the lack of disease-modifying treatments for GSDs, and the absence of agents that enhance muscle recovery and endurance underscore a profound need for novel mechanisms of glucose regulation. The identification of TCAP as a potent, insulin-independent activator of skeletal muscle glucose transport represents a transformative discovery with broad therapeutic implications. The development of TCAP-based therapeutics could revolutionize the management of diabetes, muscle wasting syndromes, and metabolic myopathies by directly targeting the root cause of energy deficit in skeletal muscle.

TCAP is administered in a purified, synthetic, or recombinant form, either systemically or locally, to achieve direct contact with skeletal muscle cells. ## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

- introduce TCAP and its effects on energy available to muscles

TCAP is a bioactive peptide that directly enhances energy availability in skeletal muscle cells by activating a novel insulin-independent pathway for glucose transport and mitochondrial metabolism. Upon binding to latrophilin receptors on the sarcolemma, TCAP initiates a signaling cascade that increases intracellular calcium concentration, which in turn stimulates the translocation of GLUT4 transporters to the cell membrane. This results in a rapid influx of glucose, which is metabolized through glycolysis and the tricarboxylic acid cycle to generate ATP and NADH. The resulting increase in cellular energy supports muscle contraction, reduces fatigue, and promotes recovery. Unlike insulin, TCAP does not require receptor tyrosine kinase activation or downstream PI3K/AKT signaling, making it effective even in insulin-resistant states.

- describe TCAP's role in regulating energy metabolism in mammals

In mammals, TCAP functions as a key regulator of energy metabolism in skeletal muscle by integrating mechanical, metabolic, and hormonal signals. TCAP expression is upregulated in response to physical activity, fasting, and metabolic stress, suggesting a physiological role in adapting muscle energy production to demand. The peptide acts locally within muscle tissue, where it is either cleaved from teneurin proteins or secreted by muscle cells themselves. Its effects are mediated through G protein-coupled latrophilin receptors, which activate phospholipase C and trigger calcium release from the sarcoplasmic reticulum. This calcium flux activates calcium-dependent kinases and metabolic enzymes, leading to increased glucose oxidation and mitochondrial biogenesis.

- detail TCAP's effects on glucose uptake and insulin resistance

TCAP significantly enhances glucose uptake in skeletal muscle cells under conditions of insulin resistance, where conventional insulin-based therapies fail. In vitro studies using C2C12 myotubes exposed to high glucose and palmitate to induce insulin resistance demonstrate that TCAP restores glucose uptake to near-normal levels, whereas insulin has no effect. In vivo, TCAP administration in diabetic rodent models reduces hyperglycemia without altering circulating insulin levels, confirming its insulin-independent mechanism. The peptide increases GLUT4 translocation via a calcium-dependent, PI3K-independent pathway, bypassing the defective signaling nodes characteristic of insulin resistance.

- discuss TCAP's potential in treating muscle trauma, diabetes, and hypoglycemia

TCAP holds therapeutic promise for muscle trauma by accelerating recovery through enhanced energy production and reduced oxidative stress. Following injury, TCAP administration increases ATP availability, supports protein synthesis, and mitigates muscle atrophy. In diabetes, TCAP provides a safe and effective means of glucose lowering without the risk of hypoglycemia associated with insulin or sulfonylureas. In hypoglycemic episodes, TCAP can be administered to stimulate endogenous glucose uptake in muscle, thereby reducing reliance on hepatic glucose output and preventing rebound hyperglycemia. Its rapid onset of action makes it suitable for acute interventions.

- describe the teneurin/TCAP-Iatrophilin system and its evolution

The teneurin/TCAP/latrophilin system is an evolutionarily ancient signaling module that predates the divergence of bilaterians. Homologs of teneurins and latrophilins are found in invertebrates such as Drosophila and C. elegans, where they regulate neuronal connectivity and muscle function. The conservation of this system across 600 million years of evolution underscores its fundamental role in coordinating metabolism with contractile activity. The acquisition of TCAP as a cleavable signaling peptide likely conferred a selective advantage by enabling dynamic, localized regulation of energy metabolism in response to environmental and physiological cues.

- summarize TCAP's effects on energy production and usage in animals

In animals, TCAP enhances energy production by increasing glucose uptake, stimulating mitochondrial respiration, and elevating ATP and NADH levels. It improves energy usage by enhancing the efficiency of muscle contraction, delaying fatigue, and promoting recovery. TCAP acts as a metabolic amplifier, allowing muscle cells to generate more ATP per unit of glucose consumed. This effect is observed in both healthy and diseased animals, indicating that TCAP restores metabolic homeostasis rather than merely stimulating anabolic pathways.

### Definitions

- define "administering to the cell(s)"

Administering to the cell(s) refers to the direct or indirect delivery of TCAP or a functional analog thereof to skeletal muscle cells, either in vivo or in vitro, such that the peptide comes into contact with the cell membrane and engages its receptor, resulting in intracellular signaling and biological effect. Administration may occur via injection, infusion, inhalation, transdermal delivery, or oral ingestion, provided the peptide reaches the target tissue in a bioactive form.

- define "effective amount" and "therapeutically effective amount"

An effective amount or therapeutically effective amount is the quantity of TCAP required to produce a measurable biological effect in a subject, such as increased glucose uptake, reduced blood glucose levels, improved muscle endurance, or enhanced recovery from trauma, without causing significant adverse effects. For systemic administration, this typically ranges from 0.1 to 100 nmol/kg body weight, depending on the route and formulation.

- define "homeostasis"

Homeostasis refers to the maintenance of stable internal conditions within the body, particularly the regulation of blood glucose levels, energy substrate availability, and metabolic balance in skeletal muscle. TCAP promotes metabolic homeostasis by enabling glucose utilization independent of insulin, thereby stabilizing energy supply during fasting, exercise, or disease states.

- define "insulin resistance"

Insulin resistance is a physiological condition in which skeletal muscle, liver, and adipose tissue exhibit diminished responsiveness to insulin, resulting in impaired glucose uptake and elevated blood glucose levels. It is a hallmark of type 2 diabetes and metabolic syndrome. TCAP overcomes insulin resistance by activating glucose transport through an alternative, insulin-independent pathway.

- define "muscle fatigue"

Muscle fatigue is the decline in the ability of a muscle to generate force during sustained or repeated contractions, often due to depletion of energy substrates, accumulation of metabolic byproducts, or impaired calcium handling. TCAP mitigates muscle fatigue by enhancing glucose delivery and mitochondrial ATP production, thereby sustaining contractile function.

- define "muscle trauma"

Muscle trauma refers to injury to skeletal muscle tissue resulting from physical exertion, mechanical damage, or disease processes such as muscular dystrophy or rhabdomyolysis. TCAP accelerates recovery from muscle trauma by increasing energy availability, reducing inflammation, and promoting repair mechanisms.

- define "neuronal cells"

Neuronal cells are specialized cells of the nervous system that transmit electrical and chemical signals. While TCAP is expressed in neuronal cells and modulates their metabolism, the present invention is specifically directed to its effects on skeletal muscle cells, distinct from its neurological actions.

- define "obvious chemical equivalents"

Obvious chemical equivalents refer to structural analogs of TCAP that retain the same biological activity due to minor modifications such as amino acid substitutions, isosteric replacements, or terminal modifications that do not alter the peptide’s conformation or receptor binding properties.

- define "pharmaceutically acceptable carrier"

A pharmaceutically acceptable carrier is a non-toxic, inert substance used to formulate TCAP for administration, including aqueous solutions, saline, buffers, liposomes, nanoparticles, or hydrogels, which facilitate delivery, stability, and absorption without interfering with the peptide’s biological activity.

- define "skeletal muscle cells"

Skeletal muscle cells are multinucleated, striated muscle fibers derived from myoblast fusion, responsible for voluntary movement and metabolic regulation. These cells express latrophilin receptors and GLUT4 transporters and are the primary target of TCAP-mediated glucose uptake.

- define "TCAP" and its variants

TCAP refers to the teneurin C-terminal associated peptide, a 40-amino acid bioactive peptide derived from the C-terminus of teneurin proteins. Variants include naturally occurring isoforms (TCAP-1, TCAP-2, TCAP-3, TCAP-4), synthetic analogs, and modified forms with enhanced stability, bioavailability, or receptor affinity, provided they retain the core sequence necessary for biological activity.

## EXAMPLES

### Example 1—Peptide Synthesis

- synthesize mouse TCAP-1 peptide

The mouse TCAP-1 peptide was synthesized using solid-phase peptide synthesis with Fmoc chemistry on a continuous flow automated peptide synthesizer. The sequence was assembled on Rink amide resin, with each amino acid coupled using HBTU and diisopropylethylamine in DMF. After chain assembly, the peptide was cleaved from the resin using a mixture of trifluoroacetic acid, water, and triisopropylsilane.

- determine sequence of mouse TCAP-1

The sequence of mouse TCAP-1 was confirmed by mass spectrometry and Edman degradation, revealing a 40-amino acid peptide with N-terminal pyroglutamate and C-terminal amide, identical to the human ortholog.

- synthesize mouse paralogue of putative peptide sequence

The paralogous sequences of mouse TCAP-2, TCAP-3, and TCAP-4 were synthesized using the same methodology, with substitutions corresponding to known isoform variations.

- use automated peptide synthesizer

An automated peptide synthesizer (CSBio, USA) was used to ensure consistent coupling efficiency and purity. The synthesizer was programmed with a double coupling protocol for sterically hindered residues.

- use continuous flow Fmoc chemistry

Continuous flow Fmoc chemistry was employed to minimize side reactions and improve yield. The flow rate was optimized to 0.5 mL/min, with each coupling step lasting 15 minutes.

- couple diisopropyl ethyl amine and Fmoc-amino acid

Each Fmoc-amino acid (10 equivalents) was activated with HBTU (10 equivalents) in the presence of diisopropylethylamine (20 equivalents) in DMF and delivered to the resin bed.

- deprotect with piperidine

The Fmoc group was removed by treatment with 20% piperidine in DMF for 15 minutes, followed by thorough washing with DMF and acetonitrile.

- cleave and deprotect final peptide

The peptide was cleaved from the resin using TFA:water:triisopropylsilane (95:2.5:2.5) for 3 hours at room temperature. The crude peptide was precipitated in cold diethyl ether and centrifuged.
