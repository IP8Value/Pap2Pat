# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNICAL FIELD

- introduce whole cell segmentation

The present invention relates to computer-implemented methods and systems for automated whole cell segmentation in two-dimensional fluorescent microscopy images, particularly those acquired using single-channel staining protocols. The invention provides a robust, scalable, and generalizable framework for delineating individual cell boundaries and their constituent sub-cellular compartments—specifically the cytoplasm and nucleus—without reliance on nuclear-specific fluorescent markers. This capability is especially valuable in high-content screening applications, live-cell imaging studies, and drug discovery workflows where the use of nuclear stains is either impractical due to phototoxicity, incompatible with long-term cell viability, or undesirable due to channel limitations in multiplexed imaging systems. The method leverages deep learning to predict pixel-level probabilities of cellular components from a single fluorescent channel that highlights the cytoplasm, followed by a cascade of image processing techniques that transform these probabilistic outputs into precise, biologically meaningful segmentations. The system is designed to operate across diverse cell types, staining modalities, and imaging magnifications, accommodating substantial heterogeneity in cell morphology, intensity distribution, and spatial arrangement. By eliminating the requirement for a second nuclear marker channel, the invention enables more efficient utilization of available fluorescence channels, thereby expanding the capacity for concurrent detection of multiple biomarkers and enhancing the biological insight obtainable from each imaging experiment.

## BACKGROUND

- motivate cell imaging

Cell imaging is a foundational technique in modern biological research, enabling the visualization and quantification of cellular structures, dynamics, and responses to stimuli at sub-micron resolution. The ability to accurately identify, count, and characterize individual cells within complex tissue-like environments underpins critical applications in drug discovery, toxicology screening, cancer biology, developmental studies, and personalized medicine. Traditional manual analysis of cellular images is labor-intensive, subjective, and inherently low-throughput, rendering it unsuitable for the large-scale datasets generated by modern automated microscopes. Automated image analysis has therefore become indispensable, offering reproducible, objective, and statistically robust quantification of cellular phenotypes across thousands of samples.

- describe cell structure

The cell is the fundamental unit of life, composed of a plasma membrane enclosing a cytoplasmic compartment that houses organelles, including the nucleus, mitochondria, endoplasmic reticulum, and other functional structures. The nucleus, typically occupying a central or off-center position, contains the genetic material and serves as the control center for cellular activity. The cytoplasm, the gel-like substance surrounding the nucleus, exhibits complex spatial heterogeneity in composition and fluorescence intensity depending on the molecular markers used for visualization. In many fluorescent imaging protocols, the cytoplasm is labeled with dyes or genetically encoded reporters that localize to cytosolic proteins, membranes, or organelles, while the nucleus remains unlabeled or minimally stained, resulting in a distinctive contrast pattern where the cytoplasm appears hyperintense and the nucleus appears hypointense relative to the surrounding background.

- explain importance of cell analysis

Accurate cell analysis is essential for extracting quantitative biological information from microscopic images. This includes determining cell count, size, shape, texture, spatial distribution, and morphological changes induced by genetic perturbations or pharmacological treatments. In high-throughput screening, for example, subtle alterations in cell morphology can serve as early indicators of drug efficacy or cytotoxicity. Similarly, in cancer research, deviations in nuclear-to-cytoplasmic ratios or irregular cell contours may signal malignant transformation. Reliable segmentation of individual cells is therefore a prerequisite for any downstream analysis, such as classification, tracking, or feature extraction, and must be performed with high precision to avoid artifacts that propagate through subsequent computational steps.

- introduce high-resolution fluorescent microscopy

Recent advances in high-resolution fluorescent microscopy have dramatically enhanced the ability to resolve sub-cellular structures with unprecedented clarity. Instruments such as widefield, confocal, and spinning disk microscopes, coupled with sensitive cameras and advanced optics, now routinely generate images with pixel sizes below one micrometer, capturing fine details of cytoplasmic organization and cell-cell interactions. These systems enable the use of multiple fluorescent labels to simultaneously visualize distinct cellular components, although the number of available spectral channels remains limited, typically ranging from three to six in most commercial platforms.

- describe advancement in computing capabilities

Parallel to these optical improvements, dramatic increases in computational power, memory capacity, and parallel processing architectures have enabled the application of sophisticated algorithms to large-scale image datasets. Modern graphics processing units (GPUs) and cloud computing infrastructures allow for the rapid training and inference of deep neural networks on high-resolution images, making previously intractable computational tasks feasible within practical timeframes. These advancements have catalyzed a paradigm shift from rule-based image analysis toward data-driven, learning-based approaches that can adapt to complex, variable biological data without requiring manual feature engineering.

- explain development of image processing techniques

Image processing techniques for cell segmentation have evolved significantly over the past decades, progressing from simple thresholding and edge detection methods to more sophisticated approaches such as watershed segmentation, active contours, and graph-based algorithms. These methods often rely on assumptions about cell shape, intensity distribution, or spatial relationships that may not hold across diverse experimental conditions. While effective under controlled settings, they frequently fail when applied to images exhibiting high variability in cell morphology, overlapping cell boundaries, or non-uniform staining intensities.

- define cell segmentation

Cell segmentation refers to the computational process of partitioning a microscopic image into distinct regions corresponding to individual cells or sub-cellular components. In its most basic form, this involves identifying pixels that belong to a cell versus those that belong to the background. In more advanced applications, segmentation extends to distinguishing between the nucleus and the cytoplasm, and further to separating touching or overlapping cells—a task complicated by the absence of clear boundaries and the presence of intensity gradients that are often ambiguous or incomplete.

- describe challenges in cell segmentation

The primary challenges in cell segmentation arise from biological and technical variability. Cells exhibit a wide range of shapes and sizes, even within the same population, due to differences in cell cycle stage, adhesion state, or response to stimuli. Cytoplasmic staining patterns can be highly heterogeneous depending on the marker used, with some dyes producing punctate, filamentous, or diffuse signals that obscure cell boundaries. Furthermore, in densely packed monolayers, adjacent cells often share indistinct or faint edges, making it difficult to determine where one cell ends and another begins. The absence of a nuclear stain exacerbates these difficulties, as traditional segmentation methods often rely on nuclei as fiducial markers to initiate cell delineation.

- introduce whole cell segmentation process

Whole cell segmentation is a specialized form of cell segmentation that aims to delineate the entire cellular boundary, including both the cytoplasm and the nucleus, as a single contiguous region. Unlike nuclear segmentation, which focuses on identifying the central, often spherical, nucleus, whole cell segmentation must account for the irregular, expansive, and frequently convoluted contours of the cytoplasm. This requires algorithms capable of modeling complex spatial relationships and adapting to variable intensity profiles across the entire cell domain.

- describe cell analysis workflow

A typical cell analysis workflow begins with image acquisition using a fluorescence microscope, followed by preprocessing steps such as background correction, illumination normalization, and noise reduction. The next stage involves segmentation, where individual cells are identified and separated. Following segmentation, features such as area, perimeter, intensity, texture, and shape descriptors are extracted from each segmented region. These features are then used for classification, clustering, or statistical analysis to derive biological insights. The accuracy and reliability of the entire workflow are critically dependent on the quality of the segmentation step.

- explain limitations of existing cell analysis workflows

Existing workflows frequently require the use of two or more fluorescent channels: one to label the nucleus (e.g., DAPI, Hoechst) and another to label the cytoplasm or cell membrane. While effective, this approach consumes valuable imaging channels that could otherwise be used to detect additional biomarkers. Moreover, nuclear stains are often phototoxic, limiting their utility in live-cell imaging experiments where prolonged observation is required. Many existing algorithms are also not generalizable across cell types or staining conditions, requiring re-optimization for each new experimental setup, which is time-consuming and impractical in high-throughput environments.

- introduce two-channel whole cell segmentation

Two-channel whole cell segmentation methods typically use the nuclear channel to identify individual nuclei as seeds, then propagate segmentation into the cytoplasmic channel using region-growing, watershed, or boundary-detection techniques. These methods have achieved high accuracy in controlled settings but are fundamentally constrained by their dependence on the availability and quality of a nuclear marker. In cases where the nuclear stain is weak, uneven, or absent, these methods fail catastrophically.

- describe limitations of two-channel whole cell segmentation

The reliance on nuclear staining introduces several critical limitations. First, it restricts the number of analyzable biomarkers to the remaining available channels. Second, nuclear dyes can interfere with cellular physiology, altering gene expression, cell cycle progression, or metabolic activity. Third, in certain cell types or under specific experimental conditions, nuclear staining may be inconsistent, diffuse, or absent altogether. Finally, the segmentation accuracy is highly sensitive to the quality of the nuclear image, and errors in nuclear detection propagate directly into errors in whole cell delineation.

- introduce single channel whole cell segmentation

Single channel whole cell segmentation seeks to overcome these limitations by performing accurate cell delineation using only a single fluorescent channel that labels the cytoplasm or other non-nuclear structures. This approach eliminates the need for a nuclear marker and allows for maximal utilization of available fluorescence channels. However, it presents a significantly more challenging problem, as the algorithm must infer the location and boundaries of the nucleus solely from the cytoplasmic signal, without direct visual or structural cues.

- describe limitations of existing single channel cell segmentation techniques

Existing single-channel methods have been largely limited to brightfield or phase-contrast images, where contrast arises from refractive index differences rather than fluorescence. These techniques are often based on thresholding, morphological operations, or shallow machine learning models that lack the capacity to learn complex, non-linear relationships between pixel intensities and cellular structures. When applied to fluorescent images with hyperintense cytoplasm and hypointense nuclei, these methods exhibit poor generalization, high false-positive rates, and an inability to resolve touching cells. Furthermore, they are rarely validated across diverse cell types, staining protocols, or imaging conditions, limiting their applicability in real-world research settings.

## SUMMARY

- introduce computer-implemented method

The present invention discloses a computer-implemented method for automated whole cell segmentation in two-dimensional fluorescent microscopy images using a single channel of cytoplasmic staining. The method employs a deep learning model trained to predict pixel-wise probabilities of three distinct classes: nucleus, cytoplasm, and background. The model generates a probability map for each class, which is subsequently processed through a series of deterministic image analysis steps to extract individual cell boundaries without requiring a nuclear marker. This approach enables accurate segmentation across diverse cell types, staining conditions, and imaging magnifications, while preserving the full capacity of multiplexed fluorescence channels for additional biomarker detection.

- describe model generation

The deep learning model is generated through a supervised training process using a dataset of manually or semi-automatically segmented images. Each training image is associated with a ground truth segmentation mask that labels each pixel as belonging to the nucleus, cytoplasm, or background. The model is implemented as a modified U-Net architecture with an asymmetric encoder-decoder structure, incorporating convolutional layers, max-pooling operations, transposed convolutions, and dropout regularization to enhance generalization. The model is trained to minimize a weighted root mean square deviation loss function that enforces both pixel-level accuracy and the biological constraint that the sum of nucleus, cytoplasm, and background probabilities at each pixel equals unity.

- explain accessing model and sample image

Once trained, the model is accessed by a computing system that receives a raw fluorescent microscopy image acquired using a single cytoplasmic marker. The image undergoes preprocessing to correct for uneven illumination and normalize pixel intensities. The preprocessed image is then divided into overlapping patches, each of which is independently processed by the deep learning model to generate probability maps for the nucleus, cytoplasm, and background. These probability maps are stitched together to reconstruct a full-resolution prediction for the entire image.

- describe generating probability maps

The deep learning model outputs three continuous probability maps, each with values ranging between zero and one, representing the likelihood that each pixel belongs to the nucleus, cytoplasm, or background class. The nucleus probability map exhibits elevated values in regions corresponding to the nuclear area, despite the absence of direct nuclear staining, due to the model’s learned inference of nuclear location based on surrounding cytoplasmic intensity gradients. The cytoplasm probability map highlights the entire cellular region with high confidence, while the background map identifies regions devoid of cellular material.

- explain extracting binary nuclear mask and nuclei seeds map

The nucleus probability map is converted into a binary nuclear mask using multi-level Otsu thresholding, which adaptively identifies optimal intensity thresholds to separate hypointense nuclear regions from the cytoplasm and background. The resulting binary mask is refined using a multi-scale Laplacian of Gaussian blob detection algorithm to enhance regions consistent with nuclear morphology across varying sizes. To resolve overlapping or touching nuclei, an inverse distance transform is computed from the binary mask, followed by an extended h-minima transform to suppress shallow intensity minima and retain only the most prominent regional minima. These minima are then used as seeds in a seeded watershed transform applied to the inverse distance transform, resulting in a nuclei seeds map where each nucleus is uniquely labeled as a distinct object.

- describe applying nuclei seeds to sample image

The nuclei seeds map is combined with the original cytoplasmic image and the cytoplasm probability map to generate an enhanced image representation. The original image is first preprocessed with Gaussian filtering to reduce noise, followed by intensity scaling and logarithmic transformation to enhance contrast. The enhanced image is then multiplied element-wise with the cytoplasm probability map to reinforce regions of high cytoplasmic confidence. The background is identified using a three-level Otsu thresholding procedure that estimates the expected total cell area based on the number of detected nuclei and prior knowledge of average cell size.

- summarize transforming sample image into segmented image

The final segmentation is achieved by applying a seeded watershed algorithm to the enhanced image, using the nuclei seeds map as the marker and the background label to define exclusion zones. The algorithm propagates segmentation boundaries from each seed until they meet at intensity gradients corresponding to cell edges. The result is a segmented image in which each cell is uniquely labeled, with its nucleus and cytoplasm delineated as a single, contiguous region. This segmented image provides a biologically accurate representation of individual cells suitable for downstream quantitative analysis, including cell counting, morphological feature extraction, and phenotypic classification.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

- introduce in situ analysis

The disclosed method is particularly suited for in situ analysis of cells within their native microenvironment, such as monolayer cultures, organoids, or tissue sections, where spatial context and cell-cell interactions are critical to biological interpretation. By enabling accurate segmentation without nuclear staining, the method preserves the integrity of live cells during prolonged imaging sessions and allows for the simultaneous detection of multiple endogenous or exogenous biomarkers in the same imaging channel. This capability is transformative for longitudinal studies of cellular dynamics, including cell division, migration, and response to dynamic stimuli.

- describe benefits of whole cell segmentation

Whole cell segmentation provides a comprehensive view of cellular morphology that is essential for detecting subtle phenotypic changes induced by genetic, chemical, or environmental perturbations. Unlike nuclear segmentation alone, which provides only a partial view of the cell, whole cell segmentation captures the full extent of cytoplasmic expansion, retraction, or deformation—phenomena that are often the earliest indicators of cellular stress, apoptosis, or activation. The ability to perform this segmentation in a single channel enhances experimental flexibility, reduces reagent costs, and minimizes phototoxicity, thereby improving the fidelity and reproducibility of biological data.

### Development of a Training Model

- introduce deep learning model

The deep learning model employed in the invention is a convolutional neural network specifically designed to predict the spatial distribution of nucleus, cytoplasm, and background in single-channel fluorescent images. The model is trained to learn hierarchical representations of cellular morphology from annotated training data, enabling it to generalize across diverse cell types and staining conditions.

- describe network architecture

The network architecture is based on a modified U-Net structure with an asymmetric encoder-decoder design. The encoder consists of five sequential convolutional and max-pooling layers, progressively reducing spatial resolution while increasing feature depth. The decoder reconstructs the spatial resolution through transposed convolutions, with skip connections linking encoder and decoder layers of corresponding resolution. The final layer outputs three probability maps corresponding to the three classes.

- describe training environment

Training is performed on a cloud-based computing environment equipped with multiple NVIDIA Tesla K80 GPUs and sufficient RAM to accommodate large image patches. The environment runs a Linux-based operating system with the MXNet deep learning framework and supporting libraries for image augmentation and data loading.

- describe training process

The training process involves feeding the network with thousands of image patches, each labeled with ground truth masks, and iteratively adjusting the model weights to minimize a composite loss function. Training continues until convergence, determined by stabilization of validation loss over multiple epochs.

- describe U-net architecture

The U-Net architecture is chosen for its ability to preserve spatial information through skip connections, allowing the network to combine low-level edge information with high-level semantic context. This is critical for accurately delineating irregular cell boundaries.

- describe convolution and pooling steps

Each convolutional layer applies 3×3 kernels with stride one, followed by ReLU activation. Max-pooling layers reduce spatial dimensions by a factor of two using a 2×2 window. The number of filters increases from 32 to 256 in the encoder and decreases symmetrically in the decoder, with minor asymmetries introduced to optimize performance for the target task.

- describe loss function

The loss function is defined as a weighted sum of root mean square deviations between predicted and ground truth probabilities for each class, augmented by a constraint term that penalizes violations of the biological constraint that the sum of nucleus, cytoplasm, and background probabilities at each pixel must equal one.

- describe training data preprocessing

Training images are preprocessed by applying top-hat filtering to correct for uneven illumination and downsampling to a standardized pixel size of approximately 0.65 μm per pixel to ensure consistency across images acquired at different magnifications.

- describe training data augmentation

Data augmentation is performed by rotating each image patch by 90, 180, and 270 degrees, effectively quadrupling the training dataset and improving the model’s rotational invariance.

### Single Channel Whole Cell Segmentation Workflow

- illustrate single channel whole cell segmentation workflow

The workflow begins with the acquisition of a single-channel fluorescent image of cells labeled with a cytoplasmic marker. The image is preprocessed and divided into overlapping patches, each of which is processed by the trained deep learning model to generate nucleus, cytoplasm, and background probability maps. These maps are stitched to form full-image predictions.

- provide input and transformed images

The input image is a grayscale representation of cytoplasmic fluorescence, with bright regions corresponding to cytoplasm and dark regions corresponding to nucleus and background. The transformed images include the nucleus probability map (showing elevated intensity at nuclear locations), the cytoplasm probability map (showing high intensity across entire cell bodies), and the background probability map (showing low intensity where cells are present).

- transform sample image into cell probability map and nuclei probability map

The deep learning model transforms the input image into two continuous probability maps: one representing the likelihood of each pixel belonging to the nucleus and another representing the likelihood of each pixel belonging to the cytoplasm.

- divide sample image into patches

The sample image is divided into overlapping patches of 176×176 pixels, with a 16-pixel overlap on each side, ensuring that the central 160×160 region of each patch is unique and used for model inference.

- assign predicted labels to each pixel

Each pixel in each patch is assigned a probability value for each of the three classes by the deep learning model, resulting in three probability maps of identical dimensions to the input image.

- stitch patches to form full predicted image

The predicted probability values from overlapping patches are averaged at their boundaries to generate a seamless, full-resolution probability map for each class.

- extract nuclei probability map and cell probability map

The nucleus and cytoplasm probability maps are extracted from the full predicted image for subsequent processing steps.

- generate binary nuclear mask from nuclei probability map

The nucleus probability map is converted into a binary mask using multi-level Otsu thresholding, which automatically selects three intensity thresholds to separate background, nucleus, and cytoplasmic regions.

- identify nuclei with different sizes

To detect nuclei of varying sizes, a multi-level Laplacian of Gaussian filter is applied to the nucleus probability map, enhancing blob-like structures across a range of scales.

- perform blob detection

Blob detection identifies regions of local intensity maxima that are consistent with nuclear morphology, generating a set of candidate nuclear locations.

- apply multi-level Laplacian of Gaussian filter

The Laplacian of Gaussian filter is applied at multiple sigma values to detect nuclei ranging from small to large, ensuring robust detection regardless of cell type or magnification.

- apply multi-level thresholding

Multi-level thresholding is applied to the Laplacian-filtered map to generate a binary mask that retains only the most salient nuclear regions.

- assign binary value to each pixel

Each pixel in the binary mask is assigned a value of one if it belongs to a detected nucleus and zero otherwise.

- extract binary nuclear mask

The binary nuclear mask is extracted as a two-dimensional array where each pixel is labeled as nuclear or non-nuclear.

- transform binary nuclear mask into nuclei seeds map

The binary nuclear mask is processed to resolve touching nuclei by computing an inverse distance transform, applying an extended h-minima transform to suppress shallow minima, and extracting regional minima as seeds for watershed segmentation.

- determine distance transform of binary nuclear mask

The distance transform computes the Euclidean distance of each pixel from the nearest background pixel, creating a gradient where the nucleus center has the highest value.

- apply extended h-minima transform

The extended h-minima transform suppresses all regional minima whose depth is less than a user-defined threshold (default 3 μm), retaining only the most prominent minima that correspond to true nuclear centers.

- extract regional minima

The regional minima are extracted as a set of discrete coordinates, each representing the centroid of a single nucleus.

- apply seeded watershed transform

The seeded watershed transform is applied to the inverse distance transform using the extracted regional minima as markers, ensuring that each nucleus is assigned a unique label.

- extract nuclei seeds map

The output of the seeded watershed transform is the nuclei seeds map, a labeled image where each nucleus is assigned a unique integer identifier.

- perform cell enhancement transformation

The original cytoplasmic image is enhanced by applying a Gaussian blur to reduce noise, followed by intensity scaling and logarithmic transformation to amplify contrast between cell interiors and boundaries.

- apply pixel-level weighting

The enhanced image is multiplied element-wise with the cytoplasm probability map, reinforcing regions of high cytoplasmic confidence and suppressing noise in low-confidence areas.

- generate enhanced sample image

The result is an enhanced sample image that emphasizes cytoplasmic structure while minimizing background interference.

- perform cell delineation transformation

The enhanced sample image is prepared for segmentation by identifying the background region using a three-level Otsu threshold, which estimates the expected total cell area based on the number of detected nuclei.

- apply nuclei seeds to sample image

The nuclei seeds map is overlaid onto the enhanced sample image to serve as markers for the seeded watershed segmentation.

- determine image background

The background region is identified as the set of pixels whose intensity falls below the lowest Otsu threshold, corresponding to areas devoid of cellular material.

- assign background labels

Each pixel identified as background is assigned a label of zero, while all other pixels are assigned to the foreground for segmentation.

- apply seeded watershed segmentation

The seeded watershed algorithm is applied to the enhanced sample image using the nuclei seeds as markers and the background label as a constraint, ensuring that segmentation boundaries terminate at the edge of the background.

- identify and separate cells

The algorithm propagates segmentation fronts from each seed until they meet at intensity gradients corresponding to cell boundaries, resulting in the separation of touching cells and the delineation of individual cell contours.

- transform sample image into segmented image

The final output is a segmented image where each cell is uniquely labeled, with its nucleus and cytoplasm contained within a single, continuous region.

### Assessment of Segmentation Results

- perform quality control assessment

A quality control assessment is performed on each segmented image to evaluate the reliability of the segmentation output. This assessment includes computation of a cell segmentation quality score based on the consistency of cell shape, the absence of over-segmentation or under-segmentation, and the agreement between predicted and expected cell area.

- generate cell segmentation quality score

The cell segmentation quality score is generated by comparing the segmented image to a reference segmentation derived from a two-channel method or expert annotation, using a similarity metric that accounts for both pixel-level overlap and one-to-one correspondence between segmented objects. The score ranges from zero to one, with values above 0.8 indicating high-quality segmentation suitable for downstream analysis.
