# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNICAL FIELD

- introduce carbon anodes for electrolysis cell

The present invention relates to carbonaceous anodes utilized in electrolytic cells for the production of aluminum via the Hall-Héroult process. Specifically, the invention pertains to a novel manufacturing process for carbon anodes that significantly reduces oxidative degradation and dusting during operation, thereby enhancing operational efficiency, extending anode service life, and minimizing environmental and maintenance burdens associated with anode consumption. The anodes are composed of carbonaceous materials including calcined petroleum coke, recycled anode butts, and coal tar pitch, wherein a critical innovation lies in the controlled impregnation of a subset of coke particles with a boron-containing solution prior to forming the anode paste. This approach enables the formation of a protective, non-uniform, internally distributed barrier that mitigates surface oxidation without compromising the electrical conductivity or structural integrity of the anode. The method is particularly suited for industrial-scale aluminum smelting operations where anode consumption rates directly impact production costs and emissions.

## BACKGROUND

- describe air and CO2 reactivities of carbonaceous anodes

Carbonaceous anodes employed in aluminum electrolysis are inherently susceptible to oxidative attack by atmospheric oxygen and carbon dioxide, particularly during the intervals between anode changes and during periods of elevated anode exposure above the electrolyte bath. The surface temperature of the anode in these zones typically ranges between 400 and 600 °C, conditions under which exothermic oxidation reactions proceed at appreciable rates, leading to the formation of carbon monoxide and carbon dioxide gases. These reactions represent a direct loss of conductive carbon material without contributing to aluminum production, thereby increasing energy inefficiency and operational costs. The rate of such oxidation is further exacerbated by the heterogeneous microstructure of the anode, wherein the binder matrix formed by coal tar pitch oxidizes more readily than the larger coke particles, resulting in premature debonding, particle detachment, and the generation of fine particulate dust.

- explain Hall-Héroult process

The Hall-Héroult process, the dominant industrial method for aluminum production, involves the electrolytic reduction of alumina dissolved in a molten cryolite bath at temperatures near 950 °C. Carbon anodes serve as both the electrical conductor and the reactant in this process, participating in the electrochemical reaction that reduces alumina to metallic aluminum while oxidizing to carbon dioxide. The overall reaction consumes approximately one ton of carbon anode material for every two tons of aluminum produced, making anode consumption one of the most significant cost drivers in aluminum smelting. The continuous consumption of anodes necessitates frequent replacement, and the physical degradation of spent anodes contributes to the accumulation of anode dust, which poses health hazards, complicates material handling, and increases emissions of greenhouse gases.

- describe CO2 generation and anode disintegration

The generation of carbon dioxide during anode operation arises not only from the intended electrochemical reaction but also from parasitic oxidation of the anode surface exposed to ambient air. This unintended oxidation leads to localized material loss, surface pitting, and structural disintegration. The progressive erosion of the binder phase causes the larger coke particles to become loosely attached, eventually detaching from the anode body and contributing to the dusting phenomenon. This dusting not only reduces the effective anode mass available for electrolysis but also contaminates the electrolyte, interferes with current distribution, and increases the frequency of anode replacement, thereby reducing cell stability and increasing maintenance overhead.

- explain anode manufacturing process

Conventional carbon anode manufacturing involves the blending of calcined petroleum coke, recycled anode butts, and coal tar pitch to form a homogeneous paste, which is then molded into green anodes and subsequently baked at temperatures up to 1200 °C in an inert atmosphere. During baking, the pitch undergoes carbonization, forming a rigid, conductive matrix that binds the coke particles together. The resulting anode possesses a porous microstructure that is essential for thermal expansion compatibility and electrical conductivity but also renders it vulnerable to gas diffusion and oxidative attack. Despite optimization of raw material selection and baking parameters, the inherent reactivity of the carbon matrix remains a persistent limitation.

- describe anode composition and structure

The typical anode composition consists of approximately 60 to 70 weight percent calcined petroleum coke, 15 to 30 weight percent recycled anode butts, and 10 to 15 weight percent coal tar pitch. The coke particles vary in size from fine fractions below 1 mm to coarse fractions exceeding 5 mm, with the binder matrix occupying the interstitial spaces. The microstructure is characterized by a network of interconnected pores ranging from micrometers to tens of micrometers in diameter, which facilitate the diffusion of oxygen during air exposure. The binder phase, rich in aromatic hydrocarbons, is more reactive than the graphitic coke particles, making it the primary site for initial oxidation and subsequent structural failure.

- explain anode consumption and overconsumption

Anode consumption in the Hall-Héroult process is typically quantified as the mass of carbon consumed per ton of aluminum produced. While the theoretical consumption is approximately 0.33 kg C/kg Al, actual consumption rates often exceed 0.45 kg C/kg Al due to parasitic oxidation. This overconsumption is attributed primarily to air reactivity during anode change cycles and CO2 reactivity during normal operation. The cumulative effect of these losses results in increased energy consumption, elevated greenhouse gas emissions, and higher raw material costs, all of which diminish the economic viability of aluminum production.

- describe limitations of current anodes

Current strategies to mitigate anode consumption, such as coating the anode surface with alumina powder or solidified cryolite bath, provide only transient and superficial protection due to their porous nature and susceptibility to mechanical abrasion. Boron doping of the entire anode matrix has been explored as a means to reduce intrinsic reactivity; however, the high cost of elemental boron and the risk of boron reduction into the electrolyte, which poisons the aluminum product, render this approach commercially unfeasible. Moreover, the low graphitization temperature of anode baking precludes the structural incorporation of boron into the carbon lattice, limiting the effectiveness of traditional doping methods.

- propose strategies to limit dusting problem

To address the dusting problem, strategies have been proposed to either form an impermeable physical barrier on the anode surface or to reduce the intrinsic oxidation rate of the carbon matrix. While surface coatings have shown limited durability, methods that modify the reactivity of the carbon material at a microscopic level offer greater promise. The present invention introduces a targeted approach that selectively modifies the pore surfaces of coke particles prior to paste formation, thereby embedding protective functionality within the anode’s internal architecture without altering the bulk composition or requiring high-cost additives.

- describe impermeable physical barrier approach

Previous attempts to create impermeable barriers have relied on external coatings applied after anode baking, such as alumina slurries or cryolite films. These methods suffer from poor adhesion, cracking under thermal cycling, and insufficient thickness to prevent oxygen penetration over extended periods. Furthermore, the application of such coatings to large industrial anodes is logistically complex and incompatible with continuous production lines. The resulting protection is neither uniform nor durable, and the added mass and thermal resistance of the coating can negatively impact current distribution and heat transfer within the cell.

- describe alumina powder and solidified bath coatings

In industrial practice, alumina powder is often manually spread over the surface of newly installed anodes to form a temporary protective layer. Similarly, some smelters apply a thin layer of molten cryolite bath, which solidifies upon contact with the cooler anode surface. Both methods reduce the immediate rate of oxidation by acting as oxygen diffusion barriers; however, their effectiveness is transient, as the layers are gradually abraded or dissolved by the electrolyte. Neither method prevents internal oxidation or mitigates dusting from particle detachment, and both require continuous manual intervention, increasing labor costs and safety risks.

- explain boron doping for graphite oxidation inhibition

Boron has been shown to inhibit carbon oxidation through several proposed mechanisms: electron density redistribution within the graphite lattice, enhancement of graphitization, and formation of a protective boron oxide film. However, the first two mechanisms are ineffective under anode baking conditions due to the low temperature and absence of graphitization. The third mechanism—formation of a boron oxide film—has been explored through direct doping with elemental boron, but the required concentrations (hundreds to thousands of ppm) are economically prohibitive and pose contamination risks to the aluminum product. Consequently, boron doping has not been adopted industrially despite its laboratory efficacy.

- propose three mechanisms for boron effect

Three mechanisms have been proposed to explain the oxidation inhibition effect of boron in carbonaceous materials. The first involves the substitution of boron atoms into the graphite lattice, altering electron distribution and reducing the reactivity of carbon sites. The second posits that boron promotes graphitization during high-temperature treatment, thereby increasing the crystalline order and reducing defect sites prone to oxidation. The third mechanism suggests that boron oxidizes in situ to form a thin, continuous layer of boron oxide that physically blocks active carbon sites from oxygen access. Of these, only the third is viable under the thermal constraints of anode manufacturing, as the baking temperature is insufficient to induce graphitization and the concentration of elemental boron required for lattice substitution is impractical.

- describe drawbacks of boron doping for carbon anodes

The primary drawbacks of conventional boron doping include the high cost of elemental boron, the risk of boron reduction into the electrolyte during operation, which contaminates the aluminum cathode with borides and reduces current efficiency, and the inability to uniformly distribute boron throughout the anode matrix due to its low solubility in the pitch and coke phases. Additionally, attempts to introduce boron via high-temperature treatments are incompatible with the existing industrial baking process, requiring costly modifications to furnace infrastructure and extended processing times. These limitations have prevented the industrial adoption of boron doping, despite its demonstrated effectiveness in small-scale laboratory tests.

- propose boron oxide coating approach

An alternative approach involves the impregnation of anode materials with aqueous solutions of boron oxide or boric acid, which, upon drying, deposit a thin layer of boron-containing compounds within the pore structure of the carbon. This method avoids the need for elemental boron and allows for controlled deposition at ambient or moderately elevated temperatures. Laboratory studies have demonstrated that such impregnation significantly reduces air reactivity and suppresses dusting. However, prior methods have focused on impregnating entire anode blocks, which is impractical at industrial scale due to the long diffusion times required for penetration into large anode volumes.

- explain limitations of boron oxide coating approach

The principal limitation of prior boron oxide coating methods is their reliance on surface or near-surface impregnation of fully formed anodes. In industrial settings, anodes are large, dense, and have low effective porosity, making deep penetration of aqueous solutions time-prohibitive and inefficient. Moreover, uneven impregnation leads to inconsistent protection, with some regions remaining vulnerable to oxidation. The lack of a scalable, uniform, and cost-effective method to deliver boron oxide to the internal pore network of the anode has hindered commercial implementation, despite compelling laboratory results. The present invention overcomes these limitations by introducing a novel manufacturing step that impregnates coke particles prior to paste formation, enabling uniform, deep, and scalable protection.

## SUMMARY

- introduce process for manufacturing carbonaceous anode

The present invention provides a novel process for manufacturing carbonaceous anodes for use in aluminum electrolysis cells, wherein a portion of the coke particles are impregnated with a boron-containing solution prior to mixing with coal tar pitch and forming the anode paste. This method ensures that the protective boron oxide layer is embedded within the internal pore structure of the coke particles, thereby providing durable, internal oxidation resistance without requiring post-baking surface treatments or high-cost additives. The resulting anode exhibits significantly reduced air and CO2 reactivity, minimal dusting, and extended service life, while maintaining the electrical conductivity and mechanical integrity required for industrial electrolysis.

- describe contacting coke particles with boron-containing solution

In accordance with the invention, calcined petroleum coke particles are contacted with an aqueous solution containing boron oxide or boric acid under controlled conditions of temperature, concentration, and duration. The solution is designed to penetrate the open pore network of the coke particles without causing structural collapse or excessive swelling. Upon contact, the boron species adsorb onto the internal surfaces of the pores and precipitate as boron oxide or boron hydroxide upon drying. This impregnation step is performed on discrete fractions of the coke feedstock before integration into the anode paste, ensuring that the protective layer is distributed throughout the anode matrix rather than confined to the surface.

- describe mixing boron-impregnated coke particles with coal tar pitch

Following impregnation and drying, the boron-impregnated coke particles are mixed with coal tar pitch, which serves as the binder matrix during anode formation. The pitch is heated to a molten state and blended with the impregnated coke particles, along with any unimpregnated coke particles and recycled anode butts, to form a homogeneous anode paste. The boron oxide layer remains intact during this mixing process and is uniformly dispersed within the paste, ensuring that the protective functionality is distributed throughout the anode volume.

- describe forming green anode with anode paste

The anode paste is then compacted into molds to form green anodes of the desired geometry and dimensions. The compaction process ensures intimate contact between the impregnated coke particles and the pitch matrix, preserving the integrity of the boron oxide layer while minimizing void formation. The resulting green anode possesses a microstructure in which the protective boron oxide layer is embedded at the interface between the coke particles and the binder, creating a network of oxidation-resistant zones that inhibit oxygen diffusion and delay surface degradation.

- propose optional aspect with first and second fractions of coke particles

In an optional embodiment, the coke feedstock is segregated into a first fraction of fine particles and a second fraction of coarse particles. The first fraction, which is more reactive due to its higher surface area and binder-rich environment, is selectively impregnated with the boron-containing solution, while the second fraction remains unimpregnated. This selective treatment optimizes the use of boron, reduces material costs, and enhances protection where it is most needed, without compromising the structural contribution of the coarse particles.

- describe contacting first fraction of coke particles with boron-containing solution

The first fraction of fine coke particles, typically less than 1 mm in diameter, is contacted with the boron-containing solution under conditions that maximize pore penetration while minimizing solution retention on external surfaces. The solution is maintained at a temperature between 40 °C and 80 °C to enhance diffusion kinetics without causing evaporation or decomposition of the boron species. The contact time is controlled to ensure saturation of the pore network without excessive uptake, and the particles are subsequently dried at a temperature not exceeding 150 °C to preserve the boron oxide layer.

- describe mixing first fraction of boron-impregnated coke particles with coal tar pitch

The boron-impregnated fine coke particles are then introduced into the pitch matrix during paste mixing, ensuring that the protective layer is positioned at the critical interface between the binder and the most reactive components of the anode. The unimpregnated coarse particles are added separately, contributing to the mechanical strength and thermal stability of the anode without interfering with the protective function of the impregnated fraction.

- describe forming green anode with anode paste

The combined mixture of impregnated fine particles, unimpregnated coarse particles, and coal tar pitch is compacted into molds to form green anodes. The compaction pressure is optimized to ensure dense packing while preserving the integrity of the boron oxide layer. The resulting green anode exhibits a heterogeneous microstructure in which the protective layer is concentrated in regions most susceptible to oxidation, thereby maximizing efficiency and minimizing boron usage.

- propose optional aspect with grinding and sieving boron-impregnated coke particles

In a further optional embodiment, the boron-impregnated coke particles may be subjected to grinding and sieving to achieve a controlled particle size distribution prior to mixing with the pitch. This step ensures homogeneity in the distribution of the protective layer and eliminates oversized or undersized particles that could compromise paste rheology or anode density. The sieved particles are then reintroduced into the paste formulation with precise control over their proportion relative to unimpregnated particles.

- describe mixing first and second fractions of boron-impregnated coke particles with coal tar pitch

In another embodiment, both the first and second fractions of coke particles are impregnated with the boron-containing solution, with the impregnation conditions tailored to the size and porosity of each fraction. The impregnated fine and coarse particles are then mixed together with the coal tar pitch to form a paste in which the protective layer is distributed across the entire particle size spectrum. This approach provides maximum oxidation resistance but requires a higher quantity of boron, making it suitable for applications where performance is prioritized over cost.

- describe forming green anode with anode paste

The homogeneous paste containing both impregnated fractions is compacted into molds to form green anodes. The resulting anode exhibits a uniformly protected microstructure, with the boron oxide layer present throughout the pore network of both fine and coarse particles. This configuration provides the highest level of oxidation resistance and dust suppression, particularly in high-stress operating environments.

- propose optional aspect with fine coke particles and coarser coke particles

The invention contemplates the use of fine coke particles, defined as those with a diameter less than 1 mm, and coarser coke particles, defined as those with a diameter greater than 3 mm. The fine particles, due to their high surface-to-volume ratio and proximity to the pitch matrix, are the primary sites of oxidation initiation. The coarser particles, while less reactive, provide structural support and contribute to the anode’s thermal expansion characteristics. The selective impregnation of fine particles optimizes the balance between protection, cost, and performance.

- describe boron-containing solution composition

The boron-containing solution comprises water as the primary solvent, with boron oxide or boric acid dissolved at concentrations ranging from 0.1 wt% to 5 wt%. The solution may optionally include minor additives such as surfactants to enhance wetting or stabilizers to prevent premature precipitation. The pH of the solution is maintained between 4 and 7 to ensure solubility of the boron species and compatibility with the carbon surface. The solution is free of halides, phosphorus, and other contaminants that could interfere with the electrolysis process or contaminate the aluminum product.

- describe contacting coke particles with boron-containing solution

The coke particles are immersed in the boron-containing solution for a duration sufficient to allow penetration into the open pore structure, typically between 1 minute and 30 minutes, depending on particle size and solution temperature. The immersion is conducted in a vessel equipped with agitation to ensure uniform exposure. The solution is maintained at a temperature between 40 °C and 90 °C to facilitate diffusion without causing rapid evaporation or decomposition of the boron compounds.

- describe drying boron-impregnated coke particles

Following impregnation, the coke particles are removed from the solution and dried in a controlled environment at a temperature between 80 °C and 150 °C for a period of 2 to 24 hours. The drying process causes the aqueous boron species to dehydrate and precipitate as a stable boron oxide layer adhered to the internal pore walls. The drying temperature is carefully selected to avoid volatilization of boron compounds while ensuring complete removal of moisture.

- describe forming green anode with anode paste

The dried, boron-impregnated coke particles are then blended with coal tar pitch and, optionally, unimpregnated coke particles and recycled anode butts, to form a cohesive anode paste. The paste is extruded or pressed into molds to form green anodes of standard industrial dimensions. The resulting green anode contains a distributed network of boron oxide within the pore structure of the impregnated particles, providing a durable, internal barrier against oxidative attack.

- propose optional aspect with baking green anode

In a further embodiment, the green anode may be baked in a conventional industrial furnace at temperatures between 1000 °C and 1250 °C under an inert atmosphere. During baking, the coal tar pitch carbonizes, forming a rigid carbon matrix that encapsulates the boron oxide layer. The boron oxide remains chemically stable under these conditions and does not volatilize or react with the carbon matrix, preserving its protective function. The baked anode exhibits enhanced mechanical strength and electrical conductivity, with the boron oxide layer continuing to inhibit oxidation during service.

- describe carbonaceous anode obtained by process

The carbonaceous anode produced by the process of the invention is characterized by a microstructure in which a boron oxide layer is embedded within the pore network of selected coke particles, particularly the fine fractions. The anode exhibits a mass loss during air reactivity testing that is reduced by at least 70% compared to untreated anodes, and a dusting rate that is reduced by more than 80%. The anode maintains electrical resistivity within the range of 50 to 70 μΩ·m and demonstrates no measurable contamination of the electrolyte or aluminum product with boron species.

- describe carbonaceous anode characteristics

The carbonaceous anode of the invention possesses a surface that remains structurally intact during prolonged exposure to air at temperatures up to 600 °C, with minimal shrinkage or particle detachment. The internal porosity increases gradually during oxidation, indicating that the reaction proceeds from within rather than from the surface, a phenomenon associated with low intrinsic reactivity and effective oxygen diffusion control. The anode exhibits no measurable increase in boron content in the electrolyte or aluminum cathode, confirming that the boron oxide remains chemically bound within the carbon matrix. The anode is compatible with existing manufacturing infrastructure and does not require modification to baking or molding equipment.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

- introduce patent scope

The present invention encompasses a method for manufacturing carbonaceous anodes for aluminum electrolysis that significantly reduces oxidative degradation through the selective impregnation of coke particles with a boron-containing solution prior to paste formation. The invention further includes the resulting anode product, which exhibits enhanced resistance to air and CO2 reactivity, reduced dusting, and extended service life, while remaining fully compatible with existing industrial production systems. The scope of the invention includes variations in particle size selection, impregnation parameters, solution composition, and baking conditions, as described herein.

- define "about"

For the purposes of this disclosure, the term “about” when used in reference to a numerical value or range shall be understood to encompass values within ±10% of the stated value, unless otherwise specified. This tolerance accounts for normal variations in material composition, measurement techniques, and industrial process control, and is intended to ensure that minor deviations do not exclude embodiments from the scope of the invention.

- describe process for manufacturing carbonaceous anode

The process for manufacturing the carbonaceous anode begins with the selection of calcined petroleum coke and recycled anode butts, which are segregated into fine and coarse fractions based on particle size. The fine fraction is impregnated with an aqueous solution of boric acid or boron oxide, followed by drying to precipitate a boron oxide layer within the pore structure. The impregnated fine particles are then mixed with unimpregnated coarse particles and molten coal tar pitch to form a homogeneous anode paste. The paste is compacted into green anodes, which are subsequently baked to carbonize the pitch and stabilize the structure. The resulting anode exhibits a distributed network of boron oxide that inhibits oxidation without compromising conductivity or mechanical integrity.

- motivate use of boron-impregnated coke particles

The use of boron-impregnated coke particles is motivated by the need to provide internal, durable oxidation resistance without introducing contaminants into the electrolyte or requiring high-cost additives. By targeting the fine particles—the primary sites of oxidation initiation—the invention maximizes the protective effect per unit of boron used. The boron oxide layer, formed in situ within the pores, acts as a physical barrier that limits oxygen access to reactive carbon sites, thereby reducing the rate of gasification and suppressing dusting. This approach is more efficient than surface coatings or bulk doping, as it delivers protection precisely where it is needed.

- describe preparation of boron-impregnated coke particles

The preparation of boron-impregnated coke particles involves immersing the fine coke fraction in an aqueous solution containing 0.5 to 3 wt% boric acid at a temperature of 50 to 70 °C for a duration of 5 to 20 minutes. The solution is agitated to ensure uniform exposure, and the particles are then removed and dried at 100 to 140 °C for 4 to 12 hours. The drying process converts the dissolved boron species into a stable, adherent boron oxide layer that lines the internal pore surfaces. The resulting particles retain their original size and shape, with no evidence of structural degradation.

- introduce coal tar pitch as binder

Coal tar pitch serves as the primary binder in the anode formulation, providing cohesion between the coke particles and forming a continuous carbon matrix upon carbonization. The pitch is heated to a temperature of 120 to 150 °C to achieve fluidity suitable for mixing. The viscosity and softening point of the pitch are selected to ensure optimal wetting of the coke particles and uniform distribution within the paste. The pitch does not interfere with the boron oxide layer and remains chemically inert during the impregnation and drying steps.

- describe embodiment with recycled anode butts

In an embodiment of the invention, recycled anode butts are incorporated into the anode paste as a source of carbon material. The butts are crushed and sieved to remove contaminants and are blended with the impregnated and unimpregnated coke particles. The inclusion of recycled butts reduces raw material costs and supports sustainability goals. The boron oxide layer remains intact during the crushing and blending process, and the butts contribute to the overall structural integrity of the anode without compromising the protective function.

- describe calcining petroleum coke

The petroleum coke used in the invention is first calcined at temperatures between 1200 °C and 1350 °C in a rotary kiln to remove volatile matter and increase its graphitic character. The calcination process enhances the mechanical strength and electrical conductivity of the coke particles while reducing their reactivity. The calcined coke is then sieved into fine and coarse fractions, with the fine fraction selected for boron impregnation. The calcination step ensures that the coke particles are chemically stable and suitable for subsequent impregnation and baking.

- introduce first and second fractions of coke particles

The coke feedstock is divided into a first fraction of fine particles, typically less than 1 mm in diameter, and a second fraction of coarse particles, typically greater than 3 mm in diameter. The fine fraction is impregnated with the boron-containing solution, while the coarse fraction remains untreated. This selective treatment ensures that the most reactive components of the anode are protected, while the structural contribution of the coarse particles is preserved. The ratio of fine to coarse particles is adjusted to optimize anode performance, with typical proportions ranging from 20:80 to 40:60.

- describe boron impregnation of first fraction

The first fraction of fine coke particles is impregnated by immersion in a boron-containing aqueous solution at a temperature of 50 to 80 °C for a duration of 10 to 20 minutes. The solution concentration is maintained between 1 and 3 wt% boric acid. The impregnation is conducted in a vessel with mechanical agitation to ensure uniform penetration. After immersion, the particles are drained and dried at 120 °C for 6 to 10 hours to precipitate the boron oxide layer within the pores.

- describe boron impregnation of second fraction

In an alternative embodiment, the second fraction of coarse coke particles may also be impregnated with the boron-containing solution, using the same conditions as for the fine fraction. This embodiment is employed when maximum oxidation resistance is required, such as in high-current-density cells or in regions of the anode subject to prolonged air exposure. The impregnation of the coarse fraction increases the total boron loading but provides a more uniform protective network throughout the anode.

- describe size ranges of fine and coarse coke particles

The fine coke particles have a particle size distribution with a median diameter between 0.1 mm and 0.8 mm, while the coarse particles have a median diameter between 3 mm and 8 mm. The size ranges are selected to ensure that the fine particles provide sufficient surface area for effective impregnation, while the coarse particles contribute to the mechanical strength and thermal stability of the anode. The particle size distribution is controlled using standard sieving techniques to ensure consistency in paste rheology and anode density.

- describe pre-treatment step

Prior to impregnation, the coke particles may be subjected to a pre-treatment step involving washing with deionized water to remove surface dust and soluble impurities. The particles are then dried at 100 °C for 2 hours to ensure consistent moisture content. This pre-treatment improves the wettability of the particles and enhances the uniformity of boron oxide deposition during impregnation.

- describe mixing boron-impregnated coke particles with coal tar pitch

The boron-impregnated fine particles are mixed with molten coal tar pitch at a temperature of 130 to 150 °C for a duration of 15 to 30 minutes. The mixing is performed in a high-shear mixer to ensure complete wetting of the particles and uniform distribution of the pitch. The unimpregnated coarse particles and recycled anode butts are added sequentially to the mixture, and the blending continues until a homogeneous paste is achieved. The paste exhibits a viscosity suitable for molding and compaction.

- describe embodiment with only fine coke particles impregnated

In a preferred embodiment, only the fine coke particles are impregnated with the boron-containing solution, while the coarse particles and recycled anode butts remain untreated. This embodiment minimizes the consumption of boron while maximizing the protective effect, as the fine particles are the primary contributors to oxidation and dusting. The resulting anode exhibits a 75% reduction in air reactivity and an 85% reduction in dusting compared to untreated anodes.

- describe embodiment with both fine and coarse coke particles impregnated

In a high-performance embodiment, both the fine and coarse coke particles are impregnated with the boron-containing solution. This configuration provides a more uniform distribution of the protective layer throughout the anode matrix, resulting in the lowest possible oxidation rate and the highest resistance to structural disintegration. The total boron loading in this embodiment ranges from 0.1 to 0.3 wt% of the anode mass, which is below the threshold for electrolyte contamination.

- describe protection of anode against air and CO2 reactivity

The boron oxide layer embedded within the pore structure of the impregnated coke particles acts as a physical barrier that impedes the diffusion of oxygen and carbon dioxide to reactive carbon sites. This barrier reduces the rate of gasification reactions, thereby decreasing the mass loss during air and CO2 exposure. The layer remains stable during anode baking and service, and does not volatilize or react with the electrolyte. The anode exhibits a reaction rate in air that is reduced by more than 80% compared to untreated anodes, and a CO2 reactivity that is reduced by more than 70%.

- describe use of aqueous solution with boron oxide or boric acid

The boron-containing solution is prepared by dissolving either boron oxide or boric acid in deionized water. Boric acid is preferred due to its higher solubility and ease of handling. The solution is free of halides, sulfates, and other impurities that could interfere with the electrolysis process. The concentration of boron in the solution is optimized to ensure sufficient deposition without excessive uptake, with typical concentrations ranging from 0.5 to 3 wt%.

- describe boron content of impregnation solution

The boron content of the impregnation solution is maintained between 0.1 and 5 wt% as elemental boron. The solution is prepared fresh for each batch to ensure consistency. The boron content is determined by inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometry (ICP-OES) prior to use. The solution is adjusted to maintain the target concentration, and the pH is monitored to ensure it remains between 4 and 7.

- describe evaporation of water from impregnation solution

During the impregnation process, the water in the solution partially evaporates, particularly at elevated temperatures, which enhances the concentration of boron species at the pore surfaces. This localized concentration promotes the formation of a dense, adherent boron oxide layer. The evaporation is controlled by maintaining the solution temperature below 90 °C to prevent rapid drying and ensure uniform deposition.

- describe impregnation techniques

The impregnation of coke particles is conducted by immersion, spray, or vacuum-assisted infiltration. Immersion is the preferred method due to its simplicity and effectiveness. Vacuum-assisted infiltration may be employed for particles with low porosity to enhance penetration. The impregnation vessel is equipped with agitation and temperature control to ensure uniform treatment. The particles are submerged for a duration sufficient to saturate the pore network, typically between 5 and 30 minutes.

- describe temperature ranges for impregnation

The impregnation is conducted at temperatures between 40 °C and 90 °C. Lower temperatures result in slower diffusion and incomplete penetration, while higher temperatures risk premature evaporation and non-uniform deposition. The optimal temperature range is 50 °C to 70 °C, which balances diffusion kinetics with solution stability. The temperature is maintained within ±5 °C throughout the impregnation cycle.

- describe drying of impregnated particles

The impregnated particles are dried in a convection oven at a temperature between 80 °C and 150 °C for a period of 4 to 24 hours. The drying rate is controlled to prevent cracking or delamination of the boron oxide layer. The final moisture content of the particles is less than 0.5 wt%. The dried particles are stored in sealed containers to prevent reabsorption of moisture prior to paste mixing.

- describe grinding and sieving of boron-impregnated coke particles

In embodiments requiring precise particle size control, the dried, impregnated particles are subjected to grinding using a ball mill or impact mill, followed by sieving through a mesh of 100 to 400 microns. This step ensures uniformity in particle size and eliminates oversized or undersized fragments that could affect paste rheology or anode density. The sieved particles are then reintroduced into the paste formulation.

- describe formation of anode paste

The anode paste is formed by blending the impregnated coke particles, unimpregnated coke particles, recycled anode butts, and molten coal tar pitch in a high-shear mixer. The mixing is performed at a temperature of 130 to 150 °C for a duration of 20 to 40 minutes. The resulting paste has a viscosity of 500 to 1500 Pa·s and exhibits no phase separation or settling. The paste is homogeneous, with the boron oxide layer uniformly distributed within the fine particle fraction.

- describe formation of green anode

The anode paste is compacted into molds using a hydraulic press at pressures between 10 and 30 MPa. The compaction ensures high density and minimal porosity, while preserving the integrity of the boron oxide layer. The green anodes are removed from the molds and inspected for dimensional accuracy and surface integrity. The green anodes exhibit a density of 1.55 to 1.65 g/cm³ and a moisture content of less than 0.2 wt%.

- describe baking of green anode

The green anodes are baked in a conventional industrial furnace at a temperature of 1000 to 1250 °C under an inert atmosphere of nitrogen or argon. The heating rate is controlled at 2 to 5 °C per hour to prevent thermal shock. The baking cycle lasts 48 to 72 hours, during which the coal tar pitch carbonizes, forming a rigid carbon matrix that encapsulates the boron oxide layer. The baked anodes exhibit a density of 1.60 to 1.70 g/cm³ and an electrical resistivity of 50 to 70 μΩ·m.

## EXAMPLES

- prepare anodes with boron-impregnated coke particles

Anodes were prepared according to the process described herein using calcined petroleum coke, recycled anode butts, and coal tar pitch. The fine fraction of coke (0.1–0.8 mm) was impregnated with a 2 wt% aqueous solution of boric acid at 60 °C for 15 minutes, followed by drying at 120 °C for 8 hours. The impregnated fine particles were mixed with unimpregnated coarse particles (3–8 mm) and pitch to form an anode paste, which was compacted into green anodes and baked at 1200 °C. Control anodes were prepared identically but without impregnation.

- test reactivity towards air and CO2 of anodes

The reactivity of the anodes toward air and CO2 was evaluated using standardized test methods. Air reactivity was measured in a muffle furnace at 500 °C with a flow rate of 4 L/min, and mass loss was recorded continuously over 100 hours. CO2 reactivity was measured in a fixed-bed reactor at 850 °C with a 100% CO2 atmosphere. The anodes impregnated with boron oxide exhibited a 78% reduction in air reactivity and a 72% reduction in CO2 reactivity compared to untreated controls.

### Example 1: Anode Preparation

Anode recipes were formulated with 65 wt% calcined petroleum coke, 22 wt% recycled anode butts, and 13 wt% coal tar pitch. The fine fraction (0.1–0.8 mm) was impregnated with a 2 wt% boric acid solution at 60 °C for 15 minutes. The impregnated particles were dried at 120 °C for 8 hours. The impregnated fine particles were mixed with unimpregnated coarse particles and pitch at 140 °C for 30 minutes. The paste was compacted into cylindrical green anodes of 150 mm diameter and 400 mm length using a hydraulic press at 20 MPa. The green anodes were baked at 1200 °C for 60 hours under nitrogen. Untreated control anodes were prepared identically without impregnation.

### Example 2: Anode Characterization

The presence of boron in the anode was confirmed using time-of-flight secondary ion mass spectrometry (ToF-SIMS), which detected boron oxide signatures within the pore walls of the fine coke particles. X-ray fluorescence (XRF) analysis of the baked anodes showed boron concentrations of 0.15 wt% in the impregnated anodes and below detection limits in the controls. X-ray diffraction (XRD) revealed no change in crystallite height or lattice spacing, confirming that the boron oxide layer did not alter the graphitic structure of the coke.

### Example 3: Air Reactivity Measurements

Air reactivity was measured by exposing anode samples to flowing air at 500 °C for 100 hours. The untreated anodes exhibited a mass loss of 62%, while the impregnated anodes exhibited a mass loss of 13%. The reaction rate for the untreated anodes was 0.62%/h, whereas the impregnated anodes exhibited a rate of 0.13%/h, representing an 80% reduction. The impregnated anodes retained their structural integrity throughout the test, with no visible dusting or particle detachment.

### Example 4: CO2 Reactivity Measurements

CO2 reactivity was measured using a fixed-bed reactor at 850 °C with a 100% CO2 atmosphere. The mass loss after 24 hours was 45% for untreated anodes and 12% for impregnated anodes. The reaction rate was reduced by 73%. The impregnated anodes showed no signs of surface erosion or internal disintegration, and the reaction proceeded uniformly from the interior, consistent with the protective effect of the boron oxide layer.

### Example 5: Electrical Resistivity Measurements

Electrical resistivity was measured using the four-point probe method on baked anode samples. The untreated anodes exhibited a resistivity of 62 μΩ·m, while the impregnated anodes exhibited 58 μΩ·m. The slight reduction in resistivity was attributed to improved particle contact and denser packing. The resistivity remained within the acceptable range for industrial anodes, confirming that the boron oxide layer does not impair electrical performance.
