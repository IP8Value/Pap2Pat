# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

Gene and cell-based therapies have emerged as transformative approaches for treating a wide range of diseases, including cancer, genetic disorders, and autoimmune conditions. These therapies involve the delivery of therapeutic genes or the transplantation of engineered cells capable of modulating biological processes at the molecular level. Despite their promise, the clinical translation of these therapies has been hindered by the inability to non-invasively monitor the spatial distribution, persistence, and functional activity of therapeutic agents within living organisms. Conventional methods such as biopsy and histological analysis provide only localized, static snapshots and are associated with significant risks, including sampling error, tissue damage, and patient discomfort. The lack of real-time, whole-body imaging capabilities limits the ability to optimize dosing regimens, assess treatment efficacy, and detect early signs of adverse events such as off-target engraftment or immune-mediated clearance.

To address this critical gap, positron emission tomography (PET) reporter gene imaging has been developed as a powerful tool for tracking therapeutic cells and genes in vivo. This approach relies on the genetic engineering of therapeutic cells to express a reporter gene that encodes a protein capable of selectively accumulating a radiolabeled probe, thereby enabling non-invasive detection via PET. The most widely utilized reporter gene systems to date are derived from viral enzymes, particularly herpes simplex virus type 1 thymidine kinase (HSV1-tk) and its engineered variant, sr39tk. These enzymes efficiently phosphorylate nucleoside analogs such as 18F-labeled fluoroganciclovir (FGCV) and 9-[4-18F-3-(hydroxymethyl)butyl]guanine (18F-FHBG), leading to intracellular trapping of the radiolabeled probe and subsequent signal amplification through catalytic turnover. While these systems have demonstrated utility in preclinical models, their clinical application has been limited by the immunogenicity of viral proteins. The human immune system recognizes HSV1-tk and sr39tk as foreign antigens, triggering T-cell-mediated elimination of the very therapeutic cells intended for treatment. This phenomenon has been documented in multiple clinical trials, resulting in reduced cell persistence and diminished therapeutic outcomes.

To overcome this limitation, researchers have turned to human-derived proteins as alternative reporter genes, aiming to minimize immune recognition while preserving enzymatic efficiency. Among these, the human mitochondrial thymidine kinase 2 (htk2) has been proposed as a candidate due to its natural expression in human tissues and its ability to phosphorylate both D- and L-configured nucleosides. However, wild-type htk2 exhibits strong feedback inhibition by thymidine triphosphate (dTTP), a natural metabolite whose intracellular concentration fluctuates during the cell cycle, thereby compromising the consistency and sensitivity of PET signal generation. Furthermore, htk2 retains significant activity toward endogenous nucleosides such as thymidine and deoxycytidine, raising concerns about potential perturbations to nucleotide pools and the risk of genotoxicity in therapeutic cells. Although truncated forms of htk2 have been explored to eliminate mitochondrial targeting and enhance cytosolic localization, their catalytic efficiency for synthetic reporter probes remains suboptimal compared to viral systems.

The development of a “second generation” HSV1-tk system, exemplified by sr39tk, improved substrate specificity and reduced off-target phosphorylation, yet did not resolve the fundamental issue of immunogenicity. Similarly, the use of 3-(2′[18F]fluoroethyl)spiperone (FESP) as a reporter probe for dopamine type 2 receptor (D2R)-based imaging systems has expanded the toolkit for receptor-mediated reporter systems, but these approaches are limited by low signal amplification due to the absence of catalytic turnover. The human netrin-1 transporter (hNET) has also been evaluated as a non-enzymatic reporter gene, but its sensitivity is orders of magnitude lower than that of kinase-based systems. These limitations underscore the urgent need for a novel PET reporter gene/reporter probe system that combines high sensitivity, minimal immunogenicity, and biological inertness—qualities essential for safe, reliable, and scalable clinical translation.

The discovery that human mitochondrial thymidine kinase 2 (htk2) can be structurally engineered to achieve enhanced specificity for non-natural L-configured nucleoside analogs, while simultaneously reducing its affinity for endogenous substrates and resisting feedback inhibition, represents a pivotal advancement. This breakthrough enables the creation of a human-derived reporter gene system that is both functionally robust and immunologically silent. The development of a mutant variant of htk2, specifically engineered to phosphorylate 2-deoxy-2-[18F]fluoro-5-methyl-1-β-L-arabinofuranosyluracil (L-[18F]FMAU) with high efficiency, while exhibiting negligible activity toward thymidine and deoxycytidine, fulfills the long-standing requirement for a clinically viable, non-immunogenic PET reporter gene system. This innovation not only overcomes the limitations of viral-based systems but also establishes a new paradigm for the design of human enzyme-based reporter platforms that are inherently compatible with human physiology.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

Embodiments of the invention provide systems, materials, and methods for the non-invasive imaging of therapeutic cells and genes in living mammals using positron emission tomography (PET) or single-photon emission computed tomography (SPECT). The invention introduces a highly sensitive, non-immunogenic reporter gene derived from human mitochondrial thymidine kinase 2 (htk2), which has been engineered through targeted amino acid substitutions to exhibit enhanced catalytic activity toward specific radiolabeled nucleoside analogs while minimizing interaction with endogenous nucleosides. This engineered reporter gene, when expressed in therapeutic cells, enables the selective accumulation and retention of novel radiolabeled probes, allowing for high-contrast, whole-body molecular imaging without triggering immune responses. The invention further encompasses novel PET and SPECT radiolabeled probes, including L- and D-enantiomer analogs of thymidine, specifically designed for optimal substrate recognition by the engineered htk2 variant.

The invention enables applications in whole-body molecular imaging across a broad spectrum of clinical and preclinical contexts, including the tracking of adoptive T-cell therapies, stem cell engraftment, oncolytic viral delivery, and gene therapy vectors. These applications are particularly valuable in clinical trials where real-time monitoring of therapeutic cell biodistribution, persistence, and functional activity is essential for correlating imaging data with therapeutic outcomes. The invention further provides PET reporter gene imaging systems that are compatible with existing clinical imaging infrastructure, facilitating seamless integration into routine diagnostic workflows. Additionally, the invention includes imaging kits comprising the engineered htk2 reporter gene, vectors for its delivery, and radiolabeled probes suitable for human use, all of which are formulated for standardized, reproducible, and scalable production under current good manufacturing practices.

The benefits of the invention include the elimination of immunogenicity associated with viral reporter genes, the preservation of normal cellular metabolism in therapeutic cells due to the reduced activity of the engineered enzyme toward endogenous nucleosides, and the high sensitivity of signal detection comparable to that of the best-performing viral systems. The use of human-derived genes ensures compatibility with the human immune system, reducing the risk of rejection and enabling long-term cell tracking in patients. The expression of the engineered htk2 reporter gene does not significantly alter intracellular nucleotide pools, thereby minimizing the potential for genotoxic effects that could compromise the safety of cell-based therapies. An enhanced version of human mitochondrial thymidine kinase 2 (htk2), designated htk2-N93D/L109F, has been developed through structure-guided mutagenesis and exhibits a marked increase in catalytic efficiency for L-[18F]FMAU, while maintaining low affinity for thymidine and deoxycytidine.

The invention further introduces a human thymidine kinase 2 polypeptide comprising specific amino acid substitutions at positions 93 and 109, wherein asparagine at position 93 is replaced by aspartic acid and leucine at position 109 is replaced by phenylalanine. A nucleic acid molecule encoding this polypeptide is provided, along with expression vectors comprising this nucleic acid sequence, which are suitable for transfection or transduction into mammalian cells. Host cells transfected or transduced with these vectors are capable of expressing the engineered htk2 polypeptide and accumulating radiolabeled probes with high specificity. The invention further encompasses a system for imaging mammalian cells using PET or SPECT, comprising a mammalian cell expressing the engineered htk2 polypeptide and a radiolabeled probe selected from the group consisting of L-[18F]FMAU, L-[18F]FEAU, and their structural analogs. The invention also includes compositions of matter comprising non-naturally occurring analogs of thymidine labeled with positron-emitting or gamma-emitting isotopes, and kits for the delivery and imaging of therapeutic cells expressing the engineered reporter gene.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

Gene therapy refers to the therapeutic delivery of nucleic acids into a patient’s cells to correct or compensate for abnormal gene function, while therapeutic cells refer to cells that have been modified ex vivo or in vivo to exert a desired biological effect, such as immune modulation, tissue regeneration, or tumor targeting. Gene expression is the process by which information encoded in a gene is used to synthesize a functional gene product, typically a protein, which mediates the biological activity of the therapeutic agent. Monitoring gene kinetics involves the quantitative assessment of the temporal and spatial dynamics of gene expression, including the onset, duration, and intensity of protein production, which is critical for evaluating the efficacy and safety of gene and cell-based therapies. Conventional treatments for many diseases, including chemotherapy, radiation, and surgical resection, are often non-specific, invasive, and associated with significant systemic toxicity. The limitations of these approaches have motivated the development of precision therapies that target disease at the molecular level, yet the inability to non-invasively track these therapies has impeded their clinical adoption.

Positron emission tomography (PET) reporter gene imaging offers a solution to this challenge by enabling the visualization of gene expression in real time, in vivo, and across the entire body. Therapeutic cells engineered to express a PET reporter gene accumulate a radiolabeled PET reporter probe through enzymatic phosphorylation, leading to intracellular trapping and signal amplification. The invention introduces a novel PET reporter gene/reporter probe (PRG-PRP) system based on a mutant form of human mitochondrial thymidine kinase 2 (htk2), designated TK2-N93D/L109F, which exhibits a unique combination of high catalytic efficiency for the radiolabeled probe L-[18F]FMAU and minimal activity toward endogenous nucleosides. This mutant enzyme is capable of efficiently phosphorylating L-[18F]FMAU, resulting in its intracellular retention and detectable PET signal, while exhibiting substantially reduced phosphorylation of thymidine and deoxycytidine, thereby preserving normal nucleotide metabolism.

The development of this system involved the rational design of mutations to reduce feedback inhibition by thymidine triphosphate (dTTP), a natural regulator of thymidine kinase activity. The N93D substitution disrupts a key hydrogen bond involved in stabilizing the closed, inhibited conformation of the enzyme, rendering it less susceptible to dTTP-mediated inhibition. The L109F substitution enhances the enzyme’s affinity for L-configured nucleosides by introducing steric constraints that favor binding of the non-natural enantiomer. The resulting double mutant, TK2-N93D/L109F, demonstrates a kcat(L-FMAU)/kcat(D-dT) ratio that is nearly 15-fold higher than that of wild-type htk2, indicating a profound shift in substrate preference. This mutant enzyme retains its catalytic activity in the presence of physiological concentrations of dTTP, ensuring consistent signal generation throughout the cell cycle.

The invention further encompasses the use of L-[18F]FMAU and L-[18F]FEAU as novel PET reporter probes, which are selectively phosphorylated by the engineered htk2 variant but not by endogenous human kinases such as TK1 or dCK. These probes are trapped intracellularly following phosphorylation, enabling high-contrast imaging of reporter gene-expressing cells. The invention also includes a safety gene mechanism, wherein the same vector encoding the reporter gene can be engineered to include a suicide gene, such as herpes simplex virus thymidine kinase or inducible caspase-9, allowing for the controlled elimination of malfunctioning or over-proliferating therapeutic cells if necessary.

The human thymidine kinase 2 polypeptide of the invention comprises amino acid substitutions at positions 93 and 109, wherein asparagine at position 93 is replaced by aspartic acid and leucine at position 109 is replaced by phenylalanine. In some embodiments, the polypeptide further comprises deletion of the first 50 amino acids to remove the mitochondrial targeting sequence, ensuring cytosolic localization. A nucleic acid molecule encoding this polypeptide is provided, including codon-optimized sequences for efficient expression in human cells. Vectors comprising this nucleic acid molecule include retroviral, lentiviral, adenoviral, and non-viral delivery systems, such as plasmids, transposons, and CRISPR-based integration platforms. Host cells transfected or transduced with these vectors include T cells, mesenchymal stem cells, induced pluripotent stem cells, and other therapeutic cell types used in clinical applications.

Compositions of matter comprising non-naturally occurring analogs of thymidine labeled with positron-emitting isotopes such as fluorine-18, carbon-11, or copper-64 are also part of the invention. These analogs include L-[18F]FMAU, L-[18F]FEAU, and their derivatives, which are synthesized using automated radiochemistry platforms to ensure high purity and specific activity. The invention further includes a system for imaging mammalian cells using PET or SPECT, comprising a mammalian cell expressing the engineered htk2 polypeptide and a radiolabeled probe selected from the group consisting of L-[18F]FMAU, L-[18F]FEAU, and their structural analogs. The system is configured to detect the spatial distribution and quantitative accumulation of the probe in vivo, enabling the visualization of therapeutic cell location, persistence, and expansion.

The thymidine kinase polypeptide of the invention is characterized by decreased susceptibility to thymidine triphosphate-mediated feedback inhibition, a property conferred by the N93D mutation. The PET reporter probe is selected based on its high affinity for the engineered enzyme, low background uptake in non-target tissues, and favorable pharmacokinetics. A kit for imaging mammalian cells using PET or SPECT is provided, comprising a vector encoding the engineered htk2 polypeptide, a radiolabeled probe, a buffer solution, and instructions for use. The method of imaging involves introducing the reporter gene into a mammalian cell via transfection or transduction, administering the radiolabeled probe to the mammal, and detecting the probe’s accumulation using PET or SPECT imaging. The polypeptide may be fused to a heterologous amino acid sequence, such as a fluorescent tag or a stabilization domain, to facilitate detection or enhance protein expression.

Four novel PET probes are disclosed, including L-[18F]FMAU, L-[18F]FEAU, D-[18F]FMAU, and D-[18F]FEAU, each of which has been evaluated for biodistribution, metabolic stability, and tissue clearance in murine and human models. Radiation dosimetry studies confirm that these probes are safe for human administration, with effective doses within acceptable limits for diagnostic imaging. The invention further includes applications in monitoring gene therapy delivery to solid tumors, tracking adoptive T-cell therapies in melanoma and leukemia, and evaluating stem cell engraftment in regenerative medicine.

## EXAMPLES

### Example 1

PET reporter probe systems were evaluated to identify optimal combinations of reporter genes and radiolabeled probes for non-invasive imaging of therapeutic cells. L-[18F]FMAU and L-[18F]FEAU were selected as candidate reporter probes based on their structural similarity to thymidine and their potential for selective phosphorylation by human nucleoside kinases. In vitro kinase assays demonstrated that L-[18F]FMAU is a substrate for human thymidine kinase 2 (htk2), with significantly higher phosphorylation efficiency compared to its D-enantiomer. Cell culture studies using L1210 murine leukemia cells transduced with htk2 variants confirmed that L-[18F]FMAU accumulation is dependent on the expression of the reporter gene and is not observed in control cells.

In vivo imaging studies in mice revealed that L-[18F]FMAU exhibits low background uptake in non-target tissues, with rapid clearance through the hepatobiliary system and minimal retention in the brain, heart, or bone marrow. The potential immunogenicity of hTK2-N93D was evaluated by analyzing peptide epitopes presented by MHC class I molecules in humanized mouse models. Candidate epitopes were identified, and mutations were introduced to abolish binding to common HLA alleles, including HLA-A2. These modifications resulted in a variant of hTK2-N93D with reduced immunogenic potential while maintaining catalytic activity. Human nucleoside kinases, including dCK, TK1, and htk2, were systematically evaluated as potential reporter genes. htk2 was selected due to its low basal expression in most adult tissues and its ability to phosphorylate L-nucleosides.

The mitochondrial targeting sequence of htk2 was truncated to ensure cytosolic localization, and the N93D mutation was engineered to reduce feedback inhibition by dTTP. Kinase assays demonstrated that the N93D mutant retained 95% of its catalytic activity toward L-[18F]FMAU in the presence of 100 μM dTTP, whereas wild-type htk2 activity was reduced by more than 50%. The catalytic efficiency of TK2-N93D for L-[18F]FMAU was 77% higher than that of wild-type htk2. Expression of TK2-N93D in L1210 cells did not alter cell proliferation, viability, or metabolic profiles, confirming its biological inertness. In vivo comparison of TK2-N93D, wild-type htk2, and HSV1-sr39tk revealed that TK2-N93D produced PET signals comparable to sr39tk when used with L-[18F]FMAU.

Adenoviral vectors encoding alternative PRGs were constructed and titered to ensure equivalent transduction efficiency. MicroPET/CT imaging of tumors expressing TK2-N93D showed a 2.6-fold increase in probe accumulation compared to wild-type htk2. The TK2-N93D/L-[18F]FMAU system was evaluated in an Ad-liver model to assess its utility in hepatic gene therapy applications. Epitope mapping confirmed the absence of immunogenic peptides in the engineered variant, supporting its potential for clinical use. Alternative approaches, including the use of codon-optimized sequences and fusion with ubiquitin ligase domains, were considered to further enhance expression stability.

### Example 2

Preclinical studies were conducted to evaluate the utility of the TK2-N93D/L-[18F]FMAU system in a murine model of hepatic colorectal cancer metastases. Human LS174T colorectal cancer cells were injected into the spleens of nu/nu mice to establish liver metastases. Optical imaging was used to monitor tumor burden over time, followed by intravenous administration of an adenoviral vector encoding TK2-N93D under the control of the Cox2 promoter, which is activated in inflamed tumor tissue. Twenty-four hours later, L-[18F]FMAU was administered, and microPET/CT imaging was performed. High signal-to-noise ratios were observed in hepatic metastases, with minimal uptake in non-target organs. Control animals injected with saline or non-transducing virus showed no detectable signal.

In a separate model of melanoma, the Pmel-1/B16 adoptive T-cell transfer system was employed to evaluate the tracking of tumor-specific T cells. T cells were transduced with a lentiviral vector encoding TK2-N93D and adoptively transferred into tumor-bearing mice. L-[18F]FMAU was administered at multiple time points, and serial microPET/CT scans revealed the migration of T cells to tumor sites, with signal intensity correlating with tumor regression. PCR analysis confirmed the presence of transduced T cells in tumor tissue, and enzyme activity assays demonstrated sustained TK2-N93D expression for over 14 days. Alternative delivery methods, including electroporation and nanoparticle-based transfection, were explored to enhance transduction efficiency in primary human T cells.

### Example 3

Products for labeling and long-term cell tracking in PET studies were developed to support preclinical and clinical translation. Commercialized kits were designed for the delivery of the TK2-N93D reporter gene into murine and human therapeutic cells, including CD8+ T cells, mesenchymal stromal cells, and induced pluripotent stem cells. The ELIXYS automated radiochemistry platform was utilized for the consistent, no-carrier-added synthesis of L-[18F]FMAU, ensuring high radiochemical purity and yield. Lentiviral vectors encoding TK2-N93D were produced under GMP conditions and validated for transduction efficiency and genomic integration safety. Non-viral delivery vectors, including transposon systems and CRISPR-based integration platforms, were developed to enable transient or stable expression without viral components.

Studies were conducted to determine conversion factors for quantifying the number of therapeutic cells in vivo based on PET signal intensity. Mouse CD8+ T cells and human mesenchymal stem cells were transduced with TK2-N93D, injected into colon cancer xenografts, and imaged using L-[18F]FMAU. A linear relationship was established between PET signal and cell number, enabling quantitative estimation of cell burden. A ΦC31 integrase-based labeling kit was developed for site-specific integration of the reporter gene into the genome, ensuring long-term expression without silencing. The commercialization strategy included partnerships with radiopharmaceutical manufacturers, imaging centers, and biotechnology firms to integrate the system into clinical trial workflows.

### Example 4

An eIND submission was prepared to support the transition of the TK2-N93D/L-[18F]FMAU system into human clinical trials. Biodistribution and dosimetry studies were conducted in mice and non-human primates to establish radiation safety profiles. Preclinical safety and toxicity studies demonstrated no adverse effects on hematopoiesis, liver function, or immune parameters following administration of the reporter gene or probe. Dosimetry calculations confirmed that the effective dose to critical organs remained below regulatory thresholds. Chemistry, manufacturing, and controls (CMC) documentation was completed for both the lentiviral vector and the radiolabeled probe, including stability, purity, and potency assays. Contingency plans were developed to address potential issues such as probe instability, vector immunogenicity, or suboptimal signal intensity. Alternative probes, including L-[18F]FEAU and 18F-FHBG, were evaluated as backup candidates.

### Example 5

The development of a novel PET reporter probe was motivated by the need for a probe with high specificity, low background uptake, and compatibility with human metabolism. The biodistribution of L-[18F]FMAU was evaluated in mice and healthy human volunteers using whole-body PET/CT imaging. In mice, probe accumulation was highest in the liver and kidneys, with rapid clearance through the biliary and urinary systems. In humans, similar patterns were observed, with additional uptake in the myocardium and liver. Rational design of mutations into human thymidine kinase 2 was guided by structural modeling and kinetic analysis, leading to the identification of the N93D and L109F substitutions. The conclusions of these studies confirmed that L-[18F]FMAU is a suitable PET reporter probe for use with engineered htk2 variants.

### Example 6

PET reporter gene imaging was employed to overcome the limitations of current cell-based therapies by enabling real-time, quantitative tracking of therapeutic cells. The TK2-N93D/L109F PRG-PRP system was developed through structure-guided enzyme engineering, resulting in a mutant enzyme with enhanced selectivity for L-[18F]FMAU and reduced activity toward endogenous nucleosides. Biodistribution studies in mice and humans confirmed favorable pharmacokinetics, with low uptake in non-target tissues and high signal-to-noise ratios in reporter gene-expressing tumors. Structure-guided modeling of human TK2 revealed key residues involved in substrate binding, leading to the generation of the double mutant TK2-N93D/L109F. Kinetic analyses demonstrated that this mutant exhibited a kcat(L-FMAU)/kcat(D-dT) ratio of 14.9, significantly higher than wild-type htk2 and comparable to HSV1-sr39tk. Immunogenicity studies confirmed the absence of T-cell epitopes in the engineered enzyme. The system was validated in multiple tumor models, and human PET/CT studies confirmed its translational potential. The development of this system represents a major advance in the field of cell tracking, providing a safe, sensitive, and clinically viable platform for monitoring gene and cell therapies.

### Example 7

L-2-O-[(Trifluoromethyl)sulfonyl]-1,3,5-tri-O-benzoyl was prepared as a precursor for the synthesis of L-[18F]FMAU. The compound was reacted with no-carrier-added [18F]fluoride ion, which was produced in a cyclotron and trapped on an anion exchange cartridge. The fluoride ion was released using a potassium carbonate solution and reacted with the precursor under anhydrous conditions at 110°C for 15 minutes. The reaction mixture was purified by HPLC, and the final product was formulated in saline with 10% ethanol. The radiochemical yield was greater than 60%, with radiochemical purity exceeding 98%.

### Example 8

Illustrative PET reporter gene probes were disclosed, including L-[18F]FMAU, L-[18F]FEAU, and their D-enantiomer counterparts. Ideal PET reporter probe conditions include high specificity for the reporter enzyme, low non-specific binding, rapid clearance from non-target tissues, and compatibility with human metabolism. Probes were evaluated for their substrate activity with TK1 and their suitability for F-18 labeling. Biodistribution and metabolic stability studies in mice confirmed that L-[18F]FMAU is resistant to degradation by nucleosidases and exhibits minimal defluorination. The probe’s favorable profile supports its potential for human studies, and its synthesis is scalable for clinical use.
