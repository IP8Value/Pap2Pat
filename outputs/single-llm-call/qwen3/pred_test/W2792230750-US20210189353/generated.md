## DESCRIPTION

- introduce Amadoriase enzyme with improved thermostability

The present invention relates to an isolated thermostable Amadoriase enzyme with significantly enhanced resistance to thermal denaturation compared to the naturally occurring wild-type enzyme. This engineered variant retains full catalytic activity toward glycated amino acids while demonstrating unprecedented stability under elevated temperatures, making it uniquely suited for industrial, diagnostic, and therapeutic applications where prolonged exposure to heat is unavoidable. The enzyme is derived from Amadoriase I of Aspergillus fumigatus and has been modified through rational design to incorporate specific disulfide bonds that lock critical structural elements into a more rigid conformation, thereby reducing the entropy-driven unfolding that typically occurs at elevated temperatures. Unlike previous attempts at enzyme stabilization through random mutagenesis or directed evolution, this invention achieves a marked improvement in thermal resilience without compromising catalytic efficiency or substrate specificity. The thermostable variant exhibits a T50 value exceeding 60 °C, a substantial increase over the wild-type enzyme, which denatures at approximately 52 °C. Remarkably, the most stable variant retains measurable enzymatic activity even after exposure to temperatures as high as 95 °C, a property that is unprecedented in the field of Amadoriase enzymes and enables its use in processes requiring extreme thermal conditions. The enzyme remains fully active across a broad pH range, with optimal activity observed between pH 8.0 and 11.0, and maintains structural integrity in the presence of common biochemical reagents, including reducing agents when used under controlled conditions. Its stability is not attributable to glycosylation or other post-translational modifications, as it is produced in prokaryotic hosts and purified to homogeneity without any such modifications. The invention thus provides a robust, scalable, and commercially viable biocatalyst that overcomes the fundamental limitations of existing Amadoriase enzymes in real-world applications.

### STATE OF THE ART

- define glycation reaction

Glycation is a non-enzymatic chemical reaction that occurs spontaneously between reducing sugars and free amino groups of proteins, lipids, or nucleic acids, resulting in the formation of stable covalent adducts known as Amadori products. This process begins with the reversible formation of a Schiff base between the carbonyl group of a sugar, such as glucose or fructose, and the ε-amino group of a lysine residue or the N-terminal α-amino group of a polypeptide chain. Over time, this intermediate undergoes an irreversible rearrangement to form a more stable ketoamine structure, commonly referred to as an Amadori product. Unlike enzymatic glycosylation, glycation is unregulated and accumulates progressively in biological systems over time, particularly under conditions of hyperglycemia. The accumulation of glycated proteins is associated with structural and functional alterations in critical biomolecules, leading to the disruption of cellular signaling, impaired enzyme activity, and the formation of advanced glycation end-products that contribute to chronic inflammation and tissue damage.

- motivate HbA1c measurement for diabetes diagnosis

The measurement of glycated hemoglobin, specifically HbA1c, serves as a clinically validated biomarker for the long-term assessment of average blood glucose levels over the preceding two to three months. Because hemoglobin has a lifespan of approximately 120 days in human erythrocytes, the proportion of hemoglobin molecules that have undergone glycation at the N-terminal valine residue reflects the cumulative glycemic exposure of the individual during this period. Unlike fasting plasma glucose or oral glucose tolerance tests, which provide only a snapshot of glucose concentration at a single point in time, HbA1c offers a stable, reliable, and reproducible indicator of glycemic control that is minimally affected by short-term fluctuations in diet, stress, or physical activity. As such, HbA1c is universally recognized by international medical guidelines as the gold standard for diagnosing diabetes mellitus and for monitoring therapeutic efficacy in patients undergoing treatment for the disease.

- summarize current HbA1c measurement methods

Current methods for quantifying HbA1c rely primarily on immunoassays, chromatographic separation techniques, or enzymatic assays that exploit the specific recognition of the glycated N-terminal valine residue of hemoglobin. Immunoassays utilize antibodies that selectively bind to the glycated epitope, while chromatographic methods such as high-performance liquid chromatography separate glycated from non-glycated hemoglobin based on differences in charge or hydrophobicity. Enzymatic methods, which are increasingly favored for their specificity and compatibility with automated clinical analyzers, involve the proteolytic digestion of hemoglobin to release the glycated valine residue, followed by its selective oxidation by a fructosyl peptide oxidase or Amadoriase enzyme. This reaction generates hydrogen peroxide as a stoichiometric byproduct, which is then detected colorimetrically using a peroxidase-coupled chromogenic system. These enzymatic assays offer high precision, rapid turnaround times, and minimal interference from other hemoglobin variants, making them ideal for high-throughput clinical laboratories.

- limitations of current methods

Despite their advantages, current enzymatic methods for HbA1c detection are limited by the inherent thermal lability of the Amadoriase enzymes used in the reaction. These enzymes are prone to rapid inactivation during storage, transport, and repeated thermal cycling, leading to inconsistent assay performance, reduced shelf life, and increased costs associated with cold-chain logistics. Furthermore, the sensitivity of the enzyme to elevated temperatures restricts its utility in point-of-care devices, resource-limited settings, or industrial applications where ambient or process temperatures exceed 40 °C. The instability of the enzyme also limits its integration into biosensors or diagnostic platforms requiring long-term operational reliability. In addition, the need for frequent enzyme replacement or recalibration undermines the economic and practical feasibility of widespread deployment, particularly in developing regions where refrigeration infrastructure is unreliable.

- introduce Amadoriase enzyme for HbA1c detection

Amadoriase enzymes, particularly Amadoriase I from Aspergillus fumigatus, catalyze the oxidation of glycated amino acids such as fructosyl-valine, yielding glucosone, ammonia, and hydrogen peroxide in a stoichiometric manner. This reaction provides a direct, specific, and quantifiable signal that can be coupled to electrochemical or optical detection systems, enabling the precise measurement of glycated hemoglobin without the need for complex separation steps. Unlike immunoassays, which may cross-react with structurally similar hemoglobin variants, Amadoriase-based assays are highly specific for the Amadori product formed at the N-terminus of the β-chain of hemoglobin, minimizing false positives and improving diagnostic accuracy. The enzymatic nature of the reaction also allows for real-time kinetic monitoring, facilitating automation and integration into multiplexed diagnostic platforms.

- motivate need for improved Amadoriase stability

The clinical and commercial viability of Amadoriase-based diagnostics is critically dependent on the enzyme’s ability to retain activity under ambient and elevated temperatures. Current formulations require refrigerated storage and exhibit significant activity loss within weeks at room temperature, necessitating costly cold-chain distribution and limiting deployment in settings without reliable refrigeration. Moreover, the enzyme’s instability precludes its use in disposable diagnostic strips, wearable biosensors, or point-of-care devices that are exposed to variable environmental conditions. A thermostable variant would eliminate these limitations, enabling the development of robust, long-lasting, and cost-effective diagnostic tools that are accessible in both high-income and low-resource environments.

- application of Amadoriase in food industry

In the food industry, Amadoriase enzymes hold promise for mitigating the formation of acrylamide, a probable human carcinogen generated during the Maillard reaction between reducing sugars and the amino acid asparagine under high-temperature processing conditions such as frying, baking, and roasting. By selectively cleaving glycated amino acids prior to thermal treatment, Amadoriase can reduce the pool of Amadori intermediates that serve as precursors to acrylamide, thereby lowering its concentration in final food products without altering flavor, texture, or nutritional quality. However, the high temperatures employed in food processing—often exceeding 170 °C—render conventional Amadoriase enzymes ineffective, as they denature rapidly under such conditions. A thermostable variant capable of functioning at temperatures above 80 °C would enable its direct incorporation into food processing lines, offering a novel, enzymatic strategy for acrylamide reduction that is both safe and scalable.

- potential therapeutic use of Amadoriase

Beyond diagnostics and food safety, Amadoriase enzymes have been proposed as potential therapeutic agents for the prevention or reversal of protein glycation in vivo. The accumulation of glycated proteins in tissues is a hallmark of diabetic complications, including nephropathy, retinopathy, neuropathy, and cardiovascular disease. By catalyzing the removal of glycated adducts from circulating or tissue-bound proteins, an engineered Amadoriase could potentially reduce the burden of advanced glycation end-products and mitigate downstream inflammatory and fibrotic pathways. However, the enzyme’s natural inability to access glycated residues buried within folded proteins, combined with its thermal instability, has hindered its therapeutic development. A thermostable variant with enhanced structural resilience represents a critical first step toward enabling further engineering efforts aimed at expanding substrate accessibility and achieving in vivo efficacy.

- motivate need for heat-resistant Amadoriase variants

There exists a clear and unmet need for Amadoriase variants with significantly enhanced thermal stability that retain catalytic activity, substrate specificity, and structural integrity under conditions that would rapidly inactivate the native enzyme. Such variants would enable the development of next-generation diagnostic kits with extended shelf life, biosensors capable of continuous operation in non-controlled environments, food processing systems that integrate enzymatic acrylamide mitigation, and therapeutic platforms designed for systemic administration. The invention disclosed herein fulfills this need by providing a family of Amadoriase enzymes with precisely engineered disulfide bonds that confer exceptional resistance to thermal denaturation without compromising function, thereby unlocking a broad spectrum of previously inaccessible applications.
