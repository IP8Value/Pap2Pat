# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

Amyloid diseases represent a class of progressive, often fatal disorders characterized by the misfolding and aggregation of specific proteins into insoluble, β-sheet-rich fibrillar structures that accumulate in tissues and disrupt normal cellular function. These diseases include Alzheimer’s disease, Parkinson’s disease, Huntington’s disease, prion encephalopathies, and systemic amyloidoses, among others. The pathological hallmark of these conditions is the formation of stable, self-propagating protein aggregates that resist degradation and induce cytotoxicity through multiple mechanisms, including membrane disruption, oxidative stress, and interference with proteostasis networks. Despite decades of research, no curative therapies exist for most amyloid diseases, and current interventions remain largely symptomatic or palliative.

Alzheimer’s disease is the most prevalent neurodegenerative disorder affecting the elderly population worldwide. It is clinically defined by progressive cognitive decline, memory loss, and behavioral disturbances, and pathologically by the presence of extracellular amyloid plaques composed primarily of the 42-amino acid amyloid-β peptide (Aβ42) and intracellular neurofibrillary tangles composed of hyperphosphorylated tau protein. Aβ42 is generated through the sequential proteolytic cleavage of the amyloid precursor protein by β- and γ-secretases. While historically considered the primary toxic species, recent evidence suggests that mature amyloid fibrils may represent relatively inert end-products of the aggregation cascade. Instead, soluble, low-molecular-weight oligomers of Aβ42—particularly dimers, trimers, tetramers, and dodecamers—are now widely regarded as the principal neurotoxic entities responsible for synaptic dysfunction, neuronal death, and the initiation of inflammatory cascades in the brain.

Current research into therapeutic strategies for Alzheimer’s disease has focused extensively on inhibiting Aβ42 fibrillization. However, many compounds that successfully block fibril formation inadvertently stabilize or promote the accumulation of these soluble oligomeric intermediates, potentially exacerbating neurotoxicity rather than alleviating it. This critical limitation underscores the urgent need for screening platforms capable of specifically detecting and quantifying the earliest stages of Aβ42 assembly—the formation of SDS-stable, low-n oligomers—rather than merely monitoring downstream fibril deposition. Existing in vitro models using purified Aβ42 peptides lack the complexity of cellular environments, including chaperone networks, proteolytic systems, and membrane interactions, which profoundly influence oligomerization kinetics and stability. Moreover, mammalian cell-based assays are often costly, low-throughput, and limited in their ability to support large-scale genetic or chemical screens.

The yeast Saccharomyces cerevisiae has emerged as a powerful model organism for studying protein misfolding and aggregation due to its genetic tractability, rapid growth, conserved cellular machinery, and well-characterized prion systems. The yeast protein Sup35p, a translation termination factor, can adopt a self-templating amyloid conformation known as [PSI+], which serves as a genetically encoded readout for protein aggregation. In its prion state, Sup35p forms SDS-stable oligomers that impair its function, leading to translational readthrough of premature stop codons. This phenotypic change can be easily visualized using reporter alleles such as ade1-14, where readthrough restores adenine biosynthesis and alters colony coloration from red to white. The chaperone Hsp104 plays a central role in the propagation of [PSI+] by fragmenting large prion aggregates into smaller, transmissible seeds. Its ATPase activity is inhibited by guanidine hydrochloride, a well-established prion-curing agent that alters aggregate dynamics.

Previous attempts to model Aβ42 aggregation in yeast have relied on expressing Aβ42 alone or fused to fluorescent tags, but these approaches lack a robust, quantifiable phenotypic readout tied directly to the functional consequences of oligomerization. Furthermore, they do not exploit the intrinsic sensitivity of cellular quality control systems to modulate aggregation states in a manner amenable to high-throughput screening. There remains a critical gap in the field for a genetically encoded, phenotypically selectable, and biochemically validated yeast-based system that directly links Aβ42 oligomerization to a measurable cellular function, enabling the discovery of agents that specifically inhibit the formation of pathogenic low-n oligomers without promoting alternative toxic conformers.

## BRIEF SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The invention provides a novel yeast-based model system for identifying agents that interfere with the earliest stages of Aβ42 oligomerization, thereby enabling the discovery of therapeutic candidates for Alzheimer’s disease and related amyloidopathies. The system is based on the fusion of the Aβ42 peptide to the middle and release factor domains of the yeast translation termination factor Sup35p, creating a chimeric protein termed AβMRF. This fusion protein retains the essential translational termination function of Sup35p while acquiring the aggregation propensity of Aβ42. When Aβ42 within the fusion protein forms SDS-stable oligomers, it impairs the ability of the MRF domain to terminate translation, resulting in readthrough of a nonsense mutation in the ade1-14 reporter gene. This readthrough is phenotypically detectable as a color change from red to white on complex media and as growth on adenine-deficient media, providing a simple, quantitative, and high-throughput readout for oligomerization status.

The invention further encompasses point mutations within the Aβ42 portion of the fusion protein that disrupt key aggregation interfaces, thereby reducing oligomerization and restoring translational termination activity. These mutants serve as critical controls for validating the specificity of the assay and for distinguishing between compounds that inhibit oligomerization versus those that non-specifically alter protein expression or stability. The system is uniquely sensitive to the activity of cellular chaperones, particularly Hsp104, which interacts with the Aβ42 moiety and modulates the equilibrium between monomeric and oligomeric states of AβMRF. Deletion of HSP104 reduces total AβMRF levels and alters oligomer distribution, while treatment with guanidine hydrochloride enhances oligomer accumulation, revealing the involvement of ATP-dependent disaggregation machinery in regulating Aβ42 assembly dynamics.

The invention enables the screening of diverse chemical, genetic, and proteinaceous agents—including small organic molecules, peptides, antibodies, antisense oligonucleotides, ribozymes, and natural product libraries—for their ability to suppress Aβ42 oligomerization. Compounds that restore translational termination activity are identified by their ability to revert the white phenotype to red and inhibit growth on adenine-deficient media. The system is compatible with high-throughput screening formats and can be adapted to automated platforms. The invention further includes genetically engineered yeast strains harboring mutations in HSP104, SUP35, or other chaperone genes to modulate sensitivity to oligomerization inhibitors. Kits comprising these strains, plasmids encoding AβMRF variants, and standardized media for phenotypic assessment are provided to facilitate widespread adoption of the system for drug discovery.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

The invention comprises novel compositions and methods for the identification of therapeutic agents capable of inhibiting the formation of soluble, SDS-stable oligomers of the Aβ42 peptide, a key pathogenic event in Alzheimer’s disease. The core innovation lies in the design of a genetically encoded, phenotypically selectable yeast reporter system in which the oligomerization state of Aβ42 is directly coupled to the functional output of a cellular process—translational termination—thereby enabling the detection of inhibitors that act at the earliest stages of aggregation. The system is based on the fusion of Aβ42 to the middle and release factor domains of Sup35p, a yeast protein whose activity can be precisely monitored through the phenotypic consequences of translational readthrough at a nonsense mutation in the ade1-14 gene.

The yeast cell-based assay utilizes strains of Saccharomyces cerevisiae that lack endogenous Sup35p and are therefore dependent on a plasmid-encoded Sup35p-derived construct for viability. These strains carry the ade1-14 nonsense allele, which introduces a premature stop codon into the adenine biosynthesis pathway. When the AβMRF fusion protein exists predominantly in a monomeric, functional state, translational termination occurs efficiently, resulting in the production of a truncated, nonfunctional Ade1p protein. This leads to the accumulation of a red pigment and an inability to grow on adenine-deficient medium. In contrast, when Aβ42 induces the formation of SDS-stable oligomers within the AβMRF fusion, the MRF domain is sequestered into aggregates, impairing its termination function. This results in translational readthrough of the ade1-14 stop codon, restoration of Ade1p activity, loss of red pigment, and growth on adenine-deficient medium. The transition from red to white colonies and from non-growth to growth on selective media provides a clear, quantifiable, and easily scored phenotypic readout for oligomerization.

The assay is performed using yeast strains engineered to express AβMRF under the control of the copper-inducible CUP1 promoter, allowing precise regulation of fusion protein expression. The AβMRF construct includes an HA epitope tag between the M and RF domains to facilitate detection and immunoprecipitation. Point mutations previously shown to disrupt Aβ42 self-assembly—specifically F19T, F20T, and I31P—are introduced into the Aβ42 sequence to generate oligomerization-deficient controls (Aβm1MRF and Aβm2MRF). These variants serve as negative controls to confirm that observed phenotypic changes are attributable to Aβ42-specific aggregation rather than non-specific effects of protein overexpression or fusion tag interference.

The assay components include yeast strains with deletions in HSP104, SUP35, or other chaperone genes to probe the role of disaggregation machinery in Aβ42 oligomerization. Strains carrying mutations in HSP104 exhibit reduced AβMRF oligomerization and decreased total protein levels, indicating that Hsp104 stabilizes the fusion protein by shielding it from degradation pathways. Guanidine hydrochloride, known to inhibit Hsp104 ATPase activity, enhances AβMRF oligomerization in wild-type strains but not in hsp104Δ strains, suggesting that the compound acts on a different ATP-dependent disaggregase. This observation reveals the existence of additional cellular factors that regulate Aβ42 assembly and positions the system as a tool for identifying novel targets within the proteostasis network.

The invention further encompasses the use of combinatorial libraries of small organic compounds, peptides, nucleic acids, and natural products for high-throughput screening. Candidate agents are added to yeast cultures expressing AβMRF, and phenotypic shifts from white to red colonies or from growth to non-growth on adenine-deficient media are scored. Compounds that restore the red phenotype and inhibit growth on -Ade are selected as potential inhibitors of Aβ42 oligomerization. The system is compatible with 96-well and 384-well plate formats, enabling screening of tens of thousands of compounds per week. Structural analogs of active compounds can be synthesized and optimized using medicinal chemistry approaches to enhance potency, solubility, and blood-brain barrier permeability.

The invention also includes kits comprising genetically modified yeast strains, plasmids encoding AβMRF and its mutants, selective media, antibodies for immunoblotting and immunoprecipitation, and detailed protocols for phenotypic screening and biochemical validation. These kits are designed for use in academic, pharmaceutical, and biotechnology settings to accelerate the discovery of therapeutics targeting the earliest pathogenic events in Alzheimer’s disease.

### Model of Aβ42 Oligomerization

The activity of the Sup35p MRF domain in translational termination is assayed in vivo by monitoring the expression of the ade1-14 nonsense allele. In strains expressing a fully functional MRF domain, termination occurs efficiently, resulting in the accumulation of a red pigment on complex medium and failure to grow on adenine-deficient medium. When the MRF domain is incorporated into the AβMRF fusion protein, the presence of the Aβ42 peptide induces the formation of SDS-stable oligomers, which impair translational termination. This leads to readthrough of the ade1-14 stop codon, resulting in white colonies and growth on -Ade. Mutations in the Aβ42 region that disrupt key aggregation interfaces—F19T/F20T (Aβm1MRF) and F19T/F20T/I31P (Aβm2MRF)—restore termination efficiency, causing the cells to revert to the red, non-growing phenotype. These results demonstrate that the oligomerization state of Aβ42 directly modulates the functional capacity of the fused MRF domain, establishing a direct causal link between Aβ42 assembly and a quantifiable cellular phenotype.

### Example 2

Yeast lysates expressing AβMRF were treated with 1% SDS at room temperature and analyzed by immunoblotting. In addition to monomeric AβMRF, multiple higher molecular weight species consistent with dimers, trimers, and tetramers were detected using antibodies against the HA tag and the Aβ42 epitope. These oligomers were resistant to SDS at room temperature but dissociated into monomers upon boiling, confirming their SDS-stable nature. The oligomers were recognized by anti-Aβ antibodies, although detection efficiency decreased with increasing oligomer size, likely due to epitope occlusion within the aggregated structure. These findings confirm that AβMRF forms low-n oligomers structurally analogous to those observed in human Alzheimer’s brain tissue.

### Example 3

Point mutations in the Aβ42 region of AβMRF were introduced to disrupt aggregation interfaces. The Aβm1MRF variant, containing F19T and F20T substitutions, showed a marked reduction in SDS-stable oligomers compared to wild-type AβMRF. The Aβm2MRF variant, with an additional I31P substitution, exhibited even further reduction, retaining only trace amounts of dimeric species. These results correlate with the phenotypic restoration of translational termination, confirming that oligomerization is directly dependent on specific residues within the Aβ42 sequence and that even partial disruption of aggregation interfaces can significantly alter the aggregation landscape.

### Example 4

Treatment of AβMRF-expressing yeast with 6.3 mM guanidine hydrochloride led to a dramatic increase in SDS-stable oligomers and a corresponding depletion of monomeric AβMRF. This effect was not observed in the Aβm2MRF control, indicating specificity for the aggregation-prone form. Deletion of HSP104, in contrast, reduced the proportion of oligomers and decreased total AβMRF levels by approximately 40%. These opposing effects suggest that guanidine and Hsp104 inhibition act through distinct mechanisms: guanidine promotes oligomer accumulation, while Hsp104 deletion reduces both oligomer and monomer pools, likely by enhancing degradation.

### Example 5

Co-immunoprecipitation experiments demonstrated that Hsp104 physically interacts with AβMRF but not with the non-aggregating MRF control. This interaction was mediated by the Aβ42 portion of the fusion protein. Deletion of HSP104 resulted in reduced total AβMRF levels and altered oligomer-monomer equilibrium, suggesting that Hsp104 stabilizes AβMRF by protecting it from degradation. These findings support a model in which Hsp104 binds to Aβ42 oligomers and shields them from cellular disaggregation and degradation machinery, thereby maintaining a pool of aggregation-competent species.

### A Suppressible Allele

The ade1-14 nonsense allele provides a powerful suppressible reporter for screening compounds that inhibit Aβ42 oligomerization. Agents that restore translational termination reduce readthrough of the ade1-14 stop codon, leading to the accumulation of red pigment and inhibition of growth on adenine-deficient medium. This phenotype allows for the direct selection of compounds that prevent Aβ42-mediated impairment of Sup35p function, providing a robust, high-throughput method for identifying inhibitors of the earliest steps in Aβ42 assembly.

### Example 7

Yeast strains were constructed by replacing the native SUP35 promoter with the copper-inducible CUP1 promoter to enable controlled expression of AβMRF. The Aβ42 coding sequence was amplified by PCR and inserted into a plasmid encoding the MRF domain. Site-directed mutagenesis was used to introduce F19T, F20T, and I31P substitutions. Plasmids were shuffled into different yeast backgrounds, and protein expression was confirmed by immunoblotting. Growth assays on complex and selective media, combined with SDS-resistant oligomer detection by agarose and polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis, validated the functionality of the system. Immunoprecipitation confirmed Hsp104 binding to AβMRF, and all constructs were verified by restriction analysis and DNA sequencing.
