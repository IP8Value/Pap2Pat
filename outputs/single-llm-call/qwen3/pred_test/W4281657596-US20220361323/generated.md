## FIELD

The present invention relates generally to the field of microfabricated electronic devices for neural interfacing, and more specifically to three-dimensional multielectrode arrays fabricated through the integration of high-resolution three-dimensional printing and conventional photolithographic techniques. The invention is particularly directed to implantable neural recording and stimulation devices that feature microscale, three-dimensional electrode structures with precisely controlled geometry, high surface-area-to-footprint ratios, and electrically exposed tips for enhanced signal transduction. These devices are designed for use in chronic in vivo applications, including but not limited to electrophysiological recordings from small animal models, cortical and subcortical neural interfaces, peripheral nerve stimulation, retinal prostheses, and bioelectric medicine systems requiring high spatial resolution, low impedance, and long-term tissue integration. The invention further encompasses methods for manufacturing such devices using a scalable, wafer-level process compatible with standard thin-film semiconductor fabrication techniques.

## BACKGROUND

Traditional neural interface technologies have long relied on planar multielectrode arrays fabricated using thin-film lithography, wherein conductive traces and electrode pads are patterned on a flat substrate using photolithographic masks and metal deposition techniques. While these devices have enabled significant advances in neuroscience research and clinical neuroprosthetics, they suffer from fundamental limitations in their ability to achieve stable, high-fidelity neural recordings and efficient stimulation over extended periods. The planar geometry of these electrodes results in a low surface-area-to-footprint ratio, which necessitates higher charge densities to achieve effective stimulation thresholds, thereby increasing the risk of tissue damage and electrode corrosion. Furthermore, the flat configuration of these electrodes limits their ability to penetrate or conform to the complex topography of neural tissue, resulting in poor electrical contact, elevated impedance, and susceptibility to motion-induced signal artifacts. The formation of glial scarring around planar implants further degrades performance over time by increasing the distance between the electrode and viable neural tissue, reducing signal amplitude and increasing noise. Existing approaches to overcome these limitations, such as the use of vertically oriented pillars or microneedles fabricated on rigid silicon substrates, are constrained by their lack of flexibility, limited geometric customization, and incompatibility with flexible, biocompatible substrates required for chronic implantation. Additionally, conventional lift-off and etching techniques are ill-suited for creating intricate, high-aspect-ratio structures with submicron precision, and the integration of such structures with underlying electrical traces remains a significant engineering challenge. The inability to selectively metallize three-dimensional polymer scaffolds without compromising their structural integrity or electrical connectivity has further hindered the development of next-generation neural interfaces. There remains a critical unmet need for a manufacturing platform capable of producing customizable, high-aspect-ratio, three-dimensional electrode arrays with precisely controlled geometry, integrated electrical pathways, and biocompatible insulation—without sacrificing scalability, reproducibility, or compatibility with standard microfabrication workflows.

## SUMMARY

The present invention introduces a novel class of three-dimensional multielectrode arrays fabricated through the synergistic integration of direct laser writing and photolithographic processes. These arrays comprise a flexible or rigid substrate upon which a plurality of three-dimensional electrode structures are formed, each structure featuring a base portion, a shaft portion, and a tip portion, wherein the tip portion is electrically exposed and metallized for neural recording or stimulation. The three-dimensional structures are fabricated using a raster-scanning direct laser writing system capable of printing submicron-resolution polymer architectures from a photosensitive resin, enabling the creation of conical, pyramidal, spike-shaped, or mesh-like geometries with aspect ratios exceeding 10:1. Each structure is electrically connected to underlying metal traces on the substrate through a precisely defined electrical contact region formed during the fabrication process. The base portion of each structure is bonded to the substrate via a region of enhanced adhesion, while the shaft and tip portions extend perpendicularly from the substrate with a height ranging from 50 micrometers to 1 millimeter. The spacing between adjacent three-dimensional electrodes is maintained at intervals between 20 micrometers and 500 micrometers, enabling high-density array configurations suitable for volumetric neural recording. The three-dimensional structures are printed directly onto a substrate pre-patterned with sacrificial metal layers defining the locations of electrical traces and contact windows. A metal layer, comprising titanium and platinum, is then conformally deposited over the entire structure, followed by selective removal of the sacrificial metal layer to expose the underlying electrical traces and define the conductive pathways. An insulating layer, such as parylene C, is subsequently applied over the entire device, with the insulating material selectively removed from the tip portions of the three-dimensional structures using femtosecond laser ablation to expose the underlying conductive metal surface. The resulting three-dimensional multielectrode array exhibits a surface-area-to-footprint ratio that is at least fivefold greater than that of conventional planar electrodes, enabling lower impedance, reduced stimulation thresholds, and enhanced signal-to-noise ratios. The substrate is composed of a biocompatible polymer such as polyimide or a silicon wafer with a thermal oxide layer, and the metal traces are formed using sputtered chromium or titanium. The method enables the fabrication of arrays with customizable electrode heights, geometries, and spacings, allowing for precise tailoring to the anatomical features of target neural tissue. The process is fully compatible with standard microfabrication equipment and can be performed at wafer scale, enabling high-throughput production of complex, high-resolution neural interfaces.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

Multielectrode arrays have long served as critical tools for interfacing with neural tissue, enabling both the recording of electrical activity from populations of neurons and the delivery of controlled electrical stimuli for therapeutic neuromodulation. Despite their widespread use, traditional multielectrode arrays fabricated via thin-film lithography remain fundamentally limited by their planar geometry, which restricts their ability to achieve intimate contact with the irregular topography of neural tissue. These planar devices suffer from high impedance, low charge injection capacity, and susceptibility to tissue encapsulation, all of which degrade recording quality and stimulation efficiency over time. The present invention overcomes these limitations by introducing a three-dimensional multielectrode array architecture in which conductive electrode structures are raised above the substrate plane, forming a three-dimensional scaffold that penetrates and conforms to the neural microenvironment. These structures are fabricated using a combination of three-dimensional printing and photolithographic processing, enabling unprecedented control over electrode geometry, density, and electrical connectivity. The fabrication process begins with the preparation of a substrate, which may be composed of a flexible polymer such as polyimide or a rigid silicon wafer with a thermally grown oxide layer. Electrical traces and contact windows are defined on the substrate using standard photolithography, wherein a sacrificial metal layer—typically chromium—is deposited and patterned to delineate the future conductive pathways. This sacrificial layer serves as a temporary mask for subsequent three-dimensional printing and is later removed to expose the underlying metal traces. A liquid photopolymer, such as OrmoComp®, is applied to the substrate and polymerized in a precise, voxel-by-voxel manner using a raster-scanning direct laser writing system. This system employs a femtosecond laser source focused through a high-numerical-aperture objective lens, enabling the direct writing of three-dimensional polymer structures with feature sizes below one micrometer. The printer is equipped with real-time imaging capabilities that allow for automated surface localization, ensuring robust adhesion between the printed structure and the substrate. After printing, the entire substrate is subjected to a non-directional sputtering process, during which a thin layer of titanium and a thicker layer of platinum are deposited uniformly over both the polymer structures and the underlying metal traces. The sacrificial metal layer is then selectively etched away using a chromium etchant, leaving behind only the electrically connected three-dimensional electrode structures and the underlying conductive traces. A biocompatible insulating layer, such as parylene C, is deposited over the entire device to prevent unintended electrical coupling with surrounding tissue. Finally, the insulating material is removed from the tips of the three-dimensional structures using femtosecond laser ablation, exposing the underlying platinum surface to create a functional recording or stimulation interface. This ablation process is performed with micron-scale precision, preserving the intricate geometry of the electrode tips—such as conical, pyramidal, or mosquito-proboscis-inspired shapes—while avoiding thermal damage or redeposition of material. The resulting device exhibits a high surface-area-to-footprint ratio, enabling low-impedance neural interfacing with reduced stimulation voltages and enhanced signal-to-noise ratios. The three-dimensional architecture also promotes tissue integration through the presence of pores, internal channels, or surface roughness features that encourage cellular ingrowth and vascularization, thereby stabilizing the implant against micromotion and fibrotic encapsulation. The method allows for the customization of electrode height, spacing, and geometry to match the anatomical features of specific neural regions, such as the cortical layers of the mouse brain or the retinal layers of the eye. The entire fabrication process is scalable, compatible with standard microfabrication tools, and applicable to both rigid and flexible substrates, making it suitable for a wide range of chronic implantable neural interfaces.
