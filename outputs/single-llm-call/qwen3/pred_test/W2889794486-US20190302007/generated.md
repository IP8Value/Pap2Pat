## BACKGROUND

- Optical extinction and absorption spectroscopy have served as foundational analytical techniques for over a century, enabling the characterization of molecular composition in liquids, solids, and biological media through the measurement of light attenuation across the ultraviolet to visible spectrum. These methods rely on the assumption that the reduction in transmitted light intensity is attributable solely to absorption by molecular species within the medium. However, in practice, the measured extinction is a composite of both absorption and scattering phenomena, where scattering arises from refractive index mismatches between structural elements in the sample, such as cellular organelles, colloidal particles, or aggregated nanoparticles. Unlike absorption, which is an intrinsic molecular property governed by electronic transitions, scattering is an extrinsic phenomenon dependent on the physical morphology, size distribution, and spatial arrangement of heterogeneities within the medium. This distinction renders conventional UV-VIS spectrophotometry fundamentally inadequate for quantifying true molecular absorption in complex, turbid environments, where scattering contributions can exceed absorption by orders of magnitude, leading to grossly inflated and misleading extinction values.

- UV-VIS spectrophotometry, as implemented in standard laboratory instruments, employs a collimated beam of light that traverses a sample contained within a cuvette, with transmitted intensity measured by a detector positioned opposite the source. The resulting spectrum is interpreted as the logarithmic ratio of incident to transmitted irradiance, commonly referred to as optical extinction. However, this measurement fails to distinguish between photons that are absorbed—thereby converting energy into heat—and those that are redirected by scattering events, which remain within the sample volume but do not reach the detector. As a result, even in simple mixtures of molecular absorbers and inert scatterers, the measured extinction spectrum deviates significantly from the sum of the individual absorption and scattering spectra, violating the principle of additivity and rendering molecular decomposition impossible. This limitation is particularly acute in biological samples, nanoparticle suspensions, polymer films, and tissue analogs, where heterogeneous microstructures introduce strong, wavelength-dependent scattering that obscures the underlying absorption signature.

- To mitigate the influence of scattering, integrating sphere systems have been developed to capture all transmitted and scattered light, thereby enabling the calculation of total attenuation coefficients. However, these systems require precise alignment, are sensitive to sample geometry and positioning, and necessitate extensive calibration across the spectral range. Furthermore, they are incompatible with standard cuvette-based workflows and cannot be readily integrated into routine analytical platforms. Alternative approaches, such as time-resolved measurements using ultra-short laser pulses, attempt to separate absorption from scattering by analyzing the temporal broadening of light propagation through the medium. These methods, while theoretically powerful, demand femtosecond or picosecond laser sources with broad tunability, high-speed detectors, and complex signal processing algorithms that are neither commercially accessible nor practical for routine use. Moreover, they are only valid under conditions of diffusive light transport, which excludes many nanoscale and thin-film systems where ballistic or quasi-ballistic propagation dominates.

- Gold nanoparticles, due to their tunable plasmonic resonances, exhibit intense absorption and scattering features that are highly sensitive to size, shape, aggregation state, and local dielectric environment. These properties make them indispensable in biomedical applications ranging from targeted drug delivery and photothermal therapy to molecular sensing and imaging. However, conventional UV-VIS spectrophotometry cannot reliably determine the true absorption component of their extinction spectra, especially when particles are clustered or internalized within cells, where additional scattering from cellular components further complicates interpretation. Photothermal spectroscopy, which detects thermal lensing induced by absorbed light, offers a partial solution by correlating beam deflection with absorption. Yet, this technique remains limited to dilute suspensions and is highly susceptible to background scattering, as the fluence distribution is altered by the medium’s optical properties, invalidating the assumption of uniform energy deposition. Consequently, a critical gap persists in the ability to measure the intrinsic absorption spectrum of optically active agents embedded within highly scattering environments without requiring invasive coupling, complex modeling, or specialized instrumentation.

## SUMMARY

- A non-contact photoacoustic spectrophotometry system is disclosed that enables the accurate, scattering-insensitive measurement of optical absorption in complex media, independent of the presence or magnitude of light scattering. The system comprises a tunable time-modulated light source capable of emitting nanosecond laser pulses across a broad spectral range, a non-contact optical detector configured to sense ultrasound transients generated at the surface of the sample without physical contact, an interferometric sensor for high-sensitivity detection of these acoustic signals, and a processor for signal acquisition, calibration, and spectral reconstruction. The tunable time-modulated light source is implemented as a diode-pumped, wavelength-switchable laser with pulse repetition rates exceeding one kilohertz and switching times under one millisecond, enabling rapid spectral acquisition. The non-contact detector employs a fiber-optic Sagnac interferometer that detects minute surface displacements induced by photoacoustic waves, with the probe beam operating at a wavelength of 1550 nm and coupled to a reflective coating on the sample chamber’s upper surface. The interferometer is configured to provide phase-sensitive detection with sub-nanometer resolution, allowing the amplitude of the photoacoustic signal to be directly correlated with the local absorption coefficient. In one embodiment, the system includes a quartz plate assembly that confines the sample in a thin diaphragm, ensuring uniform excitation geometry and minimizing edge effects. In another embodiment, the system integrates a broadband mirror and beam diameter control to maintain consistent fluence across the spectral range, while a synchronized XY translation stage ensures spatial averaging over the sample volume to mitigate heterogeneity and sedimentation. The method for constructing an absorption spectrum involves sequentially exciting the sample at discrete wavelengths, detecting the resulting photoacoustic response, and converting the signal amplitude into an absorption coefficient using a calibration standard with known absorption properties, thereby producing a scattering-independent absorption spectrum that accurately reflects the molecular fingerprint of the analyte.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

- The photoacoustic effect arises when absorbed optical energy is converted into localized thermal expansion, generating a pressure wave that propagates as an ultrasound transient. In conventional photoacoustic spectroscopy, this signal is detected using piezoelectric transducers in direct contact with the sample, which limits compatibility with standard optical platforms and introduces mechanical coupling artifacts. The disclosed system overcomes this limitation by employing a non-contact detection scheme in which the photoacoustic wave induces a transient displacement on the surface of a transparent, reflective quartz plate that forms the upper boundary of the sample chamber. This displacement is detected by a low-power, continuous-wave probe laser beam that reflects off the plate’s coated surface and interferes with a reference beam within a Sagnac interferometer, converting mechanical motion into a measurable phase shift. The amplitude of the resulting interferometric signal is directly proportional to the amount of light absorbed by the sample, and critically, it is insensitive to the scattering properties of the medium, as the photoacoustic signal originates solely from the absorbed fraction of incident light, regardless of how the unabsorbed portion is redistributed. This distinguishes the method from conventional UV-VIS spectrophotometry, which conflates absorption and scattering into a single extinction value, and from photothermal techniques, which assume uniform fluence distribution—a condition violated in scattering media.

- The extinction A, defined as the logarithmic ratio of incident to transmitted irradiance, is commonly used in radiometry to quantify overall light attenuation. However, in scattering media, extinction does not equate to absorption, nor is it governed by the Bouguer-Lambert-Beer law, which assumes a linear, unidirectional attenuation process. Scattering redistributes photons spatially, altering the effective path length and fluence distribution, thereby invalidating the assumptions underlying Beer’s law. Conventional UV-VIS spectrophotometers measure extinction, not absorption, and thus yield spectra that are distorted by the presence of scatterers, even when the absorber concentration remains constant. In contrast, the non-contact photoacoustic method measures the true absorption coefficient μₐ, which represents the fraction of incident photons converted into heat, independent of scattering. This is demonstrated in experiments with cupric sulfate solutions containing polystyrene microspheres: while UV-VIS extinction increases linearly with scatterer concentration, the photoacoustic signal remains unchanged, confirming that the method isolates the intrinsic absorption of the molecular species. Similarly, for gold nanorods, the photoacoustic spectrum reveals a distinct absorption profile that diverges significantly from the extinction spectrum, particularly for larger particles where scattering dominates. The method further enables accurate measurement of absorption spectra for nanoparticles internalized within cells, where both the particles and cellular structures contribute to scattering. Here, the photoacoustic signal captures the true absorption characteristics of the nanoparticles, even as their plasmonic resonance shifts due to clustering and endosomal confinement, providing a molecular-level readout of their intracellular state.

- The system is configured with a tunable pulsed laser source operating between 700 and 900 nm, with pulse energies attenuated to prevent thermal damage and nonlinear effects. The laser beam is focused to a spot size of approximately 0.5 mm, ensuring that the excitation volume remains smaller than the transport mean free path of photons in the medium, thereby maintaining a linear relationship between absorbed energy and signal amplitude. The sample is contained within a diaphragm formed between two quartz plates, with the upper plate coated with a reflective layer optimized for 1550 nm to facilitate interferometric detection. A continuous-wave probe laser is coupled into a Sagnac interferometer, which provides high sensitivity and common-mode noise rejection. The interferometer output is digitized and processed by a computer system that synchronizes laser firing with the movement of an XY translation stage, ensuring spatial averaging over the sample volume. Each wavelength measurement is acquired over thousands of laser pulses, with data collected at 10 nm intervals to construct a high-resolution absorption spectrum. Calibration is performed using a reference solution of cupric sulfate with a known absorption coefficient, enabling absolute quantification of μₐ in units of cm⁻¹. The system’s non-contact nature allows seamless integration with standard cuvette-based workflows, and its compatibility with transparent containers permits use in clinical, industrial, and research settings without modification of existing sample handling protocols.

- The method is applicable to a wide range of materials, including biological tissues, nanoparticle suspensions, colloidal systems, and thin films. It enables the characterization of plasmonic nanoparticles in their native environments, facilitating the optimization of photothermal therapies, the development of label-free biosensors, and the accurate quantification of contrast agents in vivo. The system’s ability to resolve true absorption spectra in the presence of intense scattering opens new avenues for point-of-care diagnostics, flow cytometry, and spatially resolved spectroscopy, where conventional optical methods fail. The disclosed system does not require fluorescent labeling, complex modeling, or invasive coupling, and it operates under ambient conditions with minimal sample preparation.
