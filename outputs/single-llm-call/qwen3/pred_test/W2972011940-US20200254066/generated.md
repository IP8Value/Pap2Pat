## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

- motivate thymus regeneration

The progressive involution of the thymus gland is a fundamental and irreversible feature of human aging, resulting in a dramatic decline in the production of naïve T lymphocytes and a corresponding collapse of the T-cell receptor repertoire. This process begins in early adulthood and accelerates after the age of 50, leading to a state of immunosenescence characterized by diminished immune surveillance, increased susceptibility to infection, cancer, and autoimmune disorders, and chronic low-grade inflammation known as inflammaging. The loss of thymic function is not merely a consequence of aging but a primary driver of age-related morbidity and mortality, as evidenced by the strong correlation between thymic mass and longevity in human populations. Centenarians consistently exhibit preserved thymic architecture and elevated levels of naïve T cells, suggesting that maintenance of thymic activity is a key determinant of healthy aging. Restoration of thymic function, therefore, represents a compelling therapeutic target for extending healthspan and reducing the burden of age-related disease.

- limitations of prior art medications

Prior approaches to counteracting immunosenescence have largely relied on exogenous cytokine administration, such as interleukin-7 or thymosin α1, which demonstrate limited efficacy in humans due to short half-lives, poor tissue penetration, and the inability to restore the thymic epithelial microenvironment necessary for T-cell maturation. Other strategies, including stem cell transplantation or thymus tissue engineering, remain experimental, prohibitively expensive, and unsuitable for broad clinical application. Pharmacological interventions targeting general aging pathways, such as rapamycin or senolytics, show promise in preclinical models but lack robust evidence of thymic regeneration in humans and carry significant risks of immunosuppression or metabolic disruption. None of these approaches have demonstrated the ability to simultaneously reverse epigenetic aging, reduce systemic inflammation, and restore functional immune cell diversity in a coordinated manner.

- problem with growth hormone

Growth hormone has long been recognized for its thymotrophic properties in animal models and its ability to stimulate thymic epithelial cell proliferation and T-cell output in immunocompromised populations such as HIV patients. However, its clinical use in aging has been severely limited by its potent induction of insulin resistance and hyperinsulinemia, which elevate the risk of metabolic syndrome, type II diabetes, and potentially accelerate tumorigenesis through downstream activation of the IGF-1 signaling pathway. These adverse effects have led to widespread caution among clinicians and regulatory bodies, effectively preventing the use of growth hormone as an anti-aging intervention despite its compelling biological rationale.

- diabetogenic effect of GH

The diabetogenic effect of growth hormone arises from its antagonism of insulin signaling in peripheral tissues, particularly skeletal muscle and adipose tissue, leading to increased hepatic glucose production and reduced glucose uptake. This results in compensatory hyperinsulinemia, which, over time, promotes adiposity, dyslipidemia, and pancreatic β-cell exhaustion. In the context of aging, where insulin sensitivity is already declining, growth hormone administration without metabolic countermeasures creates a dangerous metabolic imbalance that outweighs its potential immunological benefits.

- metabolic syndrome and type II diabetes

Metabolic syndrome and type II diabetes are among the most prevalent age-related conditions, affecting over 40% of individuals over the age of 60. These conditions are not merely comorbidities but active contributors to accelerated aging, promoting oxidative stress, endothelial dysfunction, chronic inflammation, and cellular senescence. The presence of insulin resistance significantly diminishes the capacity of immune cells to respond to antigenic challenge and impairs thymic stromal cell function, creating a vicious cycle that exacerbates immunosenescence. Therefore, any therapeutic strategy aimed at reversing aging must address metabolic dysfunction as a core component.

- Fahy's method

Prior attempts to mitigate the metabolic side effects of growth hormone have included the use of calorie restriction, intermittent fasting, or metformin monotherapy. While metformin has demonstrated insulin-sensitizing effects in preclinical and clinical studies, its use alone does not restore thymic mass or reverse epigenetic aging. The novel combination approach described herein, which integrates growth hormone with dehydroepiandrosterone and metformin in a precisely timed and monitored regimen, represents a paradigm shift from isolated pharmacological interventions to a systems-level restoration of endocrine and metabolic homeostasis.

- DHEA and glucose tolerance

Dehydroepiandrosterone, a naturally occurring adrenal steroid precursor, has been shown to improve insulin sensitivity, reduce visceral adiposity, and enhance mitochondrial function in aging humans. Unlike growth hormone, DHEA does not induce hyperinsulinemia; rather, it modulates glucose metabolism by enhancing peripheral glucose uptake and suppressing hepatic gluconeogenesis. Its ability to counteract the diabetogenic effects of growth hormone without suppressing IGF-1 production makes it an ideal companion agent in this therapeutic strategy.

- GH administration and cancer risk

The theoretical concern that growth hormone administration might promote cancer through IGF-1-mediated mitogenic signaling has been a major barrier to its clinical adoption in aging populations. However, recent evidence suggests that the relationship between IGF-1 and cancer risk is not linear but U-shaped, with both excessively low and high levels associated with increased mortality. Furthermore, the context of insulin sensitivity and immune surveillance plays a critical role: in the presence of restored thymic function and reduced systemic inflammation, the mitogenic effects of IGF-1 may be counterbalanced by enhanced immune surveillance and improved clearance of pre-malignant cells.

- IGF-1 and cancer risk

While elevated IGF-1 levels have been correlated with increased incidence of certain cancers in epidemiological studies, these associations often fail to account for confounding factors such as obesity, sedentary lifestyle, and chronic inflammation. In controlled clinical settings where insulin sensitivity is maintained and immune function is restored, IGF-1 appears to exert protective effects by enhancing T-cell proliferation, promoting thymic epithelial cell survival, and supporting tissue repair mechanisms. The invention herein leverages this nuanced understanding by using growth hormone not to maximize IGF-1, but to optimize its biological context.

- DHEA and prostate cancer

DHEA has been the subject of controversy regarding its potential to increase prostate cancer risk due to its conversion to androgens. However, clinical data from long-term supplementation studies show no increase in prostate-specific antigen (PSA) or incidence of prostate cancer in older men. In fact, the present invention demonstrates a significant and sustained reduction in total PSA and an increase in free PSA, indicating a favorable shift in prostate cancer risk profile. This suggests that the combination of DHEA with metformin and growth hormone may exert a net protective effect on prostate tissue, possibly through modulation of local androgen metabolism and suppression of inflammatory pathways.

- thymus regeneration vs "inflammaging"

Inflammaging—the chronic, low-grade, sterile inflammation that characterizes aging—is both a consequence and a driver of thymic involution. As thymic function declines, the loss of negative selection and the accumulation of senescent T cells contribute to the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-6 and TNF-α. Conversely, systemic inflammation directly suppresses thymopoiesis by altering the thymic stromal microenvironment. This bidirectional relationship creates a self-reinforcing loop that accelerates immune and systemic aging. The invention breaks this cycle by simultaneously restoring thymic architecture and reducing inflammatory mediators, thereby achieving a dual therapeutic effect.

- concern about exacerbating inflammaging

A major concern in any immune-boosting therapy is the potential to exacerbate inflammaging by activating existing senescent or autoreactive immune clones. The present invention directly addresses this concern by demonstrating a significant and sustained reduction in C-reactive protein, IL-6, and CD38+ monocytes—key markers of inflammaging—following treatment. The observed decline in these markers, coupled with the increase in naïve T cells and reduction in exhausted PD-1+ CD8 T cells, indicates that the therapy does not merely activate the immune system but reprograms it toward a more youthful, balanced, and regulated state.

- cytokine storm

The risk of cytokine storm, a life-threatening systemic inflammatory response, is a legitimate concern in any immunomodulatory therapy. However, the present regimen does not induce a broad, uncontrolled activation of immune cells. Instead, it selectively enhances thymic output and promotes the differentiation of naïve T cells while reducing the proportion of pro-inflammatory monocytes and exhausted T cells. The absence of elevated IL-6 or other acute-phase cytokines during treatment, along with the sustained reduction in CRP, provides strong evidence that the therapy avoids the risk of cytokine storm.

- clinical trial results

The clinical trial results described herein demonstrate, for the first time, that a short-term, three-agent regimen can reverse multiple biomarkers of human aging, including epigenetic age, thymic fat content, immune cell composition, and metabolic parameters. These results were achieved without serious adverse events and with persistent benefits observed for months after treatment cessation. The invention is not merely an extension of prior art but a novel, integrated, and clinically validated therapeutic strategy for reversing human aging.
