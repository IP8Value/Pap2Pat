## BACKGROUND OF THE DISCLOSURE

Current imaging techniques employed during transcatheter interventions suffer from fundamental limitations that impede precise navigation and spatial awareness within the cardiac anatomy. Fluoroscopy, while widely available and capable of real-time visualization, provides only two-dimensional projections of three-dimensional structures, resulting in a complete absence of depth perception. This forces interventionalists to mentally reconstruct the spatial relationships between catheters, devices, and intracardiac anatomy by correlating multiple orthogonal views, a process that is inherently error-prone, time-consuming, and cognitively demanding. The transparency of soft tissue under standard fluoroscopic energy levels further exacerbates this challenge, necessitating the use of iodinated contrast agents that transiently opacify chambers and vessels but introduce additional risks, including nephrotoxicity and allergic reactions, while offering no persistent anatomical context. Echocardiography, though capable of direct tissue visualization and blood flow assessment, requires specialized equipment and highly trained operators, limiting its utility in many clinical settings and introducing variability in image quality and interpretation. Preoperative imaging modalities such as computed tomography and magnetic resonance imaging provide high-resolution, volumetric representations of cardiac anatomy but are acquired under static, non-interventional conditions and are typically displayed on conventional two-dimensional monitors, which fail to convey true spatial relationships. Overlaying these static three-dimensional models onto real-time fluoroscopic images has been attempted, but such hybrid displays often obstruct the primary fluoroscopic field of view, introduce visual clutter, and lack seamless integration into the procedural workflow. Furthermore, existing augmented reality systems developed for orthopedic or neurosurgical applications are unsuitable for cardiac interventions due to their inability to provide real-time quantitative feedback on device position, their lack of compatibility with the dynamic physiological motion of the heart, and their failure to account for the unique anatomical and radiological constraints of thoracic interventions. The absence of a robust, integrated, and intuitive method for fusing preoperative anatomical data with intraoperative device tracking has resulted in prolonged procedure times, increased radiation exposure, and suboptimal procedural outcomes, particularly in complex interventions requiring millimeter-level precision such as transseptal puncture or left atrial appendage closure.

## SUMMARY OF THE DISCLOSURE

A novel image guidance method is disclosed that enables real-time, three-dimensional augmented reality visualization of transcatheter devices relative to a patient’s individual cardiac anatomy during percutaneous interventional procedures. The method begins by receiving a first image data set acquired via preoperative computed tomography, which contains volumetric information of the patient’s thoracic anatomy including the heart and spine. From this data set, a detailed three-dimensional model of the anatomical target, such as the left atrium or a specific cardiac valve, is generated through automated segmentation and post-processing techniques. Concurrently, a three-dimensional model of the spine is extracted from the same data set, serving as a stable anatomical reference point. During the procedure, a fluoroscopic imaging device captures a second image data set in the form of one or more real-time two-dimensional fluoroscopic projections. From each fluoroscopic image, a binary mask of the spine is generated through adaptive thresholding, noise reduction, and morphological filtering to isolate the vertebral structures. A three-dimensional model of the medical device, such as a catheter or guidewire, is then constructed by detecting its position in the fluoroscopic images using edge detection, gradient analysis, and centerline identification algorithms. A transformation matrix is determined by registering the spine mask from the fluoroscopic image with the corresponding two-dimensional projection of the three-dimensional spine model derived from the preoperative scan, using a Fourier-Mellin transform-based phase correlation method that decouples and quantifies scale, rotation, and translation parameters. This transformation enables the precise co-registration of the fluoroscopic coordinate system with the preoperative three-dimensional anatomical coordinate system. The medical device model is then registered into this unified coordinate system, allowing its position and orientation to be accurately rendered in real time as a three-dimensional holographic overlay within the patient’s anatomical model. The final output image is generated as a stereoscopic augmented reality display, projecting the fused anatomical and device models into the operator’s field of view through a wearable heads-up display. Variations of the method include the use of multiple fluoroscopic angles to triangulate the three-dimensional position of the device, the incorporation of motion correction algorithms that synchronize image acquisition with the cardiac cycle, and the integration of voice-activated commands or gesture recognition to manipulate the displayed models during the procedure.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The disclosed system comprises a comprehensive image guidance architecture designed for integration into the interventional suite. The system includes a first imaging device, typically a multi-slice computed tomography scanner, configured to capture high-resolution preoperative volumetric image data sets of the patient’s thorax. A second imaging device, such as a C-arm fluoroscopy system, is mounted on a mobile gantry and calibrated to capture real-time two-dimensional fluoroscopic projections from multiple angular orientations during the procedure. The system further includes a server-based processing unit equipped with a model generator, a segmentation engine, a registration engine, a motion correction engine, a display generator, and a database. The model generator receives the preoperative computed tomography data and applies intensity-based thresholding, morphological operations, and manual refinement to generate a three-dimensional mesh model of the anatomical target and the spine. The segmentation engine processes each fluoroscopic image by applying Gaussian smoothing, Otsu’s adaptive thresholding, and morphological closing to isolate the spine, producing a binary mask that is invariant to variations in contrast and noise. The universal fiducial marker—the spine—is identified in both modalities due to its rigid structure, minimal physiological motion, and consistent radiographic appearance across imaging platforms. The registration engine employs a Fourier-Mellin transform technique, converting the spine mask and its corresponding projection into polar-logarithmic coordinates to decouple scale and rotation, then applying phase correlation to determine the optimal transformation matrix that aligns the two coordinate systems. The motion correction engine captures fluoroscopic images during the diastolic inter-beat interval to minimize motion artifacts induced by cardiac contraction, improving registration fidelity. The display generator receives the registered models and the real-time position of the medical device, rendering them as stereoscopic holograms on a heads-up augmented reality display such as a Microsoft HoloLens. The database stores the preoperative image data sets, segmented spine and target models, transformation matrices, fluoroscopic image sequences, and a training library of anatomical variations for algorithm refinement. The medical device, typically a 12-French catheter, is equipped with radiopaque markers positioned along its length to enhance visibility under fluoroscopy. The position of the catheter is determined by detecting its centerline through iterative region-of-interest advancement, edge detection, and peak identification of intensity gradients perpendicular to its axis. The three-dimensional location of the catheter is calculated by triangulating its two-dimensional positions from two distinct fluoroscopic projections, using the known angular displacement between the C-arm positions to solve for depth. The system generates a secondary view that overlays the catheter’s trajectory onto cross-sectional slices of the anatomical model, providing additional spatial context. Operational sequences begin with preoperative imaging, followed by model generation, intraoperative image acquisition, spine segmentation, registration, device localization, and real-time holographic display. The system supports multiple implementations, including standalone procedural guidance, training simulations, and remote expert consultation, and is scalable to other anatomical targets and device types. The order of operations is strictly maintained to ensure geometric fidelity, with registration preceding device rendering and motion correction applied prior to image acquisition. The scope of the invention encompasses all variations of the method and system components, including those employing machine learning for segmentation, alternative fiducial markers, or different augmented reality platforms, provided the core principles of spine-based registration and holographic fusion are preserved.
