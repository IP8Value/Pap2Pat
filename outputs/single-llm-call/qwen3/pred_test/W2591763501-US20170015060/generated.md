## BACKGROUND

Additive manufacturing has evolved into a transformative technology capable of producing complex, high-performance structures with unprecedented geometric freedom and material control. Traditional manufacturing methods for fiber-reinforced composites, such as hand layup, autoclave curing, and compression molding, are inherently limited by their reliance on pre-formed fiber architectures and batch processing, which restrict design flexibility and increase production time and cost. The state of technology in this field has long been constrained by the inability to precisely orient discontinuous fibers within a thermoset matrix during real-time fabrication, resulting in isotropic or poorly controlled mechanical and electrical properties. U.S. Pat. No. 6,299,810 describes a method for fabricating composite structures using filament winding and resin infusion, but it is confined to continuous fiber reinforcement and requires rigid mandrels, limiting its applicability to axisymmetric geometries and precluding the creation of arbitrary three-dimensional microarchitectures. Carbon fiber composite manufacturing has historically depended on the alignment of continuous fibers through tensioning and winding techniques, which are incompatible with the intricate internal features and non-uniform fiber distributions required for next-generation aerospace, automotive, and electronic components. The lathe assembly used in such processes is mechanically rigid and incapable of dynamic reconfiguration, preventing the spatial modulation of fiber orientation across a single part. Resin material application in conventional systems is typically passive, relying on capillary action or vacuum infusion, which results in uneven wetting, void formation, and inconsistent fiber-matrix bonding. Carriage movement in prior systems is restricted to linear or rotational motion along fixed axes, precluding the multi-directional deposition necessary for true 3D microstructural control. Mandrel design in traditional methods imposes geometric constraints that prevent the fabrication of internal channels, hollow geometries, or topology-optimized lattices. Heating elements in prior systems are external and non-uniform, leading to thermal gradients that induce residual stresses and dimensional instability during cure. Release agent application is often manual and inconsistent, introducing contamination risks and complicating post-processing. The winding process, while effective for cylindrical parts, cannot be adapted to non-circular or multi-scale architectures without extensive tooling changes. Chaos elements, such as unpredictable fiber entanglement or resin phase separation, have historically been unavoidable due to the lack of rheological control in high-fiber-loading systems. The vacuum chamber process in prior art is employed solely for degassing or consolidation, not for active fiber alignment or interfacial enhancement. Compression and curing are typically performed in bulk, without localized control, resulting in non-uniform crosslinking density and compromised mechanical integrity. The finishing process remains labor-intensive, requiring extensive machining, sanding, or surface treatment to achieve dimensional tolerances. U.S. Published Patent Application No. 20140361460 discloses a method for printing fiber-reinforced thermoplastics using fused deposition modeling, but it is fundamentally limited to thermoplastic matrices that lack the thermal stability, chemical resistance, and mechanical performance required for high-performance applications. The supplying of unmelted composite filament in this system is incompatible with thermoset resins, which do not melt and re-solidify but instead undergo irreversible chemical crosslinking. The heating and melting process described therein is irrelevant to thermoset systems, which require catalytic curing rather than thermal softening. The ironing force application in that system is designed to compact thermoplastic layers, a mechanism that cannot be replicated in a viscous, non-melting epoxy matrix. The three-dimensional printer design in prior art is optimized for low-viscosity thermoplastics and lacks the pressure control, nozzle geometry, and flow dynamics necessary for high-fiber-loading thermoset inks. The print head design in existing systems is incapable of maintaining fiber alignment during extrusion due to insufficient shear gradients and poor fluid-particle coupling. The build platen design fails to accommodate the thermal expansion differentials between the printed structure and substrate during cure, leading to warpage and delamination. The controller function in prior systems is limited to positional control and lacks real-time feedback for rheological adaptation, fiber orientation tracking, or cure state monitoring.

## SUMMARY

The present invention introduces a novel apparatus, system, and method for the additive manufacturing of high-performance carbon fiber/epoxy composites with precisely controlled fiber alignment, spatially variable loading, and orthotropic physical properties. Unlike prior art, this invention enables the direct, real-time fabrication of complex three-dimensional structures using a thermoset resin system loaded with high-aspect-ratio carbon fibers at volume fractions exceeding one percent, without phase separation or nozzle clogging. The limitations of prior art, including the inability to align discontinuous fibers in thermoset matrices, the lack of control over local fiber orientation, the incompatibility of conventional print heads with high-viscosity, shear-sensitive inks, and the absence of integrated thermal curing protocols, are overcome by a uniquely configured extrusion system that combines a modified direct ink writing platform with a precisely engineered resin delivery mechanism and a catalytic curing sequence. The inventor’s apparatus and systems incorporate a multi-stage print head with a fiber core shaper, a resin chamber operating under atmospheric pressure, and a satellite head for real-time resin application, enabling the continuous, uniform impregnation of carbon fibers prior to extrusion. The print head and resin system are designed to maintain the thixotropic rheology of the ink throughout the extrusion process, ensuring that fibers remain suspended and aligned under shear while allowing the material to solidify immediately upon deposition. The additive manufacturing process employs a computer-controlled, seven-stage sequence that governs fiber pre-impregnation, nozzle extrusion, filament deposition, and post-print curing, allowing for the creation of structures with spatially tailored mechanical, thermal, and electrical properties. Industrial applications of this invention span aerospace, defense, automotive, and electronics, where lightweight, high-stiffness, and directionally responsive components are critical. The scope of the invention encompasses not only the apparatus and its components but also the method of fabricating composite structures with controlled orthotropy, the formulated ink compositions, and the computational models used to predict and optimize fiber alignment during extrusion.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF SPECIFIC EMBODIMENTS

High-performance carbon fiber/epoxy composites have long been the material of choice for structural applications demanding exceptional strength-to-weight ratios and thermal stability. However, current manufacturing processes, such as autoclave curing and hand layup, are constrained by their inability to precisely orient discontinuous fibers within a thermoset matrix, resulting in isotropic or poorly optimized mechanical responses. The potential of additive manufacturing and three-dimensional printing technologies lies in their capacity to deposit material with spatial control, yet existing systems for additively manufacturing continuous carbon fiber epoxy composites remain limited to thermoplastic matrices or low-fiber-loading systems that cannot achieve the mechanical performance of conventional composites. Commercial epoxy resin systems are typically formulated for two-part mixing and ambient cure, rendering them incompatible with the long shelf life and rapid thermal cure profiles required for additive manufacturing. The MarkForged 3D system, while capable of printing continuous fiber reinforcements, relies on thermoplastic encapsulation and lacks the ability to directly process aromatic thermoset epoxies, which are essential for high-temperature applications. The inventor’s apparatus, systems, and methods overcome these limitations by integrating a novel direct ink writing platform with a catalytically activated epoxy resin system and a precisely engineered print head architecture. The three-dimensional printing process begins with the preparation of a carbon fiber/epoxy ink containing 3 to 8 volume percent chopped carbon fibers, dispersed in a bisphenol-F diglycidyl ether oligomer modified with colloidal silica to impart thixotropic behavior. The print head, comprising a nozzle with a conical taper and internal fiber core shaper, is mounted on a computer-controlled, seven-axis positioning stage capable of sub-micron precision. The extruded material, upon exiting the nozzle, retains the shear-induced alignment of carbon fibers, forming a self-supporting filamentary structure with a diameter of 250 to 610 micrometers. The computer controller, synchronized with the print head and resin delivery system, governs the entire fabrication sequence, including pressure modulation, extrusion speed, and post-deposition thermal curing. The product creation process involves the sequential application of resin, fiber, and catalyst, followed by immediate deposition and subsequent thermal cure at 80°C for 12 hours, resulting in a fully dense, crosslinked composite architecture. FIG. 2 illustrates the multi-layered, unidirectional printing of a di-parabolic rocket nozzle, demonstrating the ability to vary fiber orientation and loading across the structure. The print head, designated as element 106, contains a nozzle 108 with an internal diameter of 400 micrometers and a conical transition from a 5-millimeter cylindrical barrel. The fiber core shaper 122, positioned upstream of the nozzle, ensures uniform fiber distribution and alignment prior to resin impregnation. The resin chamber 120, maintained at atmospheric pressure, allows for continuous resin flow without the need for pressurized reservoirs, minimizing shear-induced degradation. FIG. 3 depicts the internal geometry of the print head, including the fiber feed reel 123, which supplies carbon fiber to the core shaper, and the drawn fiber 102 path, which maintains tension and alignment during transport. The satellite head 124, positioned adjacent to the print head, delivers supplemental resin to specific regions of the build to modulate local fiber loading and mechanical properties. The system 100 functions through seven distinct stages: first, the carbon fiber core is pre-impregnated with resin in a controlled environment; second, the print head body remains open to atmospheric pressure to prevent bubble entrapment; third, a computer-controlled, three- to six-axis direct ink writing stage precisely positions the nozzle; fourth, the fiber-resin composite is extruded through the nozzle, inducing shear alignment; fifth, the fiber feed reel maintains consistent tension and feed rate; sixth, the drawn fiber path is stabilized by a guiding mechanism to prevent lateral displacement; and seventh, the satellite head applies localized resin to modify material composition. The system functionality is further enhanced by a resin bath and vacuum process that removes entrapped air and ensures complete fiber wetting prior to extrusion. The scope of the application encompasses the apparatus, the method of operation, the formulated ink compositions, and the computational models used to predict fiber alignment during extrusion, all of which together enable the fabrication of composites with unprecedented orthotropic properties.
