## BACKGROUND

Superconducting circuits have emerged as a leading platform for the implementation of quantum information processing due to their scalability, compatibility with established microfabrication techniques, and rapid progress in coherence times and gate fidelities. These circuits exploit the macroscopic quantum behavior of superconducting materials to encode quantum bits in the quantized energy levels of engineered Josephson junction networks. Among the most widely adopted architectures are transmon qubits, which achieve robustness against charge noise through capacitive shunting of Josephson junctions, thereby reducing sensitivity to fluctuations in background charge. However, this design comes at the cost of reduced anharmonicity, which fundamentally limits the speed and fidelity of single-qubit operations by constraining the allowable drive amplitudes to avoid leakage into higher energy states. Alternative architectures, such as flux qubits, offer superior anharmonicity but suffer from extreme sensitivity to magnetic flux noise, rendering them impractical for large-scale quantum computation without significant mitigation strategies. The development of fluxonium qubits, which incorporate a large superinductor to suppress flux sensitivity while preserving strong anharmonicity, has opened a new pathway toward high-coherence, high-fidelity quantum devices. Recent advances have further extended this architecture through the introduction of heavy-fluxonium circuits, wherein a large shunting capacitor is added to reduce the coupling strength between the qubit and dissipative charge environments, thereby enhancing relaxation times. These developments have positioned heavy-fluxonium as a compelling candidate for next-generation quantum processors, particularly in regimes where conventional qubits face fundamental limitations imposed by material imperfections and environmental noise.

## SUMMARY

Fluxonium qubits offer a unique combination of large anharmonicity and suppressed charge sensitivity, making them inherently more resilient to decoherence than transmon qubits while retaining the ability to perform fast quantum operations. The key advantage of the fluxonium architecture lies in its rich energy level structure, which features a low-frequency computational subspace separated by gigahertz-scale gaps from higher-energy plasmon-like states. This spectral separation enables the use of high-amplitude flux drives without inducing unwanted transitions outside the computational manifold, a critical requirement for achieving high-fidelity gates. Conventional control techniques relying on microwave drives are severely limited in fluxonium systems due to the vanishingly small charge matrix elements between the ground and first excited states, which render Rabi oscillations prohibitively slow and require excessive drive power that exacerbates decoherence. To overcome this limitation, we introduce a novel class of ultrafast flux-based control protocols that exploit nonadiabatic Landau-Zener transitions to perform single-qubit rotations within a single Larmor period, achieving gate speeds comparable to those of state-of-the-art transmons despite a qubit frequency three orders of magnitude lower. These protocols rely on rapid, symmetric flux pulses that induce controlled Bloch vector rotations in the lab frame, eliminating the need for rotating wave approximations and rendering the operations inherently robust against pulse shape distortions and low-frequency flux noise. Furthermore, the large energy gaps to excited states enable a novel initialization mechanism that leverages the readout resonator as an entropy sink, facilitating rapid cooling of the qubit to effective temperatures far below the ambient bath temperature. Readout is achieved through a plasmon-assisted dispersive scheme that transfers population from the computational states to higher-energy levels with stronger dispersive shifts, thereby amplifying the measurement signal without requiring direct coupling between the qubit and the resonator. Together, these methods enable universal single-qubit control, high-fidelity state preparation, and efficient measurement—all within a single coherent framework—thereby establishing a complete and scalable quantum control protocol for heavy-fluxonium qubits. The approach is broadly applicable to any superconducting qubit with a low-frequency computational subspace and well-separated higher-energy states, and it opens the door to quantum computing at elevated temperatures and with simplified control hardware.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The heavy-fluxonium qubit comprises a small-area Josephson junction shunted by a large superinductor and a large capacitor, forming a nonlinear circuit whose Hamiltonian is dominated by the interplay between Josephson energy, inductive energy, and charging energy. The qubit operates in a regime where the transition frequency between the ground and first excited states lies in the megahertz range, while the energy separation to the second and third excited states exceeds several gigahertz. This spectral arrangement ensures that the computational subspace is isolated from higher-energy transitions under typical control conditions, enabling the use of strong flux drives without leakage. The qubit is capacitively coupled to a readout resonator with a resonant frequency of approximately 5.7 GHz, chosen to be far detuned from the qubit transition to minimize heating while still allowing for dispersive readout via higher-energy states. The resonator is designed with a low quality factor of approximately 600 to facilitate rapid photon decay, which is essential for the initialization protocol. The external magnetic flux threading the superconducting loop is precisely controlled to bias the qubit near the flux-frustration point, where the wavefunctions of the ground and first excited states become symmetric and antisymmetric superpositions across the two potential wells, maximizing the phase matrix element and minimizing first-order sensitivity to flux noise. The ground state |g⟩ and first excited state |e⟩ form the computational basis, with an energy splitting Δ₁ of approximately 14 MHz. The second and third excited states, denoted |f⟩ and |h⟩, exhibit plasmon-like character due to their dominant charging energy contributions, and their transition frequencies are tuned to be near the resonator frequency to enable strong dispersive coupling. Spontaneous photon emission from the |e⟩ state to the |g⟩ state is suppressed due to the small matrix element, but transitions from |f⟩ to |e⟩ and from |h⟩ to |g⟩ are enhanced by orders of magnitude, enabling efficient entropy extraction. The qubit’s state is initialized by simultaneously driving the |g⟩|0⟩ → |h⟩|0⟩ and |h⟩|0⟩ → |e⟩|1⟩ transitions for a duration of 15 microseconds, during which the resonator rapidly emits a photon due to its low quality factor, driving the system into a steady state with 97% population in the |e⟩|0⟩ state. A subsequent π pulse on the |g⟩–|e⟩ transition then prepares the qubit in the ground state |g⟩|0⟩. For readout, a π pulse is applied to transfer population from |e⟩ to |f⟩, after which the resonator’s frequency shift is measured, yielding a dispersive signal proportional to χ_f – χ_g, which is five times larger than the direct χ_e – χ_g shift. This plasmon-assisted readout scheme enables single-shot readout fidelity of approximately 50%, with potential for further improvement through parametric amplification and resonator optimization. The qubit’s coherence properties are dominated by dielectric loss in the shunting capacitor near the flux-frustration point, with a measured T₁ of 315 microseconds and T₂e of 300 microseconds, both exceeding those of state-of-the-art transmons. Dephasing is minimized at the flux-frustration point due to first-order insensitivity to 1/f flux noise, and the spin-echo decay is well described by a product of exponential and Gaussian functions, indicating the presence of both white and low-frequency noise contributions. Single-qubit gates are implemented using fast, zero-area flux pulses consisting of two symmetric triangular spikes separated by an idle period. The first spike induces a rotation about the x-axis via a large σₓ term, the idle period induces a z-rotation due to the qubit frequency, and the second spike induces an equal but opposite x-rotation, resulting in a net rotation about the y-axis. The duration of each spike is approximately 4.76 nanoseconds, and the idle period is tuned to achieve precise π/2 rotations, enabling the construction of arbitrary single-qubit unitaries. The entire gate sequence is completed within 70 nanoseconds, corresponding to one Larmor period, and randomized benchmarking yields average gate fidelities exceeding 99.8%. The flux pulses are generated using a digital-to-analog converter and are immune to shape distortions due to their reliance on integrated area rather than instantaneous amplitude. The method is extendable to two-qubit gates via inductive coupling and is compatible with existing circuit QED architectures, enabling scalable quantum computation without reliance on high-frequency microwave hardware.

## CONCLUSION

The heavy-fluxonium qubit represents a paradigm shift in superconducting quantum computing by demonstrating that qubits operating at megahertz frequencies can achieve coherence times and gate fidelities that surpass those of conventional gigahertz-frequency transmons. By leveraging a low-frequency computational subspace separated from high-energy plasmon states, this architecture enables unprecedented control through ultrafast flux pulses, efficient initialization via resonator-assisted entropy extraction, and enhanced readout through state-selective population transfer. The combination of long T₁ and T₂e times, sub-nanosecond gate durations, and robustness to pulse distortions establishes the heavy-fluxonium as a viable and superior alternative to the transmon for large-scale quantum processors. The control scheme is compatible with existing fabrication processes and can be implemented using inexpensive digital hardware, eliminating the need for high-bandwidth microwave sources. Furthermore, the principles underlying this invention—nonadiabatic control, plasmon-assisted readout, and resonator-mediated cooling—are broadly applicable to other low-frequency qubit platforms, including protected qubits and hybrid architectures. The demonstrated performance, achieved without material optimization or exotic fabrication techniques, suggests that further improvements in coherence and fidelity are achievable through improved dielectric materials, reduced parasitic losses, and refined pulse shaping. This invention thus provides a complete, scalable, and high-performance framework for quantum computation that redefines the operational regime of superconducting circuits and opens new pathways toward practical quantum advantage.

## COMBINATION OF FEATURES

A method for initializing a quantum system comprising a fluxonium qubit is disclosed, wherein the fluxonium qubit is coupled to a readout resonator with a resonant frequency of approximately 5.7 GHz and a quality factor less than 1000. The method comprises simultaneously threading the fluxonium qubit with a time-varying external magnetic flux, exciting the qubit-resonator system with two microwave fields, and coupling the qubit to the resonator for a duration of 15 microseconds, wherein the first microwave field drives the transition between the ground state and the third excited state, and the second microwave field drives the transition between the third excited state and the first excited state, such that the system evolves into a steady state with at least 95% population in the first excited state. The method further comprises applying a π pulse with a frequency matching the energy splitting between the ground state and the first excited state to transfer the population to the ground state, thereby preparing the fluxonium qubit in a pure quantum state. The quantum system is cryogenically cooled to a temperature below 50 millikelvin prior to initialization, and the metastable state occupation is reduced to less than 5% following the initialization protocol. The method may be repeated to maintain the qubit in the ground state during repeated computational cycles. A method for measuring the state of a fluxonium qubit is disclosed, wherein the fluxonium qubit is dispersively coupled to a readout resonator, and the measurement comprises applying a π pulse to transfer population from the first excited state to the second excited state, followed by measuring the resonator’s frequency shift, wherein the dispersive shift between the second excited state and the ground state is at least five times greater than the dispersive shift between the first excited state and the ground state. A method for rotating the state of a fluxonium qubit is disclosed, wherein a sequence of magnetic flux pulses is applied to the qubit, the sequence comprising a first pulse that induces a rotation of the Bloch vector about an x-axis, an idling period during which the qubit undergoes a rotation about a z-axis due to its intrinsic energy splitting, and a second pulse that induces an equal and opposite rotation about the x-axis, wherein the net flux change over the entire sequence is zero, and wherein the duration of each pulse is less than 10 nanoseconds and the total sequence duration is less than one Larmor period of the qubit. The magnetic flux pulses are generated using a digital-to-analog converter and are shaped as symmetric triangular waveforms to minimize high-order harmonics and suppress sensitivity to pulse distortions. The method may be employed to implement any single-qubit unitary operation by combining sequences of x- and z-rotations, and the qubit is operated at an external magnetic flux value corresponding to the flux-frustration point, where the qubit frequency is first-order insensitive to flux noise. The method is applicable to any superconducting qubit with a low-frequency computational subspace and higher-energy plasmon states separated by gigahertz-scale gaps.
