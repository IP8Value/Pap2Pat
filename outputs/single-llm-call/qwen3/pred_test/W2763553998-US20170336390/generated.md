## BACKGROUND

- introduce existing methods of interrogating biological systems  
Biological systems have long been interrogated through a variety of analytical techniques designed to probe molecular structure, dynamics, and interactions in native or near-native environments. These methods aim to capture the conformational states of biomolecules under physiological conditions, providing insights that static structural techniques such as X-ray crystallography or cryo-electron microscopy may not fully reveal due to their reliance on non-physiological sample preparation or immobilization.

- describe genome analysis  
Genome analysis provides a comprehensive catalog of genetic sequences but offers no direct information regarding the functional state of encoded proteins, their post-translational modifications, or their three-dimensional conformations in solution. While genomic data can predict potential variants or mutations, it cannot determine whether these alterations lead to structural perturbations that affect protein function or ligand binding.

- describe exome analysis  
Exome analysis focuses on the protein-coding regions of the genome and enables the identification of coding variants associated with disease. However, like whole-genome sequencing, it remains silent on the dynamic structural behavior of proteins in their native milieu, offering no insight into solvent accessibility, folding intermediates, or allosteric transitions that govern biological activity.

- describe transcriptome analysis  
Transcriptome analysis quantifies the abundance of RNA transcripts and provides a snapshot of gene expression levels. While useful for identifying upregulated or downregulated genes, it does not reflect the actual protein concentrations, folding states, or spatial organization of the encoded polypeptides. Many transcripts are subject to translational regulation, and protein structure is not encoded in mRNA sequence.

- describe proteome analysis  
Proteome analysis seeks to characterize the full complement of proteins present in a biological sample, often through mass spectrometry-based approaches. While powerful in identifying protein identities and relative abundances, conventional proteomic methods typically require denaturation, enzymatic digestion, and extensive sample processing that erase critical structural information. These methods are ill-suited for detecting subtle conformational changes that occur in response to ligand binding, environmental stress, or molecular interactions.

- describe limitations of existing methods  
Existing methods for probing protein structure in solution suffer from significant limitations. Hydrogen deuterium exchange mass spectrometry requires rapid quenching at low pH and cryogenic temperatures to prevent back-exchange, making it technically demanding and prone to artifacts. Chemical footprinting reagents such as hydroxyl radical generators often rely on expensive infrastructure, hazardous chemicals, or prolonged exposure times that risk protein denaturation. Synchrotron-based radiolysis is inaccessible to most laboratories, while laser-based methods such as FPOP introduce photochemical side reactions and require the addition of hydrogen peroxide, which may alter the native chemical environment. None of these methods provide a simple, scalable, benchtop-compatible platform capable of generating precisely timed, sub-millisecond bursts of reactive species without exogenous additives, while preserving protein integrity and enabling high-resolution mapping of solvent accessibility across the entire polypeptide chain.

## SUMMARY

- introduce plasma-induced oxidation method  
A novel method for modifying biological molecules in solution has been developed, utilizing plasma-induced oxidation to generate transient bursts of hydroxyl radicals directly from water, without the need for added chemical reagents or specialized infrastructure. This method enables the rapid, controlled, and spatially resolved labeling of solvent-accessible amino acid side chains in proteins, lipids, nucleic acids, and other biomolecules under physiological conditions.

- describe modifying biological molecules in a sample  
Biological molecules within a liquid sample are modified through the interaction of reactive radical species generated by a plasma discharge with exposed residues on the molecular surface. The modification is covalent, stable, and detectable via mass spectrometry, providing a quantitative readout of solvent accessibility that correlates with three-dimensional structure.

- describe generating plasma in a fluid  
Plasma is generated within a fluid sample by applying a high-voltage, pulsed electrical signal across a dielectric barrier positioned between a grounded electrode and a plasma-emitting electrode. The plasma forms as a transient discharge that propagates through the liquid phase, initiating the dissociation of water molecules into hydroxyl radicals and other reactive oxygen species.

- describe converting marker radical precursors into marker radicals  
In embodiments where enhanced radical yield is desired, a marker radical precursor such as hydrogen peroxide, nitrate, or ozone may be introduced into the fluid, where it is rapidly converted into hydroxyl radicals or other reactive intermediates upon exposure to the plasma discharge. The conversion occurs within microseconds, ensuring minimal diffusion and spatial confinement of radical generation.

- describe modifying biological molecules using marker radicals  
The generated marker radicals diffuse through the aqueous medium and react selectively with solvent-exposed amino acid side chains, including methionine, tryptophan, tyrosine, phenylalanine, cysteine, and histidine. The reaction is fast, irreversible, and proportional to the duration and intensity of plasma exposure, enabling dose-dependent labeling that reflects molecular surface topology.

- describe method for modifying multiple biological molecules  
The method is capable of simultaneously modifying multiple distinct biological molecules within a single sample, including proteins, nucleic acids, and glycoconjugates, without cross-reactivity or preferential targeting. Each molecule is labeled according to its individual solvent accessibility profile, allowing for comparative structural analysis of complex mixtures.

- describe determining solvent accessibility of biological molecules  
Solvent accessibility is determined by quantifying the extent of covalent modification at specific residues following plasma exposure. Regions that are buried or structurally constrained exhibit minimal modification, while solvent-exposed regions show proportional increases in oxidation. Comparison of modification levels under native versus denatured conditions enables the identification of protected structural elements.

- describe assessing biological samples using plasma-induced oxidation  
Biological samples, including cell lysates, purified proteins, intact cells, and tissue homogenates, may be assessed using plasma-induced oxidation to map structural changes induced by ligand binding, post-translational modification, or environmental stress. The method preserves molecular integrity and enables real-time monitoring of conformational dynamics.

- describe system for modifying biological molecules using plasma  
A system for modifying biological molecules using plasma includes a sample chamber, a pair of electrodes separated by a dielectric barrier, a high-voltage pulse generator, a fluid delivery mechanism, and a control system configured to regulate pulse duration, frequency, voltage amplitude, and sample temperature. The system is compact, benchtop-compatible, and operable without cryogenic or vacuum conditions.

- describe system for modifying biological molecules using plasma jet  
In an alternative embodiment, a plasma jet is employed, wherein a gaseous feedstream containing a marker radical precursor is directed through a nozzle and ionized to form a focused plasma plume that impinges upon the surface of a liquid sample. The jet enables spatially selective modification and is particularly suited for surface probing or microscale applications.

- describe composition of matter including biological molecule and marker radical precursor  
A composition of matter comprises a biological molecule dissolved in an aqueous buffer containing a marker radical precursor selected from the group consisting of hydrogen peroxide, sodium nitrate, potassium nitrite, or ozone. The composition is stable prior to plasma exposure and undergoes controlled oxidation upon application of a pulsed plasma discharge.

- describe composition of matter including synthetic biological molecule  
A synthetic biological molecule engineered to contain a predictable pattern of oxidation-prone residues, such as methionine or tryptophan, is provided as a calibration standard for quantifying plasma-induced oxidation rates. The synthetic molecule exhibits a linear, dose-dependent modification profile that correlates with radical flux.

- describe kit for plasma-induced oxidation  
A kit for plasma-induced oxidation includes a plasma generation device, a set of pre-calibrated reference samples, a vial of marker radical precursor, a buffer solution optimized for biomolecule stability, and a user manual detailing operational parameters, safety protocols, and data interpretation guidelines.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

- define scope of invention  
The invention encompasses methods, systems, compositions, and kits for modifying biological molecules through plasma-induced oxidation, with particular application to the determination of solvent accessibility, conformational dynamics, and molecular interactions in native environments. The scope includes all variations in plasma generation parameters, sample types, detection modalities, and analytical interpretations that yield structural information from covalent labeling patterns.

- describe terminology and interpretation  
As used herein, “plasma-induced oxidation” refers to the covalent modification of biomolecules resulting from exposure to reactive species generated by a plasma discharge in an aqueous medium. “Solvent accessibility” denotes the degree to which a residue or region of a biomolecule is exposed to the surrounding solvent, as inferred from the extent of oxidative modification. “Marker radical precursor” refers to any chemical species that, upon interaction with plasma, generates hydroxyl radicals or other reactive intermediates capable of modifying biomolecules. “Native state” refers to the biologically active, folded conformation of a biomolecule under physiological conditions.

- introduce modifying biological molecules  
Biological molecules are modified through the transient generation of reactive oxygen species within a liquid sample, without the need for exogenous chemical initiators or prolonged exposure times. The modification occurs selectively at solvent-exposed residues, preserving the overall structural integrity of the molecule and enabling high-resolution mapping of conformational states.

### Methods

- provide various methods  
Various methods are provided for modifying biological molecules using plasma-induced oxidation, including batch processing, flow-through systems, and microfluidic integration. Each method is optimized for sample volume, throughput, and resolution.

- describe method of modifying biological molecule  
A biological molecule is placed in an aqueous buffer and subjected to a series of microsecond-duration plasma pulses generated by applying a high-voltage signal across a dielectric barrier. The plasma discharge dissociates water molecules into hydroxyl radicals, which diffuse into the sample and react with solvent-accessible amino acid side chains. The reaction is quenched by cooling or dilution, and the modified molecule is analyzed by mass spectrometry.

- generate plasma in fluid or sample  
Plasma is generated directly within the fluid containing the biological molecule, ensuring uniform radical distribution and minimizing diffusion artifacts. The plasma forms as a transient discharge that does not exceed 10 microseconds in duration, preventing thermal denaturation or nonspecific damage.

- wait a length of time  
After each plasma pulse, a waiting period of at least 100 microseconds is allowed to permit radical recombination and dissipation, ensuring that each pulse acts as an independent, discrete labeling event.

- convert marker radical precursors into marker radicals  
When a marker radical precursor is present, it is rapidly converted into hydroxyl radicals during the plasma discharge, enhancing labeling efficiency without altering the temporal profile of radical generation.

- diffuse or transport marker radicals into sample  
The marker radicals diffuse through the aqueous medium at rates consistent with Brownian motion, ensuring that modification occurs uniformly across the sample volume.

- interact with biological molecule  
The radicals interact with amino acid side chains based on their solvent accessibility, with buried residues exhibiting minimal reactivity and surface-exposed residues undergoing proportional oxidation.

- modify biological molecule  
Covalent modifications include the conversion of methionine to methionine sulfoxide, tryptophan to hydroxytryptophan, and tyrosine to dihydroxyphenylalanine, among others, each detectable by mass spectrometry.

- generate plasma from plasma jet  
In an alternative embodiment, a plasma jet is formed by ionizing a gaseous feedstream containing a marker radical precursor, which is directed onto the surface of the sample to enable localized modification.

- generate single plasma pulse or sequence of pulses  
The system may be configured to deliver a single plasma pulse or a sequence of pulses, with pulse frequency ranging from 1 Hz to 15 kHz, enabling fine control over total radical dose.

- set voltage for plasma generation  
The voltage applied to the plasma electrode is adjustable between 1 kV and 31 kV, allowing modulation of radical flux and labeling intensity.

- set pulse width and frequency for sequence of pulses  
Pulse widths are maintained between 1 and 10 microseconds, and pulse frequencies are set between 100 Hz and 15 kHz to optimize labeling efficiency while minimizing thermal accumulation.

- set total length of time for sequence of pulses  
The total exposure time is controlled from 0 to 120 seconds, with increments of 1 second, enabling precise dose-response calibration.

- configure to generate concentration of marker radicals  
The system is configured to generate a steady-state concentration of marker radicals during continuous pulsing, with peak concentrations ranging from 10 nM to 1 µM, depending on feedgas composition and pulse parameters.

- set peak concentration of marker radicals  
Peak concentrations of hydroxyl radicals are calibrated using methionine oxidation standards, with a minimum generation rate of 350 nmol/sec.

- set average concentration of marker radicals  
Average radical concentrations are maintained below 50 nM to prevent over-oxidation and preserve structural fidelity.

- elevate temperature of sample  
The sample may be elevated to temperatures up to 40°C to enhance diffusion kinetics, provided that thermal denaturation is prevented.

- limit temperature elevation to prevent denaturation  
A Peltier cooling system maintains sample temperature between 2°C and 4°C during plasma exposure, ensuring that protein structure remains intact.

- transfer energy to sample  
Energy transfer from the plasma to the sample is minimized through dielectric insulation and short pulse durations, preventing bulk heating or electrochemical side reactions.

- set volume of sample  
Sample volumes range from 1 µL to 1 mL, with optimal performance achieved at 100 µL.

- use gaseous feedgas with marker radical precursor  
In jet-based embodiments, the feedgas contains a marker radical precursor such as nitrogen dioxide or ozone at concentrations between 0.1% and 5% by volume.

- set concentration of marker radical precursor in feedgas  
The concentration of the marker radical precursor in the feedgas is optimized to maximize radical yield without inducing side reactions or sample contamination.

- use sample with marker radical precursor  
The sample may contain a dissolved marker radical precursor at concentrations between 1 µM and 1 mM, enhancing labeling efficiency for low-abundance targets.

- set concentration of marker radical precursor in sample  
The concentration of the marker radical precursor in the sample is adjusted to achieve a 2- to 5-fold increase in oxidation rate without altering the kinetics of radical diffusion.

- modify plurality of biological molecules  
The method modifies multiple biological molecules simultaneously, enabling comparative analysis of protein complexes, nucleic acid-protein interactions, or lipid membranes.

- use single sample with multiple biological molecules  
A single sample containing multiple biological molecules may be analyzed in parallel, with each molecule labeled according to its unique solvent accessibility profile.

- use multiple samples with single biological molecule each  
Multiple samples containing identical biological molecules may be treated under varying plasma conditions to generate dose-response curves for structural calibration.

- select biological molecule type  
The biological molecule may be a protein, nucleic acid, polysaccharide, lipid, or glycoconjugate, provided it is soluble in aqueous buffer.

- select sample type  
Sample types include purified proteins, cell lysates, tissue homogenates, live cells, and extracellular vesicles.

- use buffer solution with sample  
The sample is suspended in a physiological buffer such as phosphate-buffered saline, HEPES, or ammonium acetate, with pH maintained between 6.5 and 8.0.

- select buffer solution type  
Buffer selection is based on compatibility with plasma discharge and stability of the biological molecule under oxidative conditions.

- set pH value of buffer solution  
The pH of the buffer is maintained at 7.2 to ensure optimal radical reactivity and prevent protein aggregation or denaturation.

- control level of oxidation and damage  
Oxidation levels are controlled by adjusting pulse duration, frequency, and voltage, with a maximum oxidation threshold established to prevent irreversible structural damage.

- monitor using control sample  
A control sample lacking the biological molecule is processed in parallel to subtract background oxidation signals.

- determine structural information about biological molecule  
Structural information is derived from the spatial pattern of oxidation across the polypeptide chain, with protected regions identified by low modification rates and exposed regions by high modification rates.

- determine if portion of biological molecule is accessible to solvent  
A portion of the biological molecule is determined to be solvent-accessible if it exhibits a dose-dependent increase in oxidation relative to a control sample treated under identical conditions but without plasma exposure.

- assess oxidation levels  
Oxidation levels are quantified using mass spectrometry, with precursor ion intensities of modified and unmodified species compared to calculate percent modification per residue.

- generate report on solvent accessibility  
A report is generated detailing the solvent accessibility of each residue, mapped onto a three-dimensional structural model of the molecule.

- introduce cleavage factor to expose solvent inaccessible portions  
A proteolytic cleavage factor such as trypsin or Lys-C is introduced to digest the molecule, exposing previously buried regions for secondary oxidation analysis.

- oxidize biological molecules in first and second subsamples  
The biological molecule is divided into first and second subsamples, with the first subjected to plasma oxidation in its native state and the second oxidized after proteolytic digestion.

- assess difference in oxidation levels  
The difference in oxidation levels between the two subsamples identifies residues that were buried in the native state and became accessible upon cleavage.

- identify solvent inaccessible portions  
Residues exhibiting minimal oxidation in the native state but significant oxidation after digestion are identified as solvent-inaccessible.

- generate report on solvent inaccessible portions  
A report is generated identifying solvent-inaccessible regions, which correspond to structurally constrained domains, binding interfaces, or hydrophobic cores.

- use cleavage factor to expose all solvent inaccessible portions  
A combination of proteases and chemical denaturants may be used to fully expose all buried regions, enabling comprehensive structural mapping.

- select cleavage factor type  
Cleavage factors include trypsin, Lys-C, Glu-C, or chemical denaturants such as urea or guanidine hydrochloride.

- assess using mass spectrometry, gel electrophoresis, or sequencing  
Oxidation patterns are assessed using mass spectrometry for residue-level resolution, gel electrophoresis for size-based fragmentation analysis, or sequencing for nucleic acid targets.

### Systems

- provide systems suitable for use with methods and compositions  
Systems are provided for the controlled generation of plasma-induced oxidation, including integrated hardware, software controls, and safety features.

- describe system for modifying a biological molecule  
A system for modifying a biological molecule comprises a sample chamber, a ground electrode, a plasma electrode, a high-voltage power supply, a control system, and a cooling mechanism.

- introduce sample chamber  
The sample chamber is constructed from inert, non-conductive materials such as polytetrafluoroethylene or quartz and is sized to accommodate volumes from 1 µL to 1 mL.

- describe ground electrode  
The ground electrode is composed of stainless steel or platinum and is positioned beneath the sample chamber to establish a stable electrical reference.

- describe plasma electrode  
The plasma electrode is a needle-shaped conductor made of tungsten or stainless steel, positioned 0.5 to 2 mm above the sample surface.

- introduce power supply  
The power supply delivers pulsed high-voltage signals ranging from 1 kV to 31 kV with adjustable pulse widths of 1 to 10 microseconds.

- describe control system  
The control system is a microprocessor-based unit that regulates pulse frequency, duration, voltage amplitude, and sample temperature via feedback loops.

- introduce amplifier  
An amplifier boosts the voltage signal to ensure consistent plasma formation across varying sample conductivities.

- describe dielectric  
A dielectric layer of alumina or borosilicate glass is positioned between the plasma electrode and the sample to prevent arcing and ensure uniform discharge.

- describe sample chamber shape  
The sample chamber is cylindrical or rectangular, with a flat bottom to facilitate even plasma exposure.

- describe sample chamber size  
The sample chamber has an internal diameter of 5 mm and a depth of 10 mm, optimized for 100 µL volumes.

- describe sample chamber material  
The sample chamber is fabricated from polytetrafluoroethylene to resist chemical degradation and plasma erosion.

- describe ground electrode material  
The ground electrode is composed of platinum-coated stainless steel to ensure long-term stability and low corrosion.

- describe plasma electrode material  
The plasma electrode is made of tungsten due to its high melting point and resistance to oxidation.

- describe plasma source point  
The plasma source point is located at the tip of the plasma electrode, where the electric field is maximized to initiate discharge.

- describe plasma electrode shape  
The plasma electrode is needle-shaped with a tip radius of 50 µm to concentrate the electric field and promote stable plasma formation.

- describe plasma electrode translation device  
A motorized translation device allows precise vertical positioning of the plasma electrode relative to the sample surface, with resolution of 1 µm.

- describe sample chamber translation device  
A motorized stage enables lateral movement of the sample chamber to enable multi-point sampling or spatial mapping.

- describe control system components  
Control system components include a touchscreen interface, programmable logic controller, temperature sensor, and real-time data acquisition module.

- describe user interface  
The user interface provides graphical controls for setting pulse parameters, viewing real-time oxidation data, and exporting reports in standard formats.

- describe analytical device  
The analytical device is a mass spectrometer equipped with electrospray ionization and Orbitrap or TOF detection for high-resolution modification analysis.

- describe sample hopper  
A sample hopper holds up to 96 pre-loaded samples and is compatible with robotic handling for high-throughput applications.

- describe automated loading and transfer  
Automated pipetting and sample transfer systems enable unattended processing of multiple samples with minimal human intervention.

- describe optimization of operational parameters  
Operational parameters are optimized using machine learning algorithms trained on oxidation response curves from reference proteins.

- describe reference samples and kits  
Reference samples include purified methionine, bovine serum albumin, and synthetic peptides with known oxidation profiles.

- describe plasma jet  
The plasma jet is a nozzle-based system that directs a stream of ionized gas onto the sample surface, enabling localized modification.

- describe plasma jet translation device  
A robotic arm translates the plasma jet across the sample surface to enable spatially resolved modification mapping.

- describe cooling device  
A Peltier cooling device maintains sample temperature between 2°C and 4°C during plasma exposure.

- describe sample chamber holder  
The sample chamber holder is constructed from anodized aluminum and includes thermal insulation to minimize heat transfer.

- describe protective housing  
The entire system is enclosed in a grounded, shielded housing to prevent electromagnetic interference and ensure operator safety.

- describe door  
A interlocked door prevents plasma activation unless the housing is fully closed.

- describe gas manifold  
A gas manifold delivers feedgas to the plasma jet at controlled flow rates of 0.1 to 5 L/min.

- describe temperature sensor  
A thermocouple embedded in the sample chamber continuously monitors temperature and triggers shutdown if thresholds are exceeded.

- describe power supply components  
Power supply components include a high-voltage transformer, pulse modulator, and capacitor bank for energy storage and rapid discharge.

- describe feedback control  
Feedback control adjusts pulse parameters in real time based on measured oxidation levels from reference samples.

- describe sample layer of oil  
In certain embodiments, a thin layer of mineral oil is applied atop the sample to suppress evaporation and stabilize plasma discharge.

## EXAMPLE 1

### Labeling Cytochrome C

- setup plasma generation system  
A plasma generation system was assembled with a tungsten needle electrode positioned 1 mm above a 100 µL sample of cytochrome c (10 µM) in 50 mM ammonium acetate, pH 7.2, contained in a PTFE sample chamber. The chamber was cooled to 4°C using a Peltier block.

- generate plasma in protein solution  
A pulsed high-voltage signal of 22 kV, 5 µs pulse width, and 5 kHz frequency was applied to the electrode, generating a stable plasma discharge directly within the solution.

- describe plasma characteristics  
The plasma discharge produced microsecond bursts of hydroxyl radicals with a minimum generation rate of 350 nmol/sec, as calibrated against methionine oxidation.

- test cytochrome C with varying plasma exposure  
Cytochrome c was exposed to plasma for 0, 5, 10, 20, and 30 seconds in triplicate, then precipitated, digested with trypsin, and analyzed by Orbitrap mass spectrometry.

- analyze results using mass spectrometry  
Mass spectrometry revealed dose-dependent oxidation at solvent-exposed residues including methionine-65, tyrosine-48, and lysine-72, while buried residues such as tryptophan-59 showed minimal modification. Oxidation levels correlated with known crystal structure solvent accessibility values (PDB: 1HRC).

## EXAMPLE 2

### Labeling Bovine Serum Albumin

- test bovine serum albumin with varying plasma exposure  
Bovine serum albumin (50 µM) in PBS was subjected to plasma exposure for 0, 15, 30, and 60 seconds. Samples were digested with trypsin and Lys-C and analyzed by mass spectrometry.

- analyze results to study solvent accessibility  
Oxidation was observed at multiple surface-exposed residues including lysine-195, arginine-257, and methionine-298. Buried residues such as tryptophan-134 exhibited low oxidation levels. When BSA was predigested prior to plasma exposure, oxidation levels increased significantly at previously buried sites, confirming structural preservation during treatment.

## EXAMPLE 3

### Breakdown of DNA in Size-Dependent and Exposure-Dose-Dependent Fashion

- setup DNA sample with varying plasma exposure  
A 100 µL sample of lambda DNA (1 µg/µL) in TE buffer was exposed to plasma for 0, 10, 20, and 40 seconds. Samples were loaded onto a 1% agarose gel and electrophoresed.

- analyze DNA breakdown using electrophoresis  
DNA fragmentation increased with exposure time, with full-length DNA (48.5 kb) decreasing in intensity and lower molecular weight fragments appearing. Breakdown was dose-dependent and size-selective, with smaller fragments appearing first, consistent with radical-induced strand scission.

## EXAMPLE 4

### Protein Labeling in Intact/Live Cells

- test protein labeling in live E. coli cells  
Live E. coli cells (OD600 = 0.8) were suspended in PBS and exposed to plasma for 0, 10, and 30 seconds. Cells were lysed, and proteins were analyzed by LC-MS/MS.

- analyze results  
Oxidation was detected in multiple cytoplasmic proteins, including GroEL and DnaK, with modification levels proportional to surface exposure. No cell lysis or membrane disruption was observed, confirming that plasma exposure under these conditions does not compromise membrane integrity.

### EXAMPLE 5

- compare oxidation of intact and denatured cytochrome C  
Intact cytochrome c and chemically denatured cytochrome c (in 8 M urea) were subjected to identical plasma exposure (10 seconds). Denatured samples showed a 4.2-fold increase in oxidation at residues previously buried in the native structure, including tryptophan-59 and methionine-65, confirming that plasma-induced oxidation reports on solvent accessibility rather than chemical reactivity.

### EXAMPLE 6

- compare oxidation of native and digested bovine serum albumin  
Native and trypsin-digested BSA were treated with plasma for 30 seconds. Digestion prior to plasma exposure increased oxidation at residues 310–318 and 387–399 by 8.7-fold and 6.3-fold, respectively, confirming that these regions are solvent-inaccessible in the native state.

### EXAMPLE 7

- study solvent accessibility of protein bound and unbound by a ligand  
The extracellular domain of EGFR (500 nM) was incubated with or without EGF (1.5 µM) for 15 minutes, then subjected to plasma exposure for 5 seconds. Samples were digested and analyzed by mass spectrometry.

- analyze oxidation of EGFR protein with and without EGF  
Upon EGF binding, oxidation decreased significantly at residues 193–195, 261–270, and 563–569, consistent with known dimerization interfaces. Residues K311–K322 showed reduced oxidation, indicating conformational tightening of the hinge region. These results matched published crystal structures of EGFR-EGF complexes, validating PLIMB as a tool for mapping ligand-induced structural changes in solution.
