# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNICAL FIELD

- relate to doping engineered hole transport layers

The present invention relates to optoelectronic devices, particularly to perovskite-based solar cells incorporating a hole transport layer engineered through precise doping control via vacuum evaporation techniques. More specifically, the invention concerns the fabrication of a triple-layered hole transport structure composed of an n-type doped organic semiconductor sublayer, an intrinsic organic semiconductor intermediate layer, and a p-type doped organic semiconductor sublayer, all deposited sequentially under vacuum conditions to achieve enhanced charge extraction, minimized interfacial energy barriers, and superior environmental stability. The invention further encompasses the controlled modulation of the highest occupied molecular orbital energy level of the hole transport material through vapor-phase doping with volatile dopant species, enabling alignment with the valence band maximum of the underlying perovskite absorber layer. This approach overcomes the limitations inherent in solution-processed hole transport layers, including pinhole formation, dopant inhomogeneity, and poor interfacial contact, which collectively degrade device performance and accelerate degradation under ambient conditions. The method is applicable to a broad range of organometal halide perovskites and compatible with scalable vacuum deposition systems, thereby enabling high-efficiency, long-lived photovoltaic devices suitable for commercial deployment.

## BACKGROUND ART

- introduce solar cells

Solar cells are semiconductor devices that convert incident electromagnetic radiation, particularly sunlight, into electrical energy through the photovoltaic effect. The fundamental operating principle relies on the generation of electron-hole pairs upon photon absorption, followed by their separation and extraction at opposing electrodes via built-in electric fields established at p-n junctions or heterojunctions. The efficiency of such devices is governed by multiple factors, including the absorption spectrum of the photoactive material, the mobility of charge carriers, the recombination rate at interfaces, and the quality of charge-selective contacts. Over the past decade, the pursuit of high power conversion efficiencies has driven significant innovation in materials design, device architecture, and fabrication methodologies, with particular emphasis on reducing energy losses at interfaces and enhancing charge extraction kinetics.

- describe p-n junction

A p-n junction is formed at the interface between p-type and n-type semiconductor materials, creating a depletion region characterized by a built-in electric field that facilitates the directional separation of photogenerated carriers. In conventional silicon-based photovoltaics, this junction is formed through diffusion or ion implantation processes. In thin-film and solution-processed photovoltaics, including perovskite solar cells, the p-n junction is often replaced by a heterojunction formed between an electron transport layer and a hole transport layer, where the energy level alignment between adjacent materials governs the efficiency of carrier extraction and the magnitude of open-circuit voltage.

- discuss solar cell energy conversion efficiencies

The energy conversion efficiency of a solar cell is defined as the ratio of electrical power output to incident solar power under standard test conditions. State-of-the-art crystalline silicon cells achieve efficiencies exceeding 24%, while emerging technologies such as organometal halide perovskites have demonstrated laboratory efficiencies surpassing 25%, rivaling established technologies. However, the practical viability of perovskite solar cells remains constrained by instability under thermal, moisture, and oxygen exposure, which leads to rapid degradation of the perovskite lattice and delamination of charge transport layers.

- motivate organometal halide perovskites

Organometal halide perovskites, particularly methylammonium lead iodide and its halide variants, have emerged as highly promising photoactive materials due to their exceptional light absorption coefficients, long carrier diffusion lengths, tunable bandgaps, and low-cost solution processability. These properties enable the fabrication of thin, lightweight, and flexible photovoltaic devices with high short-circuit current densities and open-circuit voltages. Nevertheless, the instability of perovskite layers under ambient conditions necessitates the use of robust, pinhole-free, and chemically inert charge transport layers to shield the perovskite from environmental degradation.

- discuss limitations of existing fabrication techniques

Conventional fabrication of the hole transport layer in perovskite solar cells relies on spin-coating of organic semiconductors such as spiro-OMeTAD, doped with lithium bis(trifluoromethylsulfonyl)imide and 4-tert-butylpyridine. While this method is simple and widely adopted, it suffers from inherent limitations including the formation of pinholes due to solvent evaporation dynamics, inhomogeneous dopant distribution, and poor interfacial adhesion. These defects permit the ingress of moisture and oxygen, accelerate ion migration within the perovskite, and promote recombination at the perovskite/HTL interface, resulting in reduced fill factor, diminished open-circuit voltage, and accelerated device decay.

- motivate new fabrication technique

To overcome these limitations, a novel fabrication technique has been developed that employs vacuum evaporation to deposit a triple-layered hole transport structure composed of sequentially stacked n-type doped, intrinsic, and p-type doped spiro-OMeTAD layers. This approach enables precise control over dopant concentration, eliminates pinhole formation, ensures uniform layer thickness, and allows for the engineering of a stepwise energy level profile that enhances hole extraction while suppressing recombination. The method further enables the use of volatile dopants such as dimethylcarbamoyl chloride, whose vapor pressure can be finely tuned to achieve optimal energy level alignment with the perovskite valence band maximum, thereby maximizing device efficiency and operational stability.

## SUMMARY

- describe optoelectronic device structure

An optoelectronic device according to the invention comprises a transparent conductive substrate, a compact electron transport layer, a perovskite photoactive layer, a triple-layered hole transport layer, and a metallic electrode. The triple-layered hole transport layer consists of a first sublayer comprising an n-type doped organic semiconductor, a second sublayer comprising an intrinsic organic semiconductor, and a third sublayer comprising a p-type doped organic semiconductor, each deposited by vacuum evaporation in sequence without exposure to ambient atmosphere. The n-type doped sublayer is engineered to have a highest occupied molecular orbital energy level that aligns with the valence band maximum of the perovskite layer, facilitating efficient hole extraction. The intrinsic sublayer acts as a diffusion barrier to prevent intermixing of dopants between the n-type and p-type sublayers, while the p-type doped sublayer provides low-resistance contact to the metallic electrode. The entire structure is fabricated under high vacuum conditions using a multi-chamber or single-chamber evaporation system with independent vapor sources for the host material and dopants, enabling precise control over layer thickness, doping concentration, and interfacial quality.

## DESCRIPTION OF EMBODIMENTS

- introduce organometal halide perovskite film materials

Organometal halide perovskite film materials employed in the invention are characterized by the general formula ABX₃, wherein A is an organic cation such as methylammonium or formamidinium, B is a divalent metal cation such as lead or tin, and X is a halide anion such as iodide, bromide, or chloride. These materials are deposited as thin films on a compact electron transport layer via solution processing, followed by thermal annealing to induce crystallization. The resulting films exhibit a cubic or tetragonal crystal structure with high crystallinity, as confirmed by X-ray diffraction, and possess a bandgap tunable between 1.5 and 2.3 eV depending on halide composition.

- describe perovskite film structure

The perovskite film structure is continuous, uniform, and free of pinholes, with a thickness ranging from 300 to 600 nanometers. The surface morphology is smooth, as verified by atomic force microscopy, and the grain size is optimized to minimize grain boundary recombination. The valence band maximum of the perovskite layer is measured to lie at approximately 2.6 electronvolts below the Fermi level, as determined by ultraviolet photoelectron spectroscopy, and serves as the reference energy level for the design of the adjacent hole transport layer.

- explain application of perovskite in optoelectronic devices

The perovskite layer functions as the primary photon absorber in the optoelectronic device, generating electron-hole pairs upon illumination. The electrons are transported through the compact electron transport layer to the transparent conductive electrode, while the holes are extracted through the triple-layered hole transport structure to the metallic contact. The efficiency of this charge separation and extraction process is critically dependent on the energy level alignment between the perovskite valence band and the highest occupied molecular orbital of the adjacent hole transport material.

- describe hole transport layer (HTL) in photovoltaic devices

The hole transport layer in photovoltaic devices serves to selectively extract and transport holes from the perovskite absorber to the metallic electrode while blocking electrons to minimize recombination. Conventional HTLs based on solution-processed spiro-OMeTAD suffer from morphological defects and dopant instability, resulting in poor interfacial contact and accelerated degradation. The invention replaces this with a vacuum-evaporated, doping-engineered triple-layer structure that eliminates pinholes, ensures stoichiometric dopant incorporation, and enables precise energy level grading.

- introduce solution method for forming HTL

Solution-based methods for forming the hole transport layer involve dissolving spiro-OMeTAD and dopants in organic solvents such as chlorobenzene, followed by spin-coating onto the perovskite surface. This method is limited by solvent-induced perovskite dissolution, non-uniform drying kinetics, and the inability to control dopant concentration with sub-monolayer precision.

- describe limitations of solution-processed spiro-OMeTAD films

Solution-processed spiro-OMeTAD films exhibit a high density of pinholes, typically on the order of hundreds per square millimeter, which serve as pathways for moisture and oxygen penetration. These defects also facilitate the out-diffusion of iodide ions from the perovskite, leading to irreversible degradation. Furthermore, the dopants in solution-processed films tend to migrate over time, resulting in unstable energy level alignment and declining device performance.

- explain effects of pinholes in HTL on perovskite solar cells

The presence of pinholes in the hole transport layer directly correlates with a reduction in open-circuit voltage and fill factor due to increased recombination at the perovskite/HTL interface. Pinholes also enable the ingress of atmospheric water and oxygen, which hydrolyze the perovskite lattice and induce phase segregation. This results in a rapid decline in power conversion efficiency, particularly under ambient storage conditions, rendering solution-processed devices unsuitable for long-term operation.

- introduce vacuum evaporation method for forming HTL

The vacuum evaporation method involves the thermal sublimation of solid spiro-OMeTAD and dopant precursors under high vacuum conditions, followed by condensation onto a heated substrate to form continuous, pinhole-free thin films. This technique allows for atomic-level control over film thickness, composition, and interface quality, and is compatible with multi-layer deposition in a single vacuum chamber.

- describe advantages of vacuum-evaporated spiro-OMeTAD films

Vacuum-evaporated spiro-OMeTAD films exhibit superior morphological uniformity, absence of pinholes, enhanced film density, and improved interfacial adhesion to the perovskite layer. The films are amorphous, with a glass transition temperature of 121 °C, providing inherent thermal stability. The absence of residual solvent eliminates chemical degradation pathways, and the controlled doping process enables reproducible energy level tuning.

- explain importance of doping engineering in optoelectronic devices

Doping engineering is a critical strategy for modulating the electronic properties of organic semiconductors, enabling the adjustment of work function, Fermi level position, and charge carrier concentration. In perovskite solar cells, precise doping of the hole transport layer is essential to minimize energy barriers at the perovskite/HTL interface and maximize built-in potential, thereby enhancing charge extraction and reducing voltage losses.

- introduce triple-layer HTL structure

The triple-layer hole transport structure comprises a bottom n-type doped sublayer, a middle intrinsic sublayer, and a top p-type doped sublayer, each deposited sequentially by vacuum evaporation. This architecture creates a graded energy profile that facilitates hole transport while blocking electron leakage, and the intrinsic layer acts as a diffusion barrier to prevent dopant migration between the doped sublayers.

- describe n-type doping for HTL

N-type doping of the hole transport layer is achieved by co-evaporating spiro-OMeTAD with a n-type dopant such as dimethylcarbamoyl chloride, which acts as an electron donor, increasing the electron concentration in the host material and shifting the Fermi level toward the conduction band. The doping concentration is controlled by adjusting the vapor pressure of the dopant during evaporation.

- describe p-type doping for HTL

P-type doping is accomplished by co-evaporating spiro-OMeTAD with a p-type dopant such as F4-TCNQ, which accepts electrons from the host, creating holes and shifting the Fermi level toward the valence band. This enhances hole conductivity and improves contact with the metallic electrode.

- list examples of n-dopants and p-dopants

Examples of n-type dopants include dimethylcarbamoyl chloride, tetrakis(dimethylamino)ethylene, and 2,3,5,6-tetrafluoro-7,7,8,8-tetracyanoquinodimethane derivatives. Examples of p-type dopants include molybdenum trioxide, F4-TCNQ, lithium bis(trifluoromethylsulfonyl)imide, and 2,2′,7,7′-tetrakis(N,N-di-p-methoxyphenylamine)-9,9′-spirobifluorene derivatives.

- list examples of HTMs

Examples of hole transport materials include spiro-OMeTAD, poly(3,4-ethylenedioxythiophene):polystyrene sulfonate, PTAA, and TFB. In the invention, spiro-OMeTAD is preferred due to its high hole mobility, suitable energy levels, and compatibility with vacuum evaporation.

- illustrate energy diagram of perovskite-based device

The energy diagram of the device exhibits a stepwise alignment of the highest occupied molecular orbital levels across the triple-layered hole transport structure, with the n-type doped sublayer positioned at 2.6 eV below the Fermi level, matching the valence band maximum of the perovskite. The intrinsic sublayer lies at 1.5 eV below the Fermi level, and the p-type doped sublayer is pinned at 0.3 eV below the Fermi level, creating a favorable energy gradient for hole transport.

- explain matching of HOMO1 level with valence band maximum

The energy level of the n-type doped sublayer is optimized to match the valence band maximum of the perovskite, minimizing the energy barrier for hole extraction. This alignment is achieved by precisely controlling the vapor pressure of the n-dopant during evaporation, which directly modulates the HOMO level of the resulting film.

- describe optimal n-type doping concentration

The optimal n-type doping concentration corresponds to a dimethylcarbamoyl chloride vapor pressure of 1.0 × 10⁻⁵ Torr, which yields a HOMO level of 2.6 eV below the Fermi level, perfectly aligned with the perovskite valence band maximum. This concentration maximizes open-circuit voltage and fill factor without inducing excessive carrier recombination.

- illustrate current density vs. voltage plots of perovskite solar cells

Current density versus voltage plots reveal that devices incorporating the triple-layer HTL exhibit higher open-circuit voltage, higher fill factor, and higher short-circuit current compared to reference devices with solution-processed HTLs. The enhanced performance is attributed to reduced interfacial resistance and suppressed recombination.

- list performance metrics of perovskite solar cells

Performance metrics include power conversion efficiency exceeding 20%, open-circuit voltage greater than 1.15 V, fill factor above 78%, and short-circuit current density above 22 mA/cm² under AM1.5G illumination.

- compare performance of different HTL structures

Devices with the triple-layer vacuum-evaporated HTL outperform both solution-processed HTLs and bilayer doped HTLs in terms of efficiency, stability, and reproducibility. The triple-layer structure demonstrates a 30% higher retention of initial efficiency after 800 hours of ambient storage.

- illustrate fabrication process of perovskite-based device

The fabrication process begins with the cleaning of fluorine-doped tin oxide glass, followed by the deposition of a compact TiO₂ layer via spray pyrolysis. Perovskite is then deposited by spin-coating and annealed. Subsequently, the triple-layer HTL is deposited by vacuum evaporation in sequence, and finally, a gold electrode is thermally evaporated.

- describe formation of perovskite layer

The perovskite layer is formed by dissolving methylammonium iodide and lead chloride in N,N-dimethylformamide at a 2.5:1 molar ratio, spin-coating onto the TiO₂ layer, and thermally annealing at 100 °C for 45 minutes under inert atmosphere.

- describe formation of n-doped HTL

The n-doped HTL is formed by placing dimethylcarbamoyl chloride powder in a sealed glass ampule connected to a leak valve, heating the ampule to 100 °C under a base pressure of 10⁻⁸ Torr, and adjusting the vapor pressure to 1.0 × 10⁻⁵ Torr while co-evaporating spiro-OMeTAD at a rate of 0.5 Å/s.

- describe formation of intrinsic HTL

The intrinsic HTL is formed by continuing the evaporation of pure spiro-OMeTAD without dopant, at the same deposition rate, to a thickness of 20 nm.

- describe formation of p-doped HTL

The p-doped HTL is formed by transferring the substrate to a second evaporation chamber, where spiro-OMeTAD and F4-TCNQ are co-evaporated at rates of 0.5 Å/s and 0.1 Å/s, respectively, to form a 30 nm thick layer.

- describe formation of electrode

The electrode is formed by thermal evaporation of gold through a shadow mask to a thickness of 60 nm, yielding a top contact with low contact resistance.

- describe vacuum evaporation system

The vacuum evaporation system comprises a high-vacuum chamber with a base pressure of 10⁻⁸ Torr, a substrate stage with temperature control, multiple independent evaporation sources equipped with quartz crystal microbalances, and valves for precise control of dopant vapor pressure.

- illustrate system configuration

The system configuration includes a main chamber for spiro-OMeTAD and n-dopant evaporation, a secondary chamber for p-dopant evaporation, and a transfer mechanism to move the substrate between chambers without breaking vacuum.

- describe chamber and pump unit

The chamber is constructed from stainless steel with ceramic feedthroughs and is evacuated by a turbomolecular pump backed by a rotary vane pump, achieving a base pressure below 10⁻⁸ Torr.

- describe substrate stage and temperature control

The substrate stage is made of copper with integrated resistive heating elements and thermocouple feedback, enabling precise temperature control from room temperature to 150 °C to optimize film morphology.

- describe evaporation units and vapor generation

Evaporation units consist of tungsten boats or crucibles heated by electron beams or resistive filaments, with independent temperature control for each dopant and host material.

- describe monitor for deposition rate

Deposition rates are monitored in real time using quartz crystal microbalances calibrated for each material, with accuracy better than ±0.1 Å/s.

- describe valve for vapor flow control

A leak valve connected to the dopant ampule allows fine adjustment of vapor pressure by controlling the flow rate of dopant molecules into the evaporation zone.

- describe system advantages for volatile dopant

The system enables the use of volatile dopants such as dimethylcarbamoyl chloride, which would decompose or react in solution-based processes, by maintaining low partial pressures and preventing oxidation during evaporation.

- illustrate alternative system configuration

An alternative configuration employs a single vacuum chamber with multiple independently controllable evaporation sources, allowing sequential deposition of all three HTL layers without substrate transfer.

- describe chamber and pump unit

The alternative chamber is similarly constructed but includes additional evaporation sources and a shutter mechanism to isolate the substrate during layer transitions.

- describe substrate stage and temperature control

The substrate stage in the alternative configuration includes a rotation mechanism to ensure uniform deposition across the entire substrate area.

- describe evaporation units and vapor generation

Each evaporation source is equipped with a dedicated temperature controller and mass flow sensor for precise control of dopant flux.

- describe monitors for deposition rates

Multiple quartz crystal microbalances are positioned at different locations to monitor deposition uniformity across the substrate.

- describe shutter for substrate exposure

A mechanical shutter between the evaporation sources and the substrate allows precise timing of layer deposition, preventing cross-contamination.

- describe system advantages for uniform deposition

The alternative system enables highly uniform deposition over large-area substrates, making it suitable for roll-to-roll or panel-scale manufacturing.

- describe compatibility with dopant forms

The system is compatible with solid, liquid, or sublimable dopants, provided they can be vaporized without decomposition under vacuum.

- describe valve control for vapor pressure

Valve opening time and pressure differential are calibrated to maintain dopant vapor pressure within ±5% of target values during deposition.

- describe matching HOMO1 and VM energy levels

The HOMO level of the n-doped sublayer is matched to the valence band maximum of the perovskite by tuning the dopant vapor pressure, as confirmed by in situ UPS measurements.

- describe finding optimal vapor pressure

Optimal vapor pressure is determined by measuring the HOMO leading edge of the doped film as a function of dopant pressure, identifying the pressure at which the HOMO aligns with the perovskite VBM at 2.6 eV below EF.

- describe alternative method for non-volatile dopant

For non-volatile dopants, a co-evaporation source with a crucible heated to sublimation temperature is employed, with dopant concentration controlled by the relative evaporation rates of host and dopant.

- describe flowchart for fabricating n-i-p structured HTL

The flowchart outlines the sequential steps: substrate cleaning, TiO₂ deposition, perovskite formation, n-doped HTL evaporation, intrinsic HTL evaporation, p-doped HTL evaporation, and electrode deposition, all performed under vacuum.

- describe process steps for fabricating HTL

Each HTL layer is deposited at a controlled rate, with thickness monitored by quartz crystal microbalance, and the substrate is not exposed to air between layers.

- describe alternative processes for forming n-i-p structured HTL

Alternative processes include the use of a single evaporation source with programmable material switching or the use of pre-mixed dopant-host pellets for simplified deposition.

- describe two-system process for sequential deposition of n-i-p layers

In the two-system process, the n-doped and intrinsic layers are deposited in a first chamber, followed by transfer to a second chamber for p-doped layer deposition, ensuring dopant isolation.

- describe one-system process for sequential deposition of n-i-p layers

In the one-system process, all three layers are deposited in a single chamber using multiple evaporation sources, with shutters isolating each deposition step.

- describe process for fabricating n-i-p structured HTL on perovskite active layer

The process begins with a perovskite layer formed on a TiO₂-coated FTO substrate, followed by immediate vacuum transfer and sequential deposition of the three HTL layers without air exposure.

- describe preparation of patterned FTO glass

Patterned FTO glass is cleaned with HCl and Zn powder, rinsed with deionized water and ethanol, and dried under nitrogen.

- describe deposition of compact layer of TiO2

A 100 nm compact TiO₂ layer is deposited by spray pyrolysis using a precursor solution of titanium isopropoxide, acetylacetone, and ethanol, followed by annealing at 480 °C.

- describe fabrication of perovskite MAPbI3-XClX layer

The perovskite layer is fabricated by dissolving MAI and PbCl₂ in DMF at a 2.5:1 molar ratio, spin-coating at 2000 rpm for 45 seconds, and annealing at 100 °C for 45 minutes.

- describe deposition of n-type doped spiro-OMeTAD by vacuum evaporation

The n-type doped spiro-OMeTAD layer is deposited by co-evaporating spiro-OMeTAD and DMC at a DMC vapor pressure of 1.0 × 10⁻⁵ Torr, yielding a 30 nm thick layer with HOMO level aligned to the perovskite VBM.

- describe optimization of DMC doping concentration

Optimization is performed by measuring UPS spectra at varying DMC vapor pressures, identifying 1.0 × 10⁻⁵ Torr as the pressure yielding optimal energy alignment.

- describe deposition of intrinsic spiro-OMeTAD

The intrinsic layer is deposited at the same rate as the n-doped layer but without dopant, to a thickness of 20 nm.

- describe deposition of p-type doped spiro-OMeTAD HTL

The p-type doped layer is deposited by co-evaporating spiro-OMeTAD and F4-TCNQ at a 5:1 mass ratio, forming a 30 nm thick layer with HOMO level at 0.3 eV below EF.

- describe measurement of thicknesses of n-type doped, intrinsic, and p-type doped spiro-OMeTAD layers

Thicknesses are measured by atomic force microscopy, confirming layer thicknesses of 30 nm, 20 nm, and 30 nm, respectively.

- describe deposition of gold contacts

Gold contacts are deposited by thermal evaporation at a rate of 0.5 Å/s to a thickness of 60 nm.

- describe X-ray diffractometer measurement

X-ray diffraction confirms the crystalline structure of the perovskite layer, with peaks at 14.2° and 28.5° corresponding to (110) and (220) planes.

- describe UPS measurements

Ultraviolet photoelectron spectroscopy is performed using a He I (21.2 eV) source to determine the work function and HOMO leading edge of each HTL layer.

- describe variation of DMC vapor pressure

Variation of DMC vapor pressure from 1.0 × 10⁻⁶ to 5.0 × 10⁻⁵ Torr results in a corresponding shift of the HOMO level from 1.5 eV to 4.6 eV below EF, demonstrating tunability.

- describe measurement of work functions and HOMO leading edge

Work functions are measured relative to a gold reference, and HOMO leading edges are determined from the low-binding-energy cutoff of the UPS spectra.

- describe confirmation of n-type doping behavior

The downward shift of the Fermi level and the increase in work function confirm n-type doping behavior induced by DMC.

- describe UPS measurements on n-type doped, intrinsic, and p-type doped spiro-OMeTAD

UPS spectra show a clear stepwise shift in the HOMO level across the three layers, confirming the intended energy gradient.

- describe measurement of valence band maximum of perovskite

The valence band maximum of the perovskite is measured to be 2.6 eV below EF, matching the HOMO level of the n-doped spiro-OMeTAD.

- describe identification of optimal DMC vapor pressure

The optimal DMC vapor pressure of 1.0 × 10⁻⁵ Torr is identified as the point at which the HOMO level of the n-doped layer aligns with the perovskite VBM.

- describe measurement of HOMO levels of intrinsic and p-type doped spiro-OMeTAD

The intrinsic layer exhibits a HOMO level of 1.5 eV below EF, while the p-type doped layer is pinned at 0.3 eV below EF.

- describe determination of energy diagram for solar cell

The energy diagram is constructed from UPS and Kelvin probe measurements, showing a staircase alignment from perovskite to electrode, enabling efficient hole extraction.

- describe fabrication of reference sample with solution-processed spiro-OMeTAD

Reference samples are fabricated by spin-coating a solution of spiro-OMeTAD, tBP, and Li-TFSI in chlorobenzene, followed by drying under nitrogen.

- describe study of stability of solar cell devices

Stability is studied by measuring device performance over 890 hours under ambient air and high vacuum conditions.

- describe measurement of Voc, jsc, FF, and PCE vs. time evolution

All parameters are measured under standard illumination at regular intervals, revealing that the triple-layer HTL devices maintain or exceed initial performance, while reference devices degrade significantly.

- describe comparison of stability of reference samples and samples with n-i-p structured HTL

After 840 hours, the triple-layer HTL devices retain over 110% of initial PCE in air, while reference devices retain less than 50%.

Efficiency increases over time are attributed to gradual dopant redistribution and improved interfacial contact induced by ambient exposure.

Dopant diffusion is confirmed by secondary ion mass spectrometry, showing minimal intermixing between the intrinsic and doped layers.

The doping concentration is optimized to balance hole extraction efficiency with open-circuit voltage, with 1.0 × 10⁻⁵ Torr identified as optimal.

The triple-layer HTL structure is applicable to perovskite light-emitting diodes, photodetectors, and tandem solar cells, where efficient hole extraction and stability are critical.

Doping engineering enables precise control over carrier concentration, Fermi level position, and conductivity type, allowing tailored energy level alignment in multilayer optoelectronic architectures.
