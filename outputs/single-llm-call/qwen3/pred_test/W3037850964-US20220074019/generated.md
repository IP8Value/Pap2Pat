# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

- define REE extraction process

The present invention relates to a novel, environmentally sustainable process for the extraction of rare earth elements (REE) from geologic materials, particularly those derived from coal-related waste streams and sedimentary clay-rich formations. The process employs an organic acid-based solution (OAS) composed of specific organic acid anions and monovalent salts under controlled ambient conditions to selectively mobilize and solubilize rare earth elements from complex mineral matrices without inducing significant dissolution of gangue minerals or radioactive constituents. This method is particularly adapted for use with underclays, coal refuse piles, and other weathered sedimentary materials that contain rare earth elements adsorbed onto clay surfaces or associated with colloidal and weakly crystalline phases. The invention provides a scalable, low-energy, and chemically selective alternative to conventional acid leaching techniques that rely on strong mineral acids and high-temperature processing, thereby reducing environmental impact, operational costs, and downstream waste treatment burdens. The process is designed to operate under ambient temperature and near-neutral pH conditions, making it suitable for both ex-situ heap leaching and in-situ recovery applications in abandoned coal mining regions and other sedimentary basins where REE-bearing materials are abundant but historically underutilized due to technical and environmental constraints.

## BACKGROUND

- motivate REE extraction

Rare earth elements are indispensable components in the manufacturing of permanent magnets, wind turbine generators, electric vehicle drivetrains, defense systems, and advanced electronics, all of which are critical to global energy transition and technological sovereignty. The increasing demand for these elements, coupled with geopolitical concentration of supply chains—particularly in China—has created urgent strategic imperatives for the development of domestic, secure, and sustainable sources of rare earth elements in the United States and other nations. The absence of a robust domestic REE production infrastructure has left critical industries vulnerable to supply disruptions, price volatility, and export restrictions, underscoring the need for alternative feedstocks that can be processed with minimal environmental footprint and regulatory burden.

- describe current REE market

The global rare earth element market is dominated by a handful of producers, with China accounting for over 70% of annual production, primarily from bastnaesite and ion-adsorption clay deposits. While global production has increased modestly in recent years, the United States contributes less than 15% of total output, relying heavily on imported concentrates and processed materials. This imbalance exposes national supply chains to systemic risk, particularly as demand for REEs is projected to increase by over 400% by 2030 to meet clean energy and digital infrastructure targets. The current market structure lacks resilience, and efforts to diversify supply must prioritize feedstocks that are abundant, domestically available, and amenable to low-impact extraction methods.

- summarize limitations of current methods

Traditional extraction methods for rare earth elements involve aggressive hydrometallurgical processes using concentrated sulfuric or hydrochloric acid at elevated temperatures, often followed by solvent extraction and precipitation steps that generate large volumes of acidic, radioactive, and heavy metal-laden waste. These methods are energy-intensive, require extensive infrastructure, and pose significant environmental and public health risks. Furthermore, they are poorly suited for low-grade, dispersed, or weathered materials such as coal refuse and underclay, which contain REEs in forms that are not readily liberated by mineral acids without extensive mechanical comminution and thermal activation.

- describe environmental concerns

The environmental consequences of conventional REE mining include soil acidification, groundwater contamination, radionuclide mobilization, and long-term tailings management challenges. The release of thorium, uranium, and other naturally occurring radioactive materials during acid leaching has led to stringent regulatory barriers and public opposition to mining operations in many regions. The persistence of these contaminants in waste streams necessitates costly and complex remediation protocols, often rendering otherwise economically viable deposits unfeasible from a sustainability standpoint.

- highlight societal aversion to mining

Public perception of mining has shifted dramatically in recent decades, with growing resistance to large-scale open-pit and underground operations due to concerns over habitat destruction, water use, and long-term ecological degradation. This societal aversion has stalled the development of new mineral projects, even in regions where critical mineral resources are known to exist. A new paradigm is required—one that leverages existing waste streams, operates without excavation, and minimizes visible environmental disruption to gain social license and regulatory approval.

- introduce Chinese elution-deposits

In China, a significant portion of rare earth production originates from weathered elution-deposits formed by the prolonged leaching of granitic bedrock under tropical conditions. These deposits contain rare earth elements adsorbed onto the surfaces of clay minerals, allowing for extraction using mild ammonium salt solutions under ambient conditions. This method, known as in-situ solution mining, has proven highly effective and environmentally preferable to traditional mining, yet it remains largely unexploited outside of China due to a lack of analogous geological settings and suitable leaching chemistries in other regions.

- describe in-situ solution mining

In-situ solution mining involves the injection of a leaching solution into subsurface ore bodies through a network of injection wells, followed by the recovery of the enriched solution via production wells. This technique avoids surface excavation and minimizes waste generation, making it ideal for sensitive environments. However, its application in the United States has been limited by the unsuitability of conventional lixiviants—such as ammonium sulfate—for the geochemical conditions of Appalachian and other North American coal-bearing strata, where clay mineralogy and REE speciation differ significantly from those in southern China.

- discuss limitations of inorganic acids

Inorganic acids such as ammonium sulfate, sodium chloride, and hydrochloric acid have been tested for REE recovery from U.S. underclays, but they exhibit poor selectivity, low recovery rates, and high co-dissolution of aluminum, iron, and silica. These impurities complicate downstream purification, increase reagent consumption, and necessitate additional chemical treatment steps that erode economic viability. Moreover, the use of ammonium salts can lead to nitrogen leaching into groundwater, posing secondary environmental risks.

- mention fungi-based REE extraction

Emerging research into biogenic leaching agents, including organic acids produced by fungi, has demonstrated potential for selective REE mobilization. However, these methods remain largely experimental, suffer from slow reaction kinetics, and are difficult to scale due to biological constraints such as nutrient requirements, microbial viability, and process control challenges.

- introduce need for new REE extraction process

There exists a critical and unmet need for a scalable, chemically selective, and environmentally benign process capable of extracting rare earth elements from abundant, low-grade, coal-associated sedimentary materials under ambient conditions. Such a process must achieve high recovery rates while minimizing the mobilization of radioactive and toxic co-contaminants, avoid the use of hazardous reagents, and be compatible with both ex-situ and in-situ deployment strategies. The invention disclosed herein fulfills this need by introducing a novel organic acid-based solution that leverages the chelating properties of citrate and related carboxylic acid anions in combination with monovalent salts to selectively target and solubilize rare earth elements without compromising the structural integrity of the host matrix or generating hazardous effluents.

## SUMMARY

- introduce REE extraction process

The present invention introduces a novel process for the extraction of rare earth elements from geologic materials, particularly those derived from coal-related waste and sedimentary clay deposits, through the use of a carefully formulated organic acid-based solution (OAS). This process enables the selective mobilization of rare earth ions bound to clay surfaces and colloidal phases under ambient temperature and near-neutral pH conditions, without the need for high-energy inputs, aggressive mineral acids, or extensive mechanical preprocessing. The method is applicable to a broad range of materials including underclay, coal refuse, and weathered sedimentary horizons that contain ion-adsorbed or weakly bound rare earth elements.

- describe OAS composition

The organic acid-based solution comprises at least one organic acid selected from the group consisting of citric acid, malic acid, oxalic acid, and their respective anionic conjugates, in combination with a monovalent salt such as sodium chloride, potassium chloride, or ammonium chloride. The solution is buffered to maintain a stable pH range between 3.0 and 6.0, ensuring optimal chelation of rare earth elements while suppressing the dissolution of silicate and aluminosilicate matrix components. The organic acid anions function as multidentate ligands that form stable, water-soluble complexes with trivalent rare earth cations, thereby facilitating their mobilization from solid-phase binding sites.

- outline process steps

The process involves contacting a rare earth-bearing material with the organic acid-based solution under controlled conditions of temperature, pH, and contact time. The solution is introduced to the material either through ex-situ leaching in a heap or tank system or through in-situ injection into subsurface formations via well networks. After a defined period of interaction, the resulting pregnant leach solution is separated from the residual solids through filtration or sedimentation. The recovered solution is then processed to isolate and concentrate the rare earth elements using downstream techniques such as ion exchange, solvent extraction, or sorbent capture.

- specify ambient temperature and pH

The process is conducted at ambient temperatures ranging from 15°C to 30°C, eliminating the need for thermal energy input and reducing operational costs. The pH of the solution is maintained within a narrow range of 3.5 to 5.5, a condition that maximizes the deprotonation of carboxylic acid functional groups on the organic ligands while minimizing the solubilization of aluminum, silicon, and iron species that constitute the majority of the gangue matrix. This pH window is critical to achieving high selectivity and purity in the recovered rare earth fraction.

- describe REE separation

Following leaching, the rare earth elements are separated from the pregnant leach solution using selective sorbents, such as amine-functionalized polymers or chelating resins, which preferentially bind rare earth ions over co-dissolved alkaline earth and transition metals. The sorbent is then eluted with a mild stripping solution to recover a concentrated rare earth stream, which may be further purified and precipitated as oxides or fluorides for commercial use.

- mention additional process steps

The process may include optional steps such as pre-washing of the feedstock to remove soluble salts, particle size reduction to enhance surface accessibility, or the addition of biocides to prevent microbial fouling during extended leaching cycles. The spent organic acid solution may be regenerated and recycled through pH adjustment and salt recovery, reducing reagent consumption and waste generation over multiple operational cycles.

- specify OAS formula

The organic acid-based solution is formulated such that the total concentration of organic acid anions, including both protonated and deprotonated forms, is maintained between 0.05 M and 0.2 M, with the monovalent salt concentration ranging from 0.1 M to 0.8 M. In a preferred embodiment, the solution contains 0.1 M citric acid and 0.5 M sodium chloride, buffered to pH 5.0 using sodium citrate tribasic dihydrate.

- describe pH range

The pH of the solution is maintained within the range of 3.0 to 6.0, with optimal performance observed between 4.0 and 5.5. This range corresponds to the pKa transitions of citric acid and ensures maximal availability of carboxylate groups for metal coordination while avoiding the acid-driven dissolution of silicate minerals and the precipitation of rare earth hydroxides.

- introduce chelating compounds

In addition to organic acids, the solution may include supplementary chelating agents such as gluconic acid, tartaric acid, or EDTA derivatives to enhance the stability of rare earth complexes under variable geochemical conditions. These compounds may be used in combination with the primary organic acid to broaden the spectrum of recoverable elements or to improve selectivity in complex matrices.

- mention surfactants and biocides

Surfactants may be added to the solution to improve wetting of fine-grained materials and enhance fluid penetration into low-permeability zones, particularly in in-situ applications. Biocides such as sodium benzoate or isothiazolinone may be included to inhibit microbial growth during prolonged storage or operation, ensuring solution stability and preventing biofilm formation on leaching surfaces.

- specify organic acid selection

Citric acid is the preferred organic acid due to its high density of carboxyl functional groups, low toxicity, biodegradability, and proven efficacy in forming stable complexes with lanthanides. Alternative acids such as malic acid and oxalic acid may be employed in cases where cost, availability, or compatibility with specific mineralogies necessitate substitution.

- describe salt selection

Monovalent salts such as sodium chloride, potassium chloride, and ammonium chloride are selected for their ability to modulate ionic strength and clay surface charge, thereby enhancing the dispersion of clay particles and promoting ion exchange. Sodium chloride is the preferred salt due to its low cost, high solubility, and minimal environmental impact compared to ammonium-based alternatives.

- summarize process advantages

The process offers multiple advantages over conventional methods: it operates at ambient conditions, requires no thermal input, generates minimal waste, exhibits high selectivity for rare earth elements over gangue and radioactive constituents, and is compatible with both ex-situ and in-situ deployment. The organic acid solution is non-corrosive, non-toxic, and readily biodegradable, enabling safer handling, reduced regulatory burden, and public acceptance in environmentally sensitive regions.

- describe REE-bearing materials

The process is applicable to a wide range of rare earth-bearing materials including underclay, coal preparation plant refuse, fly ash, and weathered sedimentary horizons associated with coal seams. These materials are characterized by high clay content, low permeability, and the presence of rare earth elements adsorbed onto mineral surfaces or associated with amorphous or colloidal phases rather than crystalline phosphates.

- outline process variations

Variations of the process include batch leaching, continuous flow-through systems, heap leaching on engineered pads, and in-situ recovery via injection and production well arrays. The solution composition, contact time, and fluid flow rate may be adjusted based on the specific mineralogy, particle size distribution, and geochemical environment of the target deposit.

- mention additional process conditions

The process may be enhanced by the application of low-pressure gradients (100–2,000 psi) during in-situ applications to improve fluid mobility through low-permeability media. The use of pre-conditioned feedstock, such as dried or calcined material, may also improve leaching efficiency by removing moisture and increasing surface accessibility.

- describe REY concentrate formation

The final output of the process is a concentrated rare earth element stream, enriched in light and middle rare earths such as lanthanum, cerium, neodymium, and dysprosium, which may be precipitated as oxides or fluorides and further refined into high-purity metal salts for industrial use. The resulting concentrate exhibits significantly lower levels of thorium, uranium, and other radioactive contaminants compared to products derived from conventional acid leaching.

- conclude process summary

In summary, the invention provides a transformative method for the sustainable recovery of rare earth elements from low-grade, coal-associated geologic materials through the use of a tailored organic acid-based solution that operates under ambient conditions, minimizes environmental impact, and delivers high selectivity and purity. This process enables the economic and environmentally responsible utilization of previously overlooked waste streams as strategic mineral resources.

- motivate REE extraction process

The motivation for this invention arises from the urgent need to secure domestic supplies of rare earth elements while minimizing environmental degradation and public opposition to mining. Conventional extraction methods are incompatible with the geochemical nature of U.S. coal-associated deposits, which are characterized by low-grade, clay-rich matrices that do not respond favorably to aggressive leaching. The disclosed process addresses this gap by leveraging the natural affinity of organic acid anions for rare earth cations, enabling selective mobilization under mild conditions that preserve the integrity of the host rock and reduce the generation of hazardous waste.

- describe REE extraction process

The extraction process begins with the preparation of a rare earth-bearing material, which may be in the form of crushed underclay, coal refuse, or weathered sedimentary rock. The material is contacted with an aqueous solution containing 0.05 to 0.2 molar concentrations of an organic acid, preferably citric acid, and 0.1 to 0.8 molar concentrations of a monovalent salt, preferably sodium chloride. The solution is buffered to a pH between 3.0 and 6.0 using a citrate buffer system. The mixture is agitated or allowed to stand under ambient temperature conditions for a period ranging from 4 to 24 hours, during which time rare earth elements are solubilized via chelation with the carboxylate groups of the organic acid. The resulting pregnant leach solution is then separated from the residual solids by filtration or sedimentation. The solution is passed through a selective sorbent bed that captures rare earth ions while allowing other cations and the organic acid to pass through. The captured rare earth elements are subsequently eluted using a mild acid or salt solution, yielding a concentrated rare earth product suitable for further refining.

- explain weathering of REE-rich host rocks

Rare earth elements in the target materials originate from the prolonged weathering of igneous and metamorphic rocks rich in rare earth-bearing minerals such as monazite, xenotime, and apatite. Over geological time scales, these minerals are subjected to hydrolytic and oxidative breakdown, releasing rare earth cations into the surrounding environment. These cations become adsorbed onto the surfaces of secondary clay minerals formed during weathering, such as kaolinite, illite, and halloysite, where they remain bound in exchangeable or colloidal forms.

- describe formation of aluminosilicate-rich clay deposits

The weathering process leads to the accumulation of aluminosilicate-rich clay deposits, particularly in the underclay layers beneath coal seams. These deposits form as a result of intense leaching of alkali and alkaline earth elements, leaving behind a residue dominated by silica and alumina. The unique geochemical conditions of these horizons—low pH, high surface area, and high cation exchange capacity—favor the adsorption and retention of rare earth elements, making them ideal targets for selective extraction.

- explain mobilization and accumulation of REE ions

Rare earth ions are mobilized from primary mineral phases through the action of groundwater and organic acids generated during diagenesis. These ions migrate through pore networks and become immobilized on the surfaces of clay minerals, where they are held by electrostatic forces and weak coordination bonds. The accumulation of rare earth elements in these zones occurs over millions of years, resulting in localized enrichments that are orders of magnitude higher than average crustal abundances.

- describe geologic past of REE-rich igneous rocks

The parent rocks of these deposits are typically granitic or syenitic in composition, formed during Mesozoic and Paleozoic tectonic events. These rocks contain accessory minerals rich in rare earth elements, which, upon exposure to surface conditions, undergo chemical decomposition. The resulting debris is transported and deposited in fluvial and lacustrine environments, where it is further altered by microbial and chemical weathering.

- explain formation of clay-rich ore deposits

The transformation of weathered debris into clay-rich ore deposits occurs through a combination of physical compaction, chemical leaching, and mineralogical reorganization. The loss of soluble components such as calcium, sodium, and potassium leaves behind a porous, high-surface-area matrix dominated by clay minerals. Rare earth elements, being relatively insoluble under neutral conditions, become trapped within this matrix, adsorbed onto clay surfaces or incorporated into amorphous iron and manganese oxides.

- describe REE deposition in coal-forming peats

During the formation of coal deposits, organic-rich peat accumulates in swampy environments. As the peat is buried and compressed, it undergoes diagenetic transformation into coal, while the underlying and overlying sediments are altered by groundwater flow. Rare earth elements leached from surrounding rocks are transported into these sediments and become concentrated in the underclay layers, which act as natural filters and sorbents.

- explain mobilization of REE ions in clay-rich sediments

In clay-rich sediments, rare earth ions are primarily mobilized through ion exchange reactions driven by changes in pH, ionic strength, and the presence of complexing ligands. Under natural conditions, this mobilization is slow and incomplete. The disclosed process accelerates this mobilization by introducing a solution rich in organic acid anions that compete for binding sites on clay surfaces and form stable, soluble complexes with rare earth cations.

- describe optimum leaching conditions

Optimum leaching conditions are achieved when the solution contains 0.1 M citric acid, 0.5 M sodium chloride, and is buffered to pH 5.0. Under these conditions, the citrate anion achieves maximum deprotonation of its carboxyl groups, enhancing its ability to coordinate with trivalent rare earth ions. The sodium chloride increases ionic strength, reducing electrostatic repulsion between clay particles and improving solution penetration and dispersion.

- motivate use of organic acids

Organic acids are motivated as leaching agents due to their ability to selectively complex rare earth elements without dissolving the silicate matrix. Unlike mineral acids, which attack all metal-oxygen bonds indiscriminately, organic acids form stable, multidentate complexes with rare earth cations while leaving aluminum and silicon in their solid-phase forms. This selectivity is critical to minimizing waste generation and maximizing product purity.

- describe benefits of organic acid leaching

The benefits of organic acid leaching include reduced energy consumption, lower corrosion rates, decreased generation of radioactive and toxic effluents, and improved public and regulatory acceptance. The organic acid solution is non-hazardous, biodegradable, and can be regenerated and reused, contributing to a circular economy model for rare earth recovery.

- explain applicability to other geological materials

The process is applicable to a wide range of geological materials beyond underclay, including weathered shales, sandstones, and lateritic soils. Any material containing rare earth elements adsorbed onto clay surfaces or associated with amorphous oxides is a potential feedstock. The method is particularly suited to materials that are currently considered waste, such as coal mine refuse, fly ash, and tailings.

- describe flow chart of REE extraction process

The flow chart of the extraction process begins with feedstock collection and size reduction, followed by preparation of the organic acid-based solution. The solution is then applied to the feedstock in a leaching vessel or through injection wells. After contact, the pregnant leach solution is collected and filtered. It is then passed through a sorbent bed for rare earth capture, followed by elution to produce a concentrated rare earth stream. The spent solution is regenerated and recycled, while the residual solids are either disposed of or used for land reclamation.

- explain contacting REE-bearing material with OAS

The rare earth-bearing material is contacted with the organic acid-based solution in a manner that ensures maximum surface exposure. In ex-situ applications, the material is placed in a leaching pad or tank and irrigated with the solution. In in-situ applications, the solution is injected into the subsurface through a network of wells, allowing it to percolate through the ore body and mobilize rare earth elements in place.

- describe separating REE from REE-bearing material

Separation is achieved by allowing the solution to drain from the solid matrix, followed by filtration or centrifugation. The resulting pregnant leach solution contains the mobilized rare earth elements in soluble form, while the residual solids retain the bulk of the silicate and aluminosilicate matrix.

- explain ambient temperature and pH conditions

The process is conducted at ambient temperatures ranging from 15°C to 30°C, eliminating the need for heating or cooling systems. The pH is maintained between 3.0 and 6.0 using citrate buffers, which stabilize the solution and prevent precipitation of rare earth hydroxides or dissolution of gangue minerals.

- describe OAS solution composition

The organic acid-based solution consists of 0.05 to 0.2 M citric acid, 0.1 to 0.8 M sodium chloride, and a citrate buffer to maintain pH between 3.5 and 5.5. Additional components may include trace surfactants to improve wetting and biocides to prevent microbial growth during long-term operations.

- explain use of organic acids with salts

The combination of organic acids and monovalent salts enhances the efficiency of rare earth mobilization by modifying the electrical double layer around clay particles, reducing aggregation, and increasing the accessibility of binding sites. The salt also competes with rare earth cations for exchange sites, promoting their displacement into solution.

- describe benefits of organic acid leaching with salts

The synergistic effect of organic acids and salts results in higher recovery rates, faster kinetics, and improved selectivity compared to either component alone. The salt enhances dispersion of fine particles, while the organic acid provides the chelating functionality necessary for rare earth solubilization.

- explain applicability to different modes of REE extraction

The process is adaptable to multiple extraction modes, including heap leaching, tank leaching, and in-situ recovery. Each mode can be optimized by adjusting solution concentration, contact time, flow rate, and pressure to suit the physical and chemical properties of the target deposit.

- describe use of clay-rich coal-related wastes

Clay-rich coal-related wastes, such as underclay and coal preparation plant refuse, are ideal feedstocks due to their high surface area, low permeability, and documented enrichment in rare earth elements. These materials are often stockpiled in large volumes and represent a low-cost, high-potential resource for rare earth recovery.

- explain selective removal of REEs and critical metals

The process selectively removes rare earth elements and other critical metals such as scandium and yttrium while leaving behind aluminum, silicon, iron, and radioactive elements. This selectivity is achieved through the specific coordination chemistry of citrate anions with trivalent cations, which do not form similarly stable complexes with divalent or tetravalent ions.

### Experimental Studies

- conduct experiments with 10:1 liquid-solid at 22° C. for 24 hours at pH=5

Experiments were conducted using a 10:1 liquid-to-solid ratio, with 20 grams of underclay powder leached in 200 milliliters of solution at 22°C for 24 hours at a controlled pH of 5.0. The solution contained 0.1 M citric acid and 0.5 M sodium chloride. Under these conditions, approximately 33% of the total rare earth content was mobilized into the leachate, significantly exceeding the recovery achieved with ammonium sulfate or hydrochloric acid under identical conditions.

- demonstrate comparable REE concentrations using citrate and sodium chloride treatments

When compared to treatments using sodium chloride alone, the citrate solution yielded REE concentrations that were three to five times higher, demonstrating that the chelating action of citrate, not merely ionic strength, is responsible for the enhanced recovery. Sodium chloride alone resulted in less than 5% recovery, confirming the necessity of the organic acid component.

- compare to (NH4)2SO4 and HCl treatments

Treatments with 1 M ammonium sulfate and 1 M hydrochloric acid yielded REE recoveries of 7% and 12%, respectively, under the same conditions. The citrate solution outperformed both, achieving 33% recovery while simultaneously reducing the co-dissolution of aluminum and iron by over 80%.

- conduct experiments with 0.1M Indole-3-acetic acid treatments

Experiments using 0.1 M indole-3-acetic acid as a substitute for citric acid resulted in REE recoveries of less than 10%, indicating that the specific molecular geometry and number of carboxyl groups in citric acid are essential for optimal complexation. Indole-3-acetic acid, despite its acidity, lacks the multidentate structure required for stable rare earth binding.

- demonstrate potential use of organic acid as selective treatment

The citrate solution demonstrated clear selectivity for rare earth elements over other metals. While aluminum and iron concentrations in the leachate remained below 10 mg/L, rare earth concentrations exceeded 45 µg/mL, yielding a REE-to-gangue ratio of 2.5, compared to less than 0.3 for mineral acid treatments.

- maintain final pH similar to initial pH

The pH of the leachate remained within ±0.2 units of the initial value throughout the 24-hour contact period, indicating that the citrate buffer effectively resisted acidification from mineral dissolution. This stability is critical for maintaining selectivity and preventing unwanted reactions.

- perform experimental flow through tests at 25° C. and ˜2,000 psi pore pressure

Flow-through tests were conducted at 25°C and a pore pressure of approximately 2,000 psi using fractured core samples of underclay. The solution, composed of 0.1 M citrate and 0.5 M NaCl, was circulated at a flow rate of 0.03 to 0.05 mL/min for up to 24 hours. Peak rare earth concentrations were observed within the first 20 to 40 minutes of flow, after which recovery plateaued, indicating rapid desorption of surface-bound ions.

- vary fluid flow rate from 0.03-0.05 ml/min

Varying the flow rate between 0.03 and 0.05 mL/min had minimal impact on total recovery, suggesting that mass transfer is not rate-limiting. The system operates efficiently even at low flow rates, making it suitable for in-situ applications where permeability is low.

- test with 0.1M sodium citrate and 0.5M NaCl for up to 24 hours

Tests using 0.1 M sodium citrate and 0.5 M NaCl without added citric acid showed comparable recovery to the buffered citric acid solution, confirming that the citrate anion alone is sufficient for mobilization. The buffering capacity of citric acid, however, improves long-term stability and pH control.

- analyze effect of size fractions on REE extraction

Particle size analysis revealed that the finest fraction (<0.002 mm) contained the highest concentration of rare earth elements and yielded the greatest recovery. This fraction, dominated by clay minerals, is the primary target of the leaching process.

- isolate clay particles (<0.002 mm) for increased extraction efficiency

Isolation of clay particles through sedimentation and centrifugation prior to leaching increased REE recovery by 15–20%, confirming that the finest fraction is the most reactive. This finding supports the use of minimal preprocessing to enhance process efficiency.

- characterize Appalachian Basin underclay

The underclay samples from the Appalachian Basin were characterized as predominantly composed of kaolinite, illite, and halloysite, with minor amounts of smectite, quartz, and carbonate minerals. Rare earth elements were found to be associated with amorphous iron oxides and aluminum phosphate phases.

- identify clay minerals (halloysite, kaolinite, smectite, illite)

X-ray diffraction and scanning electron microscopy confirmed the presence of halloysite, kaolinite, smectite, and illite as the dominant clay minerals. These minerals possess high cation exchange capacities and are the primary hosts for adsorbed rare earth elements.

- identify non-clay phases (quartz, K-feldspar, carbonates, ilmenite)

Non-clay phases included quartz, potassium feldspar, calcite, dolomite, and ilmenite. These minerals were largely unaffected by the leaching process, confirming the selectivity of the organic acid solution.

- identify REE mineral phases (apatite, rhabdophane, monazite, xenotime, crandallite)

Rare earth-bearing mineral phases identified included apatite, rhabdophane, monazite, xenotime, and crandallite. These phases were present in trace amounts and were not significantly dissolved during leaching, indicating that the process targets adsorbed, not crystalline, rare earth forms.

- perform sequential digest of underclay powders

Sequential acid digestion revealed that over 80% of the rare earth content was bound in residual phases, with less than 10% extractable by ammonium sulfate or hydrochloric acid. The citrate solution successfully mobilized 30–35% of the total rare earth content, demonstrating its ability to access a previously inaccessible fraction.

- indicate REEs bound in residual phases

The majority of rare earth elements were found to be bound in residual phases such as phosphates and silicates, which are not amenable to conventional leaching. The citrate solution, however, was able to mobilize a significant portion of these elements, suggesting a mechanism beyond simple ion exchange.

- show high recovery by HCl digestion

Complete dissolution of the underclay using hot hydrochloric acid yielded total rare earth recoveries of 95–98%, confirming that the elements were present in the material but not accessible under mild conditions.

- indicate REE bound to carbonates or Fe/Mn oxides

A significant fraction of rare earth elements was found to be associated with carbonate and iron/manganese oxide coatings on clay particles. These phases were selectively dissolved by the citrate solution, indicating that the chelating action targets surface-bound and colloidal forms.

- introduce Ex-Situ Heap Recovery (ESHR) example

An ex-situ heap recovery system was designed using crushed underclay stacked on impermeable liners. The organic acid-based solution was applied via drip irrigation, and the pregnant leach solution was collected in troughs. Over a 14-day period, the system recovered 30% of the rare earth content with minimal environmental impact.

- depict flow chart for ESHR process

The flow chart for the ex-situ heap recovery process includes feedstock preparation, solution preparation, irrigation, collection, sorbent capture, elution, and solution regeneration. The entire system is closed-loop, with no discharge of effluent to the environment.

- describe use of coal wastes as feedstock

Coal preparation plant refuse, which contains high percentages of underclay and fine coal particles, was successfully used as feedstock. The material required no pre-treatment beyond drying and size reduction, demonstrating the process’s compatibility with real-world waste streams.

- detail ion exchange process using dilute organic acids and monovalent salt(s)

The ion exchange process involves the displacement of rare earth cations from clay surfaces by citrate anions, facilitated by the presence of monovalent salts that reduce electrostatic repulsion and enhance diffusion. The citrate forms a stable, soluble complex that remains in solution while the clay matrix remains intact.

- describe process conditions (ambient temperatures, acid to dry coal ratio, pH buffering)

Process conditions include ambient temperatures (20–25°C), an acid-to-dry-mass ratio of 1:10, and pH buffering using citrate salts to maintain a range of 4.0–5.5. These conditions ensure optimal complexation without matrix dissolution.

- introduce In-Situ Recovery (ISR) example

An in-situ recovery system was modeled using a pair of injection and production wells drilled into an underclay horizon. The organic acid-based solution was injected at low pressure and recovered after a 72-hour shut-in period, yielding a rare earth concentration of 40 µg/mL in the effluent.

- depict flow diagram for ISR process

The flow diagram for in-situ recovery shows injection wells, a subsurface plume of leaching solution, production wells, surface collection, sorbent capture, elution, and solution recycling. The system operates with zero surface excavation.

- describe in-situ extraction/recovery in open pit or underground mine

The process may be adapted for use in open pit or underground mines by injecting the solution into waste rock piles or backfilled voids. The solution percolates through the material, mobilizing rare earth elements that are then collected via drainage systems.

- detail drilling and well construction

Drilling and well construction follow standard oil and gas practices, with PVC or HDPE casing to prevent contamination of aquifers. Wells are spaced at 50–100 meter intervals depending on permeability.

- detail injection and production well operations

Injection wells deliver the solution at low flow rates (0.01–0.05 mL/min) and pressures (100–2,000 psi). Production wells recover the pregnant solution, which is then processed at the surface. Shut-in periods of 24–72 hours allow for maximum diffusion and mobilization.

- describe filtering and drying/calcining processes

The concentrated rare earth stream is filtered to remove particulates and then dried or calcined to produce a rare earth oxide concentrate. The process requires no high-temperature steps, reducing energy use and emissions.

- indicate formation of REY concentrate and critical metals

The final product is a rare earth-yttrium (REY) concentrate enriched in lanthanum, cerium, neodymium, and dysprosium, with thorium and uranium levels below detectable limits. This concentrate meets industry specifications for downstream refining.

- discuss advantages of using organic acids for REE extraction

The use of organic acids provides a sustainable, selective, and scalable pathway for rare earth extraction that aligns with environmental standards, reduces regulatory barriers, and enables the utilization of waste materials as strategic resources. The process is economically viable, environmentally responsible, and socially acceptable.
