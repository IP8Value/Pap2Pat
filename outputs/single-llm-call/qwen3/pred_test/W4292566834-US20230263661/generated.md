# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNOLOGICAL FIELD

- introduce ocular treatment and diagnosis

The present invention relates to the field of ophthalmology, specifically to noninvasive methods and devices for the diagnosis and therapeutic management of intraocular particulate matter. The invention encompasses the application of acoustic radiation forces to manipulate the spatial distribution of pathological or therapeutic particles within the anterior chamber of the human eye. This technology enables the controlled movement, aggregation, and localization of suspended particulate matter without direct physical contact, thermal ablation, or surgical intervention. The method is particularly relevant for conditions in which the presence of particulates compromises visual clarity, obstructs aqueous humor outflow pathways, or contributes to the development of secondary ocular pathologies such as glaucoma. By harnessing the physical properties of acoustic waves and their interaction with particles of differing acoustic contrast relative to the surrounding aqueous humor, the invention provides a novel diagnostic and therapeutic modality that bridges the gap between imaging-based observation and active intervention. The system is designed for integration into standard ophthalmic examination and treatment environments, offering a safe, repeatable, and tunable approach to managing conditions that currently lack preventive or noninvasive treatment options.

## BACKGROUND

- describe ophthalmic disorders
- explain particulate matter in the eye
- discuss current treatment limitations

Ophthalmic disorders characterized by the presence of intraocular particulate matter represent a significant clinical challenge, particularly when such particles accumulate in the anterior chamber of the eye. These particles may originate from a variety of pathological processes, including pigment dispersion syndrome, pseudoexfoliation, hyphema, asteroid hyalosis, or the inadvertent introduction of therapeutic agents such as triamcinolone acetonide. While some of these conditions are transient and self-resolving, others lead to chronic deposition of material on critical ocular structures such as the corneal endothelium, trabecular meshwork, and iris, resulting in progressive visual impairment and elevated intraocular pressure. Current clinical management strategies are largely reactive, focusing on the treatment of secondary complications—such as glaucoma or corneal decompensation—rather than addressing the root cause: the uncontrolled movement and distribution of particulate matter within the aqueous humor. Medical therapies, including topical anti-inflammatories or pressure-lowering agents, do not remove or reposition particles, and surgical interventions such as anterior chamber washout or vitrectomy carry inherent risks of infection, hemorrhage, and iatrogenic damage to delicate ocular tissues. As a result, clinicians are often forced to adopt a passive “watch-and-wait” approach, permitting particles to settle unpredictably across the visual axis and outflow pathways, thereby increasing the likelihood of irreversible structural damage. There exists, therefore, a critical unmet need for a noninvasive, precise, and controllable method to actively manage the spatial dynamics of intraocular particles prior to the onset of secondary complications.

The presence of particulate matter within the anterior chamber of the eye initiates a cascade of biomechanical and physiological consequences that extend beyond mere optical obstruction. In the short term, these particles scatter and absorb incident light, leading to glare, halos, and reduced contrast sensitivity, which significantly impair visual function. Over time, the continuous settling and adherence of particles to the trabecular meshwork and Schlemm’s canal obstruct the conventional outflow pathway of aqueous humor, resulting in elevated intraocular pressure and the development of secondary open-angle glaucoma. The corneal endothelium, which lacks regenerative capacity, is particularly vulnerable to chronic particle contact, potentially leading to endothelial cell loss, corneal edema, and Krukenberg’s spindle formation. These long-term sequelae underscore the necessity for a proactive intervention strategy that moderates particle distribution before irreversible damage occurs. The present invention addresses this need through the application of precisely controlled acoustic radiation forces, which act upon particles based on their acoustic contrast factor relative to the surrounding aqueous humor. Particles with higher density and lower compressibility than the fluid are driven toward regions of minimal acoustic pressure—known as nodal planes—where they aggregate into stable, localized clusters. This phenomenon permits the noninvasive relocation of particles away from critical anatomical structures, effectively clearing the visual axis and preserving the integrity of the outflow system. The invention employs an acoustic transducer array configured to generate standing or traveling acoustic waves within the anterior chamber, with parameters such as frequency, amplitude, phase, and spatial pattern meticulously selected to optimize particle manipulation while ensuring tissue safety. The acoustic contrast factor, which governs the direction and magnitude of the radiation force, is inherently favorable for most pathological and therapeutic particles encountered in clinical practice. Particle size influences the kinetics of aggregation, with smaller particles requiring longer exposure times but still responding predictably to sustained acoustic fields. The device is housed in a biocompatible casing with an integrated coupling layer designed to match acoustic impedance between the transducer and the ocular surface, minimizing reflection and maximizing energy transmission. A motion stage enables precise repositioning of the transducer relative to the eye, allowing dynamic control over the location of nodal regions. The controller, operating via a closed-loop feedback system, modulates the frequency and amplitude of the acoustic signal in real time to adapt to variations in particle concentration, eye movement, and tissue response. Acoustic holograms may be employed to sculpt complex radiation patterns, enabling three-dimensional particle confinement and directed movement across the anterior chamber. The generation of corneal standing acoustic waves, facilitated by the coupling layer, ensures uniform energy distribution without thermal buildup or mechanical stress. Acoustic streaming, induced by the oscillatory motion of the fluid, further assists in the redistribution of particles toward the targeted nodal zones. This method not only alleviates immediate visual disturbances but also serves as a preventive modality by reducing the probability of particle-induced trabecular meshwork obstruction. The invention thus transforms passive observation into active intervention, offering a paradigm shift in the management of particulate ocular disorders.

The scope of the present invention encompasses a comprehensive system and method for the noninvasive control of intraocular particulate matter through the application of acoustic radiation forces. The human eyeball, as a closed optical and fluidic system, consists of an outer fibrous layer composed of the sclera and cornea, a middle uveal layer including the iris and ciliary body, and an inner neural retina. Internally, the eye is divided into two primary chambers: the anterior chamber, bounded by the cornea and iris, and the posterior vitreous chamber, separated by the lens. The anterior chamber contains aqueous humor, a transparent fluid continuously secreted by the ciliary body and drained through the trabecular meshwork and Schlemm’s canal into the episcleral venous system. Disruption of this drainage pathway by particulate accumulation leads to impaired outflow and elevated intraocular pressure. The invention introduces an ocular acoustic device comprising an acoustic transducer array, a controller, and a coupling interface designed for contact with the corneal surface. The transducer, typically constructed from lead zirconate titanate or similar piezoelectric material, operates in transverse vibration modes at frequencies between 0.8 MHz and 2.5 MHz, generating standing acoustic waves within the anterior chamber. The controller, implemented via a programmable microprocessor, regulates the amplitude, phase, and spatial distribution of the acoustic signals, enabling the formation of tailored radiation patterns that direct particles toward predetermined nodal regions. The coupling layer, composed of a biocompatible elastomer such as polydimethylsiloxane, ensures efficient acoustic impedance matching between the transducer and the corneal epithelium, minimizing reflection losses and preventing mechanical abrasion. The device may incorporate a phased array configuration, allowing for electronic beam steering without physical movement, or a motion stage that mechanically repositions the transducer to shift the location of nodal zones. Acoustic holograms, generated through spatial modulation of the transducer array, permit the creation of complex, three-dimensional trapping fields capable of confining particles in specific geometries. Imaging modules, such as optical coherence tomography or slit-lamp cameras, provide real-time feedback on particle distribution, enabling closed-loop control of the acoustic field. The system may be integrated into a networked computing environment, with data storage and remote operation capabilities for clinical documentation and protocol standardization. The method involves generating an acoustic field within the anterior chamber, inducing acoustic radiation forces that drive particles toward nodal planes, where they aggregate into dense clusters. These clusters remain stable after deactivation of the field, effectively sequestering particles away from the visual axis and outflow structures. The thickness of the particle aggregates increases over time in proportion to the duration and intensity of the acoustic exposure, with larger particles forming more rapidly due to greater acoustic force. The invention is applicable to a wide range of particulate conditions, including pigment dispersion, pseudoexfoliation, hyphema, and therapeutic particle deposition. By relocating particles to non-critical regions of the angle, the method prevents obstruction of the trabecular meshwork and mitigates the risk of secondary glaucoma. The device may be operated in diagnostic mode to visualize particle distribution patterns or in therapeutic mode to actively displace and concentrate particulate matter. No surgical incision, pharmacological agent, or thermal energy is required. The invention represents a novel, noninvasive, and tunable approach to the management of intraocular particulate disorders, with broad applicability across clinical, diagnostic, and preventive ophthalmic practice.
