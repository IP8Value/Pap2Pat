# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNICAL FIELD

The present invention relates generally to the field of natural language processing and, more specifically, to systems and methods for dialogue act tagging in task-oriented dialogue systems using neural networks. The invention provides an improved approach for adapting pre-trained language models—particularly masked language models such as BERT—to perform accurate dialogue act classification across domains with limited or no labeled data in the target domain. This is achieved through a novel data augmentation technique referred to herein as “MASKAUGMENT,” which leverages stochastic masking of input tokens during training to enhance cross-domain generalization. The invention further encompasses a semi-supervised teacher-student learning framework that utilizes consistency regularization between differently augmented views of unlabeled dialogue sequences to improve model robustness and performance in low-resource and cross-domain settings.

## BACKGROUND

Neural networks have become foundational components in modern natural language understanding (NLU) systems, particularly within task-oriented dialogue systems where accurate interpretation of user intent is critical for successful interaction. Among the core NLU tasks is dialogue act (DA) tagging, which involves classifying each utterance in a conversation according to predefined communicative functions or intentions, such as “request,” “inform,” or “confirm.” Accurate DA tagging enables downstream modules—including dialogue state tracking and policy learning—to function effectively by providing structured semantic representations of conversational turns. Despite significant advances in deep learning, existing approaches to DA tagging remain constrained by their reliance on large volumes of in-domain annotated data, which are often expensive and time-consuming to produce. Moreover, the proliferation of diverse annotation schemas across datasets has hindered the development of universally applicable DA taggers capable of generalizing across domains without extensive retraining or manual schema alignment.

Limitations of current dialogue act tagging methodologies are particularly pronounced in cross-domain scenarios. Traditional supervised models trained on one domain typically exhibit degraded performance when applied to another due to differences in linguistic style, vocabulary usage, and task structure. While recent work has attempted to mitigate this issue through universal annotation schemas and transfer learning, these solutions still fall short in low-resource settings where labeled examples from the target domain are scarce or nonexistent. Furthermore, although pre-trained language models like BERT have demonstrated strong performance on many NLP benchmarks, their direct application to DA tagging often fails to fully exploit unlabeled dialogue data available in the target domain. Existing semi-supervised and domain adaptation techniques either rely on complex augmentation strategies that do not integrate well with pre-trained architectures or lack mechanisms to enforce consistency between predictions made on perturbed versions of the same input. Consequently, there remains a pressing need for a robust, efficient, and scalable method for dialogue act tagging that can generalize effectively across domains while leveraging both labeled source data and unlabeled target dialogues.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

Dialogue act tagging plays a pivotal role in enabling intelligent dialogue systems to understand and respond appropriately to user inputs. In task-oriented settings—such as booking flights, reserving restaurants, or playing music—the ability to accurately infer the speaker’s intention at each turn directly impacts system usability and success rate. However, building effective DA taggers requires overcoming several challenges, including variability in dialogue structures across domains, scarcity of labeled data in new domains, and the need for models that can adapt quickly without extensive reannotation efforts. To address these issues, the present invention introduces a novel framework that combines pre-trained masked language models with a specially designed data augmentation strategy called MASKAUGMENT, integrated within a semi-supervised teacher-student learning paradigm.

Existing dialogue act taggers suffer from multiple limitations that impede their practical deployment in real-world applications. First, most prior approaches assume access to substantial amounts of labeled data in the target domain, which is rarely feasible outside controlled research environments. Second, even when leveraging pre-trained models like BERT, standard fine-tuning procedures fail to capitalize on the wealth of unlabeled dialogue logs commonly available in production systems. Third, conventional data augmentation techniques—such as synonym replacement or back-translation—are ill-suited for multi-turn dialogues and often degrade performance when used with pre-trained transformers. These shortcomings underscore the necessity for a new approach that aligns naturally with the architecture and training dynamics of modern language models.

Embodiments of the present invention overcome these limitations by introducing MASKAUGMENT—a simple yet powerful augmentation technique that stochastically replaces tokens in dialogue sequences with the [MASK] token used during pre-training of models like BERT. This method preserves syntactic and semantic coherence better than alternative augmentations while enabling controlled perturbations that facilitate consistency-based learning. By integrating MASKAUGMENT into a teacher-student framework, the invention enables unsupervised adaptation to target domains using only unlabeled dialogue histories. Key terms used throughout this disclosure include: *dialogue act* (a communicative function assigned to an utterance), *multi-label classification* (the formulation of DA tagging where each turn may be associated with multiple acts), *pre-trained language model* (e.g., BERT, initialized with weights learned from large corpora), *mask augmentation* (stochastic replacement of input tokens with [MASK]), and *consistency regularization* (a semi-supervised learning principle encouraging stable predictions under input perturbations).

### Overview

Dialogue act tagging is formally defined as a multi-label classification problem wherein, given a dialogue history up to a certain turn, the system must predict a subset of applicable dialogue acts from a fixed schema. For example, a user utterance such as “I’d like to book a flight to New York tomorrow” might trigger both “inform” and “request” acts. The invention employs a block diagram architecture wherein a pre-trained language model processes a flattened representation of the dialogue history—comprising alternating user and system utterances prefixed with speaker tags—and outputs a probability distribution over possible acts via a sigmoid-activated linear layer atop the [CLS] token embedding.

However, pre-trained language models alone are insufficient for optimal cross-domain performance. Their internal representations, though rich, are not inherently tuned to the nuances of dialogue-specific semantics or the distributional shifts present in new domains. To bridge this gap, the invention introduces mask augmentation as a means of adapting these models efficiently. During training, input sequences are randomly masked at varying probabilities to generate augmented views that serve as inputs to both teacher and student networks within a consistency-enforcing framework.

An illustrative data segment of labeled dialogue includes utterances annotated with corresponding acts, such as “[USR] Can I get a table for two? → request-table.” In contrast, a target-domain dialogue segment may consist solely of raw conversation logs without any annotations, e.g., “[USR] Is there a vegetarian option? [SYS] Yes, we have several.” The challenge lies in transferring knowledge from the labeled source domain to such unlabeled target dialogues—a problem known as cross-domain generalization.

The invention operates within a standard computer environment comprising one or more computing devices equipped with processors, memory, and machine-readable media storing executable instructions. A dedicated dialogue act tagging module implements the core functionality, supported by several loss computation modules: a supervised tagging loss module for labeled data, a masked tagging loss module for augmented labeled inputs, a masked language model loss module for reconstructing masked tokens, and a disagreement loss module that enforces consistency between teacher and student predictions on unlabeled data.

Training proceeds through a combination of supervised and semi-supervised objectives. Labeled examples contribute to the supervised tagging loss (STL), computed via binary cross-entropy between predicted and ground-truth act labels. Unlabeled examples are processed through MASKAUGMENT to yield two augmented sequences—one lightly masked (teacher) and one more heavily masked (student)—whose output distributions are compared using a disagreement loss (DAL). Additionally, the original masked language modeling (MLM) objective may be retained during fine-tuning to preserve language modeling capabilities.

Formally, the dialogue act tagging task maps a dialogue history \( D_{:k} \) to a binary label vector \( y \in \{0,1\}^m \), where \( m \) is the number of acts in the schema. The model computes \( p_\theta(a_j | x) = \sigma(W h_{[CLS]} + b)_j \), where \( h_{[CLS]} \) is the contextual embedding of the [CLS] token, and \( \theta \) denotes all trainable parameters. The total loss is a weighted sum of active components: \( \mathcal{L} = \mathcal{L}_{STL} + \lambda_1 \mathcal{L}_{MTL} + \lambda_2 \mathcal{L}_{DAL} + \lambda_3 \mathcal{L}_{MLM} \), with \( \lambda_3 = 0.1 \) in practice.

Mask augmentation generates stochastic variants \( x^{(t)} \sim z(x|x, \epsilon_t) \) and \( x^{(s)} \sim z(x|x, \epsilon_s) \), where \( \epsilon_t < \epsilon_s \), ensuring the teacher retains more original content. Output distributions from both views are compared using binary cross-entropy to compute DAL. This mechanism encourages the model to produce invariant predictions despite input perturbations, thereby enhancing robustness and generalization.

The method for training involves receiving a dialogue history, generating its flattened representation with speaker tags, applying mask augmentation to create multiple views, computing individual losses, and aggregating them into a composite loss metric that guides parameter updates via gradient descent.

### Example Performance

Experimental validation was conducted on two benchmark datasets: GSIM and SGD. GSIM contains machine-machine dialogues in movie ticketing and restaurant reservation domains, mapped to a 13-act universal schema. SGD comprises human-human dialogues across 20 domains under a unified annotation framework, enabling rigorous cross-domain evaluation.

Results demonstrate that BERT significantly outperforms prior non-transformer baselines. More importantly, incorporating MTL and DAL objectives yields consistent improvements: up to 3% absolute F1 gain in full-data settings and up to 10% in low-resource regimes. Transformer models benefit less than BERT, highlighting the synergy between MASKAUGMENT and pre-trained masked language models. Fine-tuning with STL alone establishes a strong baseline, but adding DAL and MTL consistently boosts performance across domains.

Pre-BERT models (trained from scratch on dialogue data) show larger gaps relative to pre-trained BERT, but MASKAUGMENT narrows this difference substantially—reducing recall gaps from over 2% to less than 0.5%. MLM loss applied during target-domain fine-tuning further enhances adaptation, confirming that continued language modeling aids representation learning.

In low-resource settings (e.g., using only 5–20% of source labels), the proposed method maintains high accuracy, demonstrating its suitability for real-world deployment where labeling budgets are constrained. Domain-adaptive pre-training using MLM on target dialogues also proves beneficial, though MASKAUGMENT provides complementary gains.

Figure 9 presents micro-F1 scores per dialogue act, revealing that gains are distributed across most categories, with notable improvements in infrequent acts like “sys-notify-failure.” Example outputs illustrate correct tagging of complex intents that baseline models miss, especially in pre-BERT configurations.

All experiments were executed on standard computing devices with GPU acceleration, using machine-readable media storing code implemented via the HuggingFace Transformers library. While the disclosed method achieves state-of-the-art results, its scope is limited to text-based dialogue systems and assumes availability of unlabeled target dialogues; extensions to multimodal or speech-based systems would require additional adaptations.
