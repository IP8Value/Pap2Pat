# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

Tsunamis are large-scale oceanic waves generated by sudden displacements of water, typically resulting from subsea earthquakes, landslides, or atmospheric pressure anomalies. These waves propagate across ocean basins at high speeds, often exceeding 100 kilometers per hour in deep water, and can cause catastrophic damage upon reaching coastal regions. Early detection and accurate forecasting of tsunami arrival time and intensity are critical for effective warning systems and mitigation efforts. Historically, seismic monitoring has served as the first indication of a potential tsunami, but not all underwater earthquakes generate tsunamis, and seismic data alone cannot reliably predict the magnitude or local impact of a resulting wave. Consequently, direct observation of the tsunami itself is essential for issuing precise and timely warnings.

Deep-ocean Assessment and Reporting of Tsunami (DART) sensor systems, developed by the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration (NOAA), represent the current operational standard for direct tsunami detection. These systems consist of bottom pressure recorders anchored to the seafloor and connected to surface buoys that transmit data via satellite. While DART sensors provide valuable measurements of tsunami wave height in the deep ocean, they suffer from several limitations. The spatial distribution of DART stations is sparse, particularly in certain oceanic regions, which means that many tsunamis—especially those generated near coastlines or in areas without nearby buoys—may not be detected before making landfall. Furthermore, DART-based forecasts rely on numerical models that extrapolate deep-ocean measurements to coastal zones, often producing coarse estimates of local wave height and arrival time due to insufficient resolution of near-field bathymetry and coastal geometry. This “near-field” region—typically within 50 kilometers of the shore—is precisely where detailed, real-time information is most needed for evacuation and emergency response.

Numerical tsunami models, while powerful, also face inherent constraints. These models require accurate initial conditions, which may not be available if the tsunami is not observed by DART or other deep-ocean sensors. Additionally, model accuracy degrades in complex coastal environments where fine-scale bathymetric features, harbor resonances, and shoreline irregularities significantly influence wave behavior. As a result, there remains a critical gap in the ability to provide high-resolution, real-time detection and characterization of tsunamis in the immediate coastal zone where human populations are most vulnerable. This gap underscores the need for complementary sensing technologies capable of continuous, localized monitoring of tsunami-induced surface currents with sufficient sensitivity and spatial coverage to support actionable early warnings.

## SUMMARY

The present invention introduces a novel tsunami detection system that leverages high-frequency (HF) coastal radar networks to provide real-time, near-field monitoring of tsunami-induced orbital velocities. The system operates by receiving Bragg peak information from HF radar echoes, which contain Doppler-shifted signals corresponding to ocean surface currents. From this spectral data, the system extracts Bragg peak information to isolate the radial component of surface current velocity. A tsunami alert is then generated when a pattern recognition algorithm—specifically, a q-factor algorithm—detects characteristic spatiotemporal signatures indicative of a tsunami within the velocity field. The credibility of each alert is assessed through multiple validation mechanisms, including correlation with external sources such as seismic event notifications, comparison with stored historical correlations of tsunami signatures, and cross-verification with nearby radar systems.

Upon generating a preliminary alert, the system determines its credibility by evaluating whether the detected signal aligns with expected tsunami behavior, such as coherent oscillations across adjacent spatial bands and consistency with predicted arrival windows based on earthquake origin data. If the alert is deemed credible, it is transmitted to a tsunami warning center for further action; if not, the alert is ignored or flagged as low-confidence. The system also correlates the detected signal with external sources, such as global earthquake databases, to confirm whether a potential tsunamigenic event has occurred within a relevant time and distance window. Using numerical near-field tsunami models informed by local bathymetry, the system estimates both the tsunami’s arrival time window and its expected wave height at the coast. This estimation accounts for the complexity of ocean currents, including background flows and tidal influences, which may mask or distort the tsunami signal.

The system further resolves radial current information into components perpendicular and parallel to depth contours, enabling more robust detection in varied coastal geometries. It determines the orbital velocity of the tsunami wave—a key indicator that increases more rapidly than wave height as the tsunami approaches shallow water—and generates Doppler spectra information from raw radar returns. To enhance signal fidelity, the system employs interference removal techniques to filter out man-made radio noise and spurious echoes from ships or ionospheric reflections. The extracted Bragg peak information is then processed through the q-factor algorithm, which quantifies the degree of spatial and temporal coherence among velocity bands. Alerts are only issued when the q-factor exceeds a site-specific threshold calibrated to balance probability of detection against false-alarm rate. Finally, the system integrates all validated alerts into operational displays at warning centers, providing emergency managers with actionable intelligence on tsunami presence, estimated arrival time, and potential intensity well before coastal inundation occurs.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

High-frequency (HF) radar systems have been used for oceanographic observations since the 1960s, primarily for mapping surface currents and wave fields. These systems operate by transmitting vertically polarized radio waves over the sea surface, which interact with ocean waves of half the radar wavelength to produce backscattered echoes. The Doppler shift of these echoes provides information about the radial component of surface current velocity. Over the past two decades, national networks of coastal HF radars have been established worldwide, including the U.S. Integrated Ocean Observing System (IOOS), which comprises over 130 SeaSonde® radar sites. This existing infrastructure presents a unique opportunity for repurposing HF radars as real-time tsunami sensors in the near-field coastal zone.

The motivation for using HF radars for tsunami detection stems from their ability to continuously monitor surface currents over large areas (up to 50 km offshore) with high temporal resolution (as frequent as every 4 minutes). Unlike DART systems, which measure wave height in the deep ocean, HF radars detect the orbital velocity of the tsunami wave—a quantity that becomes increasingly pronounced as the wave moves into shallower water. Barrick first proposed this concept in 1979, suggesting that the orbital velocity signature of a tsunami could be discerned in HF radar data. However, it was not until the 2011 Tohoku, Japan tsunami—observed by numerous HF radars across the Pacific Rim—that sufficient empirical data became available to develop and validate robust detection algorithms.

Despite their promise, HF radars face challenges in tsunami detection, including masking by variable background currents, radio frequency interference, and signal aliasing. The present invention addresses these limitations through a suite of integrated signal processing and validation techniques. Systems enabled by this disclosure operate in two modes: online (real-time) and simulator (offline performance evaluation). The history of HF radar use in oceanography informs the design of these systems, which build upon decades of experience in current mapping and spectral analysis.

A core innovation is the q-factor tsunami pattern recognition algorithm, which evaluates the coherence of velocity oscillations across spatially adjacent bands oriented parallel to depth contours. This algorithm quantifies three key features: (1) significant velocity changes over consecutive time intervals within a band, (2) coincidence of velocity extrema across neighboring bands, and (3) consistent behavior across three or more adjacent bands. The resulting q-factor serves as a quantitative metric for tsunami presence, with thresholds optimized per site to balance detection probability (Pd) and false-alarm probability (Pfa).

The system incorporates a numerical near-field tsunami model based on linear shallow-water equations, which accurately predicts wave height and orbital velocity given local bathymetry. This model is essential for estimating tsunami intensity and arrival time at specific coastal locations, complementing coarse global forecasts. HF radars typically operate in the 3–30 MHz frequency range, though systems outside this band may also be adapted.

Referring to FIG. 1, a simplified diagram of a coastal HF radar site includes a transmit antenna, receive antennas, a GPS receiver for precise timing, a direct digital synthesizer for RF generation, and a microprocessor-controlled radar state machine. The GPS receiver ensures synchronization across radar networks, critical for multi-site correlation. The radar state machine manages operational modes, data acquisition, and signal processing workflows.

In simulator mode, the system evaluates site suitability by superimposing simulated tsunami velocities—derived from real events and adjusted for local bathymetry via Green’s Law—onto measured background currents. This allows optimization of detection thresholds and assessment of expected warning times. FIG. 2 illustrates the algorithmic flow of simulator mode, including band velocity simulation, q-factor calculation, and threshold tuning.

In online mode (FIG. 3), real-time radar data undergoes preprocessing to remove interference, extract Bragg peaks, and compute radial velocities. These are resolved into perpendicular and parallel components relative to bathymetric contours and averaged into 2-km-wide bands. The q-factor algorithm then analyzes temporal coherence, and alerts are subjected to credibility assessment. False alarms are mitigated by correlating alerts with seismic notifications, neighboring radar detections, and historical patterns. Arrival time is estimated using phase velocity integrals over bathymetric profiles (Equation 10), while wave height and velocity are derived from Green’s Law (Equations 6–7) or exact PDE solutions (Equations 3–4).

The system logs interference bands and correlates them with false-alarm peaks to adaptively adjust thresholds. Final processing includes transmission of validated alerts to warning centers, integration into operational dashboards, and dynamic recalibration based on changing oceanic and electromagnetic conditions. This comprehensive approach enables reliable, early detection of tsunamis in the critical near-field region, significantly enhancing coastal resilience.
