# DESCRIPTION

Microelectrodes and devices for neural interfacing applications represent a critical technological frontier in both fundamental neuroscience and clinical neuroengineering. These components serve as the primary physical interface between electronic systems and biological neural tissue, enabling bidirectional communication through the recording of endogenous electrical activity and the delivery of exogenous electrical stimulation. The development of increasingly sophisticated microelectrode arrays (MEAs) has been instrumental in advancing brain-machine interfaces, neural prostheses, and diagnostic tools for neurological disorders. Such devices must meet stringent performance criteria, including high signal fidelity, long-term stability, biocompatibility, and the capacity to operate at high spatial resolution without inducing tissue damage or immune rejection. The present invention addresses these challenges by introducing a novel microelectrode architecture that leverages the unique electrochemical and mechanical properties of synthetic diamond, particularly when structured at the nanoscale to enhance interfacial characteristics.

Neurostimulation and the recording of electric signals from neural tissue are foundational operations in modern neural interfaces. Neurostimulation involves the controlled delivery of electrical current to specific neural populations to modulate their activity, thereby restoring lost sensory or motor functions or treating pathological conditions such as epilepsy or Parkinson’s disease. Conversely, neural recording captures the spontaneous or evoked electrical activity of neurons, providing insights into network dynamics, enabling real-time decoding of neural intent, or facilitating closed-loop therapeutic interventions. Both functions demand electrodes that can efficiently transfer charge across the electrode-electrolyte-tissue interface while maintaining signal integrity and minimizing noise. The efficiency of this process is governed by the electrode’s electrochemical impedance, charge injection capacity, and signal-to-noise ratio, all of which are highly dependent on the material composition and surface morphology of the electrode.

Examples of applications that rely on such microelectrode technologies include cochlear implants for auditory restoration, retinal implants for visual prosthetics, deep brain stimulation (DBS) for movement disorders, and cortical implants for motor control or communication in paralyzed individuals. Each of these applications imposes distinct requirements on electrode size, density, and longevity. For instance, retinal implants benefit from high-density electrode arrays to achieve fine-grained visual percepts, while DBS systems prioritize long-term stability and resistance to biofouling. In all cases, the electrode material must support chronic implantation without degradation, inflammation, or loss of function.

The requirements for electrodes used in neurostimulation are particularly demanding. They must exhibit high charge injection capacity to deliver sufficient current without exceeding safe electrochemical limits, low impedance to minimize voltage drop and power consumption, and excellent biocompatibility to avoid adverse tissue reactions. Additionally, the electrode must be mechanically robust to withstand repeated handling, implantation forces, and long-term exposure to the physiological environment. These requirements are often in tension; for example, reducing electrode size to increase spatial resolution typically increases impedance and reduces charge capacity, thereby degrading performance.

Biocompatibility and the charge transfer process are central to the functionality of any neural electrode. Biocompatibility ensures that the implanted material does not elicit toxic, inflammatory, or fibrotic responses that could isolate the electrode from neural tissue or compromise its function. The charge transfer process, meanwhile, determines how effectively electrical current is exchanged between the electrode and the surrounding ionic medium. This process can occur through capacitive mechanisms (non-faradaic), faradaic redox reactions, or a combination thereof. Ideal neural electrodes maximize safe, reversible charge transfer while minimizing irreversible reactions that produce harmful byproducts such to oxygen or hydrogen evolution.

The electrochemical properties of electrodes—such as double-layer capacitance, potential window, and impedance—are key determinants of their suitability for neural interfacing. A wide potential window allows operation at higher voltages without water electrolysis, which is critical for safe stimulation. High double-layer capacitance enables greater charge storage and lower impedance, improving both recording sensitivity and stimulation efficiency. However, many conventional electrode materials, such as platinum or iridium oxide, face limitations in one or more of these domains, especially when miniaturized.

Limitations of currently used electrodes for neurostimulation include poor long-term stability, susceptibility to corrosion, limited charge injection capacity at small scales, and high intrinsic noise. Metals like platinum can degrade over time due to oxidation or dissolution, while conductive polymers may delaminate or lose conductivity in vivo. These issues are exacerbated as electrode dimensions shrink to accommodate higher-density arrays, making the search for alternative materials imperative.

Electrodes used for electro-physiological applications ex-vivo, such as in slice preparations or cell cultures, also require high sensitivity and low noise to detect small-amplitude signals from immature or sparse neural networks. The electrochemical properties required for these applications closely mirror those needed for in vivo use, emphasizing the need for materials that perform consistently across experimental and clinical contexts.

The development of electrodes with increasingly small diameters has been driven by the desire to increase spatial resolution and reduce tissue damage. However, this miniaturization leads to a reduction in geometric surface area, which directly increases impedance and decreases charge injection capacity. This trade-off has long been a bottleneck in neural interface design.

A significant problem associated with miniaturized electrodes is their gradual degradation over time, whether through electrochemical corrosion, mechanical wear, or biofouling. This degradation compromises signal quality and device longevity, limiting the utility of chronic implants.

Synthetic diamond has emerged as a promising candidate to overcome these limitations. It is chemically and biologically inert, exhibits excellent electrochemical properties, and demonstrates remarkable mechanical durability. Among diamond-based materials, boron-doped polycrystalline diamond (BDD) stands out for its semiconducting behavior and wide electrochemical potential window.

The properties of synthetic diamond—particularly its chemical inertness, resistance to biofouling, and stability under high current densities—make it ideal for chronic neural implants. BDD combines these advantages with electrical conductivity tunable via boron doping, enabling its use as an active electrode material.

The growth of synthetic diamond is typically achieved through chemical vapor deposition (CVD) techniques, which allow precise control over film thickness, doping level, and crystallinity. Methods such as microwave plasma CVD, radiofrequency CVD, and hot filament CVD are commonly employed.

Polycrystalline diamond doped with boron (BDD) offers a compelling combination of biocompatibility and electrochemical performance. However, conventional BDD suffers from a small double-layer electrochemical capacity and high impedance due to its relatively smooth surface and low intrinsic capacitance.

This limitation presents the core technical problem to be solved: how to enhance the electrochemical performance of BDD microelectrodes without compromising their biocompatibility or mechanical stability, particularly at microscale dimensions.

The object of the invention is therefore a microelectrode for neural interfacing applications that overcomes the limitations of conventional BDD by incorporating a three-dimensional nanostructured architecture that dramatically increases effective surface area while maintaining the inherent advantages of diamond.

The structure of the microelectrode comprises a stack of layers. A first layer consists of a biocompatible substrate, such as fused silica or silicon, providing mechanical support and electrical isolation. A second layer, composed of an adhesion-promoting material such as titanium nitride or non-doped polycrystalline diamond, ensures robust bonding between the substrate and the active electrode structure. A third layer forms the active portion of the microelectrode and consists of a textured material comprising hollow or solid tubes projecting away from the underlying layers.

These tubes possess a radially external peripheral layer made of boron-doped polycrystalline diamond, which provides the electrochemically active surface. The tubes are separated from each other, creating a porous, high-surface-area architecture that enhances charge transfer and reduces impedance.

Particular embodiments of the microelectrode include variations in tube diameter (nanometric to micrometric), height (typically 1 to 10 micrometers), and density, tailored to specific neural applications. The active portion may assume a cylindrical, conical, or bundled shape, with a defined electrode diameter—such as 20 micrometers—to match standard MEA geometries.

A multi-electrode network for neural interfacing applications can be fabricated by arranging multiple such microelectrodes in a planar or flexible array, enabling simultaneous recording and stimulation across distributed neural regions. Flexible implants incorporating these microelectrodes conform to curved neural surfaces, improving contact and signal quality.

The method for manufacturing the microelectrode involves a sequence of precisely controlled deposition and etching steps. The first step entails depositing a first material—comprising a biocompatible insulating substrate such as fused silica—onto which subsequent layers are built. The second step involves depositing a second material, such as titanium nitride or non-doped polycrystalline diamond, to serve as an adhesion layer and current collector. The third step deposits a third material—synthetic polycrystalline diamond—structured into a compact set of hollow or solid tubes with nanometric to micrometric dimensions.

The fourth step includes the formation of a fourth layer, which may consist of a metal contact outlet, such as chromium/gold, deposited to provide electrical connection to external circuitry. A fifth step involves patterning the electrode sites using photolithography and reactive ion etching to define individual microelectrodes. A sixth step applies a passivation layer, such as SU-8 photoresist, to insulate conductive tracks while exposing only the active electrode areas.

The seventh step may involve the growth of carbon nanotubes (CNTs) on catalytic metal nanoparticles, such as nickel, deposited selectively on the electrode sites. The eighth step deposits non-doped diamond nanoparticles onto the CNT scaffold via electrostatic grafting or inkjet printing. The ninth step grows a conformal layer of boron-doped diamond over the entire structure using microwave plasma CVD, encapsulating the CNTs and forming the final textured electrode surface.

The tenth step removes excess material outside the electrode areas via oxygen plasma etching, and the eleventh step completes the metallization and passivation. Alternative deposition methods include electrospray of diamond nanoparticles, radiofrequency CVD, or hot filament CVD, depending on desired film properties.

In particular embodiments of the manufacturing method, carbon nanotubes are grown vertically on nickel droplets formed by dewetting a thin nickel film. Diamond nanoparticles are then deposited via inkjet-type printing or electrospray, followed by CVD growth to form a continuous doped diamond layer. The resulting structure features a high-aspect-ratio, porous architecture that maximizes surface area while maintaining electrical continuity.

The second material may also serve as the adhesion material for the tubes, ensuring mechanical integrity. The microelectrode arrangement can be illustrated as a grid of 60 electrodes in a 4×15 layout, adapted for spinal cord or cortical recordings. The tube structure—whether solid or hollow—provides both enhanced electrochemical performance and topographical cues that may support neuronal adhesion and neurite extension.

Applications of MEA networks include high-resolution mapping of neural circuits, closed-loop neuromodulation, and next-generation neural prostheses. Flexible implants incorporating these microelectrodes enable chronic interfacing with dynamic neural tissues such as the cortex or peripheral nerves.

A covering matrix, such as a biocompatible polymer, may encapsulate the entire array to protect against mechanical stress and biofouling while allowing ionic conduction at the electrode sites.

The outlined method for manufacturing the microelectrode begins with the deposition of a first material—fused silica—as an electrically insulating substrate. The second step deposits a second material—titanium nitride or non-doped diamond—as an adhesion and conductive layer. The third step introduces a third material—boron-doped polycrystalline diamond—structured into vertical tubes. Subsequent steps define the electrode geometry, establish electrical contacts, and apply passivation.

Diamond synthesis methods include microwave plasma CVD, which offers high purity and controlled doping, as well as alternatives like radiofrequency or hot filament CVD for scalability. An alternative embodiment deposits a structured diamond layer directly on supports without CNT templates, using etched molds or self-assembly techniques.

The structured material serves multiple functions: increasing surface area, enhancing charge injection capacity, reducing impedance, and providing nanotopographical cues for neural integration. The electric current outlet is formed by a metal layer, such as Cr/Au, patterned to contact the base of the diamond tubes. The fourth layer thus comprises this metal contact, while the passivation layer—typically SU-8—insulates all non-active surfaces.

The method for producing the current outlet and passivation layer involves lift-off lithography and selective etching to ensure reliable electrical access without short circuits. The advantages of the invention include low impedance (~50 kΩ at 1 kHz for 20-μm electrodes), high charge storage capacity (~10 mC/cm²), mechanical robustness, reusability, and compatibility with standard microfabrication processes.

The method for manufacturing MEA multi-electrode networks follows a similar sequence: deposit diamond particles on patterned sites, grow CNTs via nickel-catalyzed PECVD, coat with nanodiamond, and overgrow with BDD. Protection of diamond structures during etching is achieved using aluminum masks, followed by removal and metallization. Metal contact outlets and passivation layers complete the array.

In a second embodiment, a TiN layer is deposited first, followed by nickel, CNT growth, diamond nanoparticle deposition, and BDD overgrowth. Third and fourth embodiments may vary the sequence of deposition, use different catalysts, or employ alternative nanostructuring strategies such as porous anodization or block copolymer templating, all aimed at achieving high-performance, durable neural interfaces based on structured diamond microelectrodes.
