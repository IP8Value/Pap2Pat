# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNICAL FIELD

The present invention pertains to the field of laser plasma physics, particularly to methods and devices for generating ultra-short, ultra-intense mid-infrared (mid-IR) laser pulses through controlled interactions between high-power laser pulses and underdense plasmas. More specifically, the invention relates to a novel plasma optical modulator that exploits relativistic laser-plasma wakefield dynamics to achieve efficient frequency downshifting of an input signal pulse into the mid-IR spectral range, yielding near-single-cycle pulses with relativistic intensities, multi-millijoule energy levels, and controllable carrier-envelope phases. The invention further encompasses compact, high-repetition-rate systems capable of producing such pulses without reliance on traditional nonlinear crystalline optics, thereby overcoming limitations associated with material damage thresholds and low conversion efficiencies.

## BACKGROUND ART

Mid-infrared band laser pulses, typically defined as electromagnetic radiation in the wavelength range of approximately 3 to 20 micrometers, have emerged as critical tools in advanced scientific and technological applications. These include ultrafast molecular spectroscopy, high-harmonic generation, attosecond pulse production, coherent control of quantum systems, and high-resolution imaging of chemical and biological processes. The utility of mid-IR pulses is significantly enhanced when they possess ultra-short durations—approaching or reaching the single optical cycle limit—and ultra-high peak intensities sufficient to drive relativistic electron dynamics in matter. Such pulses enable access to strong-field phenomena previously restricted to shorter wavelengths but with the added benefits of longer carrier wavelengths, which reduce ionization rates while increasing quiver amplitudes of electrons, thereby facilitating more efficient particle acceleration and radiation emission.

Despite their promise, the generation of ultra-short and ultra-intense mid-IR pulses has been hindered by fundamental limitations of conventional nonlinear optical technologies. Traditional approaches rely on parametric amplification or difference-frequency generation in nonlinear crystals such as lithium niobate, gallium selenide, or orientation-patterned gallium arsenide. However, these materials suffer from intrinsic constraints including narrow transparency windows, low damage thresholds, phase-matching difficulties at long wavelengths, and limited scalability to high pulse energies. Consequently, current crystal-based systems are generally restricted to non-relativistic intensities and struggle to produce multi-millijoule-level mid-IR pulses with durations below two optical cycles.

Relativistic intense laser pulses, first made possible by the advent of chirped pulse amplification (CPA), have revolutionized high-field physics by enabling light intensities exceeding 10¹⁸ W/cm². CPA circumvents the problem of optical damage during amplification by temporally stretching a femtosecond seed pulse, amplifying it at lower peak power, and then recompressing it to its original duration. This technique has enabled the routine generation of near-infrared (near-IR) pulses with terawatt-to-petawatt peak powers. However, extending CPA directly to the mid-IR remains challenging due to the lack of suitable broadband gain media and compressor materials.

In response, researchers have turned to laser plasma optics—a paradigm wherein the plasma itself serves as the active optical medium. Unlike solid-state materials, plasmas are inherently damage-resistant because they are already ionized and can sustain electric fields orders of magnitude higher than dielectric breakdown limits. Concepts such as plasma mirrors, gratings, undulators, and polarizers have demonstrated the feasibility of manipulating high-intensity laser beams using plasma structures. Notably, several studies have explored the self-modulation of relativistic laser pulses in plasmas to generate mid-IR radiation via mechanisms like stimulated Raman scattering or wakefield-induced frequency shifts. However, these methods typically require joule-class, 100-terawatt laser systems operating at low repetition rates (a few hertz), yield broad and unstructured spectra, and exhibit energy conversion efficiencies below a few percent, limiting their practical utility.

Recent advances in compact, high-repetition-rate (kHz-class) terawatt laser systems offer a promising alternative platform. Yet, existing plasma-based mid-IR generation schemes have not been optimized for compatibility with such systems. There remains a pressing need for an efficient, scalable, and robust method to produce near-single-cycle, multi-millijoule mid-IR pulses at relativistic intensities using accessible laser infrastructure. The current state-of-the-art in near-single-cycle mid-IR pulse energy stands below 1 mJ for crystal-based systems and marginally higher for plasma-based approaches, but none combine high energy, few-cycle duration, relativistic intensity, and high repetition rate in a single device. The present invention addresses this gap by introducing a novel plasma optical modulator that leverages precisely timed co-propagation of drive and signal pulses in a tailored plasma channel to achieve unprecedented performance metrics.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a laser plasma optical device for the efficient generation of near-single-cycle mid-infrared laser pulses. The device comprises a vacuum target chamber containing a plasma channel target, a high-pressure gas nozzle or capillary channel device for generating the plasma, a laser system configured to emit both a driving light pulse and a signal light pulse, a focusing element, a time delay control mechanism, and a control system for coordinating operational parameters.

The interaction of laser and matter occurs within an underdense plasma, where the driving light pulse—typically in the near-infrared range—propagates and excites a highly nonlinear plasma wake. This wake manifests as a series of moving plasma bubbles, each characterized by a sharp electron density gradient at its front. The plasma channel target is specifically engineered to support stable propagation of these structures over millimeter-scale distances.

The driving light pulse is focused into the plasma channel using a focusing element such as an off-axis parabolic mirror, initiating the formation of a density-modulated plasma wake. Subsequently, the signal light pulse—initially at the same wavelength as the driver—is injected with a precise time delay so that it loads into the density up-ramp region at the front of the second plasma bubble. Within this region, the signal pulse undergoes continuous frequency red-shifting due to the spatial variation in plasma refractive index, effectively converting its photons to longer wavelengths in the mid-IR range.

As the red-shifted signal propagates with the bubble, its group velocity decreases relative to the bubble’s phase velocity, causing the mid-IR photons to slip backward and become trapped near the bubble center. Once trapped, the pulse stabilizes spectrally and temporally, emerging as a coherent, near-single-cycle mid-IR output with high energy retention.

In a preferred embodiment, the plasma channel target is formed by a parabolic-like radial electron density profile generated via hydrodynamic expansion of a high-pressure gas jet or discharge in a capillary. The driving light pulse has a duration of 5–15 femtoseconds (FWHM), peak power of 3–10 terawatts, and pulse energy of 30–100 millijoules at a central wavelength of approximately 1 micrometer. The signal light pulse has similar wavelength but lower energy (5–20 mJ), peak power of 1–3 TW, and duration of 4–10 fs. The gas target generating device employs noble gases such as helium or hydrogen at backing pressures of 10–100 bar, producing plasma densities in the range of 10¹⁸–10¹⁹ cm⁻³ (corresponding to 0.1%–1% of critical density at 1 μm).

The control system synchronizes the timing, polarization, focus, and energy of both pulses, adjusts gas flow and pressure, monitors plasma conditions, and optimizes the delay between driver and signal to maximize conversion efficiency and spectral quality. This integrated approach enables reliable, high-repetition-rate operation compatible with commercial kHz-class laser systems.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

The laser plasma optical device of the present invention represents a significant advancement over traditional optical technologies by utilizing plasma as a resilient, high-damage-threshold medium for nonlinear frequency conversion. Unlike crystal-based systems constrained by material properties, the plasma-based approach exploits collective electron dynamics to achieve efficient, controllable, and scalable mid-IR pulse generation.

The method for generating near-single-cycle mid-infrared pulses involves the co-propagation of two synchronized laser pulses—an intense driver and a weaker signal—through a preformed plasma channel. The implementation begins with the creation of a tailored underdense plasma using either a high-pressure gas nozzle or a capillary discharge device housed within a vacuum target chamber maintained at pressures below 10⁻³ mbar to minimize background gas interference.

Traditional technical methods relying on optical parametric amplification or difference-frequency mixing are fundamentally limited by crystal absorption, thermal loading, and phase-matching bandwidths, preventing simultaneous achievement of high energy, short duration, and long wavelength. Other laser plasma methods, such as self-modulation or Raman scattering, often produce chaotic spectra and require massive laser facilities. In contrast, the present invention offers deterministic control over spectral shape, central wavelength, and temporal structure through precise manipulation of plasma wake geometry and laser injection timing.

Key advantages include energy conversion efficiencies exceeding 30%, output pulse durations of 1–2 optical cycles at 4–5 μm wavelength, relativistic normalized vector potentials (a₀ > 1), and compatibility with compact, high-repetition-rate laser systems. The control system integrates real-time diagnostics, feedback loops, and programmable delay stages to maintain optimal performance across varying operating conditions.

The laser system emits linearly polarized pulses at a fundamental wavelength near 1 μm, split into driver and signal arms using a dielectric beam splitter. A laser pre-pulse may be employed to pre-ionize the gas and form a smooth plasma channel prior to main pulse arrival, though this is optional in embodiments using capillary-guided discharges.

The driving light pulse, with a beam waist radius of 6–10 μm at focus, has a pulse width of 8–12 fs (FWHM), corresponding to ~3–4 optical cycles. Its peak power ranges from 4 to 7 TW, delivering 40–80 mJ of energy. The signal light pulse shares the same spatial mode but is delayed by 15–22 optical cycles (15–22 fs at 1 μm) and carries 8–15 mJ of energy with 1–2 TW peak power and 4–8 fs duration.

Gas target generation utilizes a supersonic gas jet or a ceramic/metal capillary (diameter 100–500 μm, length 1–5 mm) filled with helium, hydrogen, or mixtures thereof. Upon laser or electrical discharge ignition, a plasma channel forms with a parabolic-like radial density profile: nₑ(r) = n₀ + (λ₀² / π²w₀⁴)r²n_c, where n₀ is the background density (2–5 × 10⁻³ n_c), w₀ is the laser spot size, and n_c is the critical density. This profile guides both pulses over distances exceeding 1.5 mm, far beyond the Rayleigh length.

The plasma channel target is adjustable via gas pressure, capillary dimensions, or discharge current, allowing tuning of electron density from 1–6 × 10¹⁸ cm⁻³. The resulting plasma supports bubble formation with lengths scaling as λ_b ∝ √(a_d)/k_p, where a_d is the driver’s normalized amplitude and k_p the plasma wavenumber. Optimal operation occurs when the bubble size matches the desired mid-IR wavelength.

The method proceeds in three steps: (a) the driver pulse is focused into the plasma channel, exciting a nonlinear wake with multiple bubbles; (b) the signal pulse is injected with precise delay to load into the density up-ramp of the second bubble; and (c) the signal undergoes continuous red-shifting until trapped as a stable mid-IR pulse.

The generated mid-IR pulse exhibits ultra-high light intensity (>10¹⁸ W/cm²), ultra-short cycle number (1–2 cycles at 4–5 μm), ultra-high pulse energy (3–15 mJ), long carrier wavelength (3–6 μm), and controllable carrier-envelope phase (CEP shift predictable and independent of input CEP). The device thus enables new regimes of light-matter interaction.

### Example 1

In a specific embodiment, the device includes a vacuum chamber housing a 2-mm-long helium plasma channel at 3.5 × 10¹⁸ cm⁻³ density, formed via a 50-bar gas nozzle. A Ti:sapphire CPA laser delivers 55 mJ, 10-fs driver pulses (5.5 TW) and 13.7 mJ, 10-fs signal pulses (1.37 TW) at 800 nm, split and delayed by 18 optical cycles using a motorized stage. Both pulses are focused to 8-μm waists using an f/10 off-axis parabola. Simulations show output of a 4.2-μm, 2-cycle pulse with 30% energy conversion and a₀ ≈ 1.3. Advantages include kHz compatibility, no solid optics in beam path, and inherent phase stability. Potential applications span attosecond science, laser-driven particle acceleration, and nonlinear mid-IR spectroscopy.
