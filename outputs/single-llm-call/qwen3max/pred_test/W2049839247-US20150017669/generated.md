# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

Glycan analysis represents a critical frontier in the field of molecular diagnostics, particularly for complex diseases such as cancer. Glycans, or carbohydrate chains covalently attached to proteins and lipids, are among the most abundant and structurally diverse posttranslational modifications in biological systems. Their biosynthesis is not template-driven but rather orchestrated by the dynamic expression and activity of glycosyltransferases, glycosidases, and other enzymes within the endoplasmic reticulum and Golgi apparatus. This non-genetic regulation renders the glycome highly responsive to physiological and pathological states, including inflammation, infection, and malignancy. In human serum, glycans are primarily presented as N-linked oligosaccharides on secreted and circulating glycoproteins, such as immunoglobulins, acute-phase proteins, and established tumor markers like CA-125 and CEA. Consequently, alterations in serum glycan profiles have been consistently observed in patients with various cancers, reflecting underlying changes in cellular metabolism, immune surveillance, and tumor microenvironment dynamics.

The association between serum glycan changes and tumor development has been well documented across multiple cancer types, including ovarian, breast, prostate, and gastric carcinomas. These alterations often manifest as increased branching, fucosylation, sialylation, or truncation of glycan structures, which can be detected through advanced analytical techniques. Such disease-associated glycoforms offer a rich source of potential biomarkers that may surpass traditional protein-based markers in sensitivity and specificity, especially for early-stage detection. Given that many approved cancer biomarkers are themselves glycoproteins, it follows that direct analysis of their glycan moieties—rather than total protein concentration—may yield more nuanced and clinically relevant information. This rationale has motivated extensive research into glycomics as a platform for non-invasive disease diagnosis and monitoring.

Despite the promise of glycan-based diagnostics, current cancer screening methods remain limited in scope and performance. For instance, CA-125, the most widely used serum marker for ovarian cancer, exhibits poor sensitivity in early-stage disease, with elevation occurring in only about 50% of stage I cases. Moreover, CA-125 levels can be elevated in benign gynecological conditions, leading to false positives and unnecessary interventions. These limitations underscore the urgent need for more robust, multi-analyte approaches that capture the complexity of cancer biology. Glycan profiling, by virtue of its ability to interrogate hundreds of molecular features simultaneously, aligns well with this objective. However, the clinical translation of glycomics has been hindered by technical challenges related to reproducibility, sensitivity, and standardization.

Matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight (MALDI-TOF) mass spectrometry has emerged as a powerful tool for high-throughput glycan analysis due to its speed, sensitivity, and compatibility with complex biological samples. Unlike MALDI-Fourier transform ion cyclotron resonance (MALDI-FTICR) mass spectrometry, which offers ultra-high resolution but at the cost of throughput, instrument complexity, and operational expense, MALDI-TOF provides a practical balance of performance and scalability for clinical applications. Nevertheless, the reproducibility of MALDI-TOF data—particularly in quantitative terms—has historically been a major concern. Variability in sample preparation, matrix crystallization, and ion suppression effects can lead to significant fluctuations in peak intensities, undermining the reliability of biomarker discovery and validation.

A critical factor influencing reproducibility lies in the pre-analytical steps of glycan extraction and loading onto the MALDI target plate. Traditional methods involving manual pipetting, inconsistent drying, and heterogeneous matrix co-crystallization introduce substantial inter- and intra-sample variability. The coefficient of variation (CV) for peak intensities in conventional workflows often exceeds 20%, which is unacceptable for diagnostic applications requiring high precision. Therefore, there exists a compelling need for standardized, reproducible processes that minimize technical noise and maximize the fidelity of glycan signal detection. To address this gap, the present invention introduces novel, integrated methodologies for glycan extraction from human serum and controlled loading onto specialized MALDI target plates, collectively designed to achieve CV values below 15% and enable robust, high-throughput glycan profiling suitable for clinical cancer screening.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides an analytical method for the reproducible and quantitative profiling of glycans from human serum using MALDI-TOF mass spectrometry, with specific application to the diagnosis and management of cancer, particularly ovarian cancer. Central to this method is the minimization of the coefficient of variation (CV) in peak intensity measurements, which is achieved through a series of optimized and standardized sample preparation steps. By maintaining CV values below 15% across replicate analyses, the invention ensures high analytical reproducibility, a prerequisite for reliable biomarker discovery and clinical implementation.

The method begins with a sample preparation step in which a human serum sample is introduced into a specialized micro-reactor device, referred to herein as Plate #1. This plate is engineered to facilitate controlled thermal cycling and enzymatic reactions. In a preferred embodiment, the serum sample is denatured by subjecting it to alternating cycles of brief heating in boiling water and cooling at room temperature, typically four cycles of 15 seconds each, to unfold glycoproteins and expose glycosylation sites without causing irreversible aggregation. Following denaturation, the sample is treated with a glycan-releasing enzyme, preferably peptide-N-glycosidase F (PNGase F), which cleaves N-linked glycans from asparagine residues under mild conditions. In a key innovation, this enzymatic reaction is accelerated and enhanced using microwave irradiation at a controlled power output (e.g., 400 W) and temperature (e.g., 37°C) for a defined duration (e.g., 10 minutes), significantly improving reaction efficiency and consistency compared to conventional incubation.

After glycan release, the mixture undergoes protein precipitation by addition of ice-cold ethanol and incubation at −80°C, followed by centrifugation to remove denatured proteins. The supernatant containing free glycans is then subjected to solid-phase extraction (SPE) using graphitized carbon cartridges, which selectively retain glycans while excluding salts, peptides, and other contaminants. Glycans are eluted in sequential fractions using increasing concentrations of acetonitrile in water (e.g., 10%, 20%, and 40% ACN/H₂O, each with 0.05% trifluoroacetic acid), enabling fractionation based on hydrophobicity and improving detection of low-abundance species.

The extracted glycans are then loaded onto a second specialized plate, referred to as Plate #2, which serves as the MALDI sample-loading plate. Plate #2 is preferably a pre-spotted micro-focus self-condensing plate, wherein the MALDI matrix—most preferably 2,5-dihydroxybenzoic acid (DHB)—is pre-coated in discrete, uniform spots during manufacturing. This pre-coating eliminates variability associated with manual matrix application and ensures consistent co-crystallization. In a preferred embodiment, the matrix is applied using a controlled spraying process onto a heated plate, followed by drying under reduced pressure to form homogeneous, fine crystals ideal for ionization. The glycan solution is reconstituted in purified water and mixed with a small volume of a solution containing acetonitrile and sodium chloride (e.g., 70% ACN/12 mM NaCl) to promote cationization and improve spectral quality. This mixture is then precisely deposited onto the pre-spotted matrix areas of Plate #2 and rapidly dried under vacuum, ensuring uniform spot morphology and minimizing analyte delocalization.

The loaded Plate #2 is subsequently analyzed by MALDI-TOF mass spectrometry in positive-ion reflectron mode, typically scanning m/z from 1000 to 3000. Multiple laser shots are acquired per spot, and data are collected from multiple positions to generate averaged spectra with high signal-to-noise ratios. The resulting mass spectra are processed to extract peak intensities, which are normalized to total ion current to yield normalized absolute peak intensities (NAPI). These quantitative profiles are then used to identify cancer-associated glycan biomarkers and construct multivariate diagnostic models.

Preferred embodiments of the invention include the use of Plate #1 as a micro-reactor with precise thermal control, Plate #2 as a pre-coated MALDI target with micro-focus geometry, PNGase F as the glycan-releasing enzyme, DHB as the matrix, and microwave-assisted enzymatic digestion. The method further encompasses the selection of specific glycan peaks—both annotated and unannotated—as biomarkers based on statistical criteria such as P-value and area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), enabling highly sensitive and specific cancer screening.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE PREFERRED EMBODIMENTS

MALDI-TOF mass spectrometry is a cornerstone of the present invention, providing rapid, sensitive, and reproducible analysis of glycan profiles from human serum. The method is implemented through a series of meticulously controlled steps designed to maximize analytical consistency and biological relevance. Initially, serum samples are injected into Plate #1, a micro-reactor configured to support precise thermal and enzymatic manipulations. Step (a) involves the introduction of 50 μL of serum into the plate, followed by step (b), glycan extraction. This extraction comprises three substeps: (b-1) denaturing serum proteins via four cycles of 15-second boiling and 15-second room-temperature cooling; (b-2) treating the denatured sample with 1000 units of PNGase F; and (b-3) separating released glycans from proteins by ethanol precipitation and centrifugation. Critically, the enzymatic reaction in step (b-2) is conducted under microwave irradiation using a dedicated reaction-enhancing system, which maintains a constant temperature of 37°C via a water bath equipped with a cooling device to dissipate excess heat, thereby preventing enzyme denaturation while accelerating kinetics.

Following protein removal, the glycan-containing supernatant is dried and reconstituted for SPE. The purified glycans are then prepared for MALDI analysis in step (c) by reconstituting in 15 μL of purified water. In step (d), 1 μL of this glycan solution is mixed with 1 μL of 70% acetonitrile/12 mM NaCl directly onto a spot of Plate #2, which is a pre-spotted MALDI target plate featuring a micro-focus self-condensing surface. The matrix, DHB, is pre-coated during manufacturing using a spraying process onto a heated plate, followed by drying under reduced pressure to ensure uniform crystal formation. This pre-coated design eliminates operator-dependent variability in matrix application. Step (e) involves rapid drying of the loaded sample under vacuum, which promotes homogeneous co-crystallization and minimizes spot heterogeneity. Finally, in step (f), the plate is inserted into a MALDI-TOF instrument (e.g., AB SCIEX 4800 Plus), and mass spectra are acquired in reflectron mode with 1000 total laser shots per spot distributed across 40 positions.

Plate #1 functions as a micro-reactor enabling controlled denaturation and enzymatic release, while Plate #2 serves as a standardized sample-loading platform. The surface of Plate #2 is engineered to enhance analyte focusing during drying, and its pre-spotted nature ensures batch-to-batch consistency. The use of a pre-coated matrix plate is particularly advantageous, as it removes a major source of irreproducibility in traditional MALDI workflows. The entire process—from serum to spectrum—is optimized for high-throughput analysis, with minimal manual intervention.

This analytical method is applied to cancer screening by comparing glycan profiles from patient and control cohorts. In the context of ovarian cancer, serum samples are divided into training and test sets. Mass spectra from the training set are analyzed to identify tumor-specific glycan peaks exhibiting statistically significant intensity differences (P < 10⁻¹²) and high classification power (AUC > 0.72–0.75). Cut-off values for each biomarker peak are established based on ROC curve analysis at predefined sensitivities (e.g., 70%). A scoring system is then developed wherein each peak contributes +1 or −1 based on whether its intensity exceeds the cut-off, weighted by its AUC. The total score determines classification as cancer or non-cancer.

The method is also effective in differentiating benign from malignant ovarian tumors, as glycan alterations are often tumor-specific. Peak intensity comparisons, detection frequency analysis, and multivariate pattern recognition enable the establishment of robust screening standards. Blind validation tests confirm the method’s predictive power, with sensitivity and specificity exceeding those of CA-125. The invention thus provides a reproducible, high-throughput platform for glycan-based cancer screening, with clear advantages over conventional single-marker approaches.

### Glycan Analysis from Human Serum

Mass spectra of glycans from human serum reveal a complex profile of N-linked oligosaccharides in the m/z 1000–3000 range. Experimental procedures involve serum denaturation, microwave-enhanced PNGase F digestion, protein precipitation, SPE purification, and MALDI-TOF analysis on pre-spotted DHB plates. Peaks are identified by accurate mass (±10 ppm) and annotated using glycan databases such as GlycoMod. Intensities are normalized to total ion current (NAPI), and both glycan and non-glycan peaks are considered if they meet detection frequency (>95%) and reproducibility (<15% CV) criteria. Representative spectra show distinct patterns between healthy controls and ovarian cancer patients, with specific peaks consistently elevated or suppressed in disease.

### Example 2

Mass spectra from ovarian tumor patients exhibit altered intensities at specific m/z values compared to controls. Fifteen biomarker peaks were selected based on P-value and AUC thresholds, including ten annotated N-glycans (e.g., m/z 1565.5, Hex₃HexNAc₄; m/z 1909.7, Hex₄HexNAc₄Fuc₁) and five unassigned peaks. Molecular structures and exact masses were determined using GlycoMod, with mass errors <5 ppm. While some biomarkers correspond to known cancer-associated glycoforms (e.g., increased fucosylation), others remain structurally uncharacterized but demonstrate high diagnostic utility. Limitations include the inability to assign all peaks to known glycans and potential interference from non-glycan molecules, though their consistent detection and reproducibility support their inclusion as biomarkers.

### Example 3

Traditional glycan analysis relies on manual sample preparation, variable matrix application, and conventional enzymatic digestion, resulting in CV values often exceeding 20%. In contrast, the present invention employs microwave-assisted digestion, pre-spotted MALDI plates, and standardized loading protocols, achieving CV <15%. Reproducibility is demonstrated by quadruplicate analyses showing consistent peak intensities across runs. Advantages include higher throughput, improved quantitation, reduced operator bias, and enhanced detection of low-abundance glycans due to fractionated SPE. These improvements collectively enable more reliable biomarker discovery and clinical deployment.

### Example 4

Validation of reproducibility was conducted across a cohort of 137 subjects (63 controls, 77 ovarian cancer patients) with age ranging from 20 to 89 years. Mean relative standard deviation (RSD) values for peak intensities across quadruplicate MALDI spots were consistently below 15%, with an overall average of 7.28% in representative analyses. Age distribution was balanced between groups in the training set (mean age ~41 years) to minimize confounding. The low RSD confirms the robustness of the sample preparation and MALDI workflow, supporting its suitability for large-scale clinical studies.

### Example 5

Glycan profiling analysis involved dividing patients into training (n=80) and validation (n=60) sets. Cut-off values for 15 marker peaks were determined from ROC curves in the training set. Cross-validation showed 80–90% sensitivity and 70–83% specificity in the training set, outperforming CA-125 (74% sensitivity). In the blind test set, the glycan panel achieved 81–84% sensitivity and 83% specificity, again surpassing CA-125 (78% sensitivity). The method enables new quantitative analytical workflows from serum to extracted glycans, with MALDI-TOF providing high-fidelity spectra. Pattern analysis of peak intensities, combined with weighted scoring, establishes robust screening standards. Commercially available screening tests could be developed based on this platform, with modifications possible for other cancers or diseases. The invention thus provides a superior, reproducible method for ovarian tumor-specific glycan analysis with broad clinical applicability.
