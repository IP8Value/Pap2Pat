# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNICAL FIELD

The present invention relates generally to the field of electrospinning of microscale or nanoscale fibers, and more particularly to methods and apparatus for the high-throughput production of core-sheath fibers using a needleless, slit-based electrospinning platform. Core-sheath fibers are bicomponent structures in which a first material (the “core”) is fully or partially encapsulated by a second material (the “sheath”), enabling unique combinations of physical, chemical, and biological properties unattainable with monocomponent fibers. Conventional manufacturing methods for producing such fibers—particularly coaxial needle electrospinning—suffer from significant limitations including low throughput, frequent clogging, complex maintenance requirements, and scalability constraints that hinder commercial viability. Electrospinning has emerged as a promising alternative method for fabricating continuous fibers with diameters ranging from tens of nanometers to several micrometers, leveraging high-voltage electric fields to draw charged polymer jets from a liquid source toward a grounded collector. Among electrospinning techniques, coaxial electrospinning enables the formation of core-sheath architectures by simultaneously delivering two immiscible or partially miscible polymer solutions through concentrically arranged needles. While effective at laboratory scale, coaxial electrospinning remains impractical for industrial-scale production due to its inherently low volumetric output—typically less than 0.1 grams of polymer per hour—and susceptibility to nozzle blockage. To address these shortcomings, researchers have explored multi-nozzle arrays and free-surface electrospinning approaches such as the Nanospider™ technology, which utilizes a rotating roller partially immersed in a polymer bath to generate multiple Taylor cones along its surface. However, existing adaptations of these platforms for core-sheath fiber production either require strict immiscibility between core and sheath phases or fail to achieve consistent fiber morphology at high flow rates. The present invention introduces an improved approach referred to herein as Arsenal Electrospinning Technology, which employs a novel slit-fixture design to enable robust, high-throughput fabrication of core-sheath fibers without reliance on traditional needles. This apparatus comprises aligned slits configured to co-localize core and sheath polymer solutions along a linear interface, thereby facilitating the spontaneous formation of multiple core-sheath Taylor cones when subjected to a sufficiently strong electric field. The resulting electrospinning jets exhibit stable core-sheath morphology under optimized fluid dynamic conditions, enabling scalable production of advanced fibrous materials for applications in drug delivery, tissue engineering, filtration, energy storage, photonics, and smart textiles. Despite these advances, there remains a need for further improvements in process control, material compatibility, and system efficiency to fully realize the commercial potential of core-sheath electrospun fibers.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a method and apparatus for the high-throughput production of electrospun core-sheath fibers using a needleless, slit-based electrospinning platform that overcomes the limitations of conventional coaxial needle systems. In one aspect, the invention comprises a method for forming electrospun core-sheath fibers by delivering a core polymer solution and a sheath polymer solution to adjacent slits within a common fixture, applying an electrical potential sufficient to initiate multiple electrospinning jets from the co-localized fluid interface, and collecting the resulting core-sheath fibers on a grounded collector. A key feature of the invention lies in the optimization of shear stresses at the boundary between the core and sheath fluids during jet initiation, which promotes stable encapsulation of the core phase within the sheath phase. The apparatus includes a first vessel containing the core polymer solution and a second vessel containing the sheath polymer solution, each independently connected to a corresponding slit channel in the fixture. Both solutions are flowed into their respective slits under controlled pressure or pump-driven conditions, ensuring precise regulation of flow rates and interfacial dynamics. Upon application of a high-voltage direct current (typically between 70–90 kV), multiple Taylor cones form along the length of the slit interface, each evolving into a core-sheath electrospinning jet that deposits composite fibers onto a downstream collector. By adjusting parameters such as core and sheath flow rates, slit geometries, and solution viscosities, the shear stress at the fluid-fluid interface can be tuned to achieve a desired core-to-sheath ratio and fiber morphology. In various embodiments, the invention enables the fabrication of bicomponent fibers with distinct functional domains, hollow fibers via post-electrospinning core removal, fibers incorporating encapsulated particles or bioactive agents, and fibers composed of materials that are otherwise unelectrospinnable in isolation. Additional embodiments include fixtures with variable slit widths and heights, modular designs for rapid reconfiguration, and integration with automated collection systems for continuous manufacturing. The invention thus represents a significant advancement in electrospinning technology by combining high throughput, operational stability, and broad material versatility in a single platform.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention is directed to a needleless electrospinning process that enables the reliable and scalable production of core-sheath micro- and nanofibers. Unlike conventional coaxial needle systems, which rely on concentric capillaries to deliver core and sheath solutions, the disclosed method utilizes a planar slit fixture wherein core and sheath polymer solutions are delivered through adjacent, vertically aligned slits. This configuration ensures physical co-localization of the two fluids along a linear interface, promoting the spontaneous formation of multiple core-sheath Taylor cones when an electric field is applied. The formation of these Taylor cones is governed by the balance of electrostatic, capillary, and viscous forces acting at the fluid-air and fluid-fluid interfaces. Control over Taylor cone generation is achieved by modulating key process variables, including solution flow rates, slit geometries, and solution viscosities, which collectively determine the velocity profiles and shear stresses at the core-sheath boundary. Flow velocities at the slit exits are derived from the volumetric flow rates divided by the cross-sectional areas of the respective slits. For instance, the sheath flow velocity (v<sub>sheath</sub>) is approximated as (Q<sub>sheath</sub> + Q<sub>core</sub>) / A<sub>sheath</sub>, while the core flow velocity (v<sub>core</sub>) is given by Q<sub>core</sub> / A<sub>core</sub>, where Q denotes flow rate and A denotes slit exit area. Experimental data demonstrate that distinct core-sheath cone-jets form reliably only when v<sub>sheath</sub> exceeds v<sub>core</sub>, indicating that sufficient shear stress must be generated by the faster-moving sheath fluid to entrain the core fluid into the emerging jet—a phenomenon analogous to selective withdrawal in stratified fluid systems.

### Core Flow Rate

In experiments conducted using a fixed sheath flow rate of 200 mL/h, varying the core flow rate while maintaining constant slit dimensions revealed a critical threshold for stable core-sheath jet formation. At core flow rates of 20 and 40 mL/h, clear core-sheath cone-jets were observed, with the core stream visibly encapsulated within the sheath phase. However, increasing the core flow rate to 60 mL/h resulted in loss of core-sheath distinction, likely due to insufficient shear stress from the sheath fluid to fully envelop the higher-volume core stream. This confirms that core flow rate must be carefully balanced relative to sheath flow rate to maintain structural integrity of the bicomponent fiber.

### Slit Fixture Geometry Core Slit Width

When the core slit width was varied while keeping the sheath slit width constant, changes in core flow velocity directly influenced Taylor cone morphology. Narrower core slits increased v<sub>core</sub> for a given flow rate, reducing the v<sub>sheath</sub>/v<sub>core</sub> ratio and potentially destabilizing core encapsulation. Conversely, wider core slits decreased v<sub>core</sub>, enhancing the relative dominance of sheath flow and improving core-sheath definition—provided that total flow rates remained within the operational window of the electrospinning system. Optimal core slit widths were found to depend on both absolute flow rates and the viscosity contrast between core and sheath solutions.

### Sheath Slit Width

Similarly, varying the sheath slit width while holding core slit width constant demonstrated that narrower sheath slits increased v<sub>sheath</sub>, thereby enhancing shear-driven entrainment of the core fluid. A sheath slit width of 1.5 mm produced well-defined core-sheath jets under standard flow conditions, whereas a 3.0 mm slit failed to do so unless the core flow rate was reduced to compensate for the lower v<sub>sheath</sub>. These results underscore the importance of matching slit geometry to flow parameters to achieve target fiber architectures.

### Sheath Slit Width and Core Slit Width

Simultaneous variation of both sheath and core slit widths allowed fine-tuning of the v<sub>sheath</sub>/v<sub>core</sub> ratio independent of absolute flow rates. Systems designed with proportionally scaled slits maintained consistent velocity ratios across different fixture sizes, enabling predictable scaling from laboratory prototypes to industrial-scale units. Fiber quality, as assessed by uniformity of core placement and absence of phase separation, correlated strongly with the maintenance of v<sub>sheath</sub> > v<sub>core</sub>.

### Core Slit Height

Adjusting the vertical height of the core slit altered the residence time of the core fluid at the interface prior to jet initiation. Increased slit height enhanced fluid stability but risked premature mixing if contact time exceeded diffusion limits. Optimal core slit heights ranged from 0.5 to 2.0 mm, balancing interfacial coherence with efficient jet formation.

### Viscosities of the Solutions

Solution viscosity played a decisive role in core-sheath jet stability. When the sheath solution exhibited higher viscosity than the core (e.g., 760 cP vs. 500 cP), distinct core-sheath morphology was consistently achieved even at moderate flow rates. In contrast, lower-viscosity sheath solutions (280 cP) failed to encapsulate the higher-viscosity core, leading to irregular or mixed-phase jets. Thus, viscosity mismatch must be considered alongside flow velocity in process design.

### Core Sheath Fiber Applications

The versatility of the disclosed method enables diverse applications. Bicomponent fibers may combine biodegradable and non-biodegradable polymers (e.g., PLGA and Nylon) for tailored degradation profiles. Particles such as dexamethasone, proteins, fluorophores, or metal nanoparticles can be encapsulated within the core for controlled release. Unelectrospinnable materials like PDMS are rendered spinnable by coaxial sheathing, followed by optional sheath removal to yield pure cured silicone fibers. Hollow fibers are produced by dissolving a water-soluble core (e.g., PEO) post-spinning. Reactive cores and sheaths can be combined to trigger in-fiber chemistry. Cost savings arise from using expensive actives only in the core while employing inexpensive sheaths for structural support. Higher-order structures, including aligned mats or 3D scaffolds, are achievable through collector engineering.

### Advantages of the Invention as it Relates to High Throughput Open-Bath Monofiber Fabrication Systems

The invention avoids solvent evaporation issues common in open-bath systems by enabling rapid jet formation. It supports electrospinning of polymer suspensions and colloidal dispersions without clogging. Material waste is minimized due to efficient solution consumption and lack of dead volumes. Electrospinning can occur in any spatial orientation, though downward collection is preferred for gravity-assisted fiber alignment. The system produces fewer, longer continuous fibers compared to multi-needle arrays, enhancing mechanical integrity. Fiber diameter is controllable via voltage, distance, and flow parameters. Sterility is maintainable in closed-loop configurations suitable for biomedical manufacturing.
