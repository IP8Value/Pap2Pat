# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

Proprioception refers to the body’s intrinsic ability to sense the position, movement, and orientation of its limbs and joints in space without reliance on external visual or auditory cues. This somatosensory modality is mediated primarily by mechanoreceptors located in muscles, tendons, and joint capsules, which transduce mechanical deformation into neural signals that are relayed to the central nervous system. Proprioceptive information is essential for the coordination of voluntary movements, maintenance of postural stability, and the formation of a coherent internal representation of body schema. The integrity of proprioceptive function is therefore foundational to both fine motor control and gross motor behaviors, enabling individuals to perform tasks ranging from delicate finger manipulations to complex locomotor activities.

The importance of proprioception extends beyond basic motor execution; it plays a critical role in sensorimotor integration, motor learning, and neuroplastic adaptation. In healthy individuals, proprioceptive feedback continuously refines motor commands through closed-loop control mechanisms, allowing for real-time error correction and adaptive recalibration of movement trajectories. Disruptions in proprioceptive signaling—whether due to peripheral nerve damage, central nervous system lesions, or neurodegenerative processes—can lead to significant impairments in movement accuracy, balance, and functional independence. Clinical conditions such as stroke, Parkinson’s disease, multiple sclerosis, and peripheral neuropathies frequently involve proprioceptive deficits that contribute substantially to disability and reduced quality of life. In stroke survivors, for example, impaired wrist and hand proprioception is strongly correlated with poor upper limb motor recovery and diminished capacity for activities of daily living.

Current clinical methods for assessing proprioception are often limited in sensitivity, objectivity, and quantifiability. Traditional approaches include manual joint position matching tests, where a clinician passively moves a patient’s limb to a target position and asks the patient to replicate it with the contralateral limb or reproduce it actively. While these methods are simple and require no specialized equipment, they suffer from high inter-rater variability, subjective interpretation, and an inability to detect subtle changes in proprioceptive acuity. Moreover, they typically assess only gross positional awareness and fail to capture dynamic aspects of proprioceptive function, such as motion detection thresholds or velocity discrimination. These limitations hinder both accurate diagnosis and the monitoring of therapeutic progress in rehabilitation settings.

In response to these shortcomings, psychophysical threshold methods have emerged as more rigorous and quantitative alternatives. These methods employ controlled, repeatable stimuli and forced-choice paradigms to determine the smallest detectable change in joint position or movement—commonly referred to as the Just-Noticeable Difference (JND). Such approaches yield continuous, metric data that can be statistically analyzed and tracked over time, offering greater precision in evaluating proprioceptive function. Joint matching methods, while still used, have been increasingly supplemented or replaced by robotic platforms capable of delivering precise kinematic stimuli and recording high-resolution kinematic responses.

Haptic technology and robotic devices have revolutionized the field of sensorimotor assessment by enabling the delivery of highly controlled mechanical perturbations while simultaneously measuring human responses with sub-degree angular resolution and millisecond temporal precision. Robotic exoskeletons, in particular, allow for the isolation of specific joint degrees of freedom, elimination of visual confounds, and automation of psychophysical protocols. However, existing robotic systems for wrist proprioception assessment often lack full three-degree-of-freedom (3-DOF) actuation, limiting their ability to evaluate the complete biomechanical repertoire of the human wrist, which includes flexion/extension (FE), adduction/abduction (AA), and forearm supination/pronation (PS). Furthermore, many current devices are designed solely for assessment and do not integrate rehabilitative training functionalities, thereby missing opportunities for closed-loop therapy that combines evaluation with targeted intervention.

This gap underscores a critical unmet need: a comprehensive proprioceptive function assessment system that not only quantifies sensory acuity across all relevant wrist joint axes but also supports active sensorimotor training to drive neuroplastic improvements. Equally important is the need for an integrated system that seamlessly transitions between diagnostic evaluation and therapeutic exercise, enabling personalized, data-driven rehabilitation protocols. Such a system would address the limitations of fragmented approaches by unifying assessment and training within a single, calibrated hardware-software platform, thereby enhancing clinical efficiency and therapeutic efficacy.

## SUMMARY

The present invention introduces a novel wrist joint proprioception system designed to provide precise, objective, and comprehensive assessment of proprioceptive function while simultaneously enabling targeted sensorimotor training. Central to this system is a manipulandum unit configured to interface with a human wrist and support full three-degree-of-freedom movement corresponding to physiological wrist kinematics. The manipulandum unit is operably connected to a controller that executes proprietary software modules for both assessment and rehabilitation, ensuring seamless integration of diagnostic and therapeutic workflows.

The manipulandum unit comprises a base, a handle, and a multi-linkage assembly that collectively enable unconstrained yet mechanically guided motion in flexion/extension, adduction/abduction, and forearm supination/pronation. Each degree of freedom is independently actuated by dedicated motors equipped with high-resolution rotary encoders, allowing for precise control of passive joint positioning during assessment and active resistance or assistance during training. The controller, which may be implemented as a dedicated embedded system or a general-purpose computer with real-time operating capabilities, manages motor operations, records kinematic data, and implements psychophysical algorithms for threshold estimation.

In proprioception assessment operation, the system occludes visual and auditory input and delivers pairs of passive wrist displacements using an adaptive two-alternative forced-choice paradigm. The participant indicates which stimulus felt farther from neutral, and the system dynamically adjusts subsequent stimulus magnitudes using a Bayesian psychometric algorithm (e.g., QUEST or psi-marginal) to converge on the JND threshold. This process is repeated independently for each joint axis, yielding separate measures of position sense acuity in FE, AA, and PS planes.

The position sense routine specifically evaluates static joint angle discrimination by presenting a standard reference position (e.g., 15° flexion) and a comparison position of variable magnitude. The resulting JND serves as a sensitive biomarker of proprioceptive integrity. Additional embodiments of the invention include dynamic motion sense routines that assess velocity or acceleration discrimination, bilateral assessment modes for interlimb comparison, and integration with virtual reality environments to enhance engagement during training.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The manipulandum unit 22 is the core mechanical interface of the wrist proprioception system, engineered to replicate the anatomical and biomechanical constraints of the human wrist while providing precise actuation and measurement. It consists of several key components: a base 42, a handle 44, a linkage assembly 46, and multiple transmission sub-assemblies that translate motor torque into physiologically aligned joint rotations.

The base 42 serves as the structural foundation, rigidly mounted to a stable support surface and housing electronic components such as motor drivers and signal conditioning circuitry. Its ergonomic design accommodates forearm placement via an adjustable splint that immobilizes the distal radius and ulna while permitting free wrist motion. The handle 44 is contoured to fit the human hand and includes grip sensors to monitor contact force and ensure consistent hand posture across trials. It is coupled to the linkage assembly 46 through low-friction bearings that minimize parasitic torque and preserve backdrivability.

The linkage assembly 46 comprises two primary transmission sub-assemblies: the PS (pronation/supination) transmission sub-assembly 70 and the FE (flexion/extension) transmission sub-assembly 72. The PS transmission sub-assembly 70 includes a track 80, which is a curved guide rail conforming to the arc of forearm rotation. Sliding along this track are arms 82 and 84, which connect the handle to a rotary actuator via a parallelogram linkage that maintains alignment during motion. The track 80 features precision-machined surfaces and integrated limit stops to define the operational range of PS motion (typically ±60° from neutral).

The FE transmission sub-assembly 72 incorporates a guide track 120 oriented perpendicular to the PS plane, along which a carriage 122 translates. The carriage 122 supports legs 124 and 126 that link to the handle assembly, converting linear carriage motion into wrist flexion/extension. The guide track 120 is driven by a ball-screw mechanism coupled to a brushless DC motor, ensuring smooth, backlash-free actuation. Each leg features adjustable length settings to accommodate individual anthropometry.

The AA (adduction/abduction) axis is established through a gimbal-like arrangement wherein the entire FE sub-assembly pivots about a vertical axis passing through the anatomical center of the wrist. This AA axis movement is actuated by a separate motor whose output is transmitted via a timing belt to a pivot shaft. Similarly, the FE axis is established by the linear-to-rotary conversion of carriage 122 motion, while the PS axis is defined by the rotational symmetry of the track 80 about the forearm longitudinal axis.

Four motors—M1, M2, M3, and M4—provide independent actuation. Motor M1 drives the PS track 80 via a direct gear coupling, enabling pronation/supination. Motor M2 controls the FE carriage 122 through the guide track 120, governing flexion/extension. Motors M3 and M4 jointly actuate the AA axis via a differential drive mechanism that ensures balanced torque distribution and eliminates cross-coupling artifacts. Each motor is paired with a high-resolution optical encoder (≥20,000 counts/revolution) to achieve angular resolution below 0.01°.

The manipulandum unit 22 thus provides three fully decoupled degrees of freedom with ranges of motion closely matching those of the human wrist: approximately ±60° for PS, ±70° for FE, and ±25° for AA. This biomechanical fidelity ensures ecological validity in both assessment and training tasks. Operation is coordinated by the controller 24, which runs real-time firmware to synchronize motor commands, acquire sensor data at 200 Hz, and implement closed-loop control algorithms such as PID regulators tuned for minimal phase lag and overshoot.

The controller 24 includes volatile and non-volatile memory for storing calibration parameters, subject profiles, and session logs. Computer-readable storage media retain executable code for assessment program 300 and rehabilitation training program 400. The assessment program 300 includes a Position Sense category 302 with Detection Module S.1 (absolute threshold estimation) and Discrimination Module S.2 (JND calculation), as well as a Motion Sense category 304 for dynamic acuity testing. The rehabilitation training program 400 offers Discrete category 402 tasks like Center-Out Task module T.1 (target acquisition) and Follow the Target module T.2 (pursuit tracking), alongside Continuous Sensorimotor Training category 404 modules such as Virtual Object Balancing T.3 and Figure Eight Tracking T.4.

Integration of assessment and training enables adaptive therapy: baseline JND values inform training difficulty, while post-training reassessment quantifies neuroplastic gains. This closed-loop architecture enhances rehabilitation efficacy by personalizing challenge levels and objectively documenting progress, offering significant advantages over conventional, non-integrated approaches.
