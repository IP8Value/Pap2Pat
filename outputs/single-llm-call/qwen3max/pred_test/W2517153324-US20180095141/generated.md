# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNICAL FIELD

The present invention relates to methods and apparatuses for estimating the state of charge (SoC) and state of health (SoH) of electrochemical energy storage cells, particularly lithium-sulfur (Li-S) battery cells. More specifically, the invention provides a computationally efficient framework for modeling Li-S cell behavior under dynamic operating conditions, including temperature variations and complex current profiles, by integrating a parameterized equivalent circuit network (ECN) model with a memory effect module that accounts for the unique electrochemical phenomena inherent to Li-S chemistry. The disclosed methods and apparatuses are especially suited for deployment in battery management systems (BMS) of electric vehicles (EVs), where accurate real-time estimation of remaining range, usable capacity, and internal cell states is critical for performance, safety, and user confidence.

## BACKGROUND

There is a pressing need for accurate determination of the state of charge (SoC) in modern battery-powered systems, particularly electric vehicles, to mitigate range anxiety and ensure optimal energy utilization. SoC, conventionally defined as the ratio of remaining usable capacity to the total nominal capacity of a cell, is a fundamental metric for battery management. Closely related is the state of discharge (SoD), which is simply one minus the SoC. A common formula for SoC at time *t* is given by:  
\[
\text{SoC}(t) = \text{SoC}(0) - \frac{1}{Q_{\text{nom}}} \int_0^t I(\tau) \, d\tau,
\]  
where \( Q_{\text{nom}} \) is the nominal capacity and \( I(\tau) \) is the cell current (positive for discharge). However, this simplistic coulomb counting approach requires an accurate initial SoC setting and assumes constant, known capacity, both of which are problematic in practice.

Lithium-sulfur (Li-S) cells present unique challenges that render conventional SoC estimation techniques inadequate. Unlike lithium-ion batteries, which rely on intercalation chemistry and exhibit relatively stable open-circuit voltage (OCV) curves that correlate well with SoC, Li-S cells operate via conversion reactions involving multiple soluble polysulfide intermediates. This results in a highly non-monotonic and flat OCV curve with two distinct plateaus—around 2.35 V and 2.1 V—separated by a region of high internal resistance. Critically, in the low-voltage plateau, the OCV tends to relax to approximately 2.1 V regardless of the actual SoC after sufficient rest, making OCV-based SoC estimation unreliable.

Furthermore, traditional methods such as measuring internal resistance or monitoring temperature offer limited utility. Internal resistance in Li-S cells varies not only with SoC and temperature but also dynamically with current history and the precipitation of insulating lithium sulfide species. Temperature measurements alone cannot resolve the complex state dependencies of the cell’s electrochemical processes. Coulomb counting, while straightforward, suffers from significant drift due to the Li-S cell’s pronounced self-discharge (caused by the polysulfide shuttle effect) and its strong dependence of usable capacity on discharge rate, temperature, and cycling history. These factors collectively undermine the accuracy of existing SoC estimation strategies when applied to Li-S chemistry, necessitating a more sophisticated and adaptive modeling approach.

## BRIEF SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

Lithium-sulfur battery chemistry, while promising high specific energy and lower cost, is hampered by complex operational limitations including rapid degradation, high self-discharge, and a strong dependence of usable capacity on dynamic use conditions such as current profile, temperature, and prior cycling history. A key challenge is the variation of the total reversible capacity (\( Q_t \)) of the cell, which is not fixed but changes based on these operational factors—a phenomenon herein referred to as the “memory effect.” This memory effect is distinct from classical battery memory and arises from the kinetics and solubility of polysulfide species, leading to incomplete sulfur utilization under certain conditions.

To address these challenges, the invention introduces a comprehensive apparatus and method for modeling and estimating the SoC and SoH of Li-S cells. The apparatus comprises a cell model module, which implements a parameterized physics-based cell model, and a dedicated memory effect module that captures the history-dependent variation in usable capacity. The cell model module is configured to operate under varying cell operational conditions, including current, voltage, and temperature, and is based on an equivalent circuit network (ECN) model structure that has been reparameterized in behavioral terms for improved identifiability and constraint application.

The parameterized physics-based cell model draws its parameter values from a parameter value resource, which stores functions (e.g., polynomials) that describe how model parameters such as open-circuit voltage, internal resistance, and RC time constants vary with SoC and temperature. The memory effect module implements a simplified physical model of the Li-S electrochemical reactions, establishing rules for reactant species and parameterizing reaction rates to predict how operational history affects the instantaneous usable capacity (\( Q_t \)).

In operation, the apparatus receives measurements of cell operational conditions via a cell operational condition monitor module. A state estimator module, which may employ a Kalman-type filter, uses these measurements in conjunction with the cell model and memory model to estimate the internal state of the cell, including SoC and SoH. An iterative feedback loop continuously refines these estimates by minimizing prediction error between measured terminal voltage and model-predicted voltage. This framework enables accurate real-time SoC estimation, which can be used by a battery management system to estimate vehicle range or plan routes. The invention also encompasses a computer-readable medium storing instructions for performing the modeling and estimation methods, as well as methods for generating the underlying cell and memory models from controlled experimental data, including applying current pulses and analyzing voltage responses to identify and refine parameter values.

## DESCRIPTION OF THE EMBODIMENTS

Lithium-sulfur (Li-S) cells are introduced as the primary electrochemical system addressed by the present invention. These cells exhibit a distinctive memory effect, wherein the usable capacity is not a fixed property but varies depending on the cell’s operational history, including discharge rate, temperature, and rest periods. This memory effect renders conventional SoC estimation methods, which often assume a static capacity, fundamentally inaccurate. Moreover, reliance on internal resistance measurements for SoC estimation is insufficient due to the complex, multi-plateau nature of the Li-S discharge curve and the dynamic changes in resistance caused by polysulfide precipitation.

To overcome these limitations, the invention provides an integrated apparatus for modeling and estimating both SoC and state of health (SoH). The apparatus begins by collecting cumulative history data, including current, voltage, temperature, and time, which is essential for capturing the memory effect. A terminal voltage estimation method is employed, which uses a physics-informed ECN model to predict the cell’s output voltage based on its internal state and applied current.

The core of the apparatus includes cell operational condition measurement means, which may comprise sensors for current (e.g., shunt resistors or Hall-effect sensors), voltage (e.g., analog-to-digital converters), and temperature (e.g., thermistors). Internal resistance is not measured directly but is inferred as part of the model parameters. The SOC model is implemented within a cell model module, which accesses a parameter value resource—a database or lookup structure containing pre-identified functions that map SoC and temperature to model parameters such as open-circuit voltage (\( U_{OC} \)), ohmic resistance (\( R_0 \)), and polarization resistance and capacitance (\( R_p, C_p \)).

A dedicated memory effect module implements a memory model that dynamically adjusts the effective capacity (\( Q_t \)) used in SoC calculations. This memory model is based on parameterized reaction rates derived from the underlying Li-S chemistry, particularly the kinetics of polysulfide conversion and precipitation. The cell state estimator integrates these components, using a recursive algorithm (e.g., extended Kalman filter) to fuse real-time measurements with model predictions, thereby estimating the true SoC.

Alternative embodiments may vary the complexity of the ECN model (e.g., using zero, one, or multiple RC pairs) or the sophistication of the memory model (from empirical lookup tables to mechanistic differential equations). The apparatus for creating the SOC model involves offline identification procedures using pulse testing across temperatures. The cell state estimator and SOC model may be implemented in software on a microcontroller within a BMS. This BMS interfaces with an energy system controller in an EV, managing power delivery and providing range estimates. The apparatus is scalable to plural cells in a battery pack, with individual or grouped cell modeling. Specific implementations may include hardware accelerators for real-time computation or cloud-based model updating.

### Generating the Cell Model Module—Equivalent Circuit Example

The equivalent circuit model (ECN) is introduced as a computationally tractable representation of Li-S cell dynamics. To generate this model, test data is first produced by applying controlled current load profiles—specifically, mixed current pulses of varying amplitudes (e.g., 0.1C, 0.5C, 1C)—to a Li-S pouch cell held at a fixed temperature. During these tests, the terminal voltage is precisely measured using calibrated instrumentation.

An equivalent circuit network model is then selected, typically a Thevenin model comprising a voltage source for OCV, a series ohmic resistor (\( R_0 \)), and one or more parallel resistor-capacitor (RC) branches to capture transient polarization effects. The model structure selection balances accuracy against computational cost, with a single RC pair often chosen for BMS deployment.

The parameterization process involves determining numerical values for \( U_{OC} \), \( R_0 \), \( R_p \), and \( C_p \) as functions of SoC and temperature. Open-circuit voltage is calculated by measuring the relaxed voltage after long rest periods at known SoC points, though special care is taken in the low plateau where OCV is nearly constant. Ohmic resistance is derived from the instantaneous voltage drop at the onset of a current pulse, while diffuse (polarization) resistance and capacitance are extracted from the subsequent voltage relaxation curve.

A fitting procedure, such as non-linear least squares optimization, is applied to minimize the difference between measured and simulated voltage responses. This yields parameter values at discrete SoC points. These values are then compiled into look-up tables or, preferably, fitted to smooth analytical functions (e.g., polynomials) to ensure differentiability for use in gradient-based estimators like Kalman filters. Curve fitting is performed separately for the high and low voltage plateaus where parameter behavior differs significantly, with smooth transitions (e.g., via sinusoidal blending functions) between regions. The resulting fitted polynomials constitute the final parameterized model, which is validated against independent drive-cycle data to confirm predictive accuracy before deployment.

### Generating the Memory Effect Model

The lithium-sulfur memory effect is introduced as the phenomenon where the total reversible capacity (\( Q_t \)) of the cell depends on its recent operational history, particularly discharge current and temperature. This motivates the need for a dynamic capacity model, as static capacity assumptions lead to SoC estimation errors. To characterize this, a capacity variation experiment is conducted: identical Li-S cells are discharged under different current profiles (e.g., constant low current vs. pulsed high current) at the same temperature, and their delivered capacities are compared. Results illustrate that higher currents yield lower usable capacities due to kinetic limitations and passivation layer formation.

The memory effect is formally defined as the deviation of instantaneous usable capacity from a baseline value, driven by the accumulation of insoluble discharge products (e.g., Li₂S) that block active sites. A memory model is introduced to quantify this effect. The model’s functionality is to adjust the effective capacity in real-time based on operational inputs. It can be expanded to also account for long-term degradation, thereby linking to state of health (SoH), which is defined as the ratio of current maximum capacity to original capacity.

The memory model operates by tracking key state variables, such as the concentration of precipitated sulfides, inferred from current and voltage history. Rules for reactant species are established based on known Li-S reaction pathways, and reaction rates are parameterized using Arrhenius-type expressions dependent on temperature and overpotential. Parameterized values are identified by fitting the model to experimental capacity fade and rate-dependence data.

A simplified physical model is derived for computational efficiency, reducing the full reaction network to a few lumped states. This simplified model is then used to adjust the ECN model’s capacity parameter dynamically. The adjustment mechanism scales the SoC integration denominator (\( Q_t \)) based on the memory state. Modelled discharge curves generated with and without memory correction show significant differences, particularly at high rates, illustrating capacity loss. The simplified memory model is parametrized using the same pulse test data, ensuring consistency with the ECN model.

### Parameterisation by Prediction Error Minimisation (PEM)

The Prediction Error Minimisation (PEM) method is introduced as a robust system identification technique for battery modeling. Its primary advantage lies in directly optimizing model parameters to minimize the discrepancy between predicted and measured outputs, making it ideal for nonlinear, state-dependent systems like Li-S cells. The PEM procedure begins with selecting a model structure—in this case, the behavioral-parameterized ECN model described earlier.

An equivalent circuit model is illustrated, and model structure selection criteria include parsimony (minimal parameters), observability, and compatibility with real-time estimation algorithms. Parameters are fitted to experimental data by defining a prediction error as the difference between measured terminal voltage and model-simulated voltage at each time step. An iterative minimization procedure, guided by a fitness function such as the root mean square error (RMSE), adjusts parameters to reduce this error. The RMSE criterion is defined as:
\[
\text{RMSE} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} (V_{\text{meas},i} - V_{\text{pred},i})^2},
\]
and low RMSE values (e.g., < 50 mV) indicate good model fidelity.

For real-time SoC estimation, a Kalman-type filter (e.g., extended or unscented Kalman filter) is employed. This method receives measured current, voltage, and temperature, and uses them to update a state vector that includes SoC and possibly memory state variables. The ECN model predicts the next terminal voltage, and any prediction error is used to correct the state estimates via the filter’s gain matrix. SoH is estimated concurrently by tracking slow-drifting parameters like maximum capacity.

The feedback loop continuously refines parameter estimates, increasing accuracy over time. A memory-aware model is deployed, where the ECN parameters and capacity are modulated by the memory state. The model is generated from experimental pulse data across temperatures, and the ECN is implemented in embedded code. The algorithm architecture supports various deployment architectures, from centralized BMS to distributed cell-level monitors. The prediction horizon—the time window over which predictions are made—is selected to balance responsiveness and noise sensitivity, typically on the order of seconds for automotive applications.
