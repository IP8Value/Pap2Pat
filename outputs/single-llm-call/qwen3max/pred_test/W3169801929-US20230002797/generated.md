# DESCRIPTION

This application claims the benefit of U.S. Provisional Patent Application Serial No. 62/899,391, filed on September 12, 2019, entitled “Method for Producing the Sesquiterpene Viridiflorol with a Fungal Enzyme,” the entire disclosure of which is incorporated herein by reference in its entirety for all purposes.

## BACKGROUND

Fungal terpenoids represent a structurally diverse and biologically significant class of natural products with applications spanning pharmaceuticals, cosmetics, agrochemicals, and fragrances. Terpenoids are synthesized through the condensation of isoprene units and are broadly categorized based on the number of these five-carbon building blocks—monoterpenes (C10), sesquiterpenes (C15), diterpenes (C20), and higher-order variants. Among fungi, sesquiterpenoids have garnered particular interest due to their complex scaffolds and potent biological activities, including antimicrobial, cytotoxic, and signaling functions. Historically, research into fungal terpenoid biosynthesis has focused predominantly on members of the Ascomycota phylum, which includes well-characterized model organisms such as *Aspergillus* and *Penicillium*. In contrast, the Basidiomycota—a phylum encompassing mushroom-forming fungi, rusts, smuts, and endophytes—remains comparatively underexplored despite genomic evidence suggesting a rich reservoir of uncharacterized terpene synthase genes. The enzymatic machinery responsible for terpenoid scaffold formation in fungi centers on terpene synthases (TPSs), which catalyze the cyclization of linear prenyl diphosphate precursors into structurally intricate hydrocarbon skeletons. Sesquiterpene synthases (STSs) specifically utilize farnesyl diphosphate (FPP) as a substrate, initiating a cascade of carbocation-driven cyclizations, hydride shifts, and rearrangements that culminate in the release of a final sesquiterpene product, often terminated by deprotonation or water capture. Recent phylogenetic analyses have revealed that basidiomycete STSs cluster into distinct clades that correlate not only with sequence homology but also with mechanistic features of cyclization, enabling predictive functional annotation. Despite this progress, the functional characterization of individual STS enzymes from non-model basidiomycetes, particularly endophytic species with symbiotic lifestyles, remains sparse. This gap in knowledge limits both fundamental understanding of fungal specialized metabolism and the biotechnological exploitation of novel terpenoid pathways. The present invention addresses this need by identifying, characterizing, and enabling the heterologous production of a previously unknown sesquiterpene synthase from the beneficial root endophyte *Serendipita indica*, thereby providing a new enzymatic tool for the sustainable production of the high-value sesquiterpene alcohol viridiflorol.

## SUMMARY

The present invention identifies and discloses a novel terpenoid synthase gene, designated *SiTPS*, isolated from the basidiomycete fungus *Serendipita indica*. This gene encodes a functional sesquiterpene synthase enzyme that catalyzes the conversion of farnesyl diphosphate (FPP) into viridiflorol as its primary product. The invention further describes methods for the heterologous expression of the *SiTPS* gene in prokaryotic and eukaryotic host systems, enabling the scalable biosynthesis of viridiflorol without reliance on native fungal cultivation or plant extraction. Viridiflorol, a tricyclic sesquiterpene alcohol belonging to the aromadendrane family, exhibits documented antimicrobial and anti-inflammatory properties and is valued in the fragrance industry, making it a compound of significant commercial interest. The invention encompasses expression cassettes comprising the *SiTPS* coding sequence operably linked to regulatory elements suitable for driving transcription and translation in a chosen host organism. Various embodiments of the invention include recombinant nucleic acid molecules encoding the SiTPS enzyme or functional variants thereof, expression vectors containing these nucleic acids, genetically modified host cells engineered to produce viridiflorol, and kits for the production or detection of viridiflorol. Additional embodiments provide methods for manufacturing viridiflorol by cultivating transformed host cells or transgenic plants expressing *SiTPS* and isolating the terpene from culture media, cellular extracts, or plant tissues. The invention also contemplates the use of SiTPS-derived viridiflorol in agricultural biocontrol applications, leveraging its demonstrated antifungal activity against phytopathogens such as *Colletotrichum truncatum*. Collectively, these embodiments establish a robust platform for the biocatalytic production of viridiflorol and related sesquiterpenoids, offering a sustainable and tunable alternative to chemical synthesis or natural product isolation.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention centers on the discovery and functional characterization of a sesquiterpene synthase enzyme, herein referred to as SiTPS, derived from the endophytic basidiomycete *Serendipita indica*. SiTPS represents the first experimentally validated terpenoid biosynthetic enzyme identified in *S. indica*, a fungus previously thought to lack specialized metabolic capacity for terpenoid production. The SiTPS enzyme exhibits high specificity for the sesquiterpene precursor farnesyl diphosphate (E,E-FPP) and catalyzes its cyclization predominantly into viridiflorol, a commercially relevant sesquiterpene alcohol with a 7/5/3 tricyclic structure. The enzyme’s catalytic mechanism proceeds via an initial C1–C10 ring closure of E,E-FPP, consistent with its phylogenetic classification within Clade I of basidiomycete sesquiterpene synthases. The amino acid sequence of SiTPS, as deduced from the *S. indica* genome (JGI Protein ID: 77541), comprises conserved motifs characteristic of class I terpene synthases, including the DDxxD and NSE/DTE metal-binding domains essential for substrate coordination and catalysis. The invention provides the full-length nucleotide sequence encoding SiTPS (corresponding to JGI mRNA: PIIN_06735) and the derived amino acid sequence, which serve as foundational elements for genetic manipulation and heterologous expression. Enzyme variations encompassing conservative amino acid substitutions—those that preserve physicochemical properties such as charge, hydrophobicity, or size—are also within the scope of the invention, provided they retain at least partial terpene synthase activity. Such variations may arise naturally or be engineered to enhance solubility, stability, catalytic efficiency, or expression yield. The corresponding nucleic acid sequences encoding SiTPS or its functional variants may be optimized for expression in heterologous hosts through codon usage adaptation, removal of destabilizing motifs, or introduction of preferred transcriptional and translational signals, without altering the encoded polypeptide sequence. Conservative changes in the nucleic acid sequence that do not affect the amino acid sequence—such as silent mutations due to codon degeneracy—are explicitly included, as are sequences exhibiting substantial identity (e.g., ≥80%, ≥90%, or ≥95% identity over the full length) to the native *SiTPS* gene and retaining enzymatic function.

### Synthetic Pathways

Viridiflorol biosynthesis by SiTPS initiates with the ionization of E,E-farnesyl diphosphate (FPP) upon binding to a divalent metal ion cofactor (typically Mg²⁺ or Mn²⁺) coordinated by the enzyme’s active site aspartate-rich motifs. This ionization releases inorganic pyrophosphate, generating an allylic carbocation at C1 of the farnesyl chain. The enzyme then directs a specific conformational folding of the substrate, facilitating an electrophilic attack by C1 on the terminal double bond between C10 and C11, resulting in a 1,10-cyclization that forms a six-membered ring and a new tertiary carbocation at C10. Subsequent intramolecular rearrangements—including hydride shifts, methyl migrations, and additional ring closures—proceed through a series of stabilized carbocation intermediates, ultimately yielding a tricyclic cation. The reaction cascade is terminated by the stereospecific addition of a water molecule to this final cation, producing viridiflorol as the major product. Minor side products may also be formed due to alternative quenching pathways or incomplete cyclization, but viridiflorol consistently predominates in both in vitro and in vivo assays. This pathway stands in contrast to other known viridiflorol synthases, such as AaVS from *Cyclocybe aegerita*, which utilizes the (3R)-nerolidyl diphosphate isomer of FPP and belongs to a different phylogenetic clade, demonstrating convergent evolution toward the same chemical product via distinct mechanistic routes.

### Expression of Enzymes

The SiTPS enzyme can be expressed in a variety of heterologous host systems to enable the production of viridiflorol. Suitable expression systems include bacterial (e.g., *Escherichia coli*), fungal (e.g., *Saccharomyces cerevisiae*, *Aspergillus* spp.), insect, mammalian, and plant cells. Expression cassettes are constructed to contain the *SiTPS* coding sequence operably linked to appropriate regulatory elements, including a promoter, a 5' untranslated region (UTR), a start codon, and a 3' termination and polyadenylation signal. Terpene synthases like SiTPS are motivated for expression due to their ability to convert abundant endogenous or supplemented prenyl diphosphate precursors into high-value terpenoids. Host cells containing these expression systems become biocatalysts capable of synthesizing viridiflorol either intracellularly or, if equipped with secretion machinery, extracellularly. Methods of using the expressed enzymes include in vitro enzymatic assays with purified protein and in vivo fermentation or cultivation of engineered cells. Nucleic acids encoding SiTPS may be modified to improve expression, stability, or function; such modifications include codon optimization for the host organism, which replaces rare codons with synonymous codons frequently used in the host to enhance translational efficiency and protein yield. Degenerate codons—different nucleotide triplets encoding the same amino acid—allow for such optimization without altering the protein sequence. Optimized nucleic acid sequences are thus provided as part of the invention. Expression cassettes may be incorporated into plasmid vectors, viral vectors, or chromosomal integration constructs. Binary Ti plasmid vectors are particularly useful for plant transformation. Constructs and kits containing these elements facilitate research and industrial applications. Enzyme function may be further improved through targeted modifications such as deletions of non-essential domains, insertions of solubility tags, or substitutions of key residues to alter product specificity or kinetics. Nucleic acid molecules encoding such optimized or modified enzymes are encompassed herein. Expression in host cells is achieved using standard molecular biology techniques, including restriction enzyme digestion, ligation, PCR-based cloning, and Gibson or In-Fusion assembly. Promoter regions drive transcription and may be constitutive, inducible, tissue-specific, or developmentally regulated. Strong promoters (e.g., T7 in bacteria, CaMV 35S in plants) ensure high-level expression, while weak or inducible promoters (e.g., lac, tet) allow controlled expression. Plant-specific promoters such as rbcS or phaseolin enable spatial regulation. Expression cassettes are constructed by subcloning the *SiTPS* sequence downstream of the chosen promoter. Targeting sequences, including transit peptides for plastids or mitochondria and signal peptides for secretion, can be fused to direct the enzyme to specific intracellular compartments where substrates are abundant or where product sequestration is advantageous. Examples of transit peptides include those from the small subunit of Rubisco. 3' sequences ensure proper transcription termination and mRNA stability; examples include the nopaline synthase (nos) terminator or the 3' UTR of the *Arabidopsis* HSP18.2 gene. Selectable markers (e.g., antibiotic resistance genes) and screenable markers (e.g., fluorescent or colorimetric reporters) are included in vectors to identify and isolate successfully transformed cells. Common selectable markers include the *neo* gene (conferring kanamycin resistance in plants and bacteria), *bar* or phosphinothricin acetyltransferase (*pat*) genes (bialaphos resistance), EPSP synthase (glyphosate resistance), nitrilase (bromoxynil resistance), ALS (imidazolinone resistance), dalapon dehalogenase, and mutated anthranilate synthase (5-methyl tryptophan resistance). Screenable markers include β-glucuronidase (*uidA*), R-locus genes (anthocyanin pigmentation), luciferase, and green fluorescent protein (GFP). Cell wall proteins may be modified to facilitate secretion or extraction. Transformation methods vary by host: electroporation and microprojectile bombardment are common for plant cells, with protocols optimized for monocots (e.g., maize, rice) and dicots (e.g., tomato, *Arabidopsis*). Tissue sources include protoplasts, callus, embryos, or meristems. After transformation, cells are selected using appropriate agents (e.g., antibiotics, herbicides) or screened visually. Regenerated plants are grown on media containing growth regulators (e.g., auxins, cytokinins), transferred to soilless mixes, hardened, and matured. Self-pollination or crossing with inbred lines enables the production of true-breeding transgenic lines. Seed and tissue are evaluated for enzyme expression and viridiflorol production using gas chromatography (GC), mass spectrometry (MS), NMR, and molecular assays such as PCR, RT-PCR, Northern and Southern blotting, ELISA, and Western blotting.

### Hosts

A “host” refers to any cell or organism capable of receiving and expressing exogenous nucleic acid. Host cells include prokaryotic and eukaryotic cells engineered to produce SiTPS and viridiflorol. Examples include bacterial cells (e.g., *E. coli*, *Bacillus subtilis*), fungal cells (e.g., *S. cerevisiae*, *Yarrowia lipolytica*), plant cells (e.g., tobacco, tomato, *Arabidopsis*), yeast cells, insect cells (e.g., Sf9), and mammalian cells (e.g., CHO, HEK293). Within these hosts, organelles such as plastids (in plants), lipid droplets (for terpene storage), and smooth endoplasmic reticulum (involved in terpenoid metabolism) may serve as sites for enzyme localization or product accumulation.

### Manufacturing

The invention provides methods for synthesizing viridiflorol by cultivating host cells or transgenic plants expressing SiTPS under conditions conducive to enzyme activity and terpene production. Terpene is isolated from host cells via solvent extraction (e.g., hexane, ethyl acetate), distillation, or chromatography. In plant-based systems, viridiflorol may be extracted from roots, leaves, or seeds using similar techniques.

### Example 1: Materials and Methods

*S. indica* (DSM11827) was cultured in complete medium with glucose. Tomato seeds (cv. Moneymaker) were sterilized and germinated before inoculation with fungal chlamydospores. The *SiTPS* gene was discovered via genome mining of *S. indica* (JGI locus PIIN_06735). Phylogenetic analysis aligned SiTPS with basidiomycete STSs using CLUSTALW and MAFFT, and trees were built in MEGAX with 500 bootstrap replicates. SiTPS was cloned into pET28b+ with a C-terminal His-tag and expressed in *E. coli* C41 cells induced with IPTG. Protein was purified using Ni-affinity chromatography. In vitro assays incubated purified SiTPS with GPP, FPP, or GGPP; only FPP yielded products. An in vivo system co-expressed SiTPS with *Gallus gallus* FPP synthase and MEP pathway genes in *E. coli*. Compounds were extracted with hexane and analyzed by GC-MS, with spectra matched to the NIST17 database.

### Example 2: Phylogenetic Analysis on SiTPS

Phylogenetic analysis placed SiTPS in Clade I of basidiomycete STSs, which perform 1,10-cyclization of E,E-FPP. SiTPS clustered with homologs from other *Serendipita* species and a lichen (*Cladonia uncialis*), indicating evolutionary conservation. The analysis distinguished fungal from bacterial TPSs and suggested functional convergence between lichen and Sebacinales TPSs, though limitations exist due to incomplete functional data for many sequences.

### Example 3: SiTPS Encodes a Viridiflorol Synthase

In vitro and in vivo assays confirmed SiTPS converts FPP primarily into viridiflorol, identified by co-elution and spectral match with an authentic standard. Minor products were also detected. The mechanism involves C1–C10 cyclization of E,E-FPP, consistent with Clade I membership. SiTPS is the second fungal viridiflorol synthase characterized, after AaVS from *C. aegerita*.

### Example 4: SiTPS is Induced Upon Root-Colonization

RT-qPCR showed SiTPS expression is upregulated approximately three-fold in *S. indica* colonizing tomato roots compared to axenic culture, indicating induction during plant interaction.

### Example 5: SiTPS Implication in Colonization of Tomato Roots

An *SiTPS*-overexpressing *S. indica* strain was generated via PEG-mediated protoplast transformation using a vector with the FGB1 promoter. Colonization assays on tomato showed no difference in root colonization between overexpressors, empty vector controls, and wild-type fungus at 2 and 11 days post-inoculation, suggesting SiTPS does not enhance colonization. However, viridiflorol inhibited *Colletotrichum truncatum* spore germination at ≥100 µg/mL, supporting a defensive role.
