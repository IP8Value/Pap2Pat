# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention resides in the field of genetic engineering, particularly in the design, construction, and application of genetically encoded systems for the discovery, evolution, and functional characterization of biologically active agents. More specifically, the invention relates to engineered microbial biosensors that link the activity or inhibition of a target enzyme—such as a protein phosphatase—to a readily detectable cellular output, including antibiotic resistance, luminescence, or fluorescence. The invention further encompasses optogenetic constructs wherein enzyme activity is modulated by light, enabling spatiotemporal control over intracellular signaling pathways. These systems are implemented through synthetic operons, fusion proteins, and modular DNA constructs that integrate substrate recognition domains, catalytic domains, reporter genes, and regulatory elements into a unified genetic circuit. The disclosed technologies facilitate high-throughput screening, directed evolution, and structure–activity relationship studies of small molecule inhibitors, particularly natural products such as terpenoids, with applications in drug discovery for diseases involving dysregulated phosphorylation, including diabetes, obesity, and cancer.

## BACKGROUND

Protein phosphorylation is a fundamental post-translational modification that governs nearly all aspects of cellular physiology, including growth, differentiation, metabolism, and apoptosis. This dynamic process is tightly regulated by the opposing actions of protein kinases, which add phosphate groups to tyrosine, serine, or threonine residues, and protein phosphatases, which remove them. Among these, protein tyrosine phosphatases (PTPs) play critical roles in signal transduction networks, and their dysregulation is implicated in numerous pathologies, including type 2 diabetes, obesity, autoimmune disorders, and various cancers. Despite their therapeutic relevance, PTPs have proven exceptionally challenging targets for conventional drug discovery. Their catalytic sites are highly conserved, positively charged, and hydrophilic, making it difficult to develop small-molecule inhibitors that are both potent and selective while maintaining cell permeability. Allosteric inhibitors offer a promising alternative, as they bind to less conserved regions outside the active site, but identifying such compounds has been hampered by the limitations of traditional screening methods, which often rely on purified proteins, synthetic libraries, or labor-intensive natural product isolation. Moreover, existing approaches lack the ability to couple inhibitor discovery directly to biosynthetic capacity, thereby failing to prioritize molecules that are not only active but also amenable to biological production. There is thus a pressing need for genetically encoded platforms that can simultaneously produce, detect, and evolve bioactive metabolites within living cells, enabling the discovery of novel, synthetically tractable inhibitors of therapeutically relevant phosphatases.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates to genetic engineering methodologies for constructing and deploying synthetic biological systems capable of producing, detecting, and evolving biologically active agents, particularly inhibitors of protein phosphatases. In one aspect, the invention provides engineered operons designed to produce biologically active agents such as terpenoids, polyketides, or alkaloids through the coordinated expression of biosynthetic pathway genes in microbial hosts like *Escherichia coli*. These operons include sequences encoding enzymes for precursor synthesis (e.g., the mevalonate pathway for isoprenoid precursors), terpene synthases, and tailoring enzymes (e.g., cytochrome P450s, halogenases), all under the control of inducible promoters.

In another aspect, the invention discloses fusion proteins that integrate functional domains to create novel regulatory or reporting entities. A key embodiment is a fusion protein comprising a substrate recognition domain (e.g., an SH2 domain), a DNA-binding domain (e.g., the λ cI repressor), and an anchoring unit for RNA polymerase (e.g., the ω subunit of bacterial RNA polymerase). This tripartite fusion enables phosphorylation-dependent transcriptional activation: when a kinase phosphorylates a cognate substrate domain fused to the RNA polymerase anchor, the phosphorylated motif recruits the SH2-containing repressor fusion, relieving repression and allowing expression of a downstream reporter gene.

The reporter gene may encode a visual or selectable marker, such as LuxAB bioreporters (which produce bioluminescence upon addition of decanal), fluorescent proteins (e.g., mClover), or antibiotic resistance genes (e.g., spectinomycin resistance). In preferred embodiments, the system includes a first enzyme (e.g., Src kinase) that phosphorylates the substrate domain and a second enzyme (e.g., protein tyrosine phosphatase 1B, or PTP1B) that dephosphorylates it, thereby antagonistically regulating reporter expression. The entire system functions as a genetically encoded biosensor for phosphatase activity.

The invention further introduces a protein phosphatase—specifically, a protein tyrosine phosphatase such as PTP1B—as a central node in this regulatory circuit. PTP1B is selected due to its validated role in insulin and leptin signaling and its status as a high-value therapeutic target. To enable optical control, the invention provides photoswitchable enzyme constructs, such as a PTP1B-LOV2 chimera, wherein the catalytic domain of PTP1B is fused to the light-oxygen-voltage-sensing domain (LOV2) from *Avena sativa* phototropin 1. Upon blue light illumination, the LOV2 domain undergoes a conformational change that sterically blocks or enhances PTP1B activity, effectively creating a light-gated phosphatase.

In certain embodiments, the system includes a toxic product gene such as *sacB* (encoding levansucrase, which converts sucrose to toxic levan) as a counter-selectable marker, enabling stringent selection for phosphatase inhibition. Expression vectors carrying these genetic circuits are introduced into bacterial cells, which serve as living biosensors for detecting inhibitors of the target enzyme. When a microbially produced metabolite inhibits the phosphatase, the kinase-mediated phosphorylation event proceeds unchecked, leading to reporter gene expression and a measurable phenotype (e.g., growth on antibiotic).

This platform supports multiple applications: (1) detecting inhibitors of an enzyme by observing reporter activation in the presence of candidate compounds; (2) evolving inhibitors through mutagenesis of biosynthetic pathways coupled to survival selection; (3) evolving selective inhibitors by comparing responses across phosphatase-specific biosensors; (4) evolving photoswitchable enzymes via directed evolution of LOV2 or phytochrome fusions; (5) evolving selective mutants of an enzyme that respond only to specific inhibitors; and (6) evolving substrate domains with tailored specificity for particular phosphatase states.

The invention also encompasses the use of microbial biosensors for high-throughput screening. By transforming bacteria with the genetically encoded detection operon and a biosynthetic operon (e.g., for terpenoids), one can screen libraries of biosynthetic genes for their ability to confer resistance, thereby identifying novel inhibitor-producing strains. Variants of chemical structures are generated by mutating tailoring enzymes or synthases, and the resulting metabolites are evaluated in situ without purification.

A core component is a fusion protein DNA construct encoding a chimeric protein that combines a protein phosphatase (e.g., PTP1B), a protein light switch (e.g., LOV2), and optional linkers. This construct enables light-induced conformational changes that alter catalytic activity, providing temporal and spatial control over dephosphorylation events. Methods of using this fusion protein include expressing it in mammalian cells, illuminating with specific wavelengths, and measuring downstream effects on signaling pathways such as insulin receptor phosphorylation.

The invention further describes controlling cell movement and signaling by optically regulating phosphatase activity at subcellular locales, thereby exerting a modulatory effect on processes like invadopodia formation or EGFR trafficking. Illumination triggers a light-induced conformational change in the LOV2 domain, which alters the accessibility or dynamics of the PTP1B catalytic cleft, thereby modulating enzymatic turnover.

In screening applications, the method involves providing a fusion protein (e.g., PTP1B-LOV2), expressing it in a host cell, contacting the cell with a small molecule test compound, illuminating the fusion protein to toggle its activity state, and measuring a visual readout (e.g., fluorescence, luminescence). Compounds that differentially affect activity in light vs. dark conditions are identified as modulators, which may then be used to treat patients suffering from diseases associated with phosphatase dysregulation, such as type 2 diabetes or HER2-positive breast cancer.

The photoswitchable PTP1B construct features an N-terminal alpha helix derived from the Jα helix of LOV2 and a C-terminal allosteric domain region corresponding to the α7 helix of PTP1B, which together form a dynamic interface that transmits light-induced structural changes to the catalytic site. A complementary biosensor for enzyme activity employs a substrate domain fused between two fluorescent proteins (e.g., CFP and YFP) to create a FRET-based reporter of phosphorylation status.

Additionally, the invention provides a genetically encoded detection operon system comprising a first DNA region encoding a substrate domain fused to an RNA polymerase anchor, a second DNA region encoding a substrate recognition domain fused to a DNA-binding repressor, an operator sequence, an RNA polymerase binding site, and a gene of interest (e.g., antibiotic resistance). This system is used by transforming bacteria, inducing expression, and observing growth as a proxy for phosphatase inhibition.

The invention also integrates metabolic pathways, such as the mevalonate-terpene pathway operon, which includes genes for acetoacetyl-CoA thiolase, HMG-CoA synthase, HMG-CoA reductase, mevalonate kinase, phosphomevalonate kinase, mevalonate diphosphate decarboxylase, and isopentenyl diphosphate isomerase, along with a terpene synthase gene under a separate promoter. Bacteria are co-transformed with the inhibitor detection operon, the mevalonate pathway operon, and a fourth DNA sequence (e.g., GGPPS for diterpenes), grown under selection, and analyzed for inhibitor production.

Extracted terpenoids are purified and tested on mammalian cell cultures to quantify their modulatory effect on phosphatase-dependent signaling, such as insulin receptor phosphorylation. The same framework extends to polyketide and alkaloid biosynthesis, enabling broad applicability. Engineered bacterial cell lines stably produce phosphatase inhibitor molecules, and inducible promoters allow precise temporal control over pathway expression, facilitating scalable, low-cost pharmaceutical development.

## DEFINITIONS

As used herein, the term “operon” refers to a functional unit of DNA containing a cluster of genes under the control of a single promoter, which are transcribed together into a single mRNA strand and typically involved in a related metabolic pathway or cellular function. “Phosphorylation-regulating enzymes” encompass any enzyme that catalyzes the addition (kinases) or removal (phosphatases) of phosphate groups from proteins, lipids, or other biomolecules, thereby modulating their activity, localization, or interactions. “Phosphorylation” denotes the covalent attachment of a phosphate group (PO₄³⁻) to a hydroxyl group of serine, threonine, tyrosine, or other residues in a substrate molecule. An “optogenetic actuator” is a genetically encoded protein or protein chimera whose activity can be controlled by light, typically through incorporation of a photosensory domain such as LOV, cryptochrome, or phytochrome. “Dynamic range” refers to the ratio between the maximum and minimum signal output of a biosensor (e.g., luminescence or growth rate) under fully induced versus repressed conditions. “Operably linked” describes a configuration wherein a promoter is positioned relative to a coding sequence such that transcription of the coding sequence is under the regulatory control of the promoter. Other terms used herein shall have the meanings as commonly understood in the fields of molecular biology, synthetic biology, and medicinal chemistry, unless otherwise specified.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF INVENTION

The present invention is rooted in genetic engineering principles and leverages synthetic biology to create programmable microbial systems for the discovery and optimization of biologically active agents. Central to this approach is the design of synthetic operons that encode not only the biosynthetic machinery for natural product assembly but also genetic circuits that report on the functional activity of those products against a target enzyme. By integrating these components into a single host, the invention establishes a direct link between molecular biosynthesis and phenotypic selection, enabling the identification of potent, selective, and biosynthetically accessible inhibitors.

The invention introduces operons specifically tailored for the production of biologically active agents such as terpenoids, which are assembled from isoprenoid precursors via the mevalonate pathway heterologously expressed in *E. coli*. These operons are modular, allowing for the exchange of terpene synthase genes to generate diverse carbon scaffolds. Coupled with this biosynthetic capacity is a genetically encoded detection system—a bacterial two-hybrid (B2H) circuit—that senses the inhibition of a target phosphatase and converts it into a selectable output, such as antibiotic resistance. This dual-function system enables high-throughput screening of biosynthetic gene libraries without the need for compound purification or in vitro assays.

Protein tyrosine phosphatase 1B (PTP1B) serves as a valuable model system in the disclosed invention due to its well-characterized structure, established role in metabolic disease, and historical difficulty as a drug target. PTP1B is experimentally tractable in bacterial hosts when co-expressed with appropriate chaperones (e.g., Cdc37 for Src kinase), and its activity can be precisely modulated using optogenetic tools. The invention contemplates optical modulation of PTP1B to dissect its spatial regulation in intracellular signaling, particularly at the endoplasmic reticulum where it dephosphorylates the insulin receptor.

### I. Protein Tyrosine Phosphatases (PTPs) and Protein Tyrosine Kinases (PTKs) in Relation to Disease.

Protein tyrosine phosphatases (PTPs) and protein tyrosine kinases (PTKs) are master regulators of cellular signaling, and their imbalance contributes to anomalous signaling events underlying cancer, diabetes, and immune dysfunction. The invention exploits this biology by developing photoswitchable constructs of PTPs and PTKs, enabling light-controlled interrogation of their roles in health and disease. These constructs facilitate the identification of specific alleles or conformational states that are sensitive to inhibition, and provide platforms for screening and testing small molecule modulators. Building on prior art such as Fan et al., WO2011133493 (describing kinase-LOV fusions), WO2012111772 (optical control of calcium signaling), and U.S. Pat. No. 8,859,232 (protein light switches), the present invention advances the field by applying similar principles to phosphatases.

PTPs function as regulatory enzymes that maintain signaling fidelity through precise dephosphorylation. Biophysical studies of PTP1B, including X-ray crystallography and molecular dynamics simulations, have revealed an allosteric network involving the WPD loop and the α7 helix. Kinetic analyses demonstrate that disease-associated mutations can perturb this network, altering catalytic efficiency and substrate specificity. The invention leverages these insights to identify new sites for targeting allosteric inhibitors, particularly in regions that exhibit conformational plasticity upon ligand binding.

### II. Optogenetic Actuators.

Optogenetic actuators enable biochemical events to be placed under optical control, offering unparalleled spatiotemporal precision. However, existing technologies suffer from observational interference, limited spectral palettes, and reliance on caged compounds that require UV light and generate byproducts. The invention addresses these limitations through a “cage-free” approach using genetically encoded photoswitchable phosphatases. Reference WO2013016693 describes bacteriophytochrome-based actuators, but the present invention improves upon this by engineering PTP1B-LOV2 chimeras with enhanced dynamic range and recovery kinetics.

Experimental characterization of PTP1B-LOV2 fusions demonstrates light-dependent catalytic activity, with mutational analysis identifying optimal crossover points between the Jα helix of LOV2 and the α7 helix of PTP1B. Exemplary linkers and point mutations (e.g., in the A'α helix) further refine performance. Biophysical studies confirm that illumination induces a conformational change that modulates access to the catalytic cleft, thereby altering enzymatic turnover. This photoswitchable variant, termed PTP1BPS, exhibits a dynamic range suitable for optical control of cell signaling in living systems.

Imaging methodologies, including confocal microscopy and FRET-based sensors for Src kinase activity, enable real-time monitoring of subcellular signaling events. Reaction-diffusion models help interpret spatially distinct phosphorylation gradients, revealing how localized PTP1B activation influences sensor readouts. These tools are generalized to other PTPs and PTKs, such as STEP and PTK6, demonstrating the broad applicability of the platform. Contemplative embodiments include studying invadopodia formation and EGFR regulation, where cytosolic PTP1B and PTK6 act cooperatively.

### III. Genetically Encoded System for Constructing and Detecting Biologically Active Agents: Microbial Inhibitor Screening Systems.

The invention provides genetic operons that link enzyme inhibition to cellular luminescence, fluorescence, or growth, enabling detection of both native and non-native biologically active metabolites. By installing metabolic pathways for natural product biosynthesis (e.g., terpenoids), the system detects metabolites that inhibit a protein of interest directly in vivo. This approach modifies and extends methods from Moses et al. and Badran et al., using microbial hosts to construct drug leads at low cost and high throughput.

Focusing on PTP1B, the invention hypothesizes that abietic acid acts as an allosteric inhibitor and tests structural variants of abietadiene. Thermodynamic analyses (ΔG, ΔH, ΔS) reveal the molecular basis of affinity, guiding the evolution of high-affinity terpenoid inhibitors. Synthetic biology strategies overcome limitations of traditional pathway discovery by building new molecular functions through operon assembly. The detection operon offers advantages over PCA-based systems (U.S. Pat. No. 6,428,951) and WO2004048549 by enabling direct selection rather than indirect interaction proxies.

High-throughput metabolic engineering in *E. coli* produces abietadiene and derivatives, which are functionalized using P450 mutants. Biostructural analyses of PTP1B guide the design of terpenoids with varied stereochemistry, shape, and size. Halogenation via tryptophan halogenases or vanadium haloperoxidases yields additional analogs. Screens identify hits with targeted activity, demonstrating scalability and versatility over conventional methods.

### IV. Evolving High-Affinity Terpenoid Inhibitors of PTP1B.

High-throughput screens for PTP1B inhibitors employ biological selection wherein inhibitor potency dictates cell survival. Operon construction links PTP1B inhibition to spectinomycin resistance, with luminescence-based systems used for initial optimization. FRET sensors monitor PTP1B activity or conformational changes, enabling FACS-based screens. Tryptophan fluorescence assays detect binding-induced quenching.

Mutagenesis strategies include site-saturation mutagenesis to identify plastic residues in terpene synthases and error-prone PCR to diversify entire pathways. A biophysical framework compares binding pocket properties across PTP family members, using matrices to predict selectivity. This approach validates active site motifs that favor high-affinity, selective inhibition.

### VI. Evolving Optogenetic Actuators: Photoswitchable Constructs.

To overcome limitations of blue/green light (e.g., poor tissue penetration), the invention proposes red/infrared-stimulated photoswitchable enzymes using bacteriophytochromes. An operon is constructed wherein PTP1B suppression of transcription creates a growth differential under light vs. dark conditions. Initial Lux-based luminescence assays validate functionality.

FRET sensors monitor PTP1B activity during directed evolution. Libraries of PTP1B-phytochrome chimeras are screened for functional mutants, which are characterized kinetically and structurally. The approach extends to STEP and PTK6. A synthetic operon for evolving PTP1B-BphP1 chimeras includes components for pathway expression, selection, and screening. Directed evolution yields variants with improved photoswitching, demonstrating the power of operon-based evolution for optogenetic tool development.

## Examples

Statistical analysis of dose-response data employs nonlinear regression to fit inhibition models (e.g., noncompetitive, uncompetitive) and estimate IC50 values with confidence intervals derived from parameter covariance matrices. For instance, amorphadiene exhibits an IC50 of 53 ± 8 μM against PTP1B, determined by fitting initial-rate data to a mixed inhibition model and validating with F-tests and Akaike’s Information Criterion. The scope of the invention extends beyond terpenoids to any biosynthesized metabolite class, and beyond PTP1B to any enzyme whose activity can be wired into a transcriptional output, demonstrating broad utility in drug discovery and synthetic biology.
