# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNICAL FIELD

The present invention pertains to the field of digital image analysis, specifically to computer-implemented methods and systems for whole cell segmentation in two-dimensional microscopy images. More particularly, the invention relates to a deep learning-based framework that enables accurate and robust segmentation of entire cells—including both cytoplasm and nuclei—from single-channel fluorescent microscopy images without requiring a nuclear-specific stain. This technology addresses a critical need in high-throughput biological imaging where channel availability is limited, and the use of nuclear markers may be undesirable due to toxicity or experimental constraints. The invention provides a fully automated, scalable, and generalizable solution for segmenting cells exhibiting high morphological and intensity variability across diverse cell types, staining protocols, drug treatments, and magnifications.

## BACKGROUND

Cell imaging has become a cornerstone of modern biological and biomedical research, enabling scientists to visualize, quantify, and analyze cellular structures and dynamics in both normal and pathological conditions. The ability to accurately identify and delineate individual cells within complex microscopic fields is fundamental to applications ranging from basic cell biology to drug discovery and toxicology screening. In these contexts, researchers routinely assess how genetic perturbations, chemical compounds, or disease states alter cellular morphology, number, distribution, and subcellular organization. Consequently, reliable and reproducible cell analysis is essential for generating meaningful biological insights.

A typical eukaryotic cell comprises a nucleus surrounded by cytoplasm, which contains various organelles and structural components. In fluorescence microscopy, specific molecular markers are used to label distinct cellular compartments, allowing their visualization against a dark background. High-resolution fluorescent microscopy has advanced significantly in recent years, providing unprecedented detail of subcellular architecture. Concurrently, improvements in computing power and the emergence of sophisticated image processing algorithms have enabled the automation of tasks that were once performed manually by experts—a process that is subjective, time-consuming, and prone to inconsistency.

Cell segmentation—the computational process of partitioning an image into regions corresponding to individual cells—is a foundational step in automated image analysis pipelines. Historically, many segmentation methods have focused exclusively on the nucleus, which often appears as a compact, blob-like structure with relatively consistent morphology. However, whole cell segmentation, which includes both the nucleus and the surrounding cytoplasm, is far more challenging due to the irregular shape, variable size, and heterogeneous intensity patterns of the cytoplasmic region. Moreover, cells in dense cultures frequently touch or overlap, making boundary delineation difficult, especially when intercellular gradients are weak.

To address these challenges, some approaches have adopted two-channel imaging strategies: one channel stains the nucleus (e.g., with DAPI), and another labels the cytoplasm or cell membrane. Nuclei are first segmented in the nuclear channel and then used as seeds to guide segmentation of the entire cell in the cytoplasmic channel via techniques such as seeded watershed. While effective, this approach consumes two spectral channels, limiting the number of additional biomarkers that can be simultaneously imaged in multiplexed experiments. Furthermore, nuclear stains like DAPI are often cytotoxic, rendering them unsuitable for live-cell imaging.

In response, researchers have explored single-channel whole cell segmentation methods. Early attempts employed thresholding, morphological operations, or active contour models, but these typically failed under conditions of high cell density, morphological diversity, or low contrast. More recently, machine learning and deep learning techniques have been applied, yet many still require multiple channels or do not robustly separate touching cells. Existing single-channel methods often lack generalizability across different cell types, markers, or experimental conditions, particularly when the nucleus is hypointense relative to the bright cytoplasm—a common scenario in organelle-targeted fluorescent probes.

The limitations of current workflows underscore the need for a novel, single-channel whole cell segmentation technique that is both highly accurate and broadly applicable. Such a method must overcome the inherent ambiguities in cytoplasmic boundaries, handle variable nuclear appearances, and operate without reliance on nuclear-specific markers. The present invention fulfills this need by integrating deep learning with classical image processing in a unified pipeline that achieves state-of-the-art performance using only one fluorescent channel.

## SUMMARY

The present invention introduces a computer-implemented method for whole cell segmentation in single-channel fluorescence microscopy images. The method begins with the generation of a deep learning model trained to predict pixel-wise probabilities for three semantic classes: nuclei, cytoplasm, and background. During inference, the system accesses a sample image acquired using a non-nuclear cell marker—such as a mitochondrial or endosomal dye—and applies the trained model to generate two probability maps: one indicating the likelihood of each pixel belonging to a nucleus, and another indicating cytoplasmic membership. From the nuclear probability map, the system extracts a binary nuclear mask and further processes it to produce a nuclei seeds map, wherein each seed corresponds to a distinct nucleus, even in cases of touching or overlapping nuclei. These nuclei seeds are then applied to the original sample image in a seeded watershed segmentation framework, enhanced by a cell probability-weighted transformation, to delineate individual whole cells. The final output is a segmented image in which each cell—including its nucleus and cytoplasm—is uniquely labeled, thereby transforming the raw input into a structured, analyzable representation suitable for downstream quantitative analysis.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

### System Overview

The invention is implemented within an imaging system designed for in situ analysis of biological samples, such as cultured cells in multi-well plates. Whole cell segmentation provides critical benefits by enabling precise quantification of cellular phenotypes, including cell count, area, shape, texture, and spatial relationships—metrics essential for evaluating drug responses, genetic effects, or disease progression. The imaging system comprises an imager configured to capture high-resolution fluorescence images of a biological sample, which may include adherent or suspension cells labeled with a single non-nuclear fluorescent marker. System control circuitry orchestrates illumination, focus, and stage movement, while data acquisition circuitry digitizes and stores the resulting images. Data processing circuitry, which may reside locally or remotely, executes the segmentation algorithm described herein. An operator workstation equipped with a display and input devices allows users to configure parameters, initiate analysis, and review results. The system supports network and communication interfaces for data sharing, remote processing, and integration with laboratory information management systems. Multiple operator workstations may access the system concurrently, and image processing can be offloaded to cloud-based computing resources. Output metrics derived from segmentation—such as cell counts per well or morphological features—are generated for reporting and decision-making. The core computational apparatus includes a computer-readable medium storing processor-executable instructions that, when executed by a processor coupled with memory and storage devices, perform the segmentation workflow. Input/output devices facilitate user interaction through a graphical user interface, and all system components may be implemented in hardware, software, or a hybrid configuration.

### Development of a Training Model

The invention employs a deep learning model based on a modified U-Net architecture, selected for its effectiveness in biomedical image segmentation. The network consists of a contracting path with five convolutional and max-pooling layers, followed by an expanding path with transposed convolutions and skip connections that fuse high-resolution features from the encoder with upsampled representations. The architecture uses 3×3 convolution kernels and progressively increases the number of filters (32, 64, 128, 128, 256) in the contracting path, while incorporating dropout regularization at three layers to mitigate overfitting. Training occurs in a supervised environment using labeled image patches of 160×160 pixels, extracted from larger 176×176 patches with 16-pixel overlap to ensure seamless stitching. The model is trained to predict three output channels corresponding to nuclei, cytoplasm, and background. A custom loss function combines root mean square deviation (RMSD) terms for each class with a constraint enforcing that the sum of predicted probabilities equals one, ensuring mutually exclusive and exhaustive labeling. Training data undergoes preprocessing, including top-hat filtering for illumination correction and resampling to a standard pixel size (e.g., 0.65 μm), and is augmented via 90-degree rotations to enhance generalization. The model is trained for 30 epochs with a batch size of 32 and an initial learning rate of 0.001, converging to a stable solution that accurately captures the diverse appearance of cells across experimental conditions.

### Single Channel Whole Cell Segmentation Workflow

The segmentation workflow begins with a single-channel sample image acquired using a non-nuclear cell marker—such as MitoTracker Red, TexasRed, Cy5, or dsRed—which stains cytoplasmic organelles and renders the cytoplasm hyperintense while the nucleus and background appear hypointense. The image is preprocessed and divided into overlapping patches, which are fed into the trained deep learning model to produce a nuclei probability map and a cell (cytoplasm) probability map. These patches are stitched into full-resolution probability maps. From the nuclei probability map, a binary nuclear mask is generated using multi-level Laplacian of Gaussian (LoG) blob detection to enhance nuclei across multiple scales, followed by multi-level Otsu thresholding to separate nuclei from background. To resolve touching nuclei, the inverse distance transform of the binary mask is computed, and an extended h-minima transform (with default h = 3 μm) suppresses shallow minima, yielding regional minima that serve as seeds for a seeded watershed transform, producing a nuclei seeds map with one seed per nucleus. Concurrently, the original image is transformed via Gaussian denoising, intensity scaling, and log conversion, then multiplied by the cell probability map to create an enhanced sample image that emphasizes cytoplasmic regions. Background is estimated using three-level Otsu thresholding guided by expected cell area. Finally, a seeded watershed segmentation is applied to the enhanced image, using the nuclei seeds and background labels to delineate individual cells. The result is a segmented image where each cell is uniquely identified. This approach eliminates the need for a nuclei-specific cell marker stain—defined as a fluorescent dye that selectively binds nuclear components such as DNA (e.g., DAPI, Hoechst)—and instead relies solely on non-nuclear cell marker stains, which target cytoplasmic structures like mitochondria, endosomes, or membranes. Examples include MitoTracker dyes, GFP-tagged FYVE domains, and proprietary cytoplasmic reagents. The technical advantages include maximizing spectral channel usage for multiplexed biomarker discovery, avoiding cytotoxicity in live-cell assays, and achieving robust segmentation across diverse experimental conditions, thereby solving long-standing challenges in single-channel whole cell analysis.

### Assessment of Segmentation Results

Following segmentation, a quality control assessment is performed to evaluate accuracy. A cell segmentation quality score is computed using a similarity metric that compares the automated segmentation to a reference (ground truth). This metric combines the average maximum Dice overlap between each reference cell and its best-matching segmented counterpart with the ratio of correctly matched one-to-one cell pairs, weighted by a factor k=0.6. The score provides both cell-level and image-level assessments, enabling identification of problematic images and overall performance monitoring.

### Examples

The invention was evaluated using a dataset of 123 images from five cellular assays in 96-well plates, featuring cell lines including HeLa, fibroblasts, HepG2, and U2OS. Fluorescent dyes included eGFP-FYVE (targeting endosomes), MitoTracker Red, and a proprietary mitochondrial dye from GE Cytiva. Images were acquired at 10× (0.65 μm/pixel) and 20× (0.325 μm/pixel) magnifications, all with 2048×2048 resolution. Ground truth segmentation was generated semi-automatically for 10 images by expert biologists using CellProfiler, involving manual correction of initial automated segmentations. For training, automated two-channel segmentations (using nuclear and cytoplasmic channels) served as proxy ground truth. Three experiments were conducted. Experiment 1 used 108 images (datasets 1–5): 98 for training/validation and 10 with semi-automated ground truth for testing. Segmentation achieved an average cell-level quality score of 0.87 against expert ground truth and 0.86 against two-channel segmentation. Experiment 2 employed 10-fold cross-validation across the 108 images, yielding an AUC >0.95 and accuracy of 0.915 (nuclei) and 0.878 (cells). Experiment 3 trained on datasets 1–5 and tested on an independent dataset 6 (15 images), achieving an average similarity score of 0.84. Results demonstrate that the single-channel method performs comparably to two-channel approaches while conserving imaging channels and avoiding nuclear stains, offering significant technological and practical improvements for high-content screening and biological discovery.
