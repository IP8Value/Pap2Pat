# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

The present invention resides in the field of computational virology, particularly in the development of software-based methods for the rapid and accurate identification of viral strains within highly diverse families of single-stranded RNA (ssRNA) viruses. Accurate strain classification is essential for epidemiological surveillance, outbreak response, vaccine design, and diagnostic development. Among ssRNA viruses, caliciviruses and picornaviruses represent two of the most genetically heterogeneous virus families, exhibiting extensive intra- and inter-species sequence variation that complicates traditional strain identification approaches.

Single-stranded RNA viruses are characterized by high mutation rates due to the error-prone nature of their RNA-dependent RNA polymerases, which lack proofreading capabilities. This results in a dynamic quasispecies population structure, enabling rapid adaptation to host immune pressures and environmental changes. The genetic diversity of these viruses manifests as numerous genogroups, species, serotypes, and strains, necessitating robust classification systems. Caliciviruses include four recognized genera—noroviruses, sapoviruses, lagoviruses, and vesiviruses—while picornaviruses encompass nine genera, including enteroviruses, rhinoviruses, and hepatoviruses. Within these genera, further subdivisions exist based on phylogenetic and antigenic criteria, creating a complex taxonomic landscape.

Distinguishing among closely related ssRNA virus strains presents significant challenges. Traditional immunoassays often fail due to cross-reactivity or the inability to culture many human caliciviruses, such as noroviruses, which precludes antigen production. Reverse transcription-polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR)-based assays, while sensitive, rely on primer specificity that may not capture emerging variants with mutations in primer-binding regions. Moreover, both methods struggle to resolve subtle differences between strains that share high sequence homology but differ in pathogenicity or transmissibility.

Homology-based sequence comparison methods have been widely used to infer evolutionary relationships and assign strains. These approaches typically calculate percent identity or similarity scores between query sequences and reference databases. However, such methods face critical limitations. First, defining universal sequence similarity cut-off values for strain delineation is problematic; what constitutes a distinct strain in one genus may not apply to another. Second, these cut-offs become unreliable when analyzing partial sequences, which are common in clinical and environmental samples. Third, homology scoring scales poorly with large datasets, as computational time increases exponentially with the number of sequences and their lengths, hindering real-time analysis during outbreaks.

Recent efforts to genotype ssRNA viruses have employed sliding window alignments or machine learning classifiers, but these remain sensitive to parameter selection (e.g., window size, gap penalties) and require extensive training data. Furthermore, they often treat sequences as linear strings without leveraging the hierarchical evolutionary structure inherent in viral phylogenies. This oversight reduces biological interpretability and limits the ability to detect recombination or convergent evolution.

Structural studies provide additional context: crystal structures of calicivirus and picornavirus capsids reveal that surface-exposed regions, particularly the VP1 subunit in picornaviruses and the sole capsid protein in caliciviruses, harbor key neutralizing epitopes. These regions exhibit the highest variability, making them ideal markers for strain differentiation. However, translating structural insights into automated classification tools has remained elusive.

Earlier work on noroviruses demonstrated that residue-wise comparisons at phylogenetically informative sites could overcome the limitations of global similarity metrics. Building on this foundation, the present invention introduces a novel computational framework that systematically partitions phylogenetic trees into evolutionary strata, identifies characteristic residues unique to each branch, and uses these residues as discriminative features for strain assignment. This approach enables reliable automation across diverse ssRNA virus families, addressing the urgent need for scalable, accurate, and biologically grounded strain identification in the era of genomic surveillance.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a computer-implemented software product, designated RECOVIR, for the automated identification of viral strains within the calicivirus and picornavirus families using amino acid sequences of viral capsid proteins. The core innovation lies in the use of phylogenetically derived, partition-specific characteristic residues—amino acid positions that are conserved within monophyletic groups but differ between them—as discriminative markers for strain classification.

The software operates by comparing an input target sequence, which may be complete or partial, against precomputed databases containing characteristic residues organized according to a partitioned phylogenetic framework. Each partition corresponds to a specific evolutionary depth within the phylogeny, allowing the method to first resolve broad genogroup assignments and then progressively refine to species, serotype, and strain levels. This hierarchical matching process mimics natural evolutionary divergence and ensures biologically meaningful classifications.

The invention further includes a computer program product comprising machine-readable instructions stored on a non-transitory medium, configured to execute the strain identification method. The program accepts FASTA-formatted amino acid sequences as input, automatically determines the appropriate viral genus if unknown, aligns the query to a reference sequence, and performs residue-wise comparisons across ten predefined phylogenetic partitions. Output includes the most probable strain assignment along with detailed match statistics per partition.

In summary, the invention overcomes the limitations of conventional homology-based and immunoassay methods by leveraging evolutionary trace principles to identify minimal sets of discriminative residues that unambiguously define viral lineages. This enables rapid, accurate, and automated strain prediction even from short or fragmented capsid sequences, significantly enhancing capabilities for viral surveillance and diagnostics.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

The present invention is embodied in a software product named RECOVIR, designed for the computational identification of strains of caliciviruses and picornaviruses based on amino acid sequences of their capsid proteins. The application of this software spans public health laboratories, research institutions, and diagnostic facilities where rapid and precise viral strain characterization is required for outbreak investigation, vaccine matching, or evolutionary studies.

An embodiment of the present invention comprises a Perl-based computational engine integrated with a Java graphical user interface (GUI), executable on Windows XP and Linux operating systems. The method implemented by the software involves constructing partitioned phylogenetic trees from reference capsid sequences, identifying characteristic residues that distinguish monophyletic groups within each partition, and using these residues to classify unknown target sequences through a tree-guided search algorithm.

### Capsid Embodiment of the Invention

The capsid embodiment of the invention focuses on the structural proteins that form the viral outer shell. For caliciviruses, the capsid consists of a single major protein, whereas picornaviruses possess four structural proteins (VP1–VP4), with VP1 being the primary immunodominant and phylogenetically informative subunit. Thus, the invention defines “capsid residues” as the amino acid positions within these proteins that exhibit lineage-specific conservation patterns.

Databases of capsid residues were created by first aligning reference sequences from each viral genus using ClustalW with optimized parameters. Phylogenetic trees were constructed using the Fitch-Margoliash distance matrix and neighbor-joining algorithms, validated for topological robustness across multiple tree-building methods. Each tree was divided into ten equally spaced partitions (P01–P10) based on evolutionary distance from the root, with each partition acting as a similarity filter that groups sequences by shared ancestry.

Residue-wise comparisons of target sequences against these databases begin by aligning the input sequence to a default reference. Starting from partition P2 (as P1 contains all sequences), the program compares the target’s residues at characteristic positions to those stored for each group in the partition. The group with the highest number of matches is selected, and subsequent partitions only evaluate groups directly descended from this choice, ensuring computational efficiency.

Characteristic residues are identified by comparing consensus sequences of sibling groups within each partition; positions conserved within a group but differing between groups are flagged as discriminative. This information is stored in two-dimensional arrays, forming genus-specific databases for caliciviruses and picornaviruses. Strain identification proceeds partition-by-partition: for example, in noroviruses, P2 distinguishes genogroups GI and GII via residues such as 70I/V and 203T/V, while later partitions resolve sub-clusters like GII.4 or GI.1.

Ambiguities—such as equal match counts across groups or no matches—are handled by deferring decision-making to subsequent partitions without flagging residues, preserving information for later resolution. The method also detects potential recombination by comparing strain assignments across different genomic regions; incongruent phylogenetic placements suggest recombination breakpoints. Spontaneous mutations are inferred when isolated residue changes contradict otherwise consistent phylogenetic signals.

Validation using over 300 calicivirus and picornavirus sequences—including complete and partial capsids of noroviruses, polioviruses, and coxsackieviruses—demonstrated >95% concordance with established classifications. Processing times average five seconds per sequence, enabling high-throughput analysis. The GUI supports batch input, automatic genus detection, and customizable output detail, making the tool accessible to non-bioinformaticians.

In practice, the program correctly identified a partial norovirus sequence as GII.3 despite NCBI annotation as GII.4, showcasing superior resolution. Similarly, poliovirus strains were accurately assigned to serotype level using only VP1 sequences. The system’s reliance on biologically validated, structurally relevant residues ensures robustness against sequencing errors or minor mutations, establishing RECOVIR as a reliable platform for ssRNA virus strain characterization.

## CONCLUSIONS

The RECOVIR software package represents a significant advancement in computational virology by providing a reliable, automated, and biologically grounded method for strain identification of highly divergent ssRNA viruses. By leveraging partitioned phylogenetic trees and residue-wise comparisons of capsid sequences, the invention overcomes the limitations of traditional homology-based and immunoassay techniques. Its ability to accurately classify both complete and partial sequences—even as short as 20% of the full capsid—enables effective use in real-world scenarios where sample quality is variable. The hierarchical, tree-guided search strategy ensures computational efficiency while maintaining high accuracy, with processing times averaging five seconds per sequence. Furthermore, the method’s foundation in evolutionary principles allows for the detection of recombination events and spontaneous mutations, offering insights beyond mere classification. Overall, RECOVIR provides a powerful, scalable solution for viral surveillance, diagnostics, and research in the face of rapidly evolving RNA virus threats.

## Availability and Requirements

The RECOVIR software is available for execution on Windows XP and Linux operating systems. The program is implemented in Perl for core functionality and Java for the graphical user interface. To run the software in a remote Linux environment, X-Windows support such as Cygwin is required. Input sequences must be provided in FASTA format, and the system imposes no limit on the number of input files or sequences per file. The software reads only alphabetic characters, ignoring whitespace and non-standard symbols. A project homepage is maintained for distribution and updates.
