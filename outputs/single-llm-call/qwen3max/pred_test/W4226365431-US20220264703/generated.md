# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

Temperature control is a critical parameter in numerous biomedical applications, where precise manipulation of thermal conditions at the cellular or tissue level can significantly influence physiological outcomes. In neuroscience, for instance, localized heating enables the activation of thermosensitive ion channels, thereby modulating neuronal activity without direct electrical stimulation. Similarly, in oncology, hyperthermia therapy exploits elevated temperatures—typically between 43°C and 45°C—to impair cancer cells’ DNA repair mechanisms while sparing surrounding healthy tissue. Additional applications include accelerated wound healing, temperature-triggered drug release systems, and bioanalytical techniques such as polymerase chain reaction (PCR) and temperature gradient focusing (TGF). These diverse uses underscore the necessity for technologies capable of delivering spatially confined, accurately regulated heat with minimal invasiveness.

Conventional approaches to localized heating have largely relied on ohmic (resistive) or dielectric mechanisms. Ohmic heating, though widely implemented in microheater designs, necessitates direct physical contact between the heating element and the biological sample, which limits its applicability in non-invasive or deep-tissue scenarios. Moreover, resistive elements generate heat indiscriminately along their entire surface, making fine-grained spatial control challenging. Dielectric heating, which exploits differences in permittivity between materials under high-frequency electric fields, suffers from poor specificity in biological contexts due to the high water content of most tissues, which results in minimal contrast in dielectric properties between target and non-target regions. Consequently, both modalities struggle to achieve the combination of high spatial resolution, deep penetration, and selective heating required for advanced biomedical interventions.

Magnetic nanoparticles (MNPs) offer a promising alternative by enabling magnetic loss-based heating, which leverages the interaction between an alternating magnetic field and magnetic nanomaterials embedded within or near the target tissue. Since biological tissues are inherently non-magnetic, this approach provides superior selectivity: only regions containing MNPs experience significant heating. However, existing MNP-based thermal applicators operating in the kilohertz to megahertz frequency range face two fundamental limitations. First, heating efficiency is low because magnetic power loss scales linearly with frequency; thus, achieving therapeutic temperatures requires high magnetic field intensities, typically generated by bulky, high-power (kilowatt-scale) benchtop instruments equipped with water-cooling systems. Second, spatial resolution is constrained by the large size of conventional magnetic coils (often 40–130 mm in diameter), which produce diffuse field distributions that cannot be finely tuned at the sub-millimeter scale. Furthermore, even when MNPs are delivered via intravenous injection—relying on the enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effect for tumor accumulation—they may still disperse into adjacent healthy tissues during circulation, exacerbating the need for precise thermal confinement. These challenges highlight the urgent need for an integrated, scalable, and highly localized magnetic heating platform that overcomes the inefficiencies and imprecision of current technologies.

## BRIEF SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention discloses an integrated microheater array device configured to generate localized heat through the ferromagnetic resonance of magnetic nanoparticles (MNPs) under microwave-frequency alternating magnetic fields. This device comprises a two-dimensional array of independently addressable pixels, each incorporating a stacked oscillator circuit coupled to an on-chip inductor and an electro-thermal feedback loop for real-time temperature regulation. The stacked oscillator topology enables the generation of high-voltage radiofrequency (RF) swings—exceeding 19 V peak-to-peak—at frequencies between 1.2 GHz and 2.6 GHz using a single inductor footprint, thereby producing strong localized magnetic fields sufficient to induce therapeutic heating in MNP-laden regions without requiring external amplifiers or bulky field generators.

The method of localized heat generation according to the invention involves disposing MNPs in proximity to the microheater array chip, applying a tunable alternating magnetic field at microwave frequencies via the on-chip inductors, and monitoring the resulting temperature rise through an integrated Proportional-To-Absolute-Temperature (PTAT) sensor array. The electro-thermal feedback loop dynamically adjusts the biasing voltage of a tail transistor in the stacked oscillator to maintain the local temperature at a user-defined setpoint, ensuring precise thermal control. By operating at gigahertz frequencies, the invention exploits the ferromagnetic resonance mechanism of MNPs, which offers significantly higher heating efficiency and sub-millimeter spatial resolution compared to conventional low-frequency approaches. This enables targeted thermal interventions in applications such as magnetogenetics, skin cancer hyperthermia therapy, and dose-controlled drug delivery, all while minimizing collateral thermal damage to surrounding tissues.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The integrated microheater array device of the present invention represents a fully monolithic solution for programmable, localized heating with closed-loop temperature regulation. The device is fabricated using standard complementary metal-oxide-semiconductor (CMOS) silicon-on-insulator (SOI) technology, enabling high integration density and compatibility with existing microelectronic systems. Each pixel in the array functions as an autonomous microheater unit, comprising a stacked oscillator, an on-chip spiral inductor, and an electro-thermal feedback control circuit. Similarly, references to singular or plural forms are intended to encompass both unless context dictates otherwise. Terms such as “approximately” and “substantially” denote values or characteristics within ±10% of the stated figure or condition, reflecting typical engineering tolerances in microfabrication and circuit operation.

The device achieves high heating efficiency and sub-millimeter spatial resolution by leveraging the ferromagnetic resonance of MNPs at microwave frequencies. Each pixel includes an array of magnetic nanoparticles disposed proximal to the chip surface, a stacked oscillator that generates a high-amplitude RF signal, and an electro-thermal feedback loop that monitors and regulates local temperature. The MNPs, typically composed of fatty-acid-coated magnetite (Fe₃O₄) nanoparticles with diameters around 30 nm, generate localized heat when subjected to an alternating magnetic field at their resonant frequency, which lies in the 1–3 GHz range. This heat generation is highly specific to MNP-containing regions, as biological tissues exhibit negligible magnetic loss at these frequencies.

The electro-thermal feedback loop continuously senses the local temperature via a PTAT sensor array positioned beneath the inductor ground plane and adjusts the output power of the stacked oscillator accordingly. The stacked oscillator employs multiple transistors connected in series to distribute voltage stress and achieve RF voltage swings far exceeding the supply voltage—up to 26.5 V peak-to-peak in five-stacked configurations—without violating device breakdown limits. This high swing directly enhances the magnetic field strength, as power loss scales with the square of the field intensity, thereby enabling efficient heating at lower power consumption.

The method of localized heating involves generating a programmable alternating magnetic field at microwave frequencies (1.2–2.6 GHz) using the on-chip inductors driven by the stacked oscillators. The spatial resolution is maintained at the sub-millimeter scale (∼0.03 mm² per pixel) due to the compact inductor geometry (5 turns, 51 μm inner radius) and the rapid decay of the magnetic field away from the coil surface. Penetration depth can be increased for deeper-tissue applications by lowering the operating frequency into the hundreds of megahertz range, where engineered MNPs still exhibit resonant absorption, albeit with a trade-off in spatial resolution.

Compared to conventional cross-coupled LC oscillators, the stacked oscillator provides significantly higher voltage swing within the same inductor area, eliminating the need for external RF amplifiers that would otherwise increase pixel size and power consumption. The voltage swing is precisely controlled by tuning the gate capacitances and biasing conditions of the stacked transistors, ensuring robust oscillation startup and high dc-to-RF efficiency (simulated at 45%). The circuit topology is scalable: increasing the number of stacked transistors proportionally increases the achievable RF swing, allowing customization for different thermal demands.

In operation, MNPs are applied to or injected near the target tissue and positioned proximal to the microheater array chip. An alternating magnetic field of tunable intensity and frequency is generated by selecting the appropriate pixel and configuring its oscillator frequency via a capacitor bank. The localized heat produced by the MNPs is monitored in real time by the PTAT sensors, and the feedback loop adjusts the tail transistor’s bias voltage to maintain the desired temperature. This closed-loop control enables applications such as minimally invasive brain stimulation in magnetogenetics, where fine manipulation of local temperature activates thermosensitive ion channels with cellular precision. In skin cancer hyperthermia therapy, the device triggers apoptosis in malignant cells by elevating tumor temperature to 43–45°C while keeping adjacent skin below 38°C. Heat patches designed using this technology offer non-invasive, wearable treatment options.

The microheater array configuration features a spiral inductor formed from the top two metal layers of the CMOS stack, with an inner radius of 51 μm and five turns, optimized via finite-element simulations to balance inductance (4.0 nH), quality factor (9.5 at 1.5 GHz), and thermal uniformity. The inductor shape is circular to maximize field concentration at the center. The stacked oscillator is coupled to a 4-bit binary-weighted capacitor bank that enables frequency tuning across three sub-ranges (1.2–1.6 GHz, 1.5–2.1 GHz, 2.0–2.6 GHz), accommodating MNPs with varying resonant frequencies. The capacitor bank is designed with stacked switches and gate resistors to prevent RF-induced breakdown.

The electro-thermal feedback loop includes a PTAT temperature sensor array with four diode pairs placed at the inductor corners, a 4-to-1 multiplexer, and two gain stages with coarse-fine digital-to-analog converters (DACs) to set reference voltages. A 1 pF capacitor stabilizes the tail bias node. The system operates in closed-loop, open-loop, or off modes, selectable via control switches. Fabricated devices demonstrate stable six-day operation, sub-millimeter heating resolution, and temperature regulation accuracy within ±0.53°C over 37–49°C. The MNP layer is integrated via spin-coated polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) membranes containing 3.25% wt MNPs, enabling straightforward packaging and thermal interfacing.

### Examples

The design of the microheater array was motivated by the limitations of existing heat generation mechanisms. Ohmic heating, while simple, requires direct contact and lacks spatial selectivity. Dielectric heating is ineffective in aqueous biological environments due to low permittivity contrast. Magnetic heating via MNPs emerged as the optimal solution, particularly when exploiting ferromagnetic resonance at GHz frequencies, which offers orders-of-magnitude higher power loss than Néel or Brownian relaxation at kHz–MHz frequencies.

Three frequency-dependent heating mechanisms govern MNP behavior: Néel relaxation (internal moment rotation), Brownian relaxation (physical particle rotation), and ferromagnetic resonance (precessional absorption at Larmor frequency). Only the latter is viable for high-efficiency, high-resolution heating at microwave frequencies. Multiphysics modeling coupled Maxwell’s equations with the heat transfer equation, using COMSOL to simulate electromagnetic and thermal domains simultaneously. The governing equations were solved numerically over discretized voxels representing silicon, SiO₂, metal layers, and MNP-PDMS.

Inductor geometry was optimized by simulating various inner radii and turn counts. A 5-turn, 51-μm-radius inductor yielded the best trade-off between field uniformity, inductance, and quality factor. Simulations showed that a 16 Vpp swing at 1.5 GHz raises MNP-laden regions to >43°C at 80 μm depth, while non-MNP regions remain <40°C. Transient analyses confirmed rapid thermal response.

The stacked oscillator was developed to overcome the 2×VDD swing limit of conventional oscillators. Its small-signal model revealed that loop gain exceeds unity with proper sizing, ensuring reliable startup. Large-signal load-pull optimization maximized dc-to-RF efficiency by co-designing transistor dimensions, bias points, and tank impedance. Four- and five-stacked variants achieved 19.5 Vpp and 26.5 Vpp swings, respectively, with 45% simulated efficiency.

Capacitor bank design balanced tuning range and quality factor by segmenting frequency coverage and using stacked switches with gate resistors to suppress parasitic turn-on. Electro-thermal closed-loop simulations demonstrated stable regulation to 45°C within 100 ms. Fabricated chips included SPI interfaces for control and on-chip bias generation. Measurements confirmed >19 Vpp swings, linear PTAT response (27 mV/°C), and closed-loop accuracy of ±0.29°C RMS.

Localized heating demonstrations used MNP-PDMS membranes spin-coated to 36 μm thickness. Infrared thermography showed heating exclusively above active inductors (∼0.03 mm²), with MNP regions reaching 47°C and control regions staying <37.8°C. Adjacent pixel activation verified sub-millimeter resolution with minimal crosstalk due to on-chip ground shielding. The platform is extensible to lower frequencies for deeper penetration, enabling broad biomedical utility.
