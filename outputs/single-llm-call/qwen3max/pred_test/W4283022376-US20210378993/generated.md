## TECHNICAL FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates generally to pharmaceutical compositions and therapeutic methods for the treatment of cystinosis, a rare inherited metabolic disorder characterized by the pathological accumulation of cystine within lysosomes of cells throughout the body. More specifically, the invention provides novel therapeutic agents—namely N-acetyl-L-cysteine amide (NACA) and (2R,2R′)-3,3′-disulfanediyl bis(2-acetamidopropanamide) (diNACA)—formulated for systemic or localized administration, particularly for the treatment of ophthalmic manifestations of cystinosis, including corneal cystine crystal deposition. The invention further encompasses pharmaceutical dosage forms, including ophthalmic inserts, eye drops, and oral formulations, designed to deliver effective concentrations of these agents to target tissues while minimizing systemic side effects and improving patient compliance over existing therapies such as cysteamine.

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

Cystinosis is a rare autosomal recessive lysosomal storage disease caused by mutations in the CTNS gene located on chromosome 17p13, which encodes the lysosomal cystine transporter cystinosin. This transporter is essential for the efflux of cystine from lysosomes; its dysfunction results in progressive intralysosomal accumulation of cystine crystals, leading to widespread cellular toxicity and multiorgan damage. The disease affects approximately 1 in 100,000 to 200,000 live births globally, with higher prevalence in certain populations due to founder effects. Cystinosis is associated with significant morbidity and mortality, particularly in its most severe form, and remains a life-limiting condition despite advances in supportive care.

Three clinical forms of cystinosis are recognized: infantile nephropathic, intermediate (juvenile), and non-nephropathic (ocular). Infantile nephropathic cystinosis, the most common and severe variant, typically presents within the first year of life with renal Fanconi syndrome—a generalized proximal tubular dysfunction manifesting as polyuria, polydipsia, dehydration, metabolic acidosis, hypophosphatemia, and rickets. Without intervention, affected children experience failure to thrive, progressive glomerular deterioration, and end-stage renal disease by age 10. Extrarenal complications include corneal cystine crystal deposition causing photophobia, myopathy, diabetes mellitus, hepatic dysfunction, and central nervous system involvement. Corneal crystals are detectable by slit-lamp examination as early as 6–12 months of age and are universally present by 16 months.

Diagnosis of cystinosis is confirmed by measuring elevated leukocyte cystine levels or through genetic testing identifying pathogenic variants in CTNS. Historically, life expectancy was limited to the first decade of life, but the advent of renal transplantation and cystine-depleting therapy has extended survival into the fourth or fifth decade. Current standard-of-care pharmacotherapy relies exclusively on cysteamine, an aminothiol that enters lysosomes and reacts with cystine to form a mixed disulfide (cysteine-cysteamine), which exits via an alternative transporter, thereby depleting lysosomal cystine stores.

Despite its efficacy in slowing disease progression, cysteamine therapy is burdened by significant limitations. Oral formulations—CYSTAGON® (immediate-release cysteamine bitartrate) and PROCYSBI® (delayed-release)—require dosing every 6–12 hours due to rapid metabolism and short half-life, leading to poor adherence, especially in pediatric populations. Gastrointestinal side effects (nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain), halitosis, and body odor further compromise tolerability. Critically, orally administered cysteamine does not achieve therapeutic concentrations in avascular tissues such as the cornea, rendering it ineffective against ocular cystinosis.

To address corneal involvement, topical ophthalmic formulations—CYSTARAN® and CYSTADROPS® (both containing cysteamine hydrochloride)—have been developed. However, these require frequent instillation (up to hourly during waking hours), cause ocular irritation, stinging, and redness, and exhibit suboptimal corneal penetration due to rapid tear clearance and limited residence time. Consequently, patient compliance remains low, and long-term visual outcomes are often unsatisfactory. Additional ocular complications in cystinosis include retinopathy, pigmentary disturbances of the peripheral fundus, and reduced electroretinogram responses, indicating broader retinal degeneration beyond corneal crystal deposition.

Although several patents describe modified cysteamine derivatives or delivery systems, none have overcome the fundamental pharmacokinetic and tolerability barriers. There remains a compelling unmet medical need for novel agents that effectively deplete cystine in both systemic and ocular compartments, possess favorable safety profiles, and enable convenient, sustained dosing regimens. The present invention addresses this gap by introducing NACA and diNACA—structurally distinct thiol-containing compounds with enhanced bioavailability, potent cystine-depleting activity, and intrinsic antioxidant properties—as superior alternatives for the treatment of all forms of cystinosis, particularly its ophthalmic manifestations.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a method for treating cystinosis in a subject in need thereof, comprising administering a therapeutically effective amount of N-acetyl-L-cysteine amide (NACA) or (2R,2R′)-3,3′-disulfanediyl bis(2-acetamidopropanamide) (diNACA), alone or in combination with a second active agent. NACA and diNACA demonstrate superior cystine-depleting activity compared to cysteamine in human cystinotic fibroblast cultures, with NACA exhibiting the most rapid and potent effect across multiple time points (6, 24, 48, and 72 hours). Moreover, both compounds enhance cell viability without cytotoxicity at clinically relevant concentrations (25–75 µM), suggesting a dual mechanism involving both cystine depletion and cytoprotection.

NACA is a lipophilic amide derivative of N-acetylcysteine (NAC), engineered to improve blood-brain barrier and cellular membrane permeability. It functions as a prodrug that is metabolized to NAC, thereby replenishing intracellular glutathione (GSH)—a critical endogenous antioxidant depleted in cystinosis due to chronic oxidative stress. DiNACA, the disulfide dimer of NACA, serves as a prodrug to both NACA and NAC, offering prolonged release and enhanced stability. Unlike cysteamine, neither compound produces offensive odors or significant gastrointestinal distress, and both show promise in penetrating ocular tissues.

Administration may be via oral, topical ophthalmic, intravitreal, or implantable routes. Dosage forms include tablets, capsules, ophthalmic solutions, gels, ointments, and bioerodible or nonbioerodible ocular inserts designed for sustained drug release over days to weeks. Effective doses range from 1 mg to 1000 mg per administration, adjusted based on route, formulation, and patient factors. Combination therapy with cysteamine or other antioxidants is also envisioned to potentiate efficacy or reduce required doses of each agent. The invention further includes methods for preventing or ameliorating oxidative stress-related ocular disorders, such as corneal cystine crystal accumulation, retinopathy, and cataract formation, by administering NACA or diNACA to subjects suffering from or at risk of cystinosis.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

N-acetylcysteine (NAC) is a well-known antioxidant and mucolytic agent, chemically designated as N-acetyl-L-cysteine, with the molecular formula C5H9NO3S. NACA, or N-acetyl-L-cysteine amide (NPI-001), is a derivative wherein the carboxylic acid group of NAC is replaced by a carboxamide, yielding enhanced lipophilicity and membrane permeability. Its chemical structure is C5H10N2O2S. DiNACA (NPI-002), formally (2R,2R′)-3,3′-disulfanediyl bis(2-acetamidopropanamide), is a symmetric disulfide formed from two NACA molecules, with the formula C10H18N4O4S2. Both compounds function as prodrugs: NACA is hydrolyzed intracellularly to NAC, while diNACA undergoes reduction to yield two equivalents of NACA, which may then be converted to NAC.

Glutathione (GSH), a tripeptide (γ-glutamyl-cysteinyl-glycine), is the principal intracellular antioxidant, essential for maintaining redox homeostasis. Its synthesis occurs in two ATP-dependent steps: first, glutamate and cysteine are ligated by glutamate-cysteine ligase (GCL), the rate-limiting enzyme regulated by feedback inhibition from GSH itself; second, glycine is added by GSH synthetase (GS). Cysteine availability is the primary determinant of GSH production. In cystinosis, impaired cystine export leads to secondary cysteine deficiency and GSH depletion, exacerbating oxidative damage. NACA and diNACA increase intracellular cysteine pools, thereby boosting GSH synthesis and mitigating oxidative stress.

Pharmaceutical compositions of the invention comprise NACA or diNACA in a pharmaceutically acceptable carrier, optionally with excipients such as buffers, preservatives, viscosity enhancers, and permeation promoters. Ophthalmic formulations may be solutions, suspensions, gels, or solid inserts. Bioerodible inserts, composed of polymers like polylactic-co-glycolic acid (PLGA) or hydroxypropyl methylcellulose (HPMC), gradually dissolve in the tear film, releasing drug over time. Nonbioerodible inserts, made from silicone or ethylene-vinyl acetate copolymers, provide controlled release without dissolution. Intravitreal implants may utilize polyethylene glycol (PEG)-based matrices to modulate release kinetics and improve biocompatibility.

An “effective amount” refers to a quantity sufficient to achieve a desired therapeutic response without undue toxicity. “Co-administration” means simultaneous or sequential delivery of two or more agents. “Oxidative stress-related ocular disorders” include conditions where reactive oxygen species contribute to pathology, such as cystinosis-associated corneal crystals, age-related macular degeneration, cataracts, and retinitis pigmentosa. “Subject” denotes a human or animal patient diagnosed with or suspected of having cystinosis. Dosing regimens may be once-daily to once-weekly, depending on formulation. Monitoring involves clinical assessment of symptoms (e.g., photophobia), slit-lamp examination for corneal crystals, and biochemical markers of oxidative stress. The invention also encompasses prophylactic use in genetically at-risk individuals to delay or prevent disease onset.

### Cytotoxicity of NACA and diNACA in Cystinotic Fibroblast Cell Culture

Human cystinotic fibroblasts were exposed to NACA or diNACA at concentrations of 25, 50, or 75 µM for 48 or 72 hours. Cell viability was assessed using the MTT assay, which measures mitochondrial reductase activity as an indicator of metabolic health. Neither compound exhibited cytotoxicity at any concentration or time point. Remarkably, both NACA and diNACA significantly increased cell viability relative to untreated controls, with maximal effects observed at 50–75 µM. In contrast, cysteamine showed viability enhancement only at higher concentrations. These findings confirm the safety and potential cytoprotective benefits of NACA and diNACA in cystinotic cells.

### Screening of Cystine-Depleting Activity of NACA and diNACA

Cystinotic fibroblasts were treated with 50 µM NACA, diNACA, or cysteamine for 6, 24, 48, or 72 hours. Intracellular cystine was quantified by converting it to cysteine via sodium borohydride reduction, followed by enzymatic detection using a papain-based chromogenic assay. NACA induced the most rapid and profound cystine depletion, achieving statistical significance at all time points and outperforming cysteamine at 6, 24, and 48 hours. DiNACA was significantly more effective than cysteamine at 6 and 48 hours. The rank order of potency was NACA > diNACA ≥ cysteamine, demonstrating the superior anticystinotic activity of the novel compounds.

### Ophthalmic Tolerability of 1% NPI-001, 1% diNACA, 1% NAC or vehicle

Prototype ophthalmic solutions containing 1% (w/v) NACA, diNACA, NAC, or vehicle were prepared in isotonic buffered saline. These formulations were instilled into the eyes of New Zealand white rabbits twice daily for seven days. Clinical observations included slit-lamp examination for corneal opacity, conjunctival redness, and discharge. All test articles were well tolerated, with no signs of irritation, inflammation, or structural damage, confirming the ocular safety of NACA and diNACA for topical use.

### NACA (NPI-001), diNACA (NPI-002), NAC or cysteamine ophthalmic insert.

Nonbioerodible inserts were fabricated from ethylene-vinyl acetate copolymer loaded with 10% drug, while bioerodible inserts used PLGA (50:50) with 20% drug loading. Inserts were sterilized and stored desiccated. In preclinical models, they were placed in the inferior cul-de-sac of rabbit eyes. Drug release was sustained over 7–14 days, with minimal local reaction. Inserts offer a practical solution to the frequent dosing burden of eye drops, enhancing compliance and therapeutic consistency.

### Example 5

In vitro release studies were conducted in simulated tear fluid (pH 7.4) at 34°C with sampling at predetermined intervals. Drug concentrations were analyzed by HPLC/MS/MS. Release rates were modulated by polymer composition and drug loading, enabling customization for clinical needs. Stability studies under ICH conditions (25°C/60% RH and 40°C/75% RH) demonstrated shelf lives exceeding 24 months. In vivo studies in rabbits confirmed sustained delivery to aqueous humor and cornea, with tissue concentrations exceeding those required for cystine depletion. A phase 2/3 clinical trial is planned to evaluate the safety and efficacy of AGENT inserts in cystinosis patients over three months, with endpoints including corneal crystal density, visual acuity, and patient-reported outcomes.
