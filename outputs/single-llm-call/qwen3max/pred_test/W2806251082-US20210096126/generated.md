# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

Radio frequency identification (RFID) technology has emerged as a transformative tool across numerous industries, offering robust solutions for personal identification, asset tracking, and environmental monitoring. Originally developed for military and logistics applications, RFID systems now permeate everyday life—from contactless payment cards and public transportation fare systems to inventory management in retail and supply chain operations. The core functionality of RFID relies on wireless communication between a reader device and a passive or active tag, which stores and transmits data without requiring an internal power source in the case of passive tags. These tags harvest energy from the interrogating radio waves emitted by the reader, enabling data exchange over short distances. A key advantage of RFID over traditional barcoding lies in its resilience: RFID tags demonstrate high tolerance to harsh environmental conditions, including exposure to solvents, extreme temperatures, and variable pressure regimes. This durability, coupled with decreasing manufacturing costs and increasing miniaturization, has expanded the applicability of RFID into biomedical domains, including implantable devices for animal and human identification, ingestible digital pills for medication adherence monitoring, and real-time physiological sensing.

Despite these advances, the integration of RFID technology into biological research—particularly within the context of three-dimensional (3D) tissue culture—remains underexplored and technically challenging. Conventional tissue culture methods rely heavily on manual labeling, optical microscopy, and molecular assays for sample tracking and phenotypic characterization. These approaches become increasingly impractical when scaling to high-throughput screens involving hundreds or thousands of individual samples, such as those derived from diverse human induced pluripotent stem cell (iPSC) lines. Moreover, current strategies lack a reliable means to uniquely identify and trace individual biological specimens—especially complex 3D structures like organoids—throughout dynamic experimental workflows that include cryopreservation, transplantation, drug treatment, and multi-parametric phenotyping. Prior attempts to incorporate micro-RFID chips into cellular systems have been limited to simple monolayer cultures or immortalized cell lines, such as phagocytic or melanoma cells, and have failed to demonstrate long-term biocompatibility or functional preservation of native tissue architecture. Critically, no existing methodology enables the seamless integration of RFID tags into self-organizing, multicellular organoids without disrupting their structural integrity, polarization, or physiological function. This gap represents a significant bottleneck in the advancement of precision medicine, population-scale disease modeling, and automated organoid-based drug discovery platforms, where scalable, non-invasive, and persistent sample identification is essential.

## BRIEF SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a novel and robust method for the digitalization of human organoids through the integration of ultracompact radio frequency identification (RFID) microchips, herein referred to as O-Chips, into organoid structures during their self-assembly process. This integration yields RFID-integrated organoids (RiOs), which retain full morphological and functional fidelity to native organoids while enabling wireless, unique, and persistent identification of each organoid throughout its lifecycle—including during culture, cryopreservation, thawing, transplantation, and high-throughput phenotypic screening. The invention leverages the natural cavitation and lumen-forming capacity of differentiating endodermal precursor cells to encapsulate the O-Chip within the organoid’s interior, thereby shielding the chip from external mechanical stress while preserving tissue polarity and function. The O-Chip, measuring approximately 460 × 480 micrometers and possessing a 512-bit memory capacity, operates wirelessly at distances up to 1–2 millimeters and withstands standard laboratory conditions, including autoclaving, solvent exposure, paraffin embedding, and cryogenic storage in liquid nitrogen. The invention further encompasses a specialized detection device capable of simultaneously capturing fluorescence-based phenotypic data and reading the RFID signal from RiOs in a flow-through configuration, enabling high-throughput, donor-specific phenotyping in pooled organoid assays. By enabling the pooling of organoids from multiple genetic backgrounds in a single culture vessel and subsequently deconvoluting individual donor identities via RFID, the invention establishes a “forward cellomics” platform for scalable, cost-effective, and genotype-phenotype correlation studies in human disease modeling and drug development.

The terms “individual,” “host,” “subject,” and “patient” are used interchangeably and refer to any human or non-human animal, including mammals such as primates, rodents, livestock, and companion animals, to whom the methods or compositions of the invention may be applied. A “precursor cell” denotes a cell committed to a specific lineage but not yet fully differentiated, such as definitive endoderm cells capable of giving rise to hepatic, pancreatic, intestinal, or pulmonary epithelia.

RFID technology, as employed in the present invention, refers to a wireless system comprising a reader/writer device and a passive microchip tag that stores digital information and communicates via electromagnetic fields. RFID applications span logistics, security, healthcare, and consumer electronics, with recent innovations enabling miniaturization to sub-millimeter scales. In healthcare, RFID has been used for patient identification, medication tracking, and ingestible sensors. Human organoids are three-dimensional, self-organizing structures derived from stem cells that recapitulate key architectural and functional aspects of native organs. Organoid features include apical-basal polarity, lumen formation, basement membrane deposition, and expression of organ-specific markers. Potential organoid applications include disease modeling, drug toxicity testing, regenerative medicine, and personalized therapy prediction.

Digitized organoids, or RiOs, are organoids incorporating an O-Chip, thereby acquiring a machine-readable digital identity. Digitized organoid features include wireless identifiability, compatibility with standard culture and cryopreservation protocols, and retention of native biological functions. A pooled organoid composition refers to a mixture of RiOs derived from two or more genetically distinct donors, cultured together in a shared environment to enable comparative phenotyping under identical conditions. Detectable sensor forms encompass the O-Chip and any associated antenna or memory module enabling wireless signal transmission. Biological parameters include measurable physiological or molecular traits such as albumin secretion, bile acid transport, lipid accumulation, gene expression profiles, and drug response kinetics. An identifier unique to a donor is encoded in the O-Chip’s memory as an Electronic Product Code (EPC) or similar digital token, allowing unambiguous attribution of phenotypic data to a specific genetic source.

Organoid types contemplated by the invention include esophageal, stomach, small intestinal, colon, hepatic, liver, bile duct, pancreatic, lung, and kidney organoids, all derived from definitive endoderm or other lineage-committed precursors. The method of making an RFID-integrated organoid involves contacting definitive endoderm cells with a micro-RFID chip under conditions that promote co-aggregation. This co-aggregation leads to the physical entrapment of the chip within the nascent organoid structure. During subsequent maturation, the organoid develops a central lumen, with the O-Chip residing within this cavity, shielded by polarized epithelial layers. Spheroids are derived from precursor cells through forced aggregation in low-adhesion plates, followed by embedding in laminin-rich extracellular matrix to induce polarization.

Specific organoid formation protocols are tailored to lineage: esophageal organoids are generated using dual-SMAD inhibition and Wnt activation; stomach organoids employ FGF10 and retinoic acid; small intestinal organoids require Wnt3a, R-spondin, Noggin, and EGF; colon organoids use similar factors with added BMP inhibition; hepatic and liver organoids are induced via Activin A followed by FGF and BMP signaling; bile duct organoids utilize TGFβ and Notch activation; pancreatic organoids depend on retinoic acid, FGF10, and cyclopamine; lung organoids use dual-SMAD inhibition, FGF, and Wnt; and kidney organoids involve CHIR99021 and FGF9.

The FGF signaling pathway is critical for endodermal patterning and organ specification. FGF pathway activators include recombinant FGF proteins (e.g., FGF2, FGF4, FGF10) and small molecules that potentiate receptor activity. Treatment with these activators occurs at defined concentrations—typically 10–100 ng/mL—in serum-free media supplemented with insulin, transferrin, selenium, and other basal components. Wnt signaling pathway activators, such as CHIR99021 (a GSK3 inhibitor), are used at 2–6 μM to stabilize β-catenin. BMP activators like BMP4 (10–50 ng/mL) promote hepatic fate. ROCK inhibitors (e.g., Y-27632, 10 μM) enhance survival of dissociated cells during aggregation.

Organoid characterization involves immunofluorescence for lineage markers (e.g., ALB, HNF4A), RT-qPCR for gene expression, ELISA for secreted proteins, and functional assays such as Rhodamine123/CLF transport. RiO features include >95% incorporation efficiency, preserved morphology, and equivalent function to non-tagged controls. Precursor cells from multiple individuals enable population-scale studies. Screening methods involve exposing pooled RiOs to stimuli (e.g., fatty acids) and correlating RFID-identified donors with phenotypic readouts (e.g., steatosis levels), thereby linking genotype to phenotype.

### Examples

RFID technology offers a scalable solution for sample tracking in biological research. The digitalization of biological tissues addresses the growing need for automation and traceability in organoid-based platforms. The development of RiOs began with the integration of commercially available O-Chips into iPSC-derived organoids. The O-Chip, measuring 460 × 480 μm with 512-bit memory, was designed for organoid-scale compatibility. Integration was achieved by co-plating dissociated definitive endoderm spheroids with O-Chips in 96-well plates, followed by laminin-rich matrix embedding to drive self-assembly. Testing confirmed successful internalization without structural disruption.

RiOs were generated from eight donor-derived iPSC lines with >95% efficiency. Morphological comparison with control liver organoids (LO) revealed no abnormalities. Liver-specific gene expression (ALB, AFP, AAT) and albumin secretion (152.9 ng/mL, 1.02-fold of control) were equivalent. Whole-mount immunofluorescence confirmed ALB+ and HNF4A+ cells. Functional assays demonstrated intact bile transport via CLF and Rhodamine123 uptake into lumens. Lipid and iron accumulation assays further validated metabolic competence.

O-Chip durability was tested across conditions: it remained functional after autoclaving, paraffin embedding, pH shifts, and cryogenic storage (−196°C). Cryopreservation of RiOs using slow-freezing preserved morphology and function, including post-thaw steatosis induction. A custom device was developed for simultaneous RFID and fluorescence detection, featuring a syringe pump, flow path, RFID probe, and microscope. This enabled high-throughput phenotyping of BODIPY-stained RiOs in flow.

Human iPSC libraries offer unprecedented opportunities for precision medicine, but manual comparison of lines is inefficient. The RiO approach enables pooled screening with post-hoc donor identification. In a proof-of-principle study, seven iPSC lines—including two Wolman disease donors—were converted to RiOs, pooled, and exposed to fatty acids. Fluorescence imaging revealed elevated lipid accumulation in specific RiOs, which RFID decoding identified as Wolman-derived, confirming disease-specific steatosis. This demonstrates a forward cellomics strategy: phenotype-first screening followed by genetic attribution.

Applications include maintaining PSCs in mTeSR1, inducing definitive endoderm with Activin A, generating RiOs via O-Chip co-aggregation, measuring albumin by ELISA, performing whole-mount immunofluorescence, isolating RNA for RT-qPCR, conducting CLF/Rhodamine123 transport assays, live imaging of BODIPY-labeled lipids, and cryopreserving/thawing RiOs. Culture conditions use E8 medium for iPSCs; differentiation media contain RPMI/B27 with growth factors; hepatocyte media include HCM supplements. Statistical significance is assessed by ANOVA or t-tests. Reagents are sourced from commercial vendors (e.g., STEMCELL Technologies, Thermo Fisher), and equipment includes Synergy plate readers and custom RFID-fluorescence rigs.
