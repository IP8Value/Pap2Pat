# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNICAL FIELD

The present invention relates generally to boiling systems, and more particularly to actively tunable boiling systems that enable dynamic control over bubble nucleation, heat transfer performance, and critical heat flux through the application of electric potentials in the presence of charged surfactants. The invention provides a method and apparatus for selectively modulating the wettability of a boiling surface by electrostatically adsorbing or desorbing ionic surfactant molecules, thereby enabling precise spatial and temporal control over the boiling process. This technology is applicable to a wide range of thermal management systems, including but not limited to power generation, electronics cooling, microfluidic devices, and industrial heat exchangers, where adaptive and responsive boiling behavior is advantageous.

## BACKGROUND ART

Boiling is a fundamental phase-change heat transfer mechanism widely employed in engineering applications due to its high efficiency in dissipating large amounts of thermal energy. Extensive research has been conducted to understand and enhance boiling performance, with particular focus on nucleate boiling—the regime characterized by the formation, growth, and departure of vapor bubbles from a heated surface. A key factor governing nucleation is the wettability of the surface, which influences the ease with which vapor bubbles can form and detach. Wettability is typically quantified by the contact angle between the liquid and solid surface; lower contact angles (hydrophilic surfaces) generally suppress nucleation, while higher contact angles (hydrophobic surfaces) promote it.

The boiling curve, which plots heat flux against surface superheat (the temperature difference between the heated surface and the saturation temperature of the liquid), is a standard tool for characterizing boiling performance. The slope of this curve defines the heat transfer coefficient (HTC), which reflects the efficiency of heat removal. Traditional approaches to improving boiling performance have focused on static surface modifications, such as altering surface roughness, introducing micro- or nanostructures, or applying permanent hydrophobic or hydrophilic coatings. These methods, while effective in certain regimes, are inherently inflexible—they cannot adapt to changing thermal loads or operational requirements.

Surface roughness and wettability modifications have been shown to influence both the onset of nucleate boiling and the critical heat flux (CHF)—the maximum heat flux sustainable before the surface becomes blanketed by a vapor film, leading to a dangerous temperature rise. However, once fabricated, these surfaces exhibit fixed boiling characteristics. This limitation is particularly problematic in dynamic environments, such as transient power demands in data centers or variable-load power plants, where optimal boiling performance may require real-time adjustment of nucleation behavior.

Electrohydrodynamic (EHD) techniques and electrowetting have previously been explored for active control of fluid behavior. Electrowetting alters wettability by applying voltage to reduce the contact angle of conductive liquids on dielectric-coated electrodes. However, electrowetting typically requires insulating layers and operates under different physical principles than those governing boiling nucleation. Moreover, EHD boiling often involves high voltages that induce fluid motion through Coulomb forces, which can be energy-intensive and may lead to electrolysis or electrode degradation. These limitations underscore the need for a new class of boiling systems capable of tunable, reversible, and low-energy control over bubble nucleation without relying on complex surface architectures or high-voltage actuation.

## SUMMARY OF EMBODIMENTS

The present invention introduces a tunable boiling system that enables active, reversible, and rapid control over bubble nucleation and heat transfer performance through the application of low electric potentials in the presence of charged surfactants. The system comprises a conductive boiling surface serving as a working electrode, a counter electrode immersed in the boiling fluid, and a power supply configured to apply a controllable voltage between the two electrodes. The boiling fluid contains a low concentration of ionic surfactant molecules, which adsorb onto or desorb from the boiling surface in response to the applied electric potential, thereby modulating surface wettability and nucleation activity.

In one embodiment, the method of selectively boiling a fluid includes providing a boiling surface in contact with a liquid containing charged surfactant molecules, applying a first electric potential to promote adsorption of the surfactants and enhance nucleation, and subsequently applying a second electric potential to induce desorption and suppress nucleation. The transition between nucleation states occurs within sub-second timescales, enabling dynamic thermal management.

In another embodiment, the method of forming a tunable boiling system includes selecting a conductive material for the boiling surface, integrating a counter electrode into the fluid reservoir, and introducing a surfactant solution at a concentration below the critical micelle concentration to avoid bulk property changes. The system is designed to operate within a capacitive voltage window to prevent Faradaic reactions, ensuring long-term stability and reversibility.

Key features of the system include its ability to shift the boiling curve laterally—increasing or decreasing superheat for a given heat flux—modulate the heat transfer coefficient by up to 1,100%, and adjust the critical heat flux based on operational needs. The system operates at low voltages (≤2 V), consumes minimal current, and does not require microstructured surfaces, making it compatible with existing thermal hardware and scalable for industrial deployment.

## DESCRIPTION OF ILLUSTRATIVE EMBODIMENTS

The tunable boiling system is configured with a conductive boiling surface that functions as a working electrode, typically fabricated from metals such as silver, gold, copper, or platinum. A counter electrode, constructed from a high-surface-area conductive material such as titanium mesh, is immersed in the boiling fluid to complete the electrochemical cell. The fluid consists of a base liquid—such as deionized water—containing a low concentration (e.g., 2.6 mM) of ionic surfactant, such as sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) or dodecyl trimethylammonium bromide (DTAB). The concentration is maintained below the critical micelle concentration to ensure that bulk thermophysical properties remain unchanged, and only interfacial phenomena are affected.

Application of a voltage between the working and counter electrodes (Vcell = Vsurf − Vcounter) induces electrostatic adsorption or desorption of charged surfactant molecules at the solid–liquid interface. For negatively charged surfactants like SDS, a more negative surface potential repels the anions, reducing surface coverage and increasing wettability, thereby suppressing nucleation. Conversely, a less negative potential attracts SDS, enhancing hydrophobicity and promoting nucleation. The opposite effect is observed for positively charged surfactants such as DTAB. This mechanism directly links electric field control to surface tension and interfacial energy, as surfactant adsorption lowers the effective surface energy, facilitating vapor embryo formation.

Electrochemical considerations dictate that the applied voltage must remain within a capacitive charging regime to avoid redox reactions, electrolysis, or electrode degradation. Cyclic voltammetry is used to identify a safe operating window—typically between −0.1 V and −2.0 V versus a reference—where the current response remains capacitive, with steady-state currents below 60 μA cm⁻². Under these conditions, the system behaves as an electrical double-layer capacitor, enabling fully reversible operation over extended periods.

Surfactant adsorption is critical to the functionality of the system. Only ionic surfactants exhibit voltage-dependent nucleation control; nonionic surfactants (e.g., MEGA-10) and simple salts (e.g., NaBr) show no response, confirming that the mechanism relies on electrostatic interactions rather than bulk conductivity or electrohydrodynamic effects. Contact angle measurements under boiling conditions corroborate this: advancing contact angles increase with surfactant adsorption and decrease upon desorption, demonstrating direct modulation of wettability.

The system enables both spatial and temporal control of boiling. Temporal control is achieved by switching the applied voltage in square or triangle waveforms, resulting in rapid (<600 ms) transitions between nucleation states. Spatial control is implemented using patterned electrode arrays, where individual electrodes are independently addressed to activate or suppress boiling in specific regions. For example, an eight-electrode gold array on a silicon substrate, each backed by a resistive heater, allows millimeter-scale patterning of bubble activity under uniform heating.

Materials and solvents are selected based on compatibility with the capacitive regime and thermal stability. While silver and gold were used in experimental demonstrations, the phenomenon is material-independent as long as the surface is conductive and stable under boiling conditions. Fluorocarbon-based surfactants may be employed for high-temperature applications near the critical point of water, offering thermal stability up to 600 °C.

Advantages of the tunable boiling system include its simplicity, low energy consumption, reversibility, and compatibility with existing thermal systems. Boiling curves demonstrate that the heat transfer coefficient can be tuned over an order of magnitude, and superheat can be varied by more than 2 °C at constant heat flux. The ability to shift the boiling curve left or right—and even cross over—enables real-time optimization: for instance, using a high-nucleation state at low heat fluxes and a high-CHF state at elevated loads.

Potential applications span electronics cooling, where transient heat loads demand adaptive thermal response; power generation, where boiler efficiency can be modulated; and microfluidics, where bubble actuators can be precisely controlled. The system configuration includes a heater block, temperature sensors (e.g., embedded thermocouples), a voltage control circuit with PID feedback, and a condenser for closed-loop operation. Experimental setups validate the mechanism through high-speed imaging, thermal measurements, and electrochemical characterization, all confirming that surfactant adsorption governs nucleation behavior.

## EXAMPLES

An experimental setup was constructed to demonstrate active control of boiling using charged surfactants. A silver foil boiling surface (2 × 2 cm) was soldered to a copper heating block equipped with cartridge heaters and four embedded thermocouples for temperature gradient measurement. The surface was enclosed in a glass chamber filled with 400 mL of deionized water containing 2.6 mM surfactant. A titanium mesh counter electrode surrounded the chamber, and a reflux condenser maintained saturated conditions. Voltage was applied via an op-amp circuit with real-time feedback to maintain precise Vcell.

Surfactant solutions included 2.6 mM SDS (anionic), DTAB (cationic), NaBr (non-surfactant salt), and MEGA-10 (nonionic surfactant). All were prepared by diluting 173 mM stock solutions. Concentrations were kept below the critical micelle concentration to isolate interfacial effects.

Boiling curves were obtained by quasi-statically varying heater power while maintaining constant Vcell. For NaBr and MEGA-10, boiling curves at −0.1 V and −2.0 V overlapped completely, confirming no voltage response. In contrast, SDS showed a rightward shift at −2.0 V (increased wettability, suppressed nucleation), while DTAB exhibited a leftward shift (decreased wettability, enhanced nucleation). At 8.7 °C superheat, SDS’s HTC increased from 3.7 to 41 W cm⁻²—a 1,000% enhancement—when switching from −2.0 V to −0.1 V. Similarly, DTAB’s HTC rose from 1.6 to 20 W cm⁻² at 7 °C superheat under the same voltage change, representing a 1,100% increase.

High-speed photographs captured bubble nucleation ceasing within 600 ms of applying −2.0 V to SDS and resuming within 300 ms upon returning to −0.1 V. The asymmetry in response times is attributed to nucleation hysteresis.

Square wave voltage experiments (60 s period, −0.1 V to −2.0 V) revealed out-of-phase thermal responses: for SDS, temperature increased when Vcell became more negative (less nucleation, more convective heating), while HTC decreased in phase with potential. DTAB showed the opposite behavior, confirming charge-dependent adsorption.

Thermal response to voltage changes was quantified using embedded thermocouples. The system exhibited no degradation over 10+ hours of continuous operation, and behavior remained consistent across weeks, indicating robustness.

A spatially controlled boiling system was fabricated on a silicon wafer with eight 1-cm-wide gold electrodes, each backed by a platinum thin-film heater. With 2.6 mM DTAB and uniform heating (~1.5 W cm⁻²), electrodes at −2.0 V (“on”) exhibited vigorous nucleation, while those at −0.1 V (“off”) remained bubble-free. High-speed imaging confirmed sub-second switching and millimeter-scale spatial resolution.

Voltage control was achieved using dual DAC channels and SPDT switches, allowing independent addressing. Photographs showed clear demarcation between active and inactive zones, demonstrating programmable boiling patterns.

These results confirm that boiling can be turned “on and off” with high precision using low-voltage electrostatic control of surfactant adsorption. The significance lies in the creation of the first truly tunable boiling platform—capable of real-time adaptation without mechanical or structural changes. This represents a paradigm shift from static to dynamic thermal surfaces, opening avenues for intelligent heat transfer systems. In conclusion, the examples validate the core mechanism, demonstrate performance metrics, and illustrate practical implementations of the invention.
