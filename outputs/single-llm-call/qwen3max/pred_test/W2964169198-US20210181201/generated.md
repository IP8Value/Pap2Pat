# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNICAL FIELD

The present invention pertains to the field of extracellular vesicle (EV) research and diagnostics, specifically to the development and application of recombinant extracellular vesicles (rEV) as biological reference materials. The invention provides compositions and methods for the production, characterization, detection, and use of rEV that mimic the physical and biochemical properties of endogenous EV found in biofluids such as plasma, serum, urine, and conditioned cell culture media. These rEV are engineered to contain heterologous markers—such as fluorescent proteins, nucleic acids, or epitope-tagged viral structural proteins—that enable their unambiguous identification and quantification against a background of native EV. The invention further encompasses methods for calibrating analytical instruments, evaluating and optimizing EV isolation protocols, normalizing inter-user and intra-assay variability, and accurately enumerating EV in clinical samples using rEV as internal standards. The technical domain thus spans biotechnology, molecular diagnostics, nanomedicine, and clinical laboratory science, with particular relevance to liquid biopsy development and standardization in oncology and other disease areas where EV serve as biomarkers.

## BACKGROUND

Liquid biopsies represent a minimally invasive approach to disease diagnosis, prognosis, and monitoring by analyzing biomarkers present in easily accessible biofluids such as blood, urine, or saliva. Among the most promising analytes in liquid biopsies are extracellular vesicles (EV), which are nano-sized, membrane-bound particles naturally released by virtually all cell types into the extracellular space and circulation. EV carry a molecular cargo—including proteins, lipids, RNA, and DNA—that reflects the physiological or pathological state of their cell of origin, making them valuable sources of diagnostic information, particularly in cancer, neurodegenerative disorders, and autoimmune diseases. However, despite their potential, the clinical translation of EV-based diagnostics has been significantly hindered by technical challenges related to their isolation, quantification, and characterization.

Extracellular vesicles are heterogeneous in size, ranging from approximately 30 to over 1000 nanometers in diameter, and differ in biogenesis pathways; exosomes originate from multivesicular bodies and are typically 50–150 nm, while microvesicles bud directly from the plasma membrane and are generally larger. Structurally, EV consist of a phospholipid bilayer enriched in cholesterol, sphingomyelin, and phosphatidylserine, enclosing a lumen that contains cytosolic components. Their surface displays transmembrane and lipid-anchored proteins, including tetraspanins (e.g., CD9, CD63, CD81), integrins, and MHC molecules, which mediate intercellular communication and target recognition. In cancer, tumor-derived EV contribute to metastasis, immune evasion, and drug resistance, and their abundance and molecular profile often correlate with disease stage and outcome.

Despite extensive research, EV analysis remains plagued by methodological inconsistencies. Numerous isolation techniques exist—including differential ultracentrifugation (dUC), size exclusion chromatography (SEC), immunoaffinity capture, and polymer-based precipitation—each with distinct biases, recovery efficiencies, and co-isolation of contaminants such as lipoproteins or protein aggregates. Similarly, quantification methods like nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA), flow cytometry, and protein assays yield variable results due to differences in instrument calibration, detection thresholds, and assumptions about particle refractive index (RI). Critically, the lack of standardized, biologically relevant reference materials prevents accurate calibration, normalization across experiments, and objective comparison of method performance.

Current reference standards include synthetic nanoparticles such as polystyrene or silica beads, which fail to replicate key EV characteristics: they possess higher RIs (~1.59–1.61) than EV (~1.37–1.39), lack lipid bilayers and surface proteins, and do not behave commutably in complex biofluids. Biological reference materials derived from cell lines or human fluids are available but suffer from batch-to-batch variability, undefined composition, and inability to be distinguished from sample EV. Consequently, there exists an urgent and unmet need for a trackable, stable, biologically authentic reference material that mimics endogenous EV in structure, density, size, surface composition, and behavior during isolation and analysis—thereby enabling rigorous quality control, method validation, and reliable clinical interpretation of EV data.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention introduces recombinant extracellular vesicles (rEV) as a novel class of biological reference material specifically designed to address the limitations of existing standards in EV research and diagnostics. rEV are produced by transient expression in mammalian cells of a self-assembling protein derived from the HIV-1 gag polyprotein, optionally fused to a heterologous marker such as enhanced green fluorescent protein (EGFP). Upon expression, the gag fusion protein traffics to the plasma membrane via its N-terminal myristoylated matrix (MA) domain, oligomerizes, recruits components of the endosomal sorting complex required for transport (ESCRT) machinery through its p6 domain, and drives the outward budding and scission of membrane-enveloped nanoparticles that closely resemble endogenous EV in morphology, size, density, lipid composition, and protein content.

The composition of rEV comprises two essential elements: a self-assembling protein scaffold and a heterologous marker. The self-assembling protein is preferably the full-length HIV-1 gag polyprotein or a functional derivative thereof capable of inducing particle formation without requiring other viral components. This protein serves as the structural backbone of rEV, ensuring correct assembly, membrane curvature, and incorporation of host-derived lipids and proteins. The heterologous marker is a detectable moiety—such as a fluorescent protein (e.g., EGFP), an epitope tag (e.g., p24 antigen), or a unique nucleic acid sequence (e.g., EGFP mRNA)—that is genetically fused to or co-packaged with the self-assembling protein, thereby enabling specific identification and quantification of rEV distinct from endogenous sample EV.

rEV exhibit physical and biochemical characteristics that are highly congruent with those of endogenous EV. They display a cup-shaped morphology under transmission electron microscopy, a hydrodynamic diameter of approximately 100–120 nm as measured by nanoparticle tracking analysis, a buoyant density of 1.086–1.119 g/mL in iodixanol gradients, a zeta potential of around −32 mV, and a refractive index of ~1.37—values that fall within the typical range reported for exosomes and small EV. Proteomic and lipidomic analyses confirm that rEV incorporate canonical EV-associated proteins (e.g., ALIX, TSG101, flotillin-1, syntenin-1, CD9, CD81, CD63) and lipid classes (e.g., phosphatidylcholine, sphingomyelin, cholesterol), demonstrating that they faithfully recapitulate the molecular landscape of native EV despite their recombinant origin.

In contrast to endogenous EV, which are collectively referred to herein as “sample extracellular vesicles” or “sample EV,” rEV are defined by their engineered origin and detectable marker. “Endogenous EV” denotes EV naturally present in a biofluid or cell culture supernatant prior to any experimental manipulation. The term “heterologous marker” refers to any exogenous molecule introduced into rEV that is absent from sample EV and enables discrimination, including but not limited to fluorescent proteins, enzymatic reporters, epitope tags, or unique RNA sequences. Examples of suitable heterologous markers include EGFP, mCherry, luciferase, the HIV-1 p24 capsid protein, or synthetic mRNA encoding a reporter gene.

The invention provides a method for determining the recovery rate of sample EV during an isolation procedure by spiking a known quantity of rEV into a biofluid sample prior to processing. The method comprises the steps of: (a) mixing a predetermined amount of rEV with a biofluid sample containing endogenous EV; (b) subjecting the mixture to an EV isolation protocol (e.g., ultracentrifugation, SEC, immunoprecipitation); (c) detecting the amount of rEV recovered post-isolation using a technique specific to the heterologous marker; and (d) calculating the recovery rate as the ratio of detected rEV to spiked rEV, expressed as a percentage. This recovery rate can then be used to normalize the measured quantity of sample EV, correcting for procedural losses.

Detection may be achieved through various modalities depending on the nature of the heterologous marker. When the marker is a heterologous nucleic acid molecule such as EGFP mRNA, detection involves RNA extraction followed by reverse transcription quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR). If the marker is a protein, detection may employ immunoassays such as ELISA (e.g., anti-p24 ELISA), Western blotting, or high-resolution flow cytometry using fluorescently labeled antibodies. In preferred embodiments, the heterologous marker is a light-emitting protein such as EGFP, enabling direct quantification by fluorescent nanoparticle tracking analysis (fNTA) or fluorescence-triggered flow cytometry without the need for labeling or amplification.

The self-assembling protein is defined as a polypeptide capable of spontaneous oligomerization and membrane deformation to form a closed vesicular structure. Exemplary self-assembling proteins include retroviral gag polyproteins (e.g., from HIV-1, MLV, or RSV), bacterial encapsulins, or engineered protein cages. In a preferred embodiment, the HIV-1 gag polyprotein is used due to its well-characterized ability to form virus-like particles (VLPs) in the absence of other viral components and its compatibility with mammalian expression systems.

Biofluid samples encompass any liquid derived from a living organism that may contain EV, including but not limited to plasma, serum, urine, cerebrospinal fluid, saliva, breast milk, ascites, and conditioned cell culture media. Plasma and serum are particularly relevant for clinical applications, and the invention includes methods wherein rEV are spiked into these matrices to assess isolation efficiency or normalize patient EV counts.

The isolation step may employ any conventional or emerging EV separation technique. Density-based separation, such as OptiPrep density gradient (ODG) ultracentrifugation, is especially compatible with rEV due to their matching buoyant density with sample EV. In such protocols, rEV and sample EV co-fractionate in density ranges of 1.086–1.119 g/mL, and detection of rEV in these fractions confirms proper isolation. Size exclusion chromatography (SEC) is another preferred method, where rEV elute in the same early fractions (e.g., 4–6) as sample EV, allowing concurrent recovery assessment.

Additional biophysical isolation steps may be incorporated to enhance purity or enable segregation of rEV from sample EV. For instance, post-insertion PEGylation of rEV using lipid-conjugated polyethylene glycol (e.g., DMPE-PEG) masks surface epitopes, prevents protein corona formation in plasma, and allows specific immunocapture of rEV-PEG using anti-PEG antibodies after isolation, thereby facilitating downstream analysis of unmodified sample EV. Protease treatment (e.g., with proteinase K) may be applied to remove adsorbed proteins that cause artifactual density shifts in plasma, restoring rEV to their native buoyancy.

The invention further provides a method to calibrate devices used for EV analysis. Calibration herein means adjusting or validating instrument settings to ensure accurate measurement of EV properties such as size, concentration, or fluorescence. The method involves: (a) mixing rEV with a fluid (e.g., PBS or plasma); (b) introducing the mixture into the device; (c) analyzing a property of the rEV (e.g., particle count, size distribution, fluorescence intensity); and (d) deriving device settings that yield measurements consistent with the known input of rEV. Devices to be calibrated include NTA instruments, flow cytometers, microplate readers, and mass spectrometers. The term “optionally” is used in the context of optional process steps that may be included or excluded based on experimental design. A “property” refers to any measurable characteristic of EV, such as hydrodynamic diameter, refractive index, surface charge, or molecular composition.

Finally, the invention includes a method to evaluate EV isolation protocols by comparing the recovery of spiked rEV across different methods, thereby providing an objective metric of performance for method selection or optimization in research and clinical settings.
