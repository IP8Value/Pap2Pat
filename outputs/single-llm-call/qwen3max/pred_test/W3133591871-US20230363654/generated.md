## TECHNICAL FIELD

The present invention relates generally to the field of biomedical signal processing and non-invasive physiological monitoring. More specifically, the invention pertains to systems and methods for contactless cardiac rhythm assessment using active sonar techniques implemented on commodity smart speaker hardware. The invention enables the extraction of individual heartbeats, calculation of beat-to-beat intervals (R–R intervals), determination of heart rate, and assessment of heart rate variability without requiring physical contact with the subject. The technical field further encompasses adaptive beamforming algorithms, signal processing methodologies for separating cardiac motion from respiratory interference, and privacy-preserving architectures for health monitoring in domestic and clinical environments.

## BACKGROUND

Accurate heart rhythm assessment is fundamental to diagnosing a wide range of cardiac arrhythmias and evaluating autonomic nervous system function through heart rate variability (HRV) analysis. Conventional clinical practice relies on electrocardiography (ECG) to capture the electrical activity of the heart and identify individual ventricular depolarizations, from which R–R intervals are derived. While effective, ECG necessitates direct skin contact via electrodes, which presents logistical, hygienic, and comfort challenges—particularly in infectious disease settings, for patients with dermatological sensitivities, or in remote telemedicine scenarios where self-administered diagnostics are desirable. Frequency domain analysis of physiological signals offers an alternative for estimating average heart rate under conditions of regular or quasi-periodic rhythms. However, this approach fails catastrophically in the presence of irregular rhythms such as atrial fibrillation, where the absence of a dominant spectral peak renders frequency-based estimation unreliable. The energy associated with irregular heartbeats becomes diffusely distributed across a broad frequency band, precluding accurate identification of individual beats or precise timing of R–R intervals. Consequently, there exists a critical unmet need for a contactless methodology capable of resolving individual cardiac cycles irrespective of rhythm regularity, thereby enabling robust arrhythmia detection and HRV analysis outside traditional clinical settings.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The embodiments described herein address the limitations of existing cardiac monitoring technologies by transforming ubiquitous smart speaker devices into short-range active sonar systems capable of detecting sub-millimeter chest wall displacements induced by cardiac activity. This transformation leverages the widespread deployment of smart speakers—such as Amazon Echo, Google Nest, and Apple HomePod—which are already equipped with multiple microphones and high-fidelity speakers, thus providing a scalable platform for passive, contactless health monitoring without requiring specialized hardware. The core innovation lies in a suite of signal processing algorithms that overcome the inherent challenges of extracting weak cardiac motion signals from noisy acoustic reflections contaminated by larger respiratory movements and environmental interference.

Smart speaker technology has evolved beyond voice assistant functionality to include sophisticated spatial audio processing, user localization, and gesture recognition. These capabilities stem from advanced microphone arrays and real-time acoustic signal processing pipelines. By repurposing these existing features, the present invention enables continuous or spot-check cardiac rhythm monitoring in homes, hospitals, and other environments where smart speakers are already present. The advantages of contactless monitoring are manifold: it eliminates the need for skin preparation, electrode placement, or wearable adherence; reduces infection transmission risk; accommodates patients with skin conditions; and facilitates longitudinal data collection in naturalistic settings. Moreover, self-administered rhythm analysis empowers patients—especially those in rural or underserved areas—to participate actively in their cardiac care by transmitting diagnostic-quality R–R interval data to clinicians remotely.

Commodity smart speakers typically support audio transmission up to 22 kHz, with some newer models extending into the 25–30 kHz range. These inaudible frequencies are ideal for active sonar interrogation because they avoid perceptual disturbance while remaining within the operational bandwidth of standard audio hardware. The system emits frequency-modulated continuous wave (FMCW) signals in the 18–22 kHz band, which reflect off the human torso and are captured by the microphone array. The minute displacements caused by the heart’s apical impulse and arterial pulsations—on the order of 0.3–0.8 mm—modulate the phase of these reflected signals. However, this cardiac motion is dwarfed by respiratory excursions, which can exceed 5 mm and introduce nonlinear, non-sinusoidal components that generate harmonic interference overlapping with the cardiac frequency band (60–150 beats per minute, or 1–2.5 Hz).

To isolate the cardiac signal, the invention employs an adaptive, learning-based beamformer that maximizes the signal-to-interference-plus-noise ratio (SINR). Unlike conventional fixed beamformers, this algorithm does not assume periodicity in the heartbeat signal, making it uniquely suited for irregular rhythms. It operates in the frequency domain on the impulse response derived from FMCW reflections, applying complex weights across microphones and frequencies to coherently enhance cardiac motion while suppressing respiration and ambient noise. The optimization objective leverages the spectral separation between respiration (<40 CPM) and heart rate (60–150 CPM), using filtered energy proxies to guide weight adaptation without requiring ground truth labels—a form of self-supervised learning that ensures generalizability across diverse anatomies and environments.

Following beamforming, residual respiratory interference may still modulate the cardiac signal, causing it to rotate between the in-phase (real) and quadrature (imaginary) components of the complex baseband representation. A novel segmentation algorithm addresses this by simultaneously identifying beat boundaries and estimating the rotation between consecutive segments. It compares adjacent heartbeat arcs after temporal normalization and computes an optimal rotation that minimizes their mean squared difference, thereby robustly localizing individual beats even in the presence of irregular intervals and motion artifacts.

Privacy is preserved through multiple design choices: the use of inaudible frequencies that carry no speech information; the short operational range (<1 meter), which requires user proximity and implicit consent; and the fact that raw microphone data are processed locally within the device, with only derived health metrics (e.g., heart rate, R–R intervals) transmitted externally. Smart speaker manufacturers retain control over sensor access, preventing third-party apps from harvesting raw acoustic data.

The system architecture, as illustrated in FIG. 1, comprises a signal generator, a multi-element speaker, a microphone array, a digital signal processor, memory, and communication and user interfaces. The signal generator produces FMCW chirps (e.g., 18–22 kHz over 50 ms), which are emitted via the speaker. Reflected signals are captured by the microphone array (e.g., six or seven elements arranged circularly with ~4.3 cm spacing). The processor performs preprocessing—including audible noise filtering, channel impulse response extraction via frequency-domain equalization, and echo suppression beyond 1 meter—followed by adaptive beamforming, segmentation, and health metric computation. Memory stores intermediate signals, beamforming weights, and derived metrics. Communication interfaces (Wi-Fi, Bluetooth) enable data transmission to smartphones or cloud services, while the user interface allows initiation of measurements and displays of waveforms and metrics.

Signal processing begins with discrete Fourier transforms (DFTs) of received blocks, followed by phase compensation to recover the time-domain impulse response. Echo suppression uses a raised-cosine window to attenuate distant reflections smoothly. The beamformer solves an optimization problem using gradient ascent, incorporating regularization to avoid amplification of transient artifacts and dropout to prevent overfitting to noise. Machine learning—specifically self-supervised optimization—enables personalized adaptation without labeled training data. Health metrics include instantaneous heart rate, R–R intervals, HRV indices (e.g., SDNN, RMSSD), and arrhythmia flags (e.g., for atrial fibrillation based on R–R irregularity).

The system supports both spot monitoring (e.g., 60-second sessions) and potential future continuous monitoring. Motion detection can pause cardiac analysis during subject movement. In emergency scenarios, detected life-threatening arrhythmias could trigger alerts to caregivers or autonomous vehicles. Unlike ECG, which measures electrical potentials, this system senses mechanical motion, offering complementary physiological insight while avoiding electrode-related limitations.

### IMPLEMENTED EXAMPLES

A clinical validation study was conducted to evaluate the performance of the smart speaker-based cardiac monitoring system in both healthy individuals and hospitalized cardiac patients. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the University of Washington, and all participants provided informed consent. Twenty-six healthy volunteers (median age 31 years, IQR 8.5; median BMI 22, IQR 3; female-to-male ratio 0.6) with no known cardiac history were recruited. Additionally, twenty-four hospitalized cardiac patients were enrolled from the acute care cardiology unit of a tertiary medical center. Patients were categorized by a physician into regular rhythm (n=18; including sinus rhythm, paced rhythms) or irregular rhythm (n=6; atrial fibrillation or variable atrial flutter) groups based on telemetry review. Exclusion criteria included BMI >35 (in the primary cohort), inability to sit still for 15 minutes, cardiopulmonary instability, or altered mental status. Ground truth data were acquired using either a chest-worn Polar H10 ECG sensor or a fingertip CorSense PPG monitor, both validated against clinical ECG standards.

Testing sessions were conducted in controlled environments: healthy participants in a quiet university room, and patients in their hospital rooms with ambient noise sources (e.g., television) turned off. The smart speaker prototype—comprising a seven-microphone USB array and a dedicated speaker—was placed 40–60 cm from the participant’s chest at nipple level. Healthy participants completed seven 60-second sessions under varying conditions: different distances (40, 50, 60 cm), vertical offsets (+10 cm), angular misalignment (20°), background music (~75 dB), and post-exercise states (heart rate >110 BPM). Cardiac patients completed five 60-second sessions while seated upright.

Data analysis revealed high concordance between the smart speaker system and ground truth sensors. Among healthy participants, heart rate measurements showed intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) and concordance correlation coefficient (CCC) of 0.983, with a median absolute error (MAE) of 1 BPM. R–R intervals achieved ICC and CCC of 0.929 and 0.927, respectively, with MAE of 28 ms (3.6% of mean R–R interval). Performance remained robust across clothing types, minor misalignments, and moderate background noise. Errors increased slightly with distance, BMI, and post-exercise states but remained clinically acceptable.

In hospitalized cardiac patients, heart rate MAE was 2 BPM (90th percentile <3 BPM), and R–R interval MAE was 30 ms (4.0% of mean interval), with ICC and CCC of 0.901 and 0.898. Critically, accuracy was comparable between regular and irregular rhythm cohorts. For atrial fibrillation patients, the system reliably captured the hallmark R–R interval variability, with MAE of 35 ms—well below the 50 ms threshold considered clinically insignificant for AF diagnosis. Time-series plots demonstrated faithful reproduction of complex rhythms, including respiratory sinus arrhythmia and paced rhythms.

An ancillary study of five extreme obesity patients (BMI 36–40.4) revealed limited signal acquisition in four cases, consistent with known attenuation of surface cardiac motion by adipose tissue. This highlights a physiological boundary condition rather than a system flaw.

These results collectively demonstrate that the disclosed system achieves clinically relevant accuracy in extracting R–R intervals from both regular and irregular heart rhythms using only commodity smart speaker hardware and innovative signal processing. The technology holds significant promise for scalable, privacy-preserving cardiac monitoring in diverse real-world settings.
