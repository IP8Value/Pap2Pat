# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

### 1. Field

The present invention relates generally to the field of optical imaging systems, and more particularly to high-speed axial scanning technologies for use in microscopy applications. Specifically, the invention provides novel optical configurations and methods for achieving rapid, aberration-free remote focusing by converting lateral scanning motion into axial re-focusing, thereby enabling high-resolution volumetric imaging at multi-kilohertz rates. The invention is particularly applicable to advanced microscopy techniques such as axially swept light-sheet microscopy (ASLM) and two-photon raster-scanning microscopy, where high-speed volumetric imaging of dynamic biological processes is essential.

### 2. Discussion of Related Art

Traditional optical microscopes have long relied on mechanical movement of either the objective lens or the sample stage to achieve axial re-focusing. However, this approach suffers from significant limitations due to the high mass of these components, which inherently restricts the achievable scanning speed. In fields such as neuroscience, where imaging of dynamic biological processes like membrane voltage activity (with millisecond-scale dynamics) and cerebral blood flow is required, these mechanical limitations become particularly problematic, as they prevent the acquisition of high-speed volumetric data necessary for capturing rapid physiological events.

To address these limitations, various rapid axial scanning technologies have been developed, including movable-mirror systems and electrically tunable lenses (ETLs). ETLs operate by altering the refractive index of a liquid medium through electrical actuation, thereby changing the focal length of the lens. While ETLs can achieve relatively high scanning speeds, they are fundamentally limited in their ability to maintain diffraction-limited performance across their axial scan range. This limitation arises because ETLs typically approximate only a quadratic phase function for defocusing, failing to account for the higher-order aberrations necessary to maintain optimal focus quality throughout the scan range.

Mechanical actuation approaches, including those employing deformable mirrors (DMs), face similar trade-offs between speed and stroke distance. Rapid DMs typically have limited stroke ranges (on the order of one wavelength), which constrains their achievable axial focusing range. Conversely, DMs with larger stroke capabilities capable of producing substantial focus changes are generally much slower in operation. These inherent limitations have prevented the widespread adoption of these technologies for high-resolution, high-speed volumetric imaging applications.

Remote focusing techniques have emerged as an alternative approach to axial scanning, where the position of the optical focus is adjusted without physically moving the primary objective or the sample. This technique is primarily realized by altering the wavefront in the optical train, either in Fourier space (conjugate to the back focal plane of the objective) or in a region conjugate to the specimen. While remote focusing has demonstrated improved performance over traditional mechanical approaches, existing implementations still suffer from significant limitations. For instance, systems employing tuneable acoustic gradient index of refraction (TAG) lenses can achieve axial scan rates of hundreds of kilohertz but are similarly restricted to quadratic phase functions that cannot compensate for higher-order aberrations.

More recently, nanosecond-scale axial re-focusing has been achieved through laser pulse multiplexing or reverberation loop techniques. However, these approaches are constrained by technological limitations related to pulse repetition rates and photophysical constraints such as fluorescence lifetime, which restrict their application to a maximum of approximately ten focal planes. Additionally, like TAG lenses and ETLs, reverberation microscopy introduces only quadratic phase functions for re-focusing, limiting its utility for high-resolution imaging applications. Furthermore, the exponentially decaying intensity profile of the generated focal spots results in uneven illumination across different focal planes, making these techniques unsuitable for applications requiring uniform illumination across multiple axial positions.

Given these limitations of existing technologies, there remains a critical need for a high-resolution axial scanning technology capable of achieving multi-kilohertz scanning rates while maintaining aberration-free performance throughout the scan range. The present invention addresses this need by providing a novel approach that leverages established high-speed lateral scanning technologies to achieve rapid axial re-focusing through innovative optical configurations that transform lateral scanning motion into pure axial displacement without introducing spherical aberrations.

## SUMMARY

The present invention introduces an optical imaging system that achieves rapid, aberration-free axial scanning by converting lateral scanning motion into axial re-focusing. This innovative approach leverages the well-established high-speed capabilities of lateral scanning technologies, such as galvanometric mirrors and electro-optical deflectors, to overcome the limitations of traditional axial scanning methods. The system operates by scanning a laser spot laterally over a specially designed stationary mirror whose surface geometry varies in the scan direction, thereby introducing controlled defocus that translates into axial displacement of the final focus.

The invention employs a remote focusing technique that maintains aberration-free performance throughout the axial scan range. This is achieved through careful pupil matching between the remote-focusing objective and the imaging objective, ensuring that any aberrations introduced by imaging beyond the nominal focal plane are exactly compensated. The system's design allows for pure axial scan motion by perfectly compensating for lateral-scan components on the return optical path, resulting in high-quality, diffraction-limited foci at all axial positions.

In one embodiment, the invention is applied to axially swept light-sheet microscopy (ASLM), where it enables dramatic improvements in volumetric imaging speed while maintaining high spatial resolution. By replacing traditional mechanical Z-stepping with the novel remote focusing approach, the system achieves frame rates that are orders of magnitude faster than previously reported ASLM implementations, enabling the capture of rapid intracellular dynamics that were previously inaccessible.

The invention is also applicable to raster scanning microscopes, particularly two-photon microscopy systems, where it enables high-speed volumetric imaging of complex biological structures. The system's compatibility with resonant scanning technologies allows for axial scanning rates of up to 12 kHz, facilitating the study of fast physiological processes such as cardiac dynamics in living organisms.

A key feature of the invention is its ability to provide aberration-free remote focusing across the entire axial scan range. This is accomplished through precise optical design that ensures proper pupil matching between objectives and careful alignment of optical components. The system maintains diffraction-limited performance throughout the scan range, enabling high-resolution imaging of fine structural features such as dendritic spines in neuronal tissue.

The invention encompasses multiple embodiments, including a step mirror configuration and a tilted planar mirror configuration. The step mirror embodiment enables discrete axial steps with arbitrary step sizes, allowing for flexible positioning of multiple focal planes within the available field of view. The tilted planar mirror embodiment provides continuous axial scanning capability, with axial resolution limited only by the angular resolving power of the lateral scanning technology employed.

Additionally, the invention includes a diffraction grating embodiment that offers alternative approaches to achieving the desired wavefront modifications for axial scanning. The system can be extended to two scan dimensions, providing even greater flexibility in focal plane positioning and enabling more complex scanning patterns for specialized imaging applications.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention provides advanced imaging systems that enable high-speed, aberration-free axial scanning through innovative optical configurations. Two exemplary configurations are presented: a step mirror configuration capable of discrete axial steps and a tilted mirror configuration enabling continuous axial scanning. Both configurations leverage the principle of converting lateral scanning motion into axial re-focusing while maintaining diffraction-limited performance throughout the scan range.

The step mirror configuration utilizes a stationary mirror with discrete steps of varying heights positioned in the focal plane of a remote-focusing objective. When a laser spot is scanned laterally over this step mirror using a galvanometric scanning mirror, each step corresponds to a specific axial position in the final image plane. The height difference between steps determines the axial displacement between corresponding focal planes. This configuration allows for arbitrary step sizes and can accommodate large axial displacements, though practical limitations arise from the finite field of view of the remote objective, which constrains the number of steps that can be implemented within a given scan range.

The tilted mirror configuration employs a planar mirror that is slightly tilted relative to the optical axis. In this arrangement, the incoming laser focus is also tilted to ensure normal incidence on the mirror surface. Lateral scanning of the laser spot across this tilted mirror results in continuous axial displacement of the final focus, as the distance between the mirror surface and the nominal focal plane varies linearly with lateral position. This configuration provides fine axial resolution limited only by the angular resolving power of the lateral scanning technology and enables smooth, continuous axial scanning over a defined range.

Numerical simulations and experimental measurements have validated both configurations, demonstrating their ability to maintain diffraction-limited performance across their respective scan ranges. Point spread function (PSF) measurements confirm that the optical quality of the generated foci closely matches that achieved through conventional remote focusing techniques, with full width at half maximum (FWHM) values approaching the theoretical diffraction limit for the given numerical aperture and wavelength.

The invention finds particular application in axially swept light-sheet microscopy (ASLM), where it dramatically accelerates volumetric imaging speeds. By integrating the novel remote focusing approach into an ASLM system, researchers can achieve isotropic ~400-nm resolution over volumes spanning 128 × 32 × 2 μm³ at volumetric rates of 3.5 Hz, representing a 20-fold improvement over previously reported ASLM frame rates. This enhanced speed enables the tracking of rapidly diffusing particles such as genetically encoded multimeric nanoparticles (GEMs) through cellular cytoplasm, revealing short bursts of rapid translocation with instantaneous diffusion coefficients exceeding 815 nm²/s.

The imaging system 100 comprises several key components working in concert to achieve the desired functionality. A light source generates a collimated laser beam that is delivered into the system through appropriate beam conditioning optics. The system includes means for remote focusing a beam of light, which consists of a galvanometric scanning mirror (GSM) and associated optical components arranged in a 4F telescope configuration. Two objectives are employed: a remote-focusing objective (OBJ1) and an imaging objective (OBJ2), which are carefully pupil-matched to ensure aberration-free performance.

The galvanometric scanning mirror plays a crucial role in the system's operation, providing the lateral scanning motion that is converted into axial re-focusing. A beam splitter directs the laser beam into the remote-focusing arm, while a quarter-wave plate ensures proper polarization management for efficient reflection and transmission through the optical path. Detection objectives and cameras are positioned orthogonally to capture both transmitted and fluorescent signals, with appropriate optical filters ensuring signal specificity.

The step mirror and tilted mirror configurations represent the core innovations of the system. In the step mirror configuration, the mirror is fabricated with precise step heights corresponding to desired axial displacements. The tilted mirror configuration requires careful alignment to ensure that the incoming laser focus is properly oriented relative to the mirror surface, typically achieved by slightly translating the remote objective with respect to the incoming beam.

The imaging system configuration demands precise alignment of all optical components, particularly the objectives, which must be accurately pupil-matched to maintain aberration-free performance. The angular aperture of the remote objective (OBJ1) must be larger than that of the imaging objective (OBJ2) in the tilted mirror configuration to accommodate the necessary tilt without sacrificing numerical aperture. The achievable axial scan range scales approximately with the field of view of OBJ1 multiplied by the tangent of the mirror tilt angle, highlighting the importance of selecting objectives and scanning systems with large fields of view.

Experimental validation of the system demonstrates its superior performance characteristics. Images illustrating the system's performance show clear, diffraction-limited foci at multiple axial positions, with consistent optical quality across the entire scan range. The system successfully achieves axial focusing at rates of 12 kHz when using resonant galvanometric scanning, enabling unprecedented temporal resolution for volumetric imaging applications.

The invention's potential applications extend to multiphoton raster scanning microscopy, where it enables high-speed volumetric imaging of complex biological structures. Experimental results demonstrate successful imaging of fixed brain slices with Thy1-GFP-labelled neurons, revealing fine structural details including synaptic spines. The system's capability for intravital imaging is further demonstrated through successful imaging of the beating heart of zebrafish embryos, capturing cardiac dynamics at frame rates of 156 Hz in axial XZ imaging planes.

### Experimental Setup

The experimental implementation of the invention involves careful construction and alignment of optical components to achieve optimal performance. Laser light generation begins with appropriate laser sources selected based on the specific application requirements. For visible light applications, a diode-pumped solid-state 488 nm continuous wave (CW) laser is employed, while near-infrared applications utilize a Ti:sapphire laser tuned to 900 nm with appropriate pulse characteristics for two-photon excitation.

Beam cleanup and expansion are critical initial steps in the optical path, ensuring that the laser beam meets the quality requirements for high-resolution imaging. Spatial filtering through pinholes removes unwanted spatial modes, while appropriate lens combinations expand the beam to fill the available aperture of subsequent optical components. A half-wave plate is strategically positioned to control laser polarization and adjust power levels as needed for different imaging modalities.

The remote focusing arm components include a carefully selected objective lens matched to the specific application requirements. For visible light applications, a 60× 0.7 NA air objective is used, while near-infrared applications employ a 20× 0.8 NA air objective. These objectives are paired with appropriate tube lenses and scan lenses to form the 4F telescope configuration necessary for proper beam manipulation. A quarter-wave plate ensures proper polarization management, while the galvanometric scanning mirror provides the lateral scanning motion that drives the axial re-focusing mechanism.

Objective and coverslip usage follows standard microscopy practices, with appropriate coverslips glued to the front surfaces of air objectives to match their design specifications. The illumination arm components include water immersion objectives specifically selected for their numerical aperture and working distance characteristics, paired with relay lenses chosen to achieve the correct demagnification ratio between the remote mirror and the sample chamber.

A custom sample chamber is fabricated to accommodate the specific requirements of the imaging application, whether for cell culture studies or whole organism imaging. Relay lens selection is critical for maintaining proper pupil matching between the remote-focusing and imaging objectives, with demagnification ratios carefully calculated to ensure aberration-free performance. The imaging arm components include detection objectives, tube lenses, and appropriate optical filters to separate excitation and emission wavelengths.

Neutral density filters and longpass filters are strategically positioned in the detection path to manage signal intensity and ensure spectral purity. Objective piezo usage enables precise Z-stepping for calibration and validation purposes, while piezo stage usage provides accurate sample positioning during imaging experiments. The micro step mirror fabrication process involves sophisticated UV-lithography techniques to create precise step geometries with controlled heights and widths.

The UV-lithography process begins with spin-coating of SU-8 3005 photoresist onto silicon wafers, followed by soft baking and treatment according to manufacturer protocols. Microfluidic mask projection creates the desired step patterns, with post-baking and rinsing steps ensuring proper development. Platinum layer deposition through sputter coating creates highly reflective surfaces suitable for the demanding optical requirements of the system. Profilometer usage enables precise measurement and optimization of step heights and surface quality.

The method for rapid aberration-free remote focusing involves generating a beam of light and scanning it across the specially designed mirror surface. The reflections are transmitted through the first objective and provided to the illumination objective, where they form the final focused spot in the sample plane. Image data is captured through appropriate detection systems, and the resulting images are processed to generate comprehensive representations of the sample structure.

Step mirror profile views reveal the precise geometries achieved through the fabrication process, with both non-tilted and tilted surface configurations demonstrating the system's flexibility. Different step sizes can be implemented to achieve various axial displacement ranges, while tilted step mirrors combine the advantages of both discrete and continuous scanning approaches. The imaging system with diffraction grating represents an alternative embodiment that offers different trade-offs between complexity and performance.

Diffraction grating operation involves careful consideration of groove density and orientation to achieve the desired wavefront modifications. The construction of these gratings requires precision manufacturing techniques to ensure consistent performance across the entire optical surface. While offering certain advantages in terms of wavelength selectivity and dispersion control, diffraction grating embodiments may face limitations in terms of efficiency and complexity compared to mirror-based approaches.

Rapid refocusing capabilities are demonstrated through both step mirror and tilt mirror configurations, with each approach offering distinct advantages depending on the specific application requirements. The tilted step mirror configuration combines the benefits of both approaches, enabling both discrete and continuous scanning capabilities within a single optical element. These various embodiments collectively represent a comprehensive solution to the challenges of high-speed, high-resolution axial scanning in modern optical imaging systems.
