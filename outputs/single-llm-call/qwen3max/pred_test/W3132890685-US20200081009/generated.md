# DESCRIPTION

Prostate cancer represents a significant public health burden, ranking as the most commonly diagnosed malignancy among men in many developed countries and the third leading cause of cancer-related mortality in this demographic. Despite advances in screening and treatment, a substantial proportion of patients experience disease progression, particularly through local infiltration beyond the prostate capsule and subsequent metastatic dissemination to distant organs—most notably the bone. The clinical trajectory of prostate cancer is highly heterogeneous, ranging from indolent forms that may never require intervention to aggressive variants that rapidly disseminate and resist conventional therapies. Accurate stratification of disease severity and identification of molecular drivers of progression are therefore critical to optimizing therapeutic strategies and improving patient outcomes.

The TNM (Tumor, Node, Metastasis) classification system provides a standardized framework for staging prostate cancer based on tumor size and extent (T), lymph node involvement (N), and presence of distant metastases (M). This anatomical staging system is complemented by histopathological grading, such as the Gleason score, which assesses the degree of differentiation of tumor cells. Together, these parameters inform prognosis and guide therapeutic decisions. Prostate cancers are broadly classified into four stages: Stage I tumors are confined to the prostate and are typically low-grade; Stage II tumors remain organ-confined but may be larger or higher grade; Stage III tumors have extended beyond the prostate capsule, often infiltrating the periprostatic tissues; and Stage IV disease is characterized by regional lymph node involvement or distant metastases, most commonly in bone.

A key anatomical structure implicated in the progression of locally advanced prostate cancer is the periprostatic adipose tissue (PPAT), a fat depot that directly surrounds the prostate gland. PPAT is not merely a passive energy reservoir but an active endocrine and paracrine organ that secretes a variety of bioactive molecules, collectively termed adipokines. In the context of prostate cancer, PPAT has emerged as a critical microenvironmental niche that facilitates tumor cell invasion and migration. Clinical studies have consistently demonstrated that tumor extension into PPAT is associated with adverse pathological features, including higher Gleason scores, positive surgical margins, and increased risk of biochemical recurrence following radical prostatectomy.

Adipose tissue is composed primarily of mature adipocytes, but also contains a stromal vascular fraction (SVF) comprising preadipocytes, endothelial cells, fibroblasts, and immune cells. Adipocytes play a central role in systemic energy homeostasis, storing triglycerides during caloric surplus and releasing free fatty acids during energy deficit. Beyond their metabolic functions, adipocytes secrete a wide array of signaling molecules, including cytokines, chemokines, growth factors, and hormones, which modulate local and systemic physiology. In pathological conditions such as obesity, this secretory profile becomes dysregulated, promoting chronic low-grade inflammation and creating a tumor-permissive microenvironment.

Obesity is a well-established risk factor for aggressive prostate cancer, with epidemiological data linking higher body mass index (BMI) to increased incidence of high-grade tumors, advanced stage at diagnosis, and poorer survival outcomes. The mechanisms underlying this association are multifactorial but include alterations in adipokine secretion from visceral and periprostatic fat depots, which can stimulate tumor cell proliferation, migration, and survival.

Among the secreted mediators, chemokines—small chemoattractant cytokines—play a pivotal role in directing cellular migration in both physiological and pathological contexts. Chemokines are classified into four subfamilies (C, CC, CXC, and CX3C) based on the arrangement of conserved cysteine residues near the N-terminus. They exert their effects by binding to specific G-protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs) on target cells, triggering intracellular signaling cascades that regulate cytoskeletal rearrangement, adhesion, and motility. In cancer, aberrant expression of chemokine receptors on tumor cells enables them to respond to chemokine gradients established by stromal or distant tissues, thereby facilitating metastatic homing.

The CXCR4/CXCL12 axis is one of the most extensively studied chemokine pathways in prostate cancer. CXCL12 (also known as SDF-1) is constitutively expressed in bone marrow stromal cells, and its receptor CXCR4 is frequently upregulated in prostate cancer cells. This interaction has been shown to mediate homing of tumor cells to the bone marrow, where they can establish metastatic lesions. Similarly, the CCL2/CCR2 axis has been implicated in prostate cancer progression, with CCL2 (MCP-1) secreted by tumor and stromal cells promoting macrophage recruitment and tumor growth in bone.

Beyond these established pathways, emerging evidence points to the involvement of other chemokine receptors, including CCR1 and CCR3, in cancer biology. CCR3 is best known for its role in eosinophil trafficking in allergic inflammation, but recent studies have detected its expression in various malignancies, including thyroid cancer, colon cancer, and glioblastoma. Notably, CCR3 expression has been observed in kidney tumors and melanoma, suggesting a broader oncogenic role. Preliminary investigations in prostate cancer have indicated that CCR3 may be expressed in tumor cells and associated with aggressive phenotypes, though the functional significance and clinical relevance of this observation remained unclear.

Current studies suffer from several limitations, including reliance on in vitro models that do not fully recapitulate the complexity of human adipose tissue, lack of validation in primary human samples, and insufficient exploration of the role of CCR3 in bone metastasis—the most lethal complication of prostate cancer. Moreover, existing therapeutic strategies for localized prostate cancer, often guided by the D’Amico classification (which integrates PSA levels, Gleason score, and clinical stage), do not account for molecular drivers like CCR3 that may influence disease aggressiveness and recurrence risk. There is thus a pressing need for novel therapeutic approaches that target specific molecular pathways involved in tumor dissemination.

In this context, the inventors conducted a series of investigations to elucidate the role of CCR3 in prostate cancer progression. Their research revealed that CCR3 is functionally involved in the directed migration of prostate cancer cells toward adipocyte-derived signals. In vitro experiments using Boyden chamber assays demonstrated that conditioned media from periprostatic and bone marrow adipocytes robustly stimulate prostate cancer cell migration, an effect that is significantly attenuated by CCR3 inhibition. In vivo studies in mouse models further confirmed that CCR3 expression enhances tumor growth and local invasion, particularly in the context of obesity.

Critically, the inventors discovered a strong correlation between CCR3 expression levels in human prostate tumors and clinical markers of aggressiveness, including higher Gleason scores, extraprostatic extension, and biochemical recurrence after treatment. Moreover, analysis of human bone metastases revealed enrichment of CCR3-positive tumor cells compared to primary tumors or visceral metastases, suggesting a specific role for CCR3 in bone homing.

The inventors also identified medullary (bone marrow) adipocytes as a significant source of CCL7, a high-affinity ligand for CCR3. CCL7 secretion was markedly elevated in adipocytes derived from obese individuals and elderly patients, conditions known to increase prostate cancer metastasis risk. This finding establishes a mechanistic link between obesity/ageing, adipocyte dysfunction, and CCR3-dependent tumor cell migration.

Based on these discoveries, the inventors propose a novel therapeutic strategy centered on inhibiting the CCL7/CCR3 signaling axis. Inhibition can be achieved through multiple modalities, including suppression of CCR3 expression or blockade of CCL7/CCR3 interaction. A preferred embodiment of the invention involves the use of inhibitors that specifically target this pathway to prevent or treat prostate cancer, particularly in patients with high CCR3-expressing tumors or those at risk due to obesity or advanced age.

An inhibitor of CCR3 expression, as defined herein, encompasses any agent that reduces the transcription, translation, or stability of CCR3 mRNA or protein. Specific examples include short hairpin RNA (shRNA) molecules designed to target the CCR3 gene for RNA interference, thereby silencing its expression. Alternatively, antisense oligonucleotides complementary to CCR3 mRNA can be employed to block translation or induce degradation of the transcript.

An inhibitor of CCL7/CCR3 interaction, as used herein, refers to any molecule that prevents the binding of CCL7 to CCR3, thereby abrogating downstream signaling. This category includes competitive antagonists that bind CCR3 at the CCL7 docking site, as well as non-competitive allosteric modulators that induce conformational changes in the receptor. Organic small molecules identified through high-throughput screening or rational design can serve as potent CCR3 antagonists. Peptides mimicking the CCL7 binding interface or receptor extracellular domains may also disrupt the interaction. Monoclonal antibodies specifically targeting either CCR3 or CCL7 can neutralize the ligand or block receptor activation. Peptidomimetics—non-peptide compounds that mimic the structural and functional properties of peptide ligands—offer enhanced stability and bioavailability. Additionally, aptamers—single-stranded nucleic acid molecules that fold into specific three-dimensional structures capable of high-affinity binding—can be engineered to recognize and sequester CCL7 or CCR3.

The invention further provides methods for identifying such inhibitors, for example, by screening compound libraries for agents that reduce CCR3-mediated migration in response to adipocyte-conditioned media or recombinant CCL7. Identified inhibitors can be formulated with pharmaceutically acceptable excipients—such as buffers, stabilizers, solubilizers, and preservatives—to ensure stability, bioavailability, and patient safety.

Administration of these inhibitors may occur via various routes, including oral, intravenous, subcutaneous, or intraperitoneal delivery, depending on the pharmacokinetic properties of the agent. The effective dose will be determined empirically based on preclinical efficacy, toxicity profiles, and clinical trial data, but generally aims to achieve sufficient systemic or local concentrations to inhibit CCR3 signaling without causing undue side effects.

The invention also encompasses a method for preventing or treating prostate cancer in a subject in need thereof, comprising administering an effective quantity of a CCR3 or CCL7 inhibitor. An “effective quantity” is defined as the amount sufficient to reduce tumor cell migration, inhibit local invasion, delay metastasis, or improve clinical outcomes such as progression-free or overall survival.

Furthermore, the invention provides an ex vivo diagnostic method for determining the degree of aggressiveness of a prostate cancer tumor. This method involves obtaining a sample of prostate tumor cells from a subject, along with a reference sample of healthy prostate epithelium. The concentration of CCR3 receptor protein or mRNA is then measured in both samples using techniques such as immunohistochemistry, flow cytometry, or quantitative PCR. The reference concentration in healthy tissue serves as a baseline; elevated CCR3 levels in the tumor sample indicate a more aggressive phenotype. Based on this measurement, the subject can be classified into risk categories (e.g., low, intermediate, high) that inform prognosis and therapeutic planning, thereby enabling personalized management of prostate cancer.
