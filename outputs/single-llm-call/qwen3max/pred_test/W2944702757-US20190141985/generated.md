# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD OF THE DISCLOSED SUBJECT MATTER

Organ transplantation represents a critical and life-saving medical intervention for patients suffering from end-stage organ failure. Despite decades of advancements in surgical techniques, immunosuppressive therapies, and post-operative care, the field continues to face a profound and persistent limitation: the severe shortage of viable donor organs. This scarcity results in prolonged waiting times for recipients and, tragically, the death of thousands of patients annually who never receive a transplant. The challenge is particularly acute in lung transplantation, where up to eighty percent of potential donor lungs are deemed unsuitable for clinical use due to injuries sustained at or near the time of donor death—such as trauma, aspiration, ventilator-induced damage, or pulmonary contusion. Consequently, there exists an urgent and unmet need for innovative technologies capable of not only preserving but actively regenerating severely injured donor organs outside the human body, thereby expanding the pool of transplantable organs and improving patient outcomes.

## BACKGROUND OF THE DISCLOSED SUBJECT MATTER

Organ transplantation has evolved into a standard therapeutic option for irreversible organ failure, yet its full potential remains unrealized due to systemic constraints in organ availability. Current transplant procedures rely heavily on the timely procurement of organs from brain-dead or circulatory-death donors, followed by rapid cold storage and transportation to the recipient center. However, this paradigm is fundamentally limited by ischemic injury that accumurs during cold storage, which restricts the viable preservation window to mere hours—typically four to six hours for lungs. To address marginal-quality organs, ex vivo lung perfusion (EVLP) has emerged as a clinical platform that enables normothermic assessment and limited therapeutic intervention during a six-hour window. While EVLP has modestly increased the utilization of borderline donor lungs, it remains ineffective for severely damaged organs, such as those injured by gastric aspiration, which induce profound epithelial disruption, inflammation, edema, and surfactant dysfunction that EVLP cannot reverse.

Extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) and conventional EVLP systems operate using acellular or blood-based perfusates in closed circuits lacking systemic regulation, thereby failing to replicate the dynamic homeostatic environment provided by a living organism. These systems cannot sustain long-term metabolic, immunologic, or endocrine support necessary for cellular regeneration. Recognizing these limitations, initiatives to increase transplantable lungs have explored extended perfusion strategies, including xenogeneic cross-circulation using animal hosts. However, prior attempts at cross-circulation have been hampered by inadequate circuit design, poor hemodynamic control, lack of integrated monitoring, and insufficient methods for managing venous drainage and airway interventions.

Current organ preservation techniques predominantly rely on static cold storage, which halts metabolism but does not prevent progressive cellular injury. Even advanced EVLP platforms suffer from non-physiologic perfusion pressures, absence of neurohormonal feedback, and limited capacity for repeated therapeutic delivery. The benefits of physiologic preservation—maintaining normothermia, native blood composition, and systemic regulatory mechanisms—are well recognized but technically elusive. Conventional extracorporeal support systems lack auto-regulation of blood flow, precise pressure control, integrated heat exchange, and real-time diagnostic capabilities, all of which are essential for prolonged organ viability. Thus, there remains a compelling and unaddressed need for an improved extracorporeal support system that mimics in vivo physiology, enables extended normothermic perfusion, facilitates repeated interventions, and supports active regeneration of severely injured donor organs.

## SUMMARY

The disclosed subject matter introduces a novel normothermic extracorporeal support system based on cross-circulation perfusion between a living host and an extracorporeal organ, enabling unprecedented durations of organ maintenance—exceeding 36 hours—with full physiologic regulation. This system establishes a direct extracorporeal host connection wherein the donor organ is integrated into the circulatory system of a healthy host animal, receiving oxygenated, nutrient-rich, hormone-balanced, and immunologically active blood under native regulatory control. The invention comprises a comprehensive system for maintaining an extracorporeal organ, featuring an extracorporeal chamber that houses the organ in a temperature-controlled, sterile environment while allowing continuous ventilation and vascular perfusion.

A key innovation is the auto-regulation of blood flow through the extracorporeal organ, achieved by leveraging the host’s intrinsic cardiovascular feedback mechanisms, thereby maintaining physiologic trans-pulmonary pressure gradients and preventing barotrauma or hypoperfusion. The extracorporeal cross-circulation circuit includes pressure-controlled blood flow elements that ensure stable arterial inflow and venous outflow, integrated with a heat exchanger to maintain normothermia throughout the circuit. The system further incorporates remote monitoring capability via digital interfaces that track hemodynamics, gas exchange, temperature, and biochemical parameters in real time.

The extracorporeal organ chamber stand is engineered for stability and accessibility, featuring automatic height adjustability to optimize surgical access and imaging alignment. Organ-specific accessories, such as bronchoscopes, microcatheters, and imaging probes, are seamlessly integrated into the platform. A rechargeable power source back-up ensures uninterrupted operation during transport or power fluctuations. Central to vascular integration is a biologic cuff device that utilizes a segment of native vessel—such as the aortic arch—as a biological bridge between the donor organ’s pulmonary veins and the extracorporeal circuit, preserving endothelial integrity and minimizing thrombogenicity.

A specialized draining cannula with crenellated or self-expanding stent-like features ensures patent venous drainage without kinking or collapse. Patient safety is enhanced through a patient safety clamp equipped with an automatic clamping mechanism that isolates the extracorporeal circuit in the event of host instability or system failure. The invention further encompasses a method for normothermic extracorporeal support that includes treating an extracorporeal organ with acute injury—such as gastric aspiration—through repeated therapeutic interventions including bronchoalveolar lavage, surfactant replacement, and alveolar recruitment, ultimately enabling the transplantation of organs previously deemed non-viable.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE DISCLOSED SUBJECT MATTER

The disclosed invention provides a transformative platform for cross-circulation support of extracorporeal organs, overcoming the fundamental limitations of current transplant methodologies. Traditional organ preservation relies on static cold storage or short-term EVLP, both of which fail to support cellular repair or regeneration in severely injured organs. In contrast, the present system enables long-term preservation by connecting the extracorporeal organ to a living host via a closed circulatory loop, thereby providing systemic physiologic regulation—including endocrine, metabolic, immune, and hemodynamic homeostasis—that is essential for tissue recovery.

This invention is motivated by the critical need to salvage donor organs that would otherwise be discarded, particularly lungs injured by gastric aspiration, trauma, or infection. The system’s capabilities extend beyond passive support to active regeneration, facilitated by repeated, targeted therapeutic interventions delivered over days rather than hours. Applications include the recovery of human donor lungs for transplantation, the creation of human-xenogeneic bioreactors for organ regeneration, and the development of research platforms for studying organ pathophysiology and therapeutic efficacy.

The mobile platform comprises a modular system for prolonged extracorporeal support, integrating clinical-grade components within a sterile, temperature-controlled environment. The connection between the host and extracorporeal organ is established through vascular cannulation of the host’s internal jugular veins and the organ’s pulmonary artery and vein, with outflow managed via a biologic cuff device. This configuration supports not only maintenance but also recovery, regeneration, and functional assessment of the organ over extended periods.

Additional research applications include modeling human disease in extracorporeal human organs supported by animal hosts, testing novel therapeutics, and developing bioengineered grafts. The system enables cross-circulation between an organ recipient and a donor organ prior to transplantation, potentially inducing immunomodulation. Alternatively, a healthy animal—such as a swine with defined MHC haplotypes—can serve as a universal cross-circulation host for multiple human organs.

A key application is the recovery of injured human donor lungs rejected for transplantation. By connecting such lungs to a cross-circulation host, the system provides the physiologic milieu necessary for epithelial and endothelial repair, clearance of inflammatory mediators, and restoration of surfactant function. The human-xenogeneic bioreactor application leverages porcine hosts to support human organs, combining species-specific compatibility with scalable infrastructure. Benefits include sustained metabolic activity, immune cell trafficking, cytokine balance, and hormonal signaling absent in acellular systems.

The cross-circulation platform offers unparalleled opportunities for organ recovery, surpassing EVLP in duration, physiologic fidelity, and therapeutic scope. Systems, methods, and devices described herein enable normothermic perfusion exceeding 36 hours with stable hemodynamics and gas exchange. Envisioned uses include pre-transplant rehabilitation of marginal organs, personalized therapy testing, and organ banking.

Cross-circulation provides superior outcomes compared to conventional EVLP by maintaining native blood composition, auto-regulated flow, and systemic homeostasis. The cross-circulation circuit includes fluid conduits connecting the host vasculature to the organ’s inflow and outflow vessels, with auto-regulation mediated by the host’s baroreceptors and vascular tone. Integrated circuit elements include centrifugal pumps, softshell reservoirs, coated tubing, and pressure sensors.

Pre-clinical swine models confirmed haemodynamic stability over 36 hours, with no significant changes in host heart rate, blood pressure, or oxygenation. Biochemical analysis revealed stable electrolytes, lactate, and liver enzymes. Haemolytic markers (plasma free hemoglobin, D-dimer) remained within normal limits, indicating minimal circuit-induced trauma.

Pro-inflammatory markers (IL-6, IL-8, TNFα) in host serum did not elevate, while anti-inflammatory markers (IL-10) increased, suggesting a tolerogenic environment. Extracorporeal lung performance was rigorously assessed: trans-pulmonary pressure gradient was maintained at 5–15 mmHg; pressure-volume loops demonstrated progressive hysteresis recovery; dynamic compliance improved six-fold; lactate clearance indicated metabolic health; and PaO2/FiO2 ratios normalized to >640 mmHg.

Responses to performance challenges—such as FiO2 escalation and minute ventilation doubling—showed restored gas exchange capacity. Lung weight decreased significantly, indicating edema resolution. Macroscopic appearance improved from consolidated and hemorrhagic to aerated and pink. Airway bronchoscopy revealed resolving inflammation. Histologic analysis showed restoration of epithelial integrity, reduced leukocyte infiltration, and intact alveolar architecture.

Scanning and transmission electron microscopy confirmed recovery of type I pneumocyte membranes, lamellar bodies in type II cells, and microvascular endothelium. Metabolic activity assays showed preserved mitochondrial function. Vasoresponsiveness tests demonstrated intact pulmonary vascular reactivity. Neutrophil and macrophage quantification declined to baseline levels.

In the ischemic recovery group, macroscopic and bronchoscopic analyses showed progressive improvement. BAL fluid analysis revealed decreasing pepsin, protein, and inflammatory cytokines, with rising IL-10. Multiscale interventions were demonstrated, including a compact transpleural imaging system for real-time cell tracking. Microbeads and mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) were successfully delivered regionally.

Feasibility of epithelial replacement was shown via decellularization of bronchopulmonary segments followed by delivery of pulmonary epithelial cells. Viable extracorporeal lungs were maintained for 36 hours, recovering from ischemia-reperfusion injury. Benefits included extended normothermic support, physiologic pressure gradients, and recipient safety via automatic clamping.

Cross-circulation rescued severely damaged lungs, reducing inflammatory cytokines in serum and BAL, preserving structural integrity, and increasing anti-inflammatory IL-10. Lung weight and airway edema decreased, with recovery of cellular architecture. The platform enabled interventions not feasible ex vivo, such as repeated bronchoscopy and targeted cell therapy.

Global, lobar, and subsegmental delivery of MSCs was achieved. Prolonged cross-circulation between unrelated swine demonstrated feasibility for clinical translation. Clinical applications include salvaging non-transplantable lungs, personalized therapy, and organ bioengineering.

## EXAMPLES

### Methods

A pilot study was designed to test the hypothesis that interventional cross-circulation could recover severely injured lungs over 36 hours. Swine were selected from a partially inbred herd with defined MHC loci to minimize immunologic cross-reactivity. Animals were housed under AAALAC-accredited conditions and cared for per NIH guidelines. Donor lungs were injured via bronchoscopic delivery of standardized gastric contents (2 mL/kg, pH 2), followed by 6 hours of in vivo injury development.

Donors received anesthesia with Telazol and buprenorphine, then midazolam, fentanyl, and isoflurane. Heparin (30,000 U) was administered before median sternotomy. Lungs were flushed with Perfadex, explanted en bloc, and cannulated with a biologic cuff using the aortic arch. Recipients were prepared similarly, with bilateral internal jugular vein cannulation. Cross-circulation was initiated after methylprednisolone and calcium administration.

Physiological parameters—heart rate, MAP, SpO2, end-tidal CO2—were continuously monitored. Hourly blood analysis included hemogas, electrolytes, lactate, and cytokines. Extracorporeal lung monitoring included PA/PV pressures, dynamic compliance, and PaO2/FiO2. HAL analysis assessed histologic injury. BODIPY-surfactant uptake measured type II pneumocyte function.

Vasoconstriction tests used angiotensin II infusion. Metabolic activity was assayed via Alamar Blue reduction. Myeloperoxidase activity quantified neutrophil presence. Live cilia imaging used high-speed videomicroscopy. Immunohistochemical staining targeted aquaporin 5, ZO-1, SPC, and CD31. Histopathologic analysis followed a blinded, randomized protocol using a 12-region lung map.

Thermal imaging employed FLIR T430sc cameras. Electron microscopy included SEM (Zeiss GeminiSEM) and TEM (JEOL JEM-1200). Therapeutic delivery used bronchoscopes to administer surfactant, MSCs, or epithelial cells. Decellularization targeted segments via detergent perfusion. Cell delivery utilized Renegade microcatheters.

Statistical analysis used one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post hoc tests and Student’s t-tests (p<0.05 significant). Embodiments may be modified in materials, dimensions, or species without departing from the invention’s scope. The principles apply to hearts, kidneys, and livers.
