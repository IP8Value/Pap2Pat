# DESCRIPTION

The presence of metal pollutants in wood represents a significant environmental and industrial challenge, particularly when such wood is intended for use as a feedstock in biorefinery processes. Historically, wood has been treated with metal-based preservatives to enhance its durability against biological degradation, including fungal decay and insect infestation. These preservatives commonly contain toxic metals such as copper, arsenic, and chromium, which are effective biocides but pose serious health and ecological risks. The widespread historical use of chromated copper arsenate (CCA) and other similar formulations has resulted in large volumes of contaminated waste wood that cannot be safely disposed of through conventional means such as incineration or landfilling without stringent controls. Incineration of such wood releases toxic metal oxides into the atmosphere or concentrates them in ash, while uncontrolled landfilling risks leaching of these metals into groundwater systems. Consequently, disposal limitations have necessitated the development of controlled landfill methods or alternative treatment strategies that mitigate environmental hazards.

One promising approach involves thermal treatment followed by careful management of the resulting metal-enriched ash. However, this method still leaves the problem of immobilizing or recovering valuable or hazardous metals from the ash matrix. An alternative strategy entails chemical leaching of metals from treated wood or its derivatives, followed by precipitation and recovery of metal ions. Acid leaching, in particular, has shown efficacy in solubilizing metal pollutants such as Cu(II), enabling their subsequent electrodeposition from aqueous solution. This electrochemical recovery not only removes contaminants but also allows for the potential reuse of valuable metals like copper. A techno-economic model evaluating dilute acid-leaching processes has demonstrated feasibility, with operating costs for metal extraction estimated to be competitive when integrated into broader biomass valorization schemes. Importantly, these costs must be weighed against the expense of sourcing virgin wood feedstock for biofuel production, which remains economically and environmentally burdensome. Thus, the utilization of contaminated waste wood—as opposed to pristine biomass—as a feedstock for biorefinery operations presents a compelling opportunity to address both waste management and resource recovery challenges simultaneously.

Lignocellulosic biomass, the primary structural component of plant cell walls, consists predominantly of three polymeric constituents: cellulose, hemicellulose, and lignin. Cellulose is a linear homopolymer of glucose units linked by β-1,4-glycosidic bonds and forms highly ordered crystalline microfibrils that confer mechanical strength. Hemicellulose is a heterogeneous branched polymer composed of various pentoses (e.g., xylose, arabinose) and hexoses (e.g., mannose, glucose), often acetylated, which cross-links cellulose and lignin. Lignin is an amorphous, cross-linked phenolic polymer that provides rigidity and resistance to microbial attack. The production of cellulosic biofuels typically involves pretreatment to disrupt this recalcitrant structure, followed by enzymatic saccharification of cellulose to glucose and fermentation to ethanol or other products. However, the presence of metal pollutants in preservative-treated biomass severely limits its applicability in such processes, as metals can inhibit enzymes, poison catalysts, and contaminate final products.

To overcome these limitations, the present invention introduces a novel process based on ionic liquid fractionation of cellulose and lignin from metal-contaminated lignocellulosic biomass. As used herein, “extraction” refers to the removal or separation of a substance—in this case, metal pollutants—from a solid matrix into a solvent phase. “Cellulosic” or “lignocellulosic biomass” denotes plant-derived material comprising cellulose, hemicellulose, and lignin, including but not limited to wood, agricultural residues, and energy crops, whether untreated or previously subjected to chemical preservation. Metal pollutants, as defined herein, include any metallic or metalloid elements introduced into biomass via preservative treatments that adversely affect downstream biochemical or thermochemical conversion processes. Preferred metal pollutants include copper, arsenic, chromium, zinc, and boron, primarily sourced from historical wood preservatives such as CCA, ACQ (alkaline copper quaternary), and copper azole formulations. These metals interfere with enzymatic hydrolysis by denaturing cellulases, deactivate fermentation microorganisms, and complicate product purification.

The core of the invention lies in the use of specifically designed ionic liquids capable of simultaneously fractionating lignocellulose and extracting metal pollutants. Ionic liquids are low-melting organic salts composed of discrete cations and anions, exhibiting negligible vapor pressure, high thermal stability, and tunable solvation properties. The ionic liquids employed herein comprise a protic cation derived from an ammonium derivative and a hydrogen sulfate anion. The cation may be aliphatic, alkyl, alkenyl, or contain carbocyclic groups, and is preferably selected from alkylammonium species. More specifically, the cation may be monoalkylammonium, dialkylammonium, trialkylammonium, or a nitrogen-containing heterocyclic moiety such as imidazolium. In preferred embodiments, the cation is triethylammonium, forming triethylammonium hydrogen sulfate ([TEA][HSO₄]). The term “heteroaryl” as used herein refers to aromatic rings containing at least one heteroatom such as nitrogen, oxygen, or sulfur. “Aryl” or “carbocycle group” denotes aromatic or non-aromatic cyclic hydrocarbon structures, respectively. “Halogen atom” includes fluorine, chlorine, bromine, and iodine. Alkylammonium cations encompass those wherein one or more hydrogen atoms of ammonia are replaced by C₁–C₆ alkyl groups; preferred examples include triethylammonium, diethylammonium, and monoethylammonium. The cation may further incorporate C₂–C₆ alkoxy or C₂–C₆ alkoxyalkyl substituents to modulate solubility and reactivity.

The ionic liquid composition typically contains 60–95 wt% ionic liquid and 5–40 wt% water, with water playing a critical role in facilitating hydrolysis of lignin-carbohydrate linkages and reducing viscosity. The ionic liquid may be prepared by neutralizing a Brønsted acid (e.g., sulfuric acid) with a base (e.g., triethylamine), optionally in the presence of excess acid or base to tune acidity. Excess base may be present to moderate proton activity, while excess acid enhances delignification kinetics. Water content is carefully controlled to balance fractionation efficiency and metal solubility. When contacted with metal-contaminated lignocellulosic biomass under heating (typically 100–170°C) and stirring for 1–24 hours, the ionic liquid dissolves lignin and hemicellulose while leaving behind a cellulose-rich solid residue or pulp. Crucially, metal pollutants are retained in the ionic liquid phase due to complexation with the anion or solvation effects, achieving removal efficiencies exceeding 90% for copper and other common preservative metals.

Following fractionation, the cellulose-containing solid residue is separated by filtration or centrifugation and washed to remove residual ionic liquid. The ionic liquid phase, now enriched with lignin, hemicellulose derivatives, and solubilized metals, is subjected to lignin precipitation by addition of an anti-solvent such as water or ethanol. The precipitated lignin is recovered by filtration, and the anti-solvent is removed by evaporation. The remaining ionic liquid solution, containing dissolved metal ions, is then processed for metal recovery—preferably via electrodeposition of Cu(II) to yield pure copper metal—and ionic liquid recycling. The ionic liquid is dried under vacuum to restore its original water content and reused in subsequent cycles. The washed cellulose pulp undergoes enzymatic saccharification using commercial cellulase cocktails to produce glucose, which may be fermented to biofuels. This integrated process enables the safe valorization of otherwise hazardous waste wood, combining detoxification, biomass fractionation, and resource recovery in a single, economically viable platform.

## EXAMPLE 1. DECONSTRUCTION OF BIOMASS AND EXTRACTION OF METALS IN VARIOUS IONIC LIQUIDS

The deconstruction of metal-contaminated lignocellulosic biomass and concurrent extraction of metal pollutants were carried out using a series of protic ionic liquids with hydrogen sulfate anions. An ionic liquid/water mixture was prepared by combining triethylammonium hydrogen sulfate ([TEA][HSO₄]) with deionized water to achieve a final composition of 80 wt% ionic liquid and 20 wt% water. Air-dried, copper-treated Miscanthus biomass was added to this mixture at a solids loading of 10 wt%, resulting in a homogeneous slurry. The mixture was then transferred to a sealed pressure tube and incubated at 170°C for 4 hours with continuous stirring to ensure uniform heat distribution and reaction kinetics. After cooling to room temperature, the reaction mixture was transferred to a centrifuge tube and subjected to centrifugation at 10,000 rpm for 10 minutes to separate the solid pulp from the liquid phase. The solid residue was washed repeatedly with deionized water until the washings exhibited neutral pH, followed by additional washing with ethanol to remove residual lignin and ionic liquid. The washed pulp was then Soxhlet extracted with ethanol for 24 hours to ensure complete removal of soluble organics. The ethanol extract was combined with the ionic liquid-containing filtrate, and the ethanol was evaporated under reduced pressure to yield an ionic liquid/lignin mixture. Lignin was precipitated from this mixture by the slow addition of three volumes of deionized water, followed by standing overnight at 4°C. The precipitated lignin was collected by filtration, washed with water, and dried under vacuum to constant weight. Pulp and lignin yields were determined gravimetrically relative to the initial dry biomass weight. The cellulose-rich pulp was subjected to enzymatic saccharification using a standard cocktail of cellulase and β-glucosidase at 50°C for 96 hours, and the resulting glucose concentration was quantified by HPLC with refractive index detection. Metal content in the original biomass, pulp, lignin, and ionic liquid phase was analyzed by inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometry (ICP-OES). Results demonstrated that [TEA][HSO₄] achieved over 95% removal of copper from the biomass, with the metal predominantly partitioning into the ionic liquid phase. Glucose yields from saccharification exceeded 75% of theoretical maximum, confirming effective detoxification and preservation of cellulose integrity. Lignin recovery was approximately 80%, and the process maintained high efficiency across multiple ionic liquid recycles, underscoring the robustness of the method for treating metal-laden feedstocks.

## EXAMPLE 2 COPPER SOLUBILITY AND ELECTRODEPOSITION OF COPPER FROM VARIOUS IONIC LIQUIDS COPPER SOLUBILITY

The solubility of copper in protic hydrogen sulfate ionic liquids was systematically investigated to assess their capacity for metal extraction from contaminated biomass. Copper(II) oxide was added incrementally to solutions of [TEA][HSO₄] containing 20 wt% water until saturation was observed. The mixtures were stirred at 60°C for 2 hours to ensure equilibrium, then filtered through a 0.2 µm membrane to remove undissolved solids. The filtrates were analyzed by ICP-OES to determine dissolved copper concentrations. The effect of excess base—introduced by adding triethylamine beyond stoichiometric equivalence—on copper solubility was also examined, revealing that basic conditions significantly reduced copper dissolution due to precipitation as hydroxide species. Solubility data, summarized in Table 7 and illustrated in FIG. 3, showed that [TEA][HSO₄] achieved maximum copper solubilities exceeding 50 g L⁻¹ under acidic conditions, far surpassing the metal loadings typically found in preservative-treated wood. The presence of water was found to moderately enhance solubility by reducing ionic liquid viscosity and improving mass transfer, though excessive water (>40 wt%) diminished extraction efficiency by promoting metal hydrolysis. These findings confirm that the ionic liquid environment effectively stabilizes Cu(II) ions in solution, preventing precipitation and enabling high-capacity metal uptake during biomass pretreatment. This high solubility is critical for processing real-world feedstocks, as it ensures that even heavily contaminated wood can be treated without saturating the solvent. The implications for biomass pretreatment are profound: the ionic liquid not only fractionates lignocellulose but also acts as a metal scavenger, integrating detoxification directly into the biorefinery workflow without requiring separate leaching steps.
