## TECHNICAL FIELD

The present invention relates generally to ophthalmic surgical devices and methods, and more particularly to systems, devices, and methods for laser thermal conjunctivoplasty (LTC) for the treatment of conjunctivochalasis. Specifically, the invention provides a handheld laser probe system configured to deliver near-infrared laser energy at a wavelength optimized for water absorption in conjunctival tissue, thereby enabling controlled thermal shrinkage of redundant conjunctiva without damaging surrounding ocular structures. The disclosed embodiments encompass novel optical configurations, laser parameter optimization, and integrated mechanical components that facilitate precise, safe, and effective outpatient treatment of conjunctivochalasis.

## BACKGROUND

Conjunctivochalasis is a degenerative condition characterized by the loosening and redundancy of the conjunctival membrane that overlies the sclera of the eye. This condition typically manifests with age and results in the formation of excessive folds of conjunctival tissue, particularly along the inferior bulbar conjunctiva. These folds can mechanically interfere with the normal distribution of the tear film across the ocular surface, leading to symptoms such as chronic eye irritation, foreign body sensation, blurred vision, and tear instability often misdiagnosed as dry eye syndrome. In severe cases, the redundant conjunctival folds may prolapse over the lower eyelid margin, where they are repeatedly traumatized during blinking, exacerbating inflammation and discomfort. Additionally, the displacement of tears by these folds can cause secondary irritation of the adjacent eyelid skin.

Diagnosis of conjunctivochalasis is primarily clinical, involving direct visualization of redundant conjunctival folds that obliterate the inferior tear meniscus. Cross-sectional imaging modalities such as optical coherence tomography (OCT) have been employed to objectively assess the severity of tissue redundancy and to evaluate postoperative outcomes following surgical intervention. Despite its prevalence, conjunctivochalasis is frequently underdiagnosed or mismanaged due to its symptom overlap with other ocular surface disorders.

Current therapeutic approaches for conjunctivochalasis are predominantly surgical, as conventional dry eye treatments—including artificial tears, punctal plugs, and anti-inflammatory drops—are ineffective. Existing surgical techniques include thermocautery, electrocautery, direct excision with or without amniotic membrane transplantation, and the paste-pinch-cut method. However, each of these approaches suffers from significant limitations. Thermocautery and electrocautery generate temperatures exceeding the boiling point of water, resulting in full-thickness thermal injury to the conjunctival epithelium and stroma. This leads to prolonged healing times (up to one month), significant postoperative pain, risk of corneal injury if the eye moves during treatment, and potential complications such as pyogenic granuloma formation, scarring, and persistent hyperemia. Direct excision, while avoiding thermal damage, requires operating room resources, suturing or fibrin glue application, and still entails weeks of inflammatory recovery. The paste-pinch-cut technique reduces thermal injury but remains an invasive procedure requiring specialized settings and materials.

These shortcomings underscore the need for a minimally invasive, office-based surgical technique that achieves effective conjunctival shrinkage through controlled thermal denaturation of collagen without causing charring, boiling, or collateral damage. Laser-based approaches offer the potential for such precision; however, prior attempts using visible-wavelength lasers (e.g., argon laser at 532 nm) have been limited by strong absorption in hemoglobin, leading to vessel rupture and hemorrhage. A more physiologically appropriate strategy leverages the high water content (~70%) of conjunctival tissue, suggesting that near-infrared wavelengths coinciding with water absorption peaks—particularly around 1460 nm—could enable efficient, selective, and safe heating. The development of compact, efficient, and tunable diode lasers at this wavelength now makes such an approach feasible, motivating the present invention of a novel laser thermal conjunctivoplasty system and method.

The invention introduces a laser thermal conjunctivoplasty (LTC) device, system, and method designed to achieve controlled shrinkage of redundant conjunctival tissue through precise delivery of near-infrared laser energy. Central to the invention is a handheld laser probe specifically engineered for ophthalmic use. This probe integrates a line-focused laser light source with angled surgical forceps to enable targeted, uniform heating of conjunctival folds while minimizing exposure to surrounding tissue.

The handheld laser probe incorporates a line-focused laser beam generated by passing laser light from a multimode optical fiber through a cylindrical lens. This optical configuration transforms the circular output of the fiber into an elongated focal spot measuring approximately 10 mm in length and 1 mm in width. Such a line focus ensures even energy distribution across the width of a typical conjunctival fold, promoting uniform thermal shrinkage without hotspots. The design prioritizes compactness and ergonomic handling, allowing the surgeon to maintain steady control during delicate ocular procedures.

Selection of the laser wavelength is critical to the efficacy and safety of the LTC procedure. Given that conjunctival tissue is approximately 70% water by weight, the laser wavelength is chosen to coincide with a strong absorption peak of water in the near-infrared spectrum. Two primary absorption peaks exist at approximately 1460 nm and 1940 nm. While both are suitable in principle, lasers operating at 1940 nm are typically less efficient and more costly. In contrast, diode lasers emitting at 1460 nm offer high electro-optical efficiency (>40%), commercial availability, and reasonable cost. Therefore, the preferred embodiment employs a 1460-nm diode laser, though alternative wavelengths within the 1400–1500 nm range exhibiting strong water absorption may also be used.

The handheld laser probe is operated in pulsed mode to regulate tissue temperature within a therapeutic window. Collagen shrinkage occurs optimally at temperatures between 62°C and 100°C; below 62°C, insufficient denaturation occurs, while above 100°C, tissue boiling, charring, and unintended damage ensue. Pulsed operation—with a pulse repetition rate of 1 Hz and a total treatment duration of 4 seconds (yielding four pulses)—allows heat to accumulate gradually while permitting partial cooling between pulses, thereby maintaining temperature within the desired range. The interaction between the angled forceps and the line-focused laser beam is carefully coordinated: the forceps grasp and stabilize the redundant conjunctival fold, positioning it precisely within the focal plane of the laser beam, which is oriented normal to the tissue surface to ensure uniform energy delivery.

The complete LTC system includes the handheld laser probe, a fiber-coupled 1460-nm diode laser module, a control system, and a foot pedal trigger. The laser module delivers up to 12 W of continuous-wave power through a 200-μm core multimode fiber. A low-power 650-nm aiming laser is co-aligned with the treatment beam to assist in targeting. The control system, implemented via custom-built circuits and LabVIEW-based software, allows the operator to program pulse duration, duty cycle, repetition rate, peak power, and total treatment time. Activation of the laser is controlled by a foot pedal; depression initiates the preprogrammed pulse sequence, and release immediately terminates laser emission for safety. An integrated optical shutter or modulator ensures rapid on/off switching of the laser beam.

The system schematic includes the laser diode driver, modulation circuitry, fiber coupling optics, and the handheld probe assembly. The probe’s internal optics, including the cylindrical lens, are modeled using ray-trace simulations (e.g., in OpticStudio) to optimize beam shaping and minimize losses. Key components of the probe include the SMA fiber connector, collimating optics, cylindrical focusing lens, and mechanical housing that integrates with the angled forceps via a precision adaptor. This adaptor maintains a fixed working distance and ensures perpendicular incidence of the laser beam on the tissue.

The method of laser thermal conjunctivoplasty involves grasping redundant conjunctiva with angled forceps, aligning the line-focused 1460-nm laser beam across the fold, and delivering a train of laser pulses with optimized parameters. Pulse duration is selected to be shorter than the thermal relaxation time of conjunctival tissue (~0.5 seconds for a 0.5-mm thick fold), ensuring thermal confinement. Duty cycle and peak power are tuned to achieve tissue temperatures between 62°C and 100°C, as validated by thermal imaging. Theoretical modeling of heat diffusion supports the selection of a 1-Hz repetition rate to balance heating and cooling phases.

### EXAMPLES

Ex-vivo experiments were conducted to optimize laser parameters for effective and safe conjunctival shrinkage. Porcine eyes, whose conjunctival structure closely resembles that of humans, were used as test specimens. Each eye was thawed in phosphate-buffered saline and prepared by marking a 10-mm-long region with two parallel blue lines spaced 1 mm apart using a 3D-printed tissue marker. This region served as the target for laser irradiation and enabled quantitative measurement of shrinkage.

The experimental setup included a thermal camera (Fluke TiS45) positioned to record real-time temperature changes on the conjunctival surface during laser irradiation. Simultaneously, a custom 1310-nm swept-source OCT system (50 kHz A-line rate, 15 μm transverse resolution) captured cross-sectional images before, during, and after treatment to monitor structural changes. The workflow involved acquiring baseline photographs and OCT scans, delivering the laser pulse sequence, and then capturing post-treatment images for comparison.

Thermal imaging revealed that temperature rapidly increased during each laser pulse and decayed more slowly during the off period. With a peak power of 1.51 W and 10% duty cycle (100 ms pulse), temperatures remained below 40°C—insufficient for shrinkage. At 3 W peak power and 20% duty cycle (200 ms pulse), peak temperatures reached ~88°C, yielding significant shrinkage without boiling. However, at 30% duty cycle, temperatures exceeded 100°C, causing tissue damage. Higher peak powers (e.g., 5.77 W) led to boiling even at low duty cycles.

Real-time OCT imaging visualized dynamic tissue contraction during laser exposure. Post-processing using particle image velocimetry (PIV) on sequential OCT B-scans confirmed inward movement of tissue toward the heated zone, directly demonstrating shrinkage. Microscope images quantified shrinkage: at 2.33 W, 3.1 W, and 3.66 W (20% duty cycle), shrinkage was 21%, 36%, and 45%, respectively. However, 3.66 W approached damaging temperatures. Similarly, at fixed 3 W peak power, increasing duty cycle from 10% to 30% increased shrinkage from 8% to 45%, but 30% caused surface damage.

Control experiments assessed the contribution of mechanical force from the forceps alone. When only forceps were applied (no laser), a 10% reduction in marker distance occurred due to tissue compression. After LTC, total shrinkage reached 45%, confirming that the majority of contraction resulted from thermal effects.

In-vivo animal wound healing studies are planned to evaluate clinical outcomes including conjunctival hyperemia, hemorrhage, and blanching—metrics of inflammation and vascular response not assessable in ex-vivo tissue. OCT will monitor long-term changes in conjunctival thickness and surface smoothness, while histochemical staining will assess epithelial integrity, stromal repopulation, and fibrosis.

An exemplary 1460-nm programmable laser diode system was constructed using a DILAS M1F2S22-1470.10-12C-SS5.x module (12 W max CW). Light was delivered via a 200-μm, NA 0.22 multimode fiber to the handheld probe. Custom control circuits enabled precise programming of pulse parameters via LabVIEW software. A foot pedal triggered laser emission, automatically disabling the 650-nm aiming beam during treatment. The entire system was housed in a 41 × 36 × 15 cm³ enclosure.

Characterization confirmed linear relationship between driving current and output power (threshold at 6.8 A). Spectral analysis showed a stable peak at ~1456 nm with bandwidth <4 nm—negligible compared to the ~100-nm water absorption band. Temporal measurements verified accurate pulse widths (100–300 ms) and programmable pulse counts (e.g., Fibonacci sequences), demonstrating robust control essential for clinical safety and efficacy.
