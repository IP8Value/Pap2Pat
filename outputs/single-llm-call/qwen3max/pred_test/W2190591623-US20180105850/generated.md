# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNICAL FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates to genetically engineered microorganisms, particularly eukaryotic microbial hosts such as yeast, that have been modified to overproduce L-ornithine and downstream polyamines. The invention further encompasses methods for the enhanced biosynthesis of L-ornithine through coordinated genetic modifications affecting metabolic pathways, subcellular compartmentalization, and carbon flux regulation. These engineered microorganisms are useful in industrial biotechnology for the sustainable production of high-value amino acids and their derivatives, including putrescine, spermidine, and spermine, which find applications in pharmaceuticals, nutraceuticals, agriculture, and chemical synthesis.

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

L-Ornithine is a non-proteinogenic amino acid that plays a central role in the urea cycle and serves as a key precursor for the biosynthesis of polyamines—essential molecules involved in cell growth, proliferation, and stress response. Due to its biological significance and commercial value, there is considerable interest in developing efficient microbial platforms for the large-scale production of L-ornithine. Traditionally, L-ornithine has been produced via chemical synthesis or extraction from natural sources, but these methods are often costly, environmentally taxing, and yield impure products. Microbial fermentation offers a greener, more scalable alternative.

Current preparation processes for L-ornithine primarily rely on bacterial strains, especially *Corynebacterium glutamicum* and *Escherichia coli*, which have been genetically engineered to accumulate L-ornithine by disrupting feedback inhibition and deleting competing pathways. However, these prokaryotic systems face limitations, including sensitivity to product toxicity, complex regulatory networks, and difficulties in handling oxygen-sensitive reactions. Moreover, whole-cell biotransformation approaches using isolated enzymes or resting cells often suffer from cofactor imbalances, low substrate conversion efficiency, and poor stability under industrial conditions.

A promising alternative involves the use of thermophilic enzymes, which offer enhanced stability and activity at elevated temperatures, thereby improving reaction kinetics and reducing contamination risks. However, integrating thermophilic enzymes into mesophilic hosts like *E. coli* or *C. glutamicum* can lead to misfolding, aggregation, or incompatibility with host metabolism. Furthermore, many bacterial strains used for L-ornithine production exhibit inherent limitations, such as auxotrophy for arginine (due to deletion of ornithine carbamoyltransferase), which necessitates costly supplementation during fermentation and complicates process scale-up.

These challenges underscore the need for better microbial strategies that combine robust host physiology, efficient precursor supply, precise pathway regulation, and effective product export. Eukaryotic microorganisms, particularly the yeast *Saccharomyces cerevisiae*, offer several advantages, including well-characterized genetics, tolerance to low pH and high osmolarity, and the ability to perform post-translational modifications. However, native *S. cerevisiae* does not naturally overproduce L-ornithine due to tight regulatory control, compartmentalized biosynthesis, and the Crabtree effect—a phenomenon where excess glucose is diverted to ethanol even under aerobic conditions, thereby limiting flux through the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle and reducing availability of α-ketoglutarate, the key precursor for L-ornithine synthesis. Thus, there remains a critical unmet need for engineered eukaryotic cell factories capable of high-yield, high-titer L-ornithine production without reliance on exogenous supplements or complex feeding strategies.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a recombinant eukaryotic cell factory, specifically a genetically modified strain of *Saccharomyces cerevisiae*, engineered for enhanced L-ornithine biosynthesis. This is achieved through a modular pathway rewiring (MPR) strategy that systematically optimizes three interconnected metabolic modules: (1) reduction of L-ornithine consumption, (2) enhancement of L-ornithine biosynthesis from α-ketoglutarate, and (3) improvement of carbon channeling from glucose to α-ketoglutarate.

Central to the invention is the genetic modification of ornithine carbamoyltransferase (encoded by *ARG3*) to attenuate its activity, thereby reducing the conversion of L-ornithine to L-citrulline while maintaining sufficient L-arginine biosynthesis for cellular growth—creating a “leaky” arginine auxotroph that avoids the need for external arginine supplementation. Concurrently, the invention enhances L-ornithine biosynthesis by overexpressing key enzymes in the mitochondrial acetylated derivative cycle (e.g., *ARG5,6*, *ARG7*, *ARG8*, and *ARG2*) and engineering subcellular trafficking through overexpression of mitochondrial transporters such as *ORT1* (for L-ornithine export) and *AGC1* (for glutamate import). In a preferred embodiment, the entire L-ornithine biosynthetic pathway is re-localized to the cytosol by expressing heterologous bacterial genes (*argAEC*, *argBEc* from *E. coli* and *argJCg*, *argCCg*, *argDCg* from *C. glutamicum*), thereby bypassing mitochondrial constraints and improving pathway efficiency.

Furthermore, the invention enhances α-ketoglutarate biosynthesis by overexpressing TCA cycle enzymes (e.g., pyruvate carboxylase, citrate synthase, aconitase, isocitrate dehydrogenase) and modulating the Crabtree effect through overexpression of *MTH1-ΔT* (a truncated form of the hexose transporter regulator that reduces glucose uptake) or alternative NADH oxidases (e.g., *HaAOX1* from *Hansenula anomala*), thereby redirecting carbon flux from ethanol to the TCA cycle.

The engineered strains also enable the production of polyamines. By increasing ornithine decarboxylase activity (e.g., via *SPE1* overexpression) and down-regulating ornithine decarboxylase antizyme (*OAZ1*), the invention enhances flux toward putrescine. Subsequent overexpression of spermidine synthase (*SPE3*) and S-adenosylmethionine decarboxylase (*SPE2*) enables spermidine production, while introduction of heterologous spermine synthase (*SPS*) facilitates spermine biosynthesis. Additional modifications include down-regulation of polyamine uptake systems (*AGP2*, *SAM3*, *DUR3*) and overexpression of export proteins (*QDR3*, *YAP1*) to facilitate product secretion and mitigate toxicity.

Thus, the invention provides a versatile platform for the sustainable, high-yield production of L-ornithine and its derivatives in a eukaryotic host, overcoming the limitations of conventional bacterial systems and native yeast metabolism.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention is motivated by the selection of *Saccharomyces cerevisiae* as a chassis host for metabolic engineering due to its well-established use in industrial fermentation, GRAS (Generally Recognized As Safe) status, and extensive genetic toolkit. *S. cerevisiae* offers distinct advantages over bacterial hosts, including superior tolerance to organic acids, robustness under varying pH and osmotic conditions, and the capacity for complex metabolic engineering through homologous recombination and CRISPR-based techniques. However, native *S. cerevisiae* tightly regulates L-arginine biosynthesis, which indirectly controls L-ornithine levels through feedback inhibition and transcriptional repression. Moreover, the L-ornithine biosynthetic pathway is compartmentalized: α-ketoglutarate is generated in the mitochondria via the TCA cycle, converted to L-glutamate in the cytosol, and then reimported into the mitochondria for conversion to L-ornithine through a five-step acetylated derivative cycle. This spatial separation creates bottlenecks in metabolite trafficking and limits pathway flux.

Constructing an L-ornithine over-producing yeast strain is challenging due to multiple layers of regulation, including nitrogen catabolite repression, arginine-mediated feedback inhibition, and the Crabtree effect. To address these challenges, the invention employs conventional molecular biology techniques, including PCR-based gene assembly, promoter replacement, chromosomal integration, and plasmid-based expression, guided by the MOPE (Modular Pathway Engineering) and DNA assembler strategies.

As used herein, a “modified” host organism refers to a microorganism whose genome has been altered by genetic engineering to introduce, delete, or modulate the expression of one or more genes. An “open reading frame (ORF)” denotes a continuous stretch of codons that begins with a start codon and ends with a stop codon, encoding a polypeptide. “Recombinant” describes nucleic acids, cells, or organisms that contain genetic material introduced by artificial means. “Recombinant eukaryotic cells” and “recombinant prokaryotic cells” refer to genetically engineered eukaryotic or prokaryotic hosts, respectively. “Endogenous” nucleic acids or proteins are those naturally present in the host, whereas “heterologous” sequences are derived from a different species. A “gene” is a segment of DNA encoding a functional product, and the “genome” is the complete set of genetic material in an organism. “Nucleic acid” encompasses DNA and RNA. “Peptide,” “polypeptide,” and “protein” refer to chains of amino acids of varying lengths, with “enzyme” denoting a catalytic protein. A “promoter” is a DNA sequence that initiates transcription, and a “terminator” signals its end. “Overexpression” means increased transcription and/or translation relative to wild-type levels, leading to “overproducing” phenotypes. The “Crabtree effect” describes the tendency of *S. cerevisiae* to produce ethanol aerobically at high glucose concentrations. A “vector” is a DNA molecule used to deliver foreign genetic material. “Sequence identity” refers to the percentage of identical residues between two aligned sequences.

The invention provides a modified eukaryotic cell for L-ornithine overproduction, preferably from the genus *Saccharomyces*, including species such as *S. cerevisiae*, *S. bayanus*, and *S. paradoxus*. Prokaryotic cell types may include Gram-negative bacteria (e.g., *Escherichia*, *Pseudomonas*, *Salmonella*) and Gram-positive bacteria (e.g., *Bacillus*, *Corynebacterium*, *Streptomyces*).

The modified yeast cell is engineered to reduce L-ornithine consumption by attenuating ornithine carbamoyltransferase activity via promoter replacement of *ARG3* with weaker promoters (e.g., *HXT1* or *KEX2*), and by deleting *CAR2* to eliminate ornithine transaminase activity. Simultaneously, arginase activity is increased by overexpressing *CAR1*, enhancing the conversion of L-arginine to L-ornithine and reducing feedback inhibition.

To enhance carbon channeling from α-ketoglutarate, the invention overexpresses enzymes in the L-ornithine biosynthetic pathway, including *ARG5,6*, *ARG7*, *ARG8*, and *ARG2*, and re-localizes the pathway to the cytosol using heterologous bacterial genes. Mitochondrial trafficking is optimized by overexpressing *ORT1* and *AGC1*. Glutamate biosynthesis is enhanced via *GDH1* overexpression.

For α-ketoglutarate supply, the invention overexpresses pyruvate carboxylase (*PYC2*), citrate synthase (*CIT1*), aconitase (*ACO2*), isocitrate dehydrogenase (*IDP1*), and the E1 alpha subunit of pyruvate dehydrogenase (*PDA1*). Carbon flux is further improved by overexpressing alternative oxidases (*HaAOX1*, *NDI1*) and attenuating glucose uptake via *MTH1-ΔT* overexpression.

The engineered cells convert carbon sources (e.g., glucose) to L-ornithine, which can be incorporated into pharmaceuticals, food additives, or polymer precursors. Genetic modifications also enable polyamine production: putrescine via ornithine decarboxylase (*SPE1*) overexpression and *OAZ1* deletion; spermidine via *SPE2* and *SPE3* overexpression; and spermine via heterologous *SPS*. Export is facilitated by overexpressing *QDR3* and *YAP1*, while uptake is blocked by deleting *AGP2*, *SAM3*, and *DUR3*. Toxicity resistance is enhanced by down-regulating *SR* kinase, *FMS1*, and polyamine acetyltransferase.

Agmatine production is achieved by deleting *CAR1*, overexpressing *ARG3*, *ARG1*, *ARG4*, and introducing heterologous arginine decarboxylase (*speA* from *E. coli*). Putrescine is then produced via agmatinase. Spermidine can also be synthesized via condensation of L-aspartate semialdehyde and putrescine using heterologous enzymes (*CANSDH*, *CANSDC*).

In prokaryotic embodiments, *E. coli* WL3110 is modified by overexpressing ornithine biosynthetic genes and *speE/speD* for spermidine production. *C. glutamicum* ATCC 13032 is engineered by deleting *argF* and overexpressing ornithine decarboxylase.

## EXAMPLES

### Strain Construction of Ornithine-Overproducing Strains

Engineered yeast strains were constructed using the MOPE strategy and DNA assembler method. Promoters, terminators, and genes were amplified from genomic DNA and assembled into expression modules via one-step PCR. These modules were co-transformed into *S. cerevisiae* CEN.PK113-11C, and correct assembly was verified by PCR and restriction digestion. The *ARG3* promoter was replaced with weaker promoters (*HXT1*, *KEX2*), and genes were integrated into the chromosome using homologous recombination.

### L-Arginine Leaky Auxotroph Enables L-Ornithine Overproduction

The L-ornithine pathway is regulated by arginine-mediated feedback. Replacing the *ARG3* promoter with *HXT1* or *KEX2* reduced OTC activity, enabling L-ornithine accumulation (24–42 mg/L) while maintaining sufficient arginine for growth. Intracellular arginine decreased only 30%, suggesting compensatory upregulation of the ornithine pathway via Gcn4p.

### Pathway Re-Localization and Subcellular Trafficking Engineering Elevates L-Ornithine Synthesis

Overexpression of mitochondrial pathway genes (*ARG5,6*, *ARG7*, *ARG8*) increased titers to 59 mg/L. Adding *ARG2* (acetylornithine synthase) raised it to 80 mg/L. Overexpressing *ORT1* (ornithine exporter) and *AGC1* (glutamate importer) further increased titers to 149 mg/L. Cytosolic relocation of the entire pathway using bacterial genes yielded 192 mg/L, outperforming mitochondrial engineering.

### ‘Crabtree Negative’ S. cerevisiae Construction Enables Efficient Carbon Channeling to L-Ornithine

Overexpressing *PDA1*, TCA enzymes, and *HaAOX1/NDI1* increased titers to 278 mg/L. Most dramatically, *MTH1-ΔT* overexpression abolished ethanol production and boosted L-ornithine to 778 mg/L by reducing glucose uptake. Fed-batch fermentation of an optimized strain achieved 5.1 g/L.

### Example 5

Urea cycle engineering via *CAR1* overexpression increased titers to 1,041 mg/L. For polyamines, *OAZ1* deletion and *SPE1* overexpression enhanced putrescine. *SPE2/SPE3* overexpression enabled spermidine production. Heterologous *CANSDH/CANSDC* provided an alternative route. Spermine was produced by expressing *SPS*. Polyamine export was enhanced by deleting uptake genes (*AGP2*, *SAM3*, *DUR3*) and overexpressing exporters (*QDR3*, *YAP1*). Agmatine production was achieved by deleting *CAR1*, overexpressing arginine biosynthetic genes, and introducing *E. coli speA*. In *E. coli* WL3110, ornithine and putrescine production was enhanced by overexpressing *speE/speD*. In *C. glutamicum* ATCC 13032, ornithine production was increased by deleting *argF* and overexpressing ornithine decarboxylase.
