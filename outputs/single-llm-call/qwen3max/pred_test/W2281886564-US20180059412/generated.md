# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates generally to the field of integrated photonics and, more specifically, to micro- and nano-photonic resonators. These resonators are critical components in a wide range of photonic devices, including optical sensors, filters, modulators, switches, lasers, and quantum information processing systems. Micro- and nano-photonic resonators function by confining light within sub-wavelength volumes for extended periods, thereby enhancing light-matter interactions through high quality (Q) factors and small mode volumes. Such resonators are typically fabricated from high-refractive-index semiconductor materials such as gallium arsenide (GaAs), silicon (Si), or gallium nitride (GaN), and can take various geometric forms, including disks, rings, photonic crystal cavities, and slot waveguides. The performance of these devices is highly sensitive to their physical dimensions and surface morphology, which directly determine their resonant wavelengths and optical losses. Consequently, precise post-fabrication tuning of resonant frequencies is essential to compensate for inevitable nanofabrication imperfections and to enable large-scale integration of multiple resonators with spectrally matched responses. The invention disclosed herein provides a novel, scalable, and high-precision method for permanently tuning the optical resonance of such micro- and nano-photonic resonators through a resonant cavity-enhanced photoelectrochemical (PEC) etching process.

## PRIOR ART

Traditional fabrication of micro- and nano-photonic resonators relies heavily on clean room techniques such as electron-beam lithography, reactive ion etching, and selective wet etching. While these methods enable the creation of intricate photonic structures, they suffer from inherent limitations in dimensional control at the atomic scale, leading to unavoidable variations in resonator size and shape across a single chip or wafer. These nanofabrication imperfections result in significant deviations—often several nanometers—in the actual resonant wavelengths compared to design targets, thereby hindering the practical deployment of devices requiring spectral uniformity, such as wavelength-division multiplexing circuits or coupled-resonator optical waveguides. Reproducibility constraints further exacerbate this issue, as even minor fluctuations in etch depth, sidewall roughness, or material composition can drastically alter optical performance.

Optical resonators have found diverse applications due to their ability to enhance light-matter interactions. In sensing, shifts in resonance frequency upon analyte binding enable label-free detection with high sensitivity. In display technologies, resonators can generate structural color or modulate light emission. As optical transistors or switches, they offer ultrafast modulation with low power consumption. Research into miniaturized optical resonators has intensified with the push toward on-chip photonic integration, where footprint, energy efficiency, and compatibility with CMOS processes are paramount.

To address fabrication-induced spectral mismatches, numerous tuning techniques have been explored. These include thermal tuning via localized heaters, carrier injection, mechanical stress application, and deposition of functional coatings. Among prior art methods for active tuning, nitrogen deposition alters the local refractive index but lacks permanence and precision. Photochromic thin films offer reversible tuning but suffer from fatigue and limited environmental stability. Tension application through piezoelectric actuators enables mechanical deformation but is complex to implement at scale and may introduce mechanical instability. Photo-addressable polyelectrolyte functionalization allows spatially selective index changes but requires chemical functionalization steps incompatible with many semiconductor platforms. Mechanical action via atomic force microscopy tips can locally deform resonators but is serial, slow, and impractical for mass production.

A major limitation of existing tuning approaches is their inability to provide permanent, high-precision, and scalable correction of resonant wavelengths without degrading optical quality. Most methods either induce temporary changes, require complex external actuation, or lack the spatial selectivity needed to tune individual resonators within dense arrays. Furthermore, many techniques inadvertently increase optical losses or are incompatible with standard photonic integration workflows. There remains a critical unmet need for a post-fabrication tuning method that is permanent, fast, scalable to large numbers of resonators, compatible with industrial manufacturing, and capable of sub-picometer spectral precision without compromising the quality factor of the resonator.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention introduces an improved method for tuning optical resonators based on resonant cavity-enhanced photoelectrochemical (PEC) etching. This method overcomes the limitations of prior art by enabling permanent, high-precision, and scalable adjustment of resonant wavelengths through controlled material removal. The core of the invention involves injecting light into an optical resonator at or near its resonant wavelength while the resonator is immersed in or exposed to a fluid containing ions capable of participating in a PEC reaction. The injected light is amplified within the resonator due to its high quality factor, leading to localized generation of charge carriers—even when the photon energy is below the semiconductor bandgap—via mechanisms such as two-photon absorption or excitation through mid-gap states. These carriers drive a localized electrochemical reaction that etches the resonator material, thereby reducing its physical dimensions and inducing a predictable blue shift in its resonant wavelength.

The tuning procedure is executed through consecutive wavelength sweeps of the input laser, allowing real-time monitoring and incremental adjustment of the resonance. Optical coupling to the resonator is achieved via far-field or near-field techniques, preferably using an integrated waveguide that supports evanescent coupling. In the preferred embodiment, a suspended waveguide with nanoscale transverse dimensions is positioned in proximity to the resonator, enabling efficient transfer of light into whispering gallery modes (WGMs) of disk or ring resonators. The fluid environment, which may be a liquid (e.g., water, ammonia, isopropanol) or a humid gas, provides the ionic species necessary for the PEC process. Critically, etching occurs predominantly within the spatial region of the optical mode due to resonant enhancement, ensuring high spatial selectivity and minimal impact on non-resonant structures.

This method offers several key advantages: it is permanent, as it physically modifies the resonator geometry; it is scalable, as a single laser can simultaneously tune multiple resonators through a collective alignment process; it is fast, achieving nanometer-scale shifts in seconds to minutes; and it is compatible with industrial settings, requiring only standard optical probing and fluid handling equipment. The invention also encompasses photonic devices obtainable by this method, including arrays of spectrally aligned resonators for advanced photonic integrated circuits.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

### The Photonic Device

The photonic device of the present invention comprises at least one optical resonator fabricated from a semiconductor material susceptible to photoelectrochemical etching, such as gallium arsenide (GaAs), silicon, or gallium nitride. In a preferred embodiment, the resonator is a disk-shaped structure supporting whispering gallery modes (WGMs), with a thickness of approximately 320 nm and a radius of about 1 μm, though other geometries—including rings, photonic crystals, and slot cavities—are equally applicable. The resonator is typically isolated from the underlying substrate by a pedestal layer of a selectively etchable material, such as aluminum gallium arsenide (AlGaAs) in the case of GaAs resonators, to minimize substrate-induced optical losses. Optical coupling to the resonator is facilitated by an adjacent waveguide, preferably fabricated from the same semiconductor material and suspended in close proximity to the resonator to enable strong evanescent field overlap. This waveguide integrates a taper section with nanoscale transverse dimensions to efficiently couple light from an external source into the resonator’s WGMs. The entire structure is designed to maintain optical confinement even when immersed in a transparent fluid, owing to the high refractive index contrast between the semiconductor and the surrounding medium.

### Installation Set-Up

In the installation set-up, light from a tunable laser source is coupled into the input port of the integrated waveguide. The laser wavelength is swept across a range encompassing the expected resonant wavelength of the target mode. As light propagates through the waveguide, a portion evanescently couples into the resonator when the laser wavelength matches a resonant mode, resulting in a dip in the transmitted optical power. This transmission spectrum is monitored in real time using a photodetector placed at the waveguide output. The depth, width, and position of the resonance dip provide information about the coupling strength, quality factor (Q), and resonant wavelength, respectively. This spectroscopic feedback loop is integral to the tuning process, enabling closed-loop control of the etching progression.

### Method for Tuning an Optical Resonator (FIG. 7)

The method for tuning an individual optical resonator begins with immersing the photonic chip in a fluid containing ions that support PEC reactions—such as deionized water, which provides hydroxide ions for GaAs etching. A tunable laser is then set to the initial resonant wavelength of the target mode and light is injected into the waveguide. Due to the high Q factor of the resonator, the circulating optical intensity inside the cavity is significantly enhanced, even with modest input power. This enhanced field generates electron-hole pairs via sub-bandgap absorption mechanisms, likely mediated by surface mid-gap states. These carriers drive the oxidation and dissolution of the semiconductor material at the resonator surface in contact with the fluid, effectively etching the structure. As material is removed, the resonator’s effective size decreases, causing a continuous blue shift of its resonant wavelength. The etching process is halted by switching off the laser once the desired resonance is achieved. In a preferred embodiment, infrared light (e.g., around 1300–1600 nm) is used to pump GaAs resonators, leveraging two-photon absorption or defect-mediated linear absorption to initiate etching despite the photon energy being below the GaAs bandgap (~870 nm). This approach ensures high selectivity, as only resonators at resonance experience sufficient field enhancement to trigger etching, while off-resonant structures remain unaffected.

### Method for Tuning Several Resonators at a Targeted Value (FIG. 8)

To tune multiple resonators to a common target wavelength, the method exploits the resonant nature of the PEC process. Initially, an array of nominally identical resonators exhibits a distribution of resonant wavelengths due to fabrication variations. The chip is immersed in the PEC-active fluid, and the laser is tuned to the longest-wavelength resonance (corresponding to the largest resonator). Etching begins selectively in this resonator, blue-shifting its resonance. As the laser wavelength is slowly swept toward shorter wavelengths, the etching continues, and once the shifting resonance aligns with that of the next-longest resonator, both structures begin to etch simultaneously. This process cascades: as more resonators spectrally align, they are collectively etched, converging toward a common resonant wavelength. After a single continuous sweep or a series of discrete steps, all resonators in the array achieve spectral overlap. This collective tuning requires no individual addressing or identification of each resonator, making it inherently scalable to large arrays. Experimental demonstrations have shown successful alignment of five GaAs disk resonators with final resonance matching better than 10 pm.

### Different Embodiments for Varying the Laser Wavelength in Order to Tune Optical Resonators

Two primary operational modes are envisioned for wavelength variation during tuning. In the discrete sweeps technique, the laser rapidly scans back and forth across the resonance, with each sweep constituting a “tuning cycle” that imparts a quantized blue shift (e.g., 7.2 pm per cycle in GaAs). This mode enables precise, stepwise control and facilitates real-time spectral monitoring. Alternatively, in the continuous shift technique, the laser wavelength is dynamically adjusted to track the moving resonance in real time, maintaining optimal etching conditions throughout. This mode achieves even higher precision—potentially below 1 pm—and is particularly advantageous for collective tuning of multiple resonators. Both techniques are applicable to single or multiple resonator tuning scenarios and can be selected based on required speed, precision, and available instrumentation.

### Parameters and Experimental Data

The PEC fluid may be aqueous or organic, provided it contains ionic species that facilitate semiconductor dissolution. For GaAs, water suffices due to native oxide formation and hydrolysis; for silicon, fluoride-based solutions are preferred. Fluid conductivity directly influences etching rate, with higher ion concentration accelerating material removal. The method is flexible with respect to resonator geometry and material system, having been demonstrated on GaAs disks but extendable to Si rings, GaN photonic crystals, and others. Sweeping rate controls etching speed: slower sweeps allow more material removal per unit wavelength shift. Laser power linearly scales the etching rate—e.g., 1 μW of coupled power yields ~0.5–1 nm radius reduction per second in GaAs. Tuned resonators exhibit preserved or improved quality factors (e.g., Q increasing from 41,000 to 70,000 post-tuning), indicating surface smoothing during etching. The technique is scalable across resonator types and compatible with wafer-level processing.

### Characteristics of the Tuned Optical Resonator

Tuned resonators display sharp, high-contrast resonance dips with quality factors equal to or exceeding pre-tuning values, confirming that the etching process does not introduce additional scattering losses. Spectral precision is exceptional: shifts as small as 8 pm in effective radius (less than 1/30th of an atomic monolayer) are achievable, corresponding to sub-picometer resonance control. The tuning is permanent, as it results from irreversible material removal. In collective tuning, multiple resonators converge to a common wavelength with errors below 10 pm, enabling dense wavelength-channel multiplexing. The spatial selectivity of etching—confined to the optical mode volume—ensures neighboring structures remain intact. The method applies universally to any semiconductor exhibiting PEC activity and any resonator architecture supporting high-Q modes, offering unmatched precision, permanence, and scalability for next-generation photonic integrated circuits.
