# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

Prion diseases, also known as transmissible spongiform encephalopathies (TSEs), constitute a group of invariably fatal neurodegenerative disorders affecting both humans and animals. These diseases are characterized by the accumulation of an abnormal, misfolded isoform of the cellular prion protein (PrPC), designated PrPSc, which exhibits partial resistance to proteolytic degradation and possesses infectious properties in the absence of nucleic acid. Human prion diseases include sporadic, genetic, and acquired forms such as Creutzfeldt-Jakob disease (CJD), Gerstmann-Sträussler-Scheinker syndrome (GSS), and fatal familial insomnia (FFI). Animal counterparts include scrapie in sheep and goats, bovine spongiform encephalopathy (BSE) in cattle, and chronic wasting disease (CWD) in cervids. The central pathological hallmark of these conditions is the progressive spongiform degeneration of the central nervous system, accompanied by neuronal loss, astrogliosis, and microglial activation, ultimately leading to severe neurological dysfunction and death.

Prion propagation occurs through a conformational conversion process wherein the normal, alpha-helix-rich PrPC is refolded into the beta-sheet-enriched, aggregation-prone PrPSc. This newly formed PrPSc acts as a template to catalyze further conversion of PrPC, resulting in exponential accumulation of the pathological isoform. The mechanism of human prion disease transmission varies: sporadic CJD arises spontaneously with unknown etiology; familial forms result from inherited mutations in the PRNP gene; and acquired forms may occur through iatrogenic exposure (e.g., contaminated surgical instruments or growth hormone preparations) or dietary intake of infected tissue, as seen in variant CJD linked to BSE. Despite decades of research, no effective therapeutic intervention has been established for any form of human prion disease, underscoring the urgent need for novel anti-prion agents.

Numerous experimental anti-prion drugs have been evaluated in preclinical models, yet most have failed to demonstrate efficacy in human trials. Compounds such as pentosan polysulfate, quinacrine, chlorpromazine, flupirtine, and tetracyclines have shown variable activity in vitro or in animal models but have not translated into clinically meaningful outcomes. A major limitation of many candidate therapeutics is their inability to cross the blood-brain barrier at concentrations sufficient to exert anti-prion effects without causing significant toxicity. Additionally, the lack of robust, high-throughput screening platforms that accurately model human prion pathogenesis has hindered drug discovery efforts.

To address this gap, cell culture models of persistent prion infection have been developed and widely adopted for the initial screening of potential anti-prion compounds. These models, including prion-infected murine neuroblastoma (ScN2a), catecholaminergic neuronal (ScCAD5), and mouse embryonic fibroblast (ScMEF) cell lines, recapitulate key aspects of prion replication and allow for quantitative assessment of PrPSc levels via immunoblotting, immunofluorescence, and real-time quaking-induced conversion (RT-QuIC) assays. Such systems provide a critical bridge between in vitro biochemical studies and in vivo animal models, enabling rapid evaluation of compound efficacy, cytotoxicity, and mechanism of action.

One promising therapeutic strategy involves the induction of autophagy—a conserved intracellular degradation pathway that delivers cytoplasmic cargo, including protein aggregates, to lysosomes for clearance. Pharmacological stimulation of autophagy has been shown to enhance the degradation of PrPSc in multiple experimental models. Agents such as rapamycin, lithium, trehalose, and imatinib have demonstrated anti-prion effects attributable, at least in part, to autophagy-mediated clearance. This approach leverages the cell’s intrinsic quality control machinery to eliminate the pathological prion isoform, offering a potentially broad-spectrum and physiologically relevant intervention.

AR-12 (also known as OSU-03012) is a celecoxib-derived small molecule that lacks cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2) inhibitory activity but retains potent anti-tumor and anti-infective properties. Originally developed as a phosphoinositide-dependent kinase-1 (PDK1) inhibitor, AR-12 has demonstrated activity against a range of pathogens, including viruses, bacteria, and fungi. Mechanistically, AR-12 downregulates host chaperone proteins such as GRP78, leading to endoplasmic reticulum stress, upregulation of PERK and Atg13, and subsequent induction of autophagy. Importantly, AR-12 is orally bioavailable, crosses the blood-brain barrier efficiently, and has undergone Phase I clinical trials in oncology, establishing a preliminary safety profile in humans. These attributes position AR-12 as a compelling candidate for repurposing in the treatment of prion diseases, particularly given its demonstrated ability to stimulate autophagy—a pathway directly implicated in PrPSc clearance.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides methods for reducing prion levels in a subject afflicted with or at risk of developing a prion disease by administering a therapeutically effective amount of AR-12. The methods encompass the use of AR-12 to decrease the levels of the pathological prion protein isoform PrPSc in neuronal and non-neuronal cells, both in vitro and in vivo. AR-12 achieves this reduction through the induction of autophagy, a cellular degradation pathway that facilitates the lysosomal clearance of aggregated proteins, including PrPSc. The invention further includes pharmaceutical compositions comprising AR-12 formulated for various routes of administration, including oral, parenteral, intranasal, and intrathecal delivery, as well as kits containing AR-12 for diagnostic or therapeutic use in prion disease management.

Additionally, the invention provides methods for reducing prion levels using AR-14, a structural analogue of AR-12. AR-14 demonstrates potent anti-prion activity at nanomolar to low micromolar concentrations, effectively reducing PrPSc levels in multiple prion-infected cell lines, including ScN2a, ScCAD5, and ScMEF cells. Like AR-12, AR-14 induces autophagy, as evidenced by increased conversion of LC3-I to LC3-II, and its anti-prion effect is partially dependent on functional autophagy machinery. Long-term treatment with AR-14 results in sustained clearance of prion seeding activity in certain cell models, although its efficacy may vary depending on the cell type and prion strain. The invention thus encompasses the use of AR-14 alone or in combination with other therapeutic agents for the treatment of prion diseases, leveraging its favorable pharmacokinetic and safety profile.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

Prion diseases are a class of fatal neurodegenerative disorders caused by the misfolding of the cellular prion protein (PrPC) into a pathological, self-propagating isoform known as PrPSc. The term "prion" refers to this infectious protein particle, which lacks nucleic acid yet is capable of transmitting disease through conformational templating. Prions are composed primarily of PrPSc, which aggregates into amyloid fibrils and accumulates in the brain, leading to neuronal dysfunction and death. The invention provides methods of reducing prion levels by administering AR-12 or AR-14, which significantly decrease PrPSc in persistently infected cell cultures.

The methods of reducing prion levels involve contacting prion-infected cells with AR-12 under conditions that promote autophagy and PrPSc degradation. In specific embodiments, AR-12 treatment is carried out at concentrations ranging from 0.5 µM to 3 µM for neuronal cell lines such as ScN2a and ScCAD5, and from 0.5 µM to 3 µM for non-neuronal ScMEF cells. Treatment for 72 hours with a single dose of AR-12 results in a dose-dependent reduction of PrPSc, with an EC50 of approximately 1.5 µM in ScN2a cells, 4 µM in ScCAD5 cells, and 2 µM in ScMEF cells. Critically, these effective concentrations are below the cytotoxic threshold, as the LD50 for AR-12 is 5 µM in ScN2a and ScMEF cells and 9 µM in ScCAD5 cells, indicating a favorable therapeutic window.

Similarly, methods of reducing prion levels using AR-14 involve treatment of prion-infected cells with concentrations from 0.1 µM to 2 µM for 72 hours. AR-14 exhibits an EC50 of 1.5 µM in ScN2a cells and demonstrates significant PrPSc reduction at 1.5 and 2 µM. Cytotoxicity assays reveal that AR-14 is non-toxic up to 2 µM in ScN2a cells (LD50 = 4 µM) and up to 3 µM in ScMEF cells (LD50 = 5 µM), supporting its use at efficacious yet safe doses.

AR-12, chemically known as 3-(4-fluorophenyl)-1-(4-methylsulfonylphenyl)-1H-pyrazole-4-carboxylic acid, is a celecoxib derivative lacking COX-2 inhibitory activity. Analogs of AR-12, including AR-14, share core structural features that confer anti-prion and autophagy-inducing properties. AR-14 is defined by its specific chemical structure, which enhances potency and selectivity for prion clearance.

A key diagnostic feature distinguishing PrPSc from PrPC is its relative resistance to proteinase K (PK) digestion. PrPC is completely degraded by PK, whereas PrPSc yields a protease-resistant core fragment detectable by immunoblotting. This property is exploited in the invention to quantify prion levels following AR-12 or AR-14 treatment.

Cell culture media used include OptiMEM Glutamax supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum for N2a and CAD5 cells, and DMEM Glutamax with 10% FBS for MEFs. Immunoblotting techniques involve cell lysis in Triton X-100/DOC buffer, PK digestion (20 µg/ml, 30 min, 37°C), methanol precipitation, SDS-PAGE, and probing with anti-PrP monoclonal antibody 4H11. Cell lysis and PK analysis are performed in parallel with and without PK to confirm PrPSc identity.

Immunoblot analysis confirms dose-dependent PrPSc reduction by AR-12 and AR-14 across cell lines. Cell culture conditions maintain 5% CO2 at 37°C. Cell lines used include ScN2a (22L-infected neuroblastoma), ScCAD5 (22L-infected catecholaminergic neurons), and ScMEF (22L-, RML-, or Me7-infected fibroblasts).

Viability is assessed via XTT assay, measuring mitochondrial reductase activity. The real-time quaking-induced conversion (RT-QuIC) assay detects prion seeding activity by amplifying minute amounts of PrPSc using recombinant PrP substrate and Thioflavin T fluorescence. Recombinant mouse PrP (aa 23–231) is expressed in E. coli, purified via Ni-NTA chromatography, and refolded for RT-QuIC.

RT-QuIC involves 100 µl reactions with 0.1 mg/ml rPrP, 10 µM ThT, and serial dilutions of cell lysate, incubated at 42°C with cyclic shaking. Fluorescence is monitored every 15 min; positivity is defined as >50% replicates exceeding cutoff (mean + 5 SD of negative controls).

Immunoblot analysis of AR-12-treated cells shows near-complete PrPSc elimination after 72 hours. AR-12 induces autophagy, evidenced by time-dependent LC3-II accumulation in N2a, CAD5, and MEF cells within 2–6 hours. Non-toxic concentrations (≤3 µM) are used throughout.

In ScN2a cells, AR-12 reduces PrPSc by >90% at 3 µM. In ScMEFs, 3 µM AR-12 eliminates detectable PrPSc. AR-12 exhibits broad anti-prion activity across prion strains (22L, RML, Me7).

Similarly, AR-14-treated cells show PrPSc reduction, with 2 µM AR-14 decreasing PrPSc to <10% in ScN2a. Non-toxic concentrations (≤2 µM) are employed. AR-14 exerts anti-prion effects at nanomolar concentrations in sensitive assays.

Long-term AR-12 treatment (3 µM, 20 days) cures ScN2a cells of 22L infection, with no PrPSc or RT-QuIC signal post-treatment and after 20 drug-free days. RT-QuIC confirms loss of prion conversion activity. In ScMEF cells, both AR-12 and AR-14 achieve complete cure, with no prion recurrence after treatment cessation.

Autophagy induction is confirmed in N2a, MEF, and CAD5 cells via LC3-II upregulation. ATG5 knock-out (KO) in N2a cells abolishes LC3-II formation, confirming autophagy deficiency. AR-12 reduces PrPSc in both wild-type and Atg5-KO ScN2a cells, though less potently in KO cells, indicating partial autophagy dependence. Similarly, AR-14 shows reduced efficacy in KO cells, confirming autophagy involvement.

Administration routes include oral (tablets, capsules), parenteral (IV, IM), sublingual, intranasal, intrathecal, and topical. Pharmaceutical preparations comprise AR-12 or AR-14 with pharmaceutically acceptable carriers. Unit dosage forms contain 1–500 mg. Liposomal suspensions enhance delivery. Combination therapy with PDE5 inhibitors or other anti-prion agents is contemplated.

Solubilization uses co-solvents (e.g., DMSO, ethanol) or surfactants. Controlled release formulations employ biodegradable polymers (e.g., PLGA). Kits include vials of AR-12/AR-14 with instructions. Multiple-dose containers ensure stability.

Concentrations in compositions range from 0.1–100 mg/mL. Dosage schedules include once to four times daily. Doses may be administered as single or divided doses. Enteric coatings protect from gastric degradation. Oral compositions include antacids, inert diluents, or edible carriers. Solid forms contain binders (e.g., cellulose), excipients (e.g., lactose), and lubricants (e.g., magnesium stearate). Syrups include sweeteners and preservatives.

Parenteral solutions/suspensions use sterile diluents (e.g., saline), antimicrobials (e.g., benzyl alcohol), and antioxidants (e.g., ascorbate). Liposomal suspensions for injection enhance CNS delivery. Controlled release parenteral formulations provide sustained drug levels.

Compounds may be administered enterally or parenterally. Sustained-release solid dosage forms prolong effect. Dosage frequency ranges from 1–4 times daily. Therapeutically effective amounts are determined by achieving PrPSc reduction over time. Systemic administration maintains blood levels of 0.5–10 µM. Localized delivery permits lower concentrations.

Dosage varies with patient age, weight, condition, and concomitant medications. Additional uses include treatment of other protein misfolding diseases (e.g., Alzheimer’s, Parkinson’s) where autophagy induction is beneficial. Variations include prodrugs, salts, and stereoisomers of AR-12 and AR-14.
