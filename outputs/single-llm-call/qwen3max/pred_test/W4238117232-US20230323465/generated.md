# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates to the field of oncology, specifically to the diagnosis and management of thyroid cancer. More particularly, the invention provides novel molecular diagnostic methods and compositions for distinguishing benign from malignant thyroid lesions, including the accurate classification of noninvasive follicular thyroid neoplasm with papillary-like nuclear features (NIFTP) versus invasive follicular variant of papillary thyroid carcinoma (FVPTC). The invention further encompasses kits, assays, and therapeutic strategies guided by the detection of specific splice variant isoforms of a defined gene panel in fine needle aspiration (FNA) samples obtained from patients with thyroid nodules.

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

Thyroid cancer is among the most rapidly increasing malignancies worldwide, with its incidence in the United States having nearly quadrupled over the past fifteen years, primarily driven by the rising detection of papillary thyroid carcinoma (PTC). Thyroid nodules are exceedingly common, detectable by ultrasound in more than half of the adult population, and fine needle aspiration (FNA) cytopathology remains the cornerstone of preoperative evaluation. Despite its widespread use, FNA yields indeterminate results in up to 30% of cases, categorized under the Bethesda System as atypia of undetermined significance/follicular lesion of undetermined significance (Bethesda III), follicular neoplasm/suspicious for a follicular neoplasm (Bethesda IV), and suspicious for malignancy (Bethesda V). This diagnostic ambiguity stems from inherent limitations in morphological assessment alone, often leading to unnecessary diagnostic surgeries for benign disease.

The diagnostic landscape has been further complicated by the recent reclassification of a subset of tumors previously diagnosed as follicular variant of papillary thyroid carcinoma (FVPTC) to noninvasive follicular thyroid neoplasm with papillary-like nuclear features (NIFTP). NIFTP is now recognized as an indolent neoplasm with minimal to no malignant potential, necessitating less aggressive clinical management compared to true carcinomas. Critically, NIFTP cannot be differentiated from invasive FVPTC based on cytological features alone; definitive diagnosis requires postoperative histopathological examination of the entire nodule to assess for capsular or vascular invasion. This limitation underscores a significant unmet need for preoperative diagnostic tools capable of accurately identifying NIFTP.

In response to these challenges, molecular testing has emerged as a complementary strategy to refine preoperative risk stratification. Commercially available platforms such as ThyroSeq v3, Afirma GSC, and ThyraMIR/ThygenX analyze mutations, gene fusions, or gene expression profiles to aid in decision-making. However, these tests suffer from notable limitations. Many driver mutations, such as RAS, are shared across benign adenomas, NIFTP, and malignant carcinomas, reducing specificity. Moreover, because these tests were developed and validated before the introduction of NIFTP, they typically classify NIFTP as “suspicious” or “positive” for malignancy, thereby inflating false-positive rates and undermining their positive predictive value (PPV). Recent studies have demonstrated a substantial decline in PPV for both mutation-based and expression-based assays when NIFTP is correctly categorized as non-malignant, highlighting the urgent need for next-generation molecular diagnostics that account for this updated pathological classification.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides an improved molecular diagnostic test for the preoperative classification of thyroid nodules, particularly those yielding indeterminate FNA results. The invention is based on the discovery that specific splice variants of a select panel of genes exhibit differential expression between benign thyroid lesions—including adenomatoid nodules (AN), follicular adenomas (FA), Hürthle cell adenomas (HA), and NIFTP—and malignant tumors, namely invasive FVPTC and classical PTC. This splice-variant-specific diagnostic gene panel demonstrates high sensitivity and specificity, even in the context of the newly defined NIFTP entity.

In one aspect, the invention provides a method for identifying a thyroid tumor in a subject as benign or malignant, comprising measuring the expression levels of five specific splice variant markers: CDH3, FNDC4, HMGA2, KLK7, and PLAG1, in a biological sample obtained from the subject’s thyroid nodule. The method further includes normalizing the expression of these markers to a thyrocyte-specific load control, preferably TPO1, to account for variable cellular content and contamination by non-thyroid cells such as peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs).

The biological sample is typically a fine needle aspiration (FNA) specimen, which may be processed to extract total RNA. The presence of thyroid epithelial cells in the sample is confirmed by detecting expression of the TPO1 isoform, with samples exhibiting a threshold cycle (Ct) value greater than 30 in quantitative reverse transcription PCR (RT-qPCR) being excluded due to insufficient thyrocyte content. RT-qPCR is then performed using primers specifically designed to amplify the target splice variants, and the resulting expression data are normalized and transformed to generate a composite diagnostic score.

A key advantage of the invention is its ability to distinguish NIFTP from invasive FVPTC and PTC, a distinction not achievable by current cytopathology or commercial molecular tests. Based on the composite expression score, a tumor is classified as benign if the score is below a predetermined threshold (e.g., −1), and malignant if above. This classification directly informs clinical management: benign classifications may support surveillance or lobectomy, while malignant classifications justify total thyroidectomy and potential adjuvant therapies.

For patients diagnosed with malignant thyroid cancer via this method, the invention further encompasses treatment options, including surgical resection (lobectomy or total thyroidectomy), TERT inhibitor therapy, BRAF inhibitor therapy, MEK inhibitor therapy, external beam radiation, thyroid hormone suppression therapy, and administration of anti-cancer agents such as Acivicin, Aclarubicin, Anastrozole, and others. The diagnostic result thus guides a personalized therapeutic regimen tailored to the molecular profile of the tumor.

### II. Sample Preparation for Detection of Expression

A biological sample is obtained from a subject suspected of having a thyroid lesion, typically via fine needle aspiration (FNA) biopsy. The sample is processed to extract, isolate, and/or purify nucleic acids and/or proteins using standard techniques, including phenol-chloroform extraction (e.g., Trizol), silica-membrane columns (e.g., RNeasy), or single-cell RNA kits. Processing may involve centrifugation, ultracentrifugation, filtration, or magnetic bead separation. In some embodiments, detection is performed directly on minimally processed samples. Whole cells or tissue sections may be analyzed via immunohistochemistry or flow cytometry. The sample may be homogenized in a suitable lysis buffer containing RNase inhibitors. Contaminants and assay inhibitors (e.g., heme, heparin) are removed through purification steps. A biomarker capture reagent, such as an antibody or oligonucleotide probe, may be added to form a target biomarker:capture reagent complex for subsequent detection.

### III. Detection of Target Nucleic Acid

The level of biomarker nucleic acid expression is measured, optionally after amplification. Amplification methods include reverse transcription (RT), polymerase chain reaction (PCR), real-time quantitative PCR (qPCR), nucleic acid sequence-based amplification (NASBA), ligase chain reaction, multiplex ligation-dependent probe amplification, Invader technology, rolling circle amplification, in vitro transcription, strand displacement amplification, transcription-mediated amplification, and RNA amplification. In a preferred embodiment, RT is followed by qPCR (qRT-PCR) using a hot-start polymerase to enhance specificity. Detection employs labels, dyes, or labeled probes/primers, which may contain natural or modified bases. Labels provide detectable signals (e.g., fluorescence), interact with secondary labels to modify signals (e.g., FRET), stabilize hybridization, or serve as binding partners (e.g., biotin-streptavidin). Direct detection uses labeled probes; indirect methods employ biotinylated probes detected via streptavidin-conjugated dyes. Detection platforms include microarrays and microfluidic cards.

### IV. Detection of Target Protein(s)

Protein biomarkers are detected using immunoassays employing biospecific capture reagents, typically antibodies produced via hybridoma or recombinant methods. Traditional immunoassays include ELISA, radioimmunoassay, and Western blotting. Other methods include nephelometry, SELDI-based assays, and biochip analysis. Expression levels are quantified by detecting antibody binding to biomarkers using labeled secondary antibodies or direct labeling. Kits may include immobilized capture agents on solid supports (e.g., microtiter plates). Competitive binding assays and multiplex formats (e.g., multiplex ELISA, electrochemiluminescent assays) enable simultaneous detection of multiple targets. Alternative binding agents such as aptamers may be used.

### V. Treatment of Patients Guided by Target Biomarker Panel

Patients diagnosed with malignant thyroid cancer based on the biomarker panel are treated with modalities including surgical procedures (lobectomy, total thyroidectomy), TERT inhibitor therapy (e.g., BIBR1532), BRAF inhibitor therapy (e.g., vemurafenib, dabrafenib), MEK inhibitor therapy (e.g., trametinib, cobimetinib), external beam radiation, and thyroid hormone therapy (levothyroxine). Additional anti-cancer drugs include Acivicin (glutamine antagonist), Aclarubicin (anthracycline), Acodazole Hydrochloride (antimitotic), Acriflavine (intercalator), Adozelesin (alkylating agent), Aldesleukin (IL-2), Altretamine (alkylating agent), Ambomycin (antibiotic), Aminoglutethimide (steroid synthesis inhibitor), Amsacrine (topoisomerase inhibitor), Anastrozole (aromatase inhibitor), and Anthramycin (DNA binder).

### VI. Kits for Detection of Target Biomarkers

Kits for practicing the invention include reagents for quantifying biomarker expression, packaged in containers with instructions for use. Components may include primers, probes, enzymes (reverse transcriptase, DNA polymerase), buffers, controls, and solid supports. Immunoassay kits contain antibodies, detection reagents, and washing solutions. Nucleic acid-based kits include PCR master mixes and target-specific primers. Devices may include microfluidic cartridges or microarray slides. Optional reagents include RNA extraction kits and magnetic beads for cell enrichment.

## EXAMPLES

The following examples illustrate the practice of the invention without limiting its scope. Variations in reaction conditions, reagent concentrations, and incubation times may be optimized for specific applications.

### Materials and Methods

Clinical FNA samples were collected intraoperatively from thyroid nodules and preserved in ethanol or RNALater. RNA was isolated using Trizol/RNeasy or single-cell kits and reverse-transcribed with SuperScript III. PCR used Platinum Taq polymerase; products were resolved by agarose gel electrophoresis. Real-time qPCR employed Power SYBR Green on a Bio-Rad iQ5 cycler (40 cycles). Data analysis included semi-quantitative densitometry (BioRad Quantity One), z-transformation of expression levels, and ROC analysis to determine diagnostic performance.

### Results

Semi-quantitative PCR of 22 isoforms from 12 genes in tumor tissues and PBMCs identified five isoforms (CDH3, FNDC4, HMGA2, KLK7, PLAG1) with high differential expression between benign/malignant tumors and no PBMC expression. TPO1 was selected as a thyrocyte-specific load control due to stable expression in thyroid tumors and absence in PBMCs. In 137 qualified FNA samples (TPO1 Ct ≤30), the 5-isoform panel yielded an AUC of 0.86 in ROC analysis, with 75% sensitivity and 91% specificity at a composite z-∆Ct threshold of −1. The panel significantly distinguished NIFTP from FVPTC/PTC (p=0.0002) and NIFTP from invasive FVPTC (p<0.05). Bayes Rule estimated NPV=91% and PPV=74%.

### Discussion

Current molecular tests lack specificity in the NIFTP era. Our splice-variant panel addresses this by leveraging isoform-specific expression absent in PBMCs and normalized to TPO1. The high NPV supports ruling out malignancy, potentially avoiding surgery for NIFTP and benign nodules. Limitations include limited FNA availability and exclusion of FC/HC cases; prospective validation is ongoing.

### Conclusions

A 5-splice-variant panel (CDH3, FNDC4, HMGA2, KLK7, PLAG1) normalized to TPO1 enables accurate preoperative classification of thyroid nodules, including NIFTP, with high sensitivity and specificity.

## Example 2: Assays for Sample Preparation and Detection of 5 Biomarkers and 1 Load-Control Marker.

FNA samples were processed for total RNA isolation using GenElute kits. cDNA synthesis used the entire 11 µl RNA eluate. Real-time qPCR quantified five biomarkers and TPO1 load control using gene-specific primers and SYBR Green chemistry.

### Regions for the Primer Design:

Primers targeted unique exon-exon junctions of: CDH3 (exons 7–8), FNDC4 (exons 2–3), HMGA2 (exons 4–5), KLK7 (exons 3–4), PLAG1 (exons 2–3), and TPO1 (exons 10–11).

### Example 3: Development of a High Sensitivity and Specificity Test to Evaluate Preoperative Thyroid Fine Needle Aspiration Cytology Material.

To overcome FNA limitations, we explored splice variants of 12 candidate genes. Quantitative RT-PCR assays were developed for isoforms undetectable in PBMCs. Blood contamination was mitigated by using TPO1—a thyrocyte-specific load control—as inclusion criterion (Ct ≤30). Testing on 159 archival FNAs showed that shifting from GapDH to TPO1 improved diagnostic accuracy. The final 5-isoform panel achieved 86% AUC in distinguishing benign/NIFTP from malignant tumors in a validation cohort, demonstrating utility in correctly classifying NIFTP.
