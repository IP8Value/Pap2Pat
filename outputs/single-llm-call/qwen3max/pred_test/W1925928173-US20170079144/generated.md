# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNICAL FIELD

The present invention pertains to the field of flexible electronic devices, particularly those incorporating thin-film sensors and circuits fabricated on flexible or stretchable substrates. More specifically, the invention relates to scalable, cost-effective microfabrication methods for producing flexible electronic devices that integrate electrical and mechanical sensing functionalities directly onto adhesive surfaces without the need for sacrificial layers or intermediate transfer printing steps. The disclosed techniques are especially suited for applications in wearable electronics, biomedical monitoring, Internet of Things (IoT) systems, and other domains requiring high-volume, low-cost production of conformal, skin-like, or disposable electronic interfaces. The invention further encompasses methodologies for fabricating both single-layer and multi-layer flexible circuits, including passive and active electronic components, using a weakly-adhesive donor substrate that enables direct peel-off integration with a target receiving adhesive. This approach facilitates compatibility with industrial roll-to-roll manufacturing processes and supports the development of next-generation flexible hybrid electronics systems.

## BACKGROUND

Flexible electronic devices represent a transformative evolution from conventional rigid printed circuit boards and silicon-based microelectronics, offering conformability, lightweight form factors, and mechanical resilience essential for integration with biological tissues, curved surfaces, and dynamic environments. Such devices typically comprise conductive traces, sensing elements, and insulating layers deposited on polymeric or elastomeric substrates such as polyimide, polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS), or polyethylene terephthalate (PET). Historically, fabrication of these systems has relied on adaptations of standard semiconductor microfabrication techniques, including photolithography, metal deposition, and etching, often augmented by transfer printing strategies to relocate pre-fabricated circuits from rigid donor wafers onto flexible receivers. These transfer methods commonly involve sacrificial layers—such as poly(methyl methacrylate) (PMMA), poly(acrylic acid) (PAA), or water-soluble tapes—that are dissolved or mechanically released to separate the functional device stack from the original substrate. Subsequently, an intermediate stamp (e.g., PDMS) is used to pick up the pattern and transfer it under controlled pressure to a final adhesive or flexible substrate. While effective in laboratory settings, these multi-step processes are time-consuming, prone to pattern deformation during release, and difficult to automate at scale. Moreover, they often require precise control over solvent exposure, interfacial adhesion, and alignment, limiting yield and throughput. Alternative approaches such as screen printing offer scalability but lack the resolution and integration density required for high-performance biosensors or complex circuitry. Consequently, there remains a critical unmet need for a fabrication paradigm that combines the precision of cleanroom microfabrication with the simplicity, speed, and compatibility with industrial post-processing necessary for widespread commercial deployment of flexible electronics.

## SUMMARY

The growing demand for inconspicuous, high-performance sensors in wearable health monitors, smart textiles, and IoT networks necessitates a scalable and cost-effective fabrication methodology that overcomes the limitations of existing transfer printing and manual assembly techniques. To address this need, the present invention introduces a novel microfabrication approach for producing flexible electronic devices directly integrable with adhesive substrates through a simplified, inverted process flow. The invention discloses flexible circuits formed on flexible substrates using a weakly-adhesive donor interface that eliminates the requirement for sacrificial layers, solvent immersion, or intermediate transfer stamps. This cost-effective fabrication method significantly reduces the number of processing steps—by approximately fifty percent compared to conventional transfer printing—thereby enhancing yield, reducing processing time, and enabling automation. The method further outlines the integration of micron-scale structures with high fidelity, supporting the formation of both single-layer and multi-layer circuit architectures. High-volume manufacturing is facilitated through compatibility with roll-to-roll post-processing, where arrays of devices can be continuously peeled from a donor web and laminated onto adhesive tapes or films. The invention introduces a method to fabricate an electronic device comprising the steps of forming a conductive layer and an insulating layer on a weakly-adhesive donor substrate, patterning the layers to define a circuit, attaching a flexible substrate or adhesive to the circuit, and detaching the resulting flexible electronic device via a simple mechanical peel-off. Circuit formation and attachment are achieved through precision etching and direct lamination, respectively, without intermediate handling. The method to fabricate a circuit leverages reversed metallization sequences and photo-definable polymers to enable simultaneous patterning and encapsulation. Electronic component placement is supported through embodiments that incorporate wells or recesses in the donor substrate for embedding passive or active components prior to circuit formation. The disclosed technology finds application in electroencephalography (EEG), electrocardiography (EKG), strain sensing, and other modalities requiring conformal, disposable, or high-fidelity biointerfaces, thereby enabling next-generation healthcare, industrial, and consumer electronic systems.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

### Exemplary Embodiments

The present invention introduces advanced fabrication methods that overcome longstanding challenges in the production of flexible electronic devices. Conventional manual fabrication is labor-intensive, inconsistent, and unsuitable for mass production, while foundry-based approaches, though precise, are costly and inflexible for rapid prototyping or customization. The disclosed techniques are motivated by the need for a middle ground: a process that retains the precision of microfabrication yet simplifies post-processing to enable scalability. Conventional microfabrication typically involves sequential deposition and patterning on rigid substrates, followed by complex transfer steps to achieve flexibility. In contrast, the disclosed fabrication methods employ an inverted architecture and a weakly-adhesive interface to enable direct integration with adhesives.  

Fabrication method 100 begins with providing a support substrate 111, which may be a silicon wafer, glass slide, or flexible polymer film. A weakly-adhesive layer 112—such as a thin coating of PDMS with a controlled crosslinker ratio—is formed on the support substrate to create a releasable interface. A conductive layer 114, typically comprising a bilayer of chromium (Cr) and gold (Au), is then deposited, with the Au layer adjacent to the weakly-adhesive layer and Cr exposed outward. An insulating layer 116, preferably a photodefinable polyimide (PPI), is deposited and simultaneously patterned via photolithography to define the sensor or circuit geometry. The exposed conductive layer 114 is etched to form circuit 120, leaving only the desired conductive traces encapsulated by the insulating layer. A flexible substrate 118—such as an adhesive tape or medical-grade film—is then attached to the circuit side. The entire flexible electronic device 130 is detached from the weakly-adhesive layer 112 via a simple peel-off, requiring minimal force (e.g., 0.22 N). The resulting device is ready for use or post-processing, and the support substrate 111 can be cleaned and reused, enhancing cost efficiency.  

Fabrication method 150 extends this to multi-layer circuits. A first layer of circuit 120 is formed as above. A second conductive layer 124 is deposited, and vias or interconnections between conductive layers are formed through selective etching or laser ablation. The first layer is encapsulated, and a second insulating layer 126 is applied and patterned. This process repeats to form additional layers, culminating in a multi-layered circuit. The entire stack is then attached to a flexible substrate and peeled off as a unit. The use of a weakly-adhesive layer avoids the pitfalls of conventional “glue” methods, which often leave residues or require aggressive release mechanisms. Photo-definable polymers like PPI enable simultaneous formation and patterning, reducing steps. The resulting flexible electronic device 130 exhibits high fidelity, consistent geometry, and robust electrical performance, as demonstrated in EEG and strain sensing applications.  

The Adhesive-Peel (AP) technique offers significant advantages over Transfer Printing (TP). AP sensors show minimal pattern variability, whereas TP sensors often suffer from deformation during solvent release. Processing time is halved, and yield is improved due to elimination of intermediate steps. Automated post-processing is feasible, as demonstrated by successful peeling using a consumer lint roller. Quantitative EEG tests confirm that AP sensors achieve Pearson correlation coefficients exceeding 0.95 relative to TP counterparts, validating signal fidelity. Strain sensors fabricated via AP exhibit predictable gauge factors, and EKG recordings demonstrate clear P, QRS, and T waves. Roll-to-roll compatibility is proven using Kapton-based flexible donors. Materials selection—such as PDMS stiffness, metal composition (Au, Ag, Pt), and insulating polymers—is tunable for specific applications. The technology enables integrated systems, strengthens manufacturing infrastructure, and supports single-use clinical sensors, concluding a transformative advance in flexible electronics fabrication.

### Additional Exemplary Embodiments

The invention further encompasses fabrication methods for flexible circuits integrating passive and active electronic components. Fabrication method 1000 begins by producing a patterned processing substrate with microwells. Electronic components—such as resistors, capacitors, or IC chips—are placed into these wells. A conductive layer is deposited over the assembly, forming electrical interconnects to the components. A single-layer circuit is thus formed, encapsulated if needed, and a flexible substrate is attached. The entire structure is detached from the weakly-adhesive layer, yielding a flexible integrated device. Components may range from tens of microns to millimeters in size, accommodated by well depth and surface topology.  

Method 1100 extends this to multi-layer configurations. A template structure defines the weakly-adhesive layer with a flat upper surface for circuit fabrication and non-flat bottoms in wells for component seating. After forming a single-layer circuit, additional layers are built upward, with interconnects linking to embedded components. Post-processing may include pick-and-place of surface-mounted parts or laser trimming. The result is a flexible integrated electronic device with heterogeneous functionality.  

Exemplary implementations include: (1) fabricating an electronic device using a Si wafer donor; (2) forming a processing substrate with PDMS; (3) selecting weakly-adhesive materials like low-crosslink PDMS; (4) using medical adhesives as flexible substrates; (5) employing glass as support; (6) depositing Cr/Au conductive layers; (7) using PPI as insulator; (8) alternative conductive layers like Ag or Pt; (9) detaching via peel-off; (10) reusing substrates; (11) using Kapton as flexible donor; (12) flexible support structures; (13) UV patterning of insulators; (14) laser etching for precision; (15) multi-layer stacks; (16) partial encapsulation for contact pads; (17) alternative insulators like SU-8; (18) full encapsulation via lamination; (19) biocompatible encapsulation; (20) attaching SMD components; (21) circuit fabrication via evaporation; (22) detachment without damage; (23) substrate reuse; (24) component integration pre-metallization; (25) structured donors; (26) grayscale lithography; (27) laser ablation; (28) roll-to-roll donors; (29) adhesive lamination; (30) 3D circuit stacking; and (31) end-to-end device production. These examples underscore the versatility, scalability, and industrial readiness of the disclosed technology.
