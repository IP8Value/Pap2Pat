# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

The present invention resides in the field of functionalized biopolymeric materials, particularly nanocellulosic substrates engineered for use in diagnostic and biomedical applications. More specifically, the invention relates to surface-modified nanofibrillated cellulose (NFC) films that have been topographically and chemically tailored to enable controlled immobilization of biomolecules such as antibodies, thereby providing a sustainable, high-performance platform for immunoassays, biosensors, and related analytical devices.

Polymeric materials have long served as foundational supports in medical diagnostics, including lateral flow assays, microarrays, and biosensor platforms. Conventional polymers used for these purposes are typically derived from nonrenewable petroleum-based feedstocks. While effective in certain contexts, their widespread commercial deployment raises significant environmental concerns due to issues of non-biodegradability, persistence in ecosystems, and reliance on finite resources. Moreover, many synthetic polymers exhibit undesirable properties for biological interfacing, such as hydrophobicity, non-specific protein adsorption, or insufficient mechanical stability under aqueous conditions.

In response to these limitations, researchers have increasingly turned to natural biopolymers, with cellulose and its derivatives emerging as particularly promising alternatives. Cellulose is the most abundant organic polymer on Earth, derived primarily from plant biomass, and offers inherent advantages including biocompatibility, non-toxicity, hydrophilicity, and chemical resistance. These attributes make cellulose-based materials attractive candidates for diagnostic substrates where minimal interference with biological recognition events is critical.

Among cellulose derivatives, nitrocellulose has historically been employed as a support matrix in immunoassays due to its ability to passively adsorb proteins. However, nitrocellulose suffers from several drawbacks that limit its utility in advanced diagnostic applications. It exhibits relatively low mechanical strength, especially when wet, rendering it prone to tearing or deformation during handling. Additionally, nitrocellulose is highly flammable, posing safety and storage challenges. Perhaps more critically, its strong and often non-specific affinity for proteins can lead to high background signals and reduced assay specificity, undermining sensitivity and reproducibility.

Nanofibrillated cellulose (NFC), produced by mechanical or chemical disintegration of wood pulp fibers into nanoscale fibrils, represents a significant advancement over traditional cellulose derivatives. NFC possesses a high specific surface area, exceptional mechanical strength, and an extensive network of surface hydroxyl groups capable of forming robust hydrogen bonds. These properties enable the fabrication of dense, smooth, and nearly transparent films with low porosity—features highly desirable for optical detection methods and controlled molecular interactions at solid-liquid interfaces.

Recent developments have demonstrated the feasibility of producing NFC films with enhanced water resistance through controlled processing of low-charge precursor pulps, resulting in compact, less porous structures that impede water penetration while maintaining structural integrity. However, unmodified NFC films lack sufficient chemical functionality for covalent biomolecule conjugation and may still exhibit non-specific binding due to residual hydroxyl-mediated interactions.

To address this, TEMPO-mediated oxidation has emerged as a selective chemical method to convert primary hydroxyl groups on cellulose surfaces into carboxyl groups. This reaction, catalyzed by the stable nitroxyl radical 2,2,6,6-tetramethylpiperidine-1-oxyl (TEMPO) in the presence of sodium hypochlorite and sodium bromide, proceeds under mild alkaline conditions and targets C6 hydroxyls with high specificity. The resulting carboxylated NFC provides not only enhanced hydrophilicity but also reactive sites amenable to further chemical derivatization.

Despite these advances, a key challenge remains: balancing water resistance with surface reactivity. Fully oxidized NFC gels often swell excessively in aqueous environments, compromising film integrity. The present invention overcomes this by applying TEMPO oxidation selectively to the surface of pre-formed, water-resistant NFC films, thereby introducing carboxyl functionality without disrupting the internal dense structure that confers mechanical and hydration stability. This strategic surface modification enables the creation of a robust, renewable diagnostic platform with tunable topography and chemistry.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention is motivated by the need for sustainable, high-performance solid supports in diagnostic applications that combine mechanical robustness, controlled surface chemistry, and compatibility with precision biomolecule patterning techniques such as inkjet printing. Traditional polymeric and cellulosic substrates fail to simultaneously satisfy these requirements, particularly in terms of minimizing non-specific binding while enabling covalent, oriented immobilization of capture molecules like antibodies.

Accordingly, the invention provides surface-modified nanofibrillated cellulose (NFC) films wherein the outermost layer has been chemically and topographically engineered to present amine-reactive functional groups suitable for covalent conjugation of biomolecules. These films retain the bulk water resistance and mechanical integrity of native NFC while offering a tailored surface for specific bio-recognition events.

The method for functionalization involves a two-step sequential process. First, a dense NFC film is subjected to a brief, controlled TEMPO-mediated oxidation, which selectively introduces carboxyl groups onto the surface fibrils without penetrating deeply into the film matrix. This surface-limited oxidation preserves the film’s low porosity and water resistance while generating a high density of anionic sites. Second, the carboxylated surface is activated using 1-ethyl-3-(3-dimethylaminopropyl)carbodiimide (EDC) and N-hydroxysuccinimide (NHS) to form NHS esters—highly reactive intermediates that readily couple with primary amines present on proteins, peptides, or other biomolecules under mild aqueous conditions.

The resulting activated NFC films offer numerous advantages over existing diagnostic substrates. They are derived from renewable resources, exhibit negligible auto-fluorescence in relevant spectral ranges, and demonstrate excellent compatibility with both physical adsorption and covalent immobilization strategies. Critically, the covalent attachment via NHS-ester chemistry ensures stable, irreversible binding of capture molecules, which withstands stringent washing protocols and maintains biological activity over time. Furthermore, the surface can be efficiently blocked to minimize non-specific protein adsorption, enhancing signal-to-noise ratios in immunoassays.

The invention also enables precise spatial patterning of biomolecules through inkjet printing, allowing for multiplexed assay formats with minimal reagent consumption. The combination of topographical uniformity, chemical specificity, and process scalability makes these surface-modified NFC films ideal for next-generation point-of-care diagnostics, biosensors, and lab-on-a-chip systems.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF PREFERRED EMBODIMENTS OF THE INVENTION

The preferred method for preparing the surface-modified NFC films of the invention begins with TEMPO-mediated oxidation of pre-formed NFC films. This oxidation is conducted under carefully controlled conditions to ensure surface-selective carboxylation. Specifically, a solution containing TEMPO (0.13 mmol), sodium bromide (4.7 mmol), and sodium hypochlorite (5.65 mmol) in 100 mL of deionized water is prepared, and the pH is adjusted to 10 using 1 M hydrochloric acid. A dry NFC film, typically 2.5 cm × 2.5 cm in dimension, is immersed in this solution for a duration ranging from 10 to 300 seconds, depending on the desired degree of surface modification.

As illustrated in FIG. 1, which schematically depicts the sequential modification process, the oxidation reaction is rapidly quenched by the addition of ethanol, followed by extensive rinsing with water to remove residual reagents. This step yields a carboxylated NFC film with enhanced surface hydrophilicity but retained bulk water resistance, as confirmed by immersion tests showing no disintegration even after prolonged exposure to water and ultrasonication.

Following oxidation, the NFC film undergoes further surface modification via EDC/NHS chemistry. The carboxylated film is immersed in an activation solution containing 0.1 M EDC and 0.4 M NHS in 10 mM sodium acetate buffer at pH 5.0 for 20 minutes. This reaction converts surface carboxyl groups into NHS esters, which are highly reactive toward primary amines. After activation, the film is rinsed thoroughly with water and dried between absorbent blotting boards to prevent buckling, yielding a stable, amine-reactive substrate ready for biomolecule conjugation.

The functionalization step is particularly advantageous because it occurs under aqueous, near-physiological conditions, preserving the structural integrity of the NFC film while enabling efficient coupling. The NHS-ester groups remain stable for hours when stored dry, allowing for batch processing and delayed use.

Conjugation of biomolecules—such as bovine serum albumin (BSA), polyclonal anti-human IgG, or other antibodies—is achieved by either physical adsorption or covalent coupling. In covalent immobilization, the activated NFC film is incubated with a solution of the target biomolecule (e.g., 100 µg/mL anti-human IgG in 10 mM acetate buffer, pH 5) for 20–30 minutes. Unreacted NHS esters are then quenched by treatment with 0.1 M ethanolamine at pH 8.5, and non-specific binding sites are blocked using commercial blocking agents such as Superblock.

Alternatively, biomolecules can be precisely patterned onto the activated NFC surface using inkjet printing technology. An Epson R800 piezoelectric inkjet printer, loaded with cartridges containing fluorescently labeled antibodies (e.g., FITC- or dansyl-conjugated anti-human IgG at 1 mg/mL in phosphate buffer, pH 7.4), deposits picoliter-scale droplets onto defined regions of the film. Post-printing, the film is treated with ethanolamine to cap unreacted esters and rinsed to remove loosely bound material, resulting in spatially resolved, covalently attached antibody arrays.

Applications of these topographically modified NFC films span a wide range of diagnostic and analytical fields. They serve as superior solid supports in enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays (ELISAs), lateral flow devices, fluorescence-based microarrays, and quartz crystal microbalance (QCM)-based biosensors. Their renewable origin, optical clarity, low background, and compatibility with high-throughput manufacturing position them as sustainable replacements for conventional plastic or nitrocellulose membranes in clinical, environmental, and food safety testing.

### EXAMPLES

The materials used in the exemplary embodiments include bleached hardwood pulp (birch), TEMPO free radical, sodium bromide, sodium hypochlorite, EDC, NHS, ethanolamine, BSA, human IgG, anti-human IgG, FITC, dansyl chloride, and standard laboratory buffers. Water was purified to MilliQ grade. An unmodified Epson R800 inkjet printer with CD-printing tray and clean ink cartridges was employed for printing experiments.

Water purification was not a focus of the invention, but the NFC films’ low porosity inherently limits analyte diffusion, enhancing assay specificity—a feature beneficial in complex sample matrices. Experimental techniques included QCM-D, XPS, AFM, CLSM, contact angle goniometry, and conductometric titration to characterize chemical and topographical changes.

The inkjet printer and cartridges were used without modification; cartridges were filled with antibody solutions or buffer as needed. Preparation mechanisms involved mechanical fibrillation of pulp via Masuko grinding and microfluidization, followed by filtration and rolling to produce dense NFC films.

Surface carboxylation was achieved via TEMPO oxidation as described. Activation used EDC/NHS in acetate buffer, and protein adsorption was tested with BSA and anti-human IgG. Surface morphology was analyzed by AFM and CLSM, revealing nanoscale fibrillar structures and macro-scale uniformity.

Reactions were monitored in real time using QCM-D, which tracked mass changes during oxidation, activation, and protein binding. Amine reactivity was confirmed by covalent BSA attachment resistant to alkaline washes. Anti-human IgG was successfully installed via inkjet printing, yielding patterned, fluorescent features.

In Example 1, NFC films and model surfaces were prepared. Films were made by filtering NFC gel and rolling; model surfaces for QCM were spin-coated onto PEI-functionalized SiO₂ crystals.

Example 2 detailed functionalization: TEMPO oxidation for 10–300 s, EDC/NHS treatment, and verification of water tolerance—films remained intact after water immersion.

Example 3 analyzed unmodified and activated films. Topography by AFM showed RMS roughness of ~35 nm (unmodified), increasing slightly after modification. Carboxyl content rose from 72 to 237 µeq/g with oxidation time. Hydrophilicity increased, with contact angles dropping below 15°.

XPS analysis confirmed surface cleanliness and detected nitrogen only after EDC/NHS activation, proving NHS-ester formation. AFM showed gel-like layers post-oxidation, reverting after activation.

QCM-D monitored reactions: oxidation caused frequency drops due to hydration; activation reversed swelling. BSA adsorption was higher on activated films and partially irreversible, confirming covalent binding.

Antibody attachment was tested: anti-human IgG adsorbed strongly on activated films, verified by fluorescence and XPS (6 wt% N). Activity persisted after drying, as shown by BSA binding post-storage.

Conjugated anti-human IgG captured human IgG specifically, with minimal non-specific binding after blocking. QCM-D showed hIgG adsorption only on antibody-functionalized surfaces.

Example 5 demonstrated inkjet printing: printed anti-human IgG showed clear fluorescence patterns. CLSM and AFM confirmed uniform coverage and reduced roughness (~25 nm), indicating conformal antibody layers. Topographical changes post-printing were consistent with biomolecular deposition.

Collectively, these examples confirm that surface-modified NFC films provide a robust, renewable, and functionalizable platform for diagnostic applications, combining sustainability with high performance.
