# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates generally to the field of electrochemical energy storage devices, and more specifically to flexible and stretchable lithium-ion batteries (LIBs) that are capable of undergoing large mechanical deformations while maintaining stable electrochemical performance. The invention further pertains to the design, fabrication, and application of such batteries in wearable electronic devices, particularly those requiring conformability, durability, and high energy density under dynamic mechanical strain. The disclosed technology leverages principles of kirigami—a traditional Japanese art form involving cutting and folding paper—to engineer battery architectures that exhibit exceptional stretchability, robustness, and compatibility with conventional battery manufacturing processes. This innovation addresses a critical need in the development of next-generation wearable and implantable electronics, where rigid, bulky power sources have historically limited device form factor, comfort, and functionality.

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

The rapid proliferation of wearable electronics—such as smartwatches, fitness trackers, health monitors, and augmented reality glasses—has intensified the demand for power sources that are not only energy-dense but also mechanically compliant. Conventional lithium-ion batteries, while offering high energy density and long cycle life, are inherently rigid and brittle due to their layered construction of metallic current collectors, ceramic-coated electrodes, and polymeric separators. These structural characteristics render them unsuitable for applications requiring repeated bending, twisting, or stretching, as such mechanical stresses induce delamination, cracking, and electrical failure. Various strategies have been explored to impart flexibility to LIBs, including the use of serpentine interconnects, buckled structures, fiber-based designs, and intrinsically stretchable materials. However, these approaches often suffer from significant drawbacks: serpentine designs sacrifice areal energy density; buckled structures offer limited stretchability and poor out-of-plane stability; fiber batteries face challenges in scalability and packaging; and intrinsically stretchable composites typically exhibit low ionic/electronic conductivity and reduced electrochemical performance.

Prior art in flexible batteries has largely focused on origami-inspired folding or elastic substrates, but these methods either restrict deformation to in-plane bending or rely on soft matrices that compromise energy density. Kirigami, which involves strategic cutting and folding of planar sheets, has recently emerged as a promising alternative for creating stretchable metamaterials. In non-battery contexts, kirigami has been used to design stretchable conductors and sensors. However, its application to full electrochemical cells—particularly multilayered, sealed, liquid-electrolyte-containing LIBs—has remained unexplored due to the perceived incompatibility of cutting with the integrity of battery components and the risk of internal short circuits. Moreover, no prior art has demonstrated a kirigami-based LIB that maintains high Coulombic efficiency, stable cycling, and sufficient power output under repeated large-strain deformation while being fabricated using standard industrial electrode processing techniques. There remains a critical unmet need for a scalable, high-performance stretchable battery architecture that overcomes the limitations of existing approaches and enables seamless integration into commercial wearable devices without compromising user experience or device functionality.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a novel kirigami-based lithium-ion battery that achieves high stretchability, excellent electrochemical stability, and robust mechanical performance through a unique combination of geometric patterning and conventional battery materials. The invention introduces a battery architecture wherein all constituent layers—including anode, cathode, separator, current collectors, and packaging—are uniformly patterned with a kirigami cut-and-fold design, enabling coordinated deformation without delamination or fracture. The kirigami patterns include, but are not limited to, zigzag-cut, cut-N-twist, and cut-N-shear configurations, each engineered to control the mode of deformation during stretching.

The fabrication process involves first designing a specific kirigami pattern, then cutting all battery layers—copper anode current collector, aluminum cathode current collector, separator, and laminated packaging film—using a customized puncher or laser cutter to ensure precise alignment. Electrode slurries containing conventional active materials (e.g., graphite for anode, LiCoO₂ for cathode) are coated onto the pre-cut current collectors, dried, and calendared. The layers are then stacked in a defined sequence, sealed on three sides, filled with liquid electrolyte, and hermetically sealed on the fourth side within an inert atmosphere. The resulting battery cell is then folded and manipulated according to the kirigami pattern to achieve its compact state.

A key feature of the invention is the stress release mechanism inherent in the kirigami geometry. During stretching, deformation is accommodated either through out-of-plane buckling (as in the zigzag-cut pattern) or through in-plane rotation at the cut sites (as in the cut-N-twist and cut-N-shear patterns), thereby preventing crack propagation in the brittle electrode layers. The stretchability, defined as the maximum reversible strain the battery can undergo without functional degradation, exceeds 100% in certain embodiments and reaches up to 150% in the cut-N-shear configuration. This level of stretchability is achieved without sacrificing energy density, with demonstrated values of 87 Wh/kg and projected capacities exceeding 700 mAh in scaled-up watchband-integrated designs. The invention thus provides a practical, manufacturable solution for powering commercial wearable electronics under real-world mechanical demands.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE PREFERRED EMBODIMENTS

The invention encompasses several kirigami patterns, each tailored to specific mechanical and electrochemical requirements. The zigzag-cut pattern is formed by asymmetrically cutting a folded stack of foil between adjacent creases, resulting in a longitudinal zigzag profile. Upon stretching, this pattern undergoes out-of-plane deformation near the cuts, allowing extension beyond its planar length. The amplitude of buckling governs the degree of stretchability, which can be tuned by adjusting cut length and spacing.

In contrast, the cut-N-twist pattern eliminates out-of-plane deformation by symmetrically cutting all creases in a folded stack, unfolding to a planar state, and then twisting the ends to form a locked, cord-like structure analogous to a telephone cable. Stretching induces rotational motion at the cuts rather than buckling, constraining deformation to the plane and enhancing structural stability. The packing density of this pattern—defined as the ratio of active material area to total footprint—is determined by the width of each face segment.

To further increase packing density, the cut-N-shear pattern is introduced. This involves symmetric cutting followed by refolding and application of shear force, effectively doubling the number of active segments per unit length compared to the cut-N-twist design. Like the cut-N-twist, it accommodates stretching via in-plane rotation without out-of-plane buckling, achieving stretchability up to 150% while doubling energy capacity for a given linear dimension.

Demonstrated kirigami LIBs utilize conventional materials: graphite anode and LiCoO₂ (LCO) cathode active materials, with copper and aluminum foils as current collectors, respectively. Slurries are prepared using standard formulations—cathode slurry comprising LCO, PVDF binder, carbon black conductive agent, and NMP solvent; anode slurry comprising graphite, CMC/SBR binders, carbon black, and water. These are coated onto pre-cut current collectors, dried, and pressed to form condensed electrodes. The separator is polypropylene (Celgard 2500), and the packaging consists of aluminized polyethylene laminated film.

Assembly follows a multilayer stacking order: packaging/cathode/separator/anode/packaging. All layers are cut to identical kirigami patterns using a custom puncher to ensure alignment, a critical factor for preventing internal shorts. Cells are sealed on three sides via impulse sealing, filled with 1M LiPF₆ in EC:DMC:DEC (1:1:1) electrolyte in an argon glovebox, and sealed on the fourth side. The flat planar schematic precedes folding into the final compact configuration.

Electrochemical testing reveals exceptional performance. At C/3 rate, the cut-N-twist LIB delivers 30 mAh capacity with 85% retention and 99.8% Coulombic efficiency over 100 cycles, even when cycled alternately between compact and stretched states. Rate capability is robust: capacity recovers fully after high-rate discharge (C-rate), indicating minimal polarization. Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy shows negligible change in charge-transfer resistance after 100 mechanical cycles, confirming interface stability.

Mechanically, the battery maintains a steady 3.83 V output across 90% stretchability over 3,000 cycles. Maximum output power is 4.1 W, sufficient to drive LEDs without dimming. SEM analysis of anode (Cu) and cathode (Al) current collectors after cycling shows no cracks at cut sites, validating the deformation mechanism.

Theoretical analysis based on Griffith’s criterion explains this resilience. Two competing modes exist under applied moment: crack growth (driven by elastic energy release) and plastic rolling (driven by plastic dissipation). The critical moment for crack growth is \( M_c^{\text{crack}} = \sqrt{\frac{2\gamma E H^3}{6A(\theta)}} \), while for plastic rolling it is \( M_c^{\text{roll}} = 2\beta H \). Finite element simulations using ABAQUS, with Al material properties (E = 69 GPa, γ = 0.868 N/m), show that for realistic folding radii (500–800 μm), β ≈ 1 MPa, ensuring \( M_c^{\text{roll}} < M_c^{\text{crack}} \). Thus, plastic rolling dominates—a “safe zone” that prevents fracture.

This principle was validated in a Samsung Gear 2 smartwatch. The original 300 mAh battery was replaced with an 80 mAh kirigami LIB (cut-N-twist), sewn into an elastic watchband. The device functioned normally during wrist-to-arm stretching (30% strain) and elbow flexion (additional 15% strain), displaying video and handling calls. Standby time was 24.5 hours; calling time was 90 minutes—matching simulated discharge tests at 2.9 mA (C/30) and 48 mA (C/2). Thermal tests showed negligible temperature rise versus the original bulky battery, owing to the kirigami’s high surface-to-volume ratio.

Materials selection is critical. Anode current collectors are 20 μm Cu foil, produced via electrolytic deposition for uniformity. Cathode current collectors are 10 μm Al foil. Cathode materials may include LCO, manganese dioxide (high voltage, low cost), fluorinated carbons (high energy density), sulfides (flexibility), or lithium-containing composite oxides (e.g., NMC). Conductive agents include carbon black; binders include PVDF (cathode) or CMC/SBR (anode), chosen for adhesion and elasticity. The polymer electrolyte may be gel-based for enhanced safety. Packaging uses laminated film with a barrier layer (aluminum) and resin layers (polyethylene), 100–200 μm thick, with resin properties ensuring seal integrity and flexibility.

### EXAMPLES

Kirigami LIBs were fabricated using zigzag-cut, cut-N-twist, and cut-N-shear patterns. The cut-N-shear variant achieved 150% stretchability and 87 Wh/kg energy density. All patterns powered a Samsung Gear 2 smartwatch, demonstrating real-world applicability. Multilayer stacking enabled scalable production: graphite and LiCoO₂ were used as active materials; slurries were coated on Al and Cu foils, dried, and pressed. Custom punchers ensured aligned cutting across all layers. Cells were assembled, sealed, and filled with standard electrolyte.

Electrochemical characterization included cyclic galvanostatic charge/discharge at C/3, showing 85% capacity retention over 100 cycles for cut-N-twist and similar results for other patterns. Maximum output power was calculated from open-circuit voltage and internal resistance (1.8 Ω), yielding 4.1 W. Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) was performed before and after stretching; spectra were analyzed using equivalent circuit models, revealing stable interfacial resistance.

For cut-N-shear, cyclic stability and rate performance mirrored cut-N-twist, with 150% stretchability confirmed via tensile testing. SEMs of anode and cathode current collectors post-cycling showed no cracks, consistent with plastic rolling dominance. Crack growth analysis employed Griffith’s criterion; J-integral calculations from FEM simulations confirmed safe-mode operation. Plastic rolling was quantified via dissipated plastic energy density, derived from folding simulations with varying radii.

In summary, the examples demonstrate that kirigami patterning transforms conventional LIBs into highly stretchable, durable power sources without altering core chemistry, enabling disruptive integration into wearable electronics.
