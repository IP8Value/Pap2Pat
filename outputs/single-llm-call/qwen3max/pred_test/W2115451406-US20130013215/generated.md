# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

The three-dimensional structure of a protein is fundamental to its biological function, governing interactions with other biomolecules, enzymatic activity, cellular localization, and stability. Accurate determination of protein structure is therefore essential for understanding biological mechanisms, designing therapeutics, and engineering novel proteins. Historically, experimental techniques such as X-ray crystallography, nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy, and cryo-electron microscopy have been employed to resolve protein structures at atomic resolution. However, these methods are often time-consuming, resource-intensive, and technically challenging, particularly for large, membrane-bound, or intrinsically disordered proteins. Moreover, many proteins resist crystallization or yield poor signal-to-noise ratios in NMR, limiting the scope of experimentally tractable targets. These limitations underscore the critical need for robust computational methods capable of predicting protein structure from amino acid sequence alone.

Computational approaches to protein structure prediction have evolved significantly over the past several decades. Early efforts focused on homology modeling, which relies on the availability of experimentally determined structures of evolutionarily related proteins (templates). While effective when high-sequence-identity templates exist, homology modeling fails in the absence of suitable homologs—a common scenario for novel proteins or those from understudied organisms. This gap has motivated the development of *ab initio* or template-free methods that do not depend on prior structural knowledge. Among these, physics-based simulations grounded in molecular mechanics and thermodynamics aim to model the physical forces driving protein folding. Central to these methods are energy minimization and molecular dynamics (MD) simulations, which integrate Newtonian equations of motion to explore conformational space. However, conventional MD remains computationally prohibitive for most proteins, requiring supercomputing resources and weeks to months of simulation time to achieve meaningful folding, even for small polypeptides. Furthermore, these simulations often suffer from force field inaccuracies, inadequate sampling, and trapping in local energy minima, leading to suboptimal structural predictions.

Prior art in physics-based protein structure prediction includes coarse-grained models, Monte Carlo sampling, and enhanced sampling techniques designed to accelerate conformational search. While these methods reduce computational cost, they frequently sacrifice atomic detail or rely on empirical corrections that obscure the underlying physical principles. A representative prior art method employs all-atom MD with implicit solvation and simplified electrostatics to simulate folding trajectories, but even this approach demands extensive computational resources and yields limited accuracy for proteins beyond 100 residues. The fundamental limitation across existing physics-based frameworks is the inability to balance computational efficiency with predictive accuracy while maintaining a physically interpretable model of the folding process. There remains a pressing need for a computationally tractable, first-principles-based method that accurately captures the interplay of electrostatic, hydrophobic, and entropic forces governing protein folding, operates without reliance on homologous templates, and delivers reliable secondary and tertiary structure predictions on standard desktop hardware within practical timeframes. Such a method would enable rapid structural insights for proteins lacking experimental data, facilitate functional annotation, and support drug discovery and protein design initiatives.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides a novel computational method for predicting protein structure from amino acid sequence using a physically grounded, drift-diffusion-based simulation framework. This method uniquely integrates explicit representations of key intermolecular forces—specifically electrostatic, electrostatic displacement, and thermal energy forces—into a kinetic model that simulates the dynamic folding process. Unlike statistical or machine learning approaches that infer structure from evolutionary patterns, the disclosed method operates *ab initio*, relying solely on the physicochemical properties of amino acid residues and fundamental physical laws. The invention recognizes that the balance between attractive hydrophobic interactions and repulsive electrostatic forces, modulated by thermal fluctuations, dictates the formation of secondary structural elements and the overall tertiary fold.

Central to the invention is a screening method for secondary structure determination that systematically scans the amino acid sequence to identify regions predisposed to form alpha helices or beta sheets. This screening leverages the relative positions, charges, hydrophobicities, and sizes of residues to evaluate local energetic propensities. For alpha helix determination, the method initiates a scanning bracket upon encountering a hydrophilic residue following a hydrophobic segment and evaluates the next six residues for the presence of a terminating hydrophilic residue. Within this window, the algorithm assesses the net charge, charge product, and hydrophobic character to determine whether hydrophobic collapse is unopposed by sufficient electrostatic repulsion; if so, an alpha helix is predicted. Conversely, if intervening charged residues generate a repulsive electrostatic displacement force that counteracts hydrophobic aggregation, the region is instead classified as a beta sheet. This rule-based approach directly links physical force balances to secondary structure outcomes, enabling accurate and interpretable predictions.

For beta sheet determination, the method identifies unstructured regions—those not assigned to alpha helices—and applies a five-residue scanning bracket. It computes the sum of absolute charges and the sum of hydrophobic character within the bracket and applies conditional inequalities to classify the segment as a beta sheet. This dual-pathway screening ensures comprehensive identification of canonical secondary structures based on local sequence features. Following secondary structure assignment, the invention constructs an initial three-dimensional representation by embedding predicted alpha helices and beta sheets as rigid bodies, tagged with aggregate physicochemical properties derived from their constituent residues. The remaining unstructured segments are modeled as flexible chains. The method then performs a physics-based simulation of tertiary folding, wherein the protein undergoes iterative rotations about backbone torsional angles driven by the net effective force acting on each alpha carbon. This force is derived from the vector sum of electrostatic, electrostatic displacement, and thermal energy components, with global entropy changes incorporated into a mobility parameter that modulates conformational exploration. The simulation progresses until a stable, low-energy conformation is achieved, which may be further refined via molecular dynamics relaxation. The result is a complete, atomically resolved protein structure predicted entirely from sequence, with high fidelity to experimental data and minimal computational overhead.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention discloses a computational method for protein structure prediction that operates on first principles of physics, specifically modeling the drift and diffusion of protein substructures under the influence of multiple energy fields. The method begins by assigning physicochemical properties—namely net charge and hydrophobicity—to each amino acid residue, which are then mapped to the corresponding alpha carbon atom in the protein backbone. This assignment forms the basis for calculating the forces that govern folding dynamics. The primary forces considered are electrostatic, electrostatic displacement, and thermal energy forces, each derived from fundamental physical relationships.

The electrostatic force between two charged residues is defined by Coulomb’s law:  
\[ F_{\text{elect}} = \frac{q_1 q_2}{4\pi \epsilon_w r^2} \]  
where \( q_1 \) and \( q_2 \) are the charges of the residues, \( r \) is the distance between them, and \( \epsilon_w \approx 78 \epsilon_0 \) is the dielectric constant of water, which serves as the solvent medium. This force accounts for both attractive and repulsive interactions between charged and polar side chains.

The electrostatic displacement force arises from the interaction between polar solvent (water) and nonpolar (hydrophobic) residues in the presence of an electric field. As polar water molecules are drawn toward regions of high electric field, they displace nonpolar residues, effectively pushing them toward regions of lower field strength. This force is derived from the electric field energy \( W \) in a dielectric medium:  
\[ W = -\frac{1}{2} (\epsilon_w - \epsilon_0) \vec{E} \cdot \vec{E}_0 \Delta V \approx -\frac{\beta |q|^2}{r^4} \]  
where \( \beta \) is a constant incorporating the nonpolar volume and permittivity differences. The resulting displacement force is:  
\[ F_{\text{disp}} = -\frac{\partial W}{\partial r} = -\frac{4\beta |q|^2}{r^5} \]  
This repulsive force explains why hydrophobic residues may remain separated despite their intrinsic tendency to aggregate, particularly when intervening charged residues create strong local electric fields.

The thermal energy force represents the randomizing effect of temperature-driven molecular motion. It is formulated as:  
\[ F_{\text{thermal}} = \gamma \sqrt{kT} \]  
where \( k \) is Boltzmann’s constant, \( T \) is temperature in Kelvin, and \( \gamma \) is a scaling factor derived from mobility and time-step parameters. This force introduces stochasticity into the simulation, preventing premature convergence and enabling escape from local energy minima.

These three forces are combined to yield an effective force acting on each alpha carbon:  
\[ F_{\text{eff}} = F_{\text{elect}} + F_{\text{disp}} + F_{\text{thermal}} \]  
Global entropy changes, which typically decrease upon folding, are incorporated into a drift mobility parameter \( \mu \), which modulates the response of the protein to applied forces. The net torque about each backbone bond is computed, and the bond experiencing the greatest torque is rotated in each simulation time step, allowing the protein to evolve toward a lower-energy conformation.

Concurrently, a screening method determines secondary structure. For alpha helix prediction, the algorithm scans the sequence for a hydrophilic residue following a hydrophobic stretch and opens a six-residue scanning bracket. If a second hydrophilic residue is found within this window, the region is evaluated using conditional rules based on summed charge, charge product, and hydrophobic character. If hydrophobic attraction dominates, an alpha helix is assigned; otherwise, the region is flagged for beta sheet analysis. Beta sheet determination involves scanning unstructured five-residue segments and applying inequalities involving summed absolute charge and hydrophobicity to classify beta strands.

Validation on ubiquitin demonstrated >70% residue-level accuracy in secondary structure prediction, outperforming or matching commercial tools like PSIPRED in core region identification. For tertiary structure, predicted secondary elements are embedded as rigid bodies in an initial 3D model. The full chain then undergoes simulated folding via torsional rotations driven by effective forces. Applied to the Villin headpiece (PDB: 1VII), the method achieved a backbone RMSD of 3.7 Å relative to NMR structure within minutes on a desktop PC. Across a test set of 30–157 residue proteins, average RMSD was 4.9 ± 1.08 Å. For the 320-residue human tyrosine phosphatome, RMSD was ~8 Å—competitive with state-of-the-art *ab initio* methods. Final refinement via AMBER10 molecular dynamics improved RMSD by 2–3%.

The method enables rapid classification of unknown proteins, investigation of folding pathways, and analysis of mutation effects (e.g., predicting alpha-to-beta transitions in disease-related variants). Alternative force formulations, modified screening rules, or integration with machine learning are contemplated within the invention’s scope. Systems implementing this method—including software modules executing on general-purpose computers—are also disclosed. The invention thus provides a fast, accurate, and physically interpretable platform for protein structure prediction, advancing structural biology, drug discovery, and protein engineering.
