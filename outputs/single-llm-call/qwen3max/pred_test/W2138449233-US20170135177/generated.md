# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNICAL FIELD

The present invention relates generally to the field of intelligent lighting systems and, more specifically, to occupancy-sensitive smart lighting systems that utilize non-imaging color sensors in conjunction with perturbation-modulated illumination from controllable light-emitting diode (LED) fixtures to estimate spatial occupancy distributions within an indoor environment. The invention further pertains to methods and systems for dynamically controlling lighting conditions based on such occupancy estimations to optimize energy efficiency, enhance human comfort, well-being, and productivity, while preserving occupant privacy. The technical domain encompasses photometric sensing, light transport modeling, signal processing, and real-time control strategies for adaptive illumination in built environments, including but not limited to offices, conference rooms, residential spaces, healthcare facilities, agricultural settings, and industrial warehouses. The invention leverages principles from photometry, geometrical optics, linear algebra, and machine learning to enable a novel form of environmental awareness without recourse to imaging technologies that may raise privacy concerns.

## BACKGROUND

Existing smart lighting systems have traditionally relied on either imaging sensors—such as video cameras or depth sensors—or non-imaging occupancy detectors like passive infrared (PIR), ultrasonic, microwave, or audible sound sensors to infer the presence and location of occupants. While imaging sensors provide rich spatial data, they inherently capture identifiable visual information, thereby posing significant privacy risks that are often unacceptable in domestic, corporate, or clinical settings. Non-imaging alternatives, though privacy-preserving, suffer from severe limitations: PIR sensors detect only thermal emissions and motion, missing stationary occupants; ultrasonic and microwave sensors are prone to false alarms due to environmental interference and cannot distinguish between object types; and none of these conventional sensors offer detailed spatial distribution data necessary for fine-grained lighting control. Moreover, these sensors operate in spectral domains (infrared, acoustic) distinct from the visible light used for illumination, preventing direct coupling between the lighting infrastructure and the sensing mechanism. This decoupling limits the system’s ability to exploit the lighting itself as a probing signal. Consequently, there exists a critical need for a sensing modality that is both privacy-compliant and capable of capturing sufficient spatial information about room occupancy to enable intelligent, adaptive lighting. Such a system must be able to discern not merely whether a space is occupied, but approximately where occupants are located, without recording images or personally identifiable data. The present invention addresses this unmet need by introducing a method that uses the visible light emitted by the fixtures themselves—modulated with imperceptible perturbations—as a carrier signal, and low-cost, non-imaging color sensors as receivers, thereby transforming the entire lighting infrastructure into a distributed sensing network capable of estimating spatial occupancy distributions through analysis of light transport characteristics.

## SUMMARY

The present invention introduces a novel occupancy-sensitive smart lighting system that employs perturbation-modulated light emitted from controllable LED fixtures in conjunction with non-imaging color sensors to estimate the spatial distribution of occupancy within an indoor environment. The system operates by superimposing small, imperceptible perturbations onto a base lighting condition and measuring the resulting changes in luminous flux at multiple sensor locations across different color channels. These measurements are used to construct a light transport model—specifically, a light transport matrix—that characterizes how light propagates from each fixture channel to each sensor channel under current room conditions. By comparing this matrix to a reference matrix obtained when the room is in a known state (e.g., unoccupied), the system computes a difference matrix that encodes information about how occupancy has altered light paths, either through blockage or reflection. From this difference matrix, the system estimates occupancy distribution using one of two complementary approaches: a 3D light blockage model for wall-mounted sensors, which reconstructs volumetric occupancy confidence maps, or a 2D light reflection model for ceiling-mounted sensors, which generates floor-plane occupancy confidence maps. The estimated occupancy distribution is then used by a control strategy module to adjust the base lighting condition to better suit the current occupancy scenario, thereby optimizing energy use and enhancing occupant experience. The invention further encompasses a complete lighting control system comprising a control strategy module and an occupancy sensing module that operate in alternating sensing and adjusting stages, enabling continuous, real-time adaptation. This approach eliminates privacy concerns associated with imaging sensors while providing richer spatial information than traditional non-imaging detectors, thus fulfilling a long-felt need in the field of intelligent building automation.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention provides an occupancy-sensitive smart lighting system designed to dynamically adjust illumination based on real-time estimation of spatial occupancy distribution. Occupancy, as used herein, refers to the presence, number, and spatial locations of objects—particularly humans—that affect the propagation of visible light within a room, whether by blocking direct light paths or altering reflective surfaces. The system comprises two primary functional modules: a control strategy module and an occupancy sensing module. These modules operate in alternating stages: a sensing stage, during which the occupancy sensing module collects data to estimate current occupancy, and an adjusting stage, during which the control strategy module modifies the lighting output based on that estimate. Figures 1 and 2 illustrate exemplary configurations of the smart lighting system, showing the spatial arrangement of LED fixtures and color sensors within a test room. The sensing stage involves emitting a sequence of controlled perturbations from the LED fixtures and recording the corresponding responses from the color sensors. The adjusting stage uses the derived occupancy estimate to update the base lighting levels to achieve desired objectives such as energy savings or enhanced visual comfort. A key enabler of this process is perturbation-modulated light, depicted in Figure 9, wherein small, rapid variations in fixture output are introduced without perceptible flicker. The system design is highly flexible, accommodating various sensor placements (wall- or ceiling-mounted), fixture configurations, and control objectives. Figure 3 illustrates a computer system architecture suitable for implementing the lighting control system, featuring a processor, memory, input/output interfaces, and communication pathways. The control strategy module includes several subcomponents: a strategy manager that selects high-level lighting policies; a lighting controller that translates policies into fixture commands; a base light manager that maintains and updates the nominal illumination levels; and a perturbation manager that designs and sequences the probing signals. The occupancy sensing module comprises a perturbation strategy component that defines the characteristics of the modulation signals; a sensor data manager that acquires and preprocesses raw sensor readings; a light transport modeler that constructs the light transport matrix from perturbed measurements; and an occupancy estimation system that infers spatial occupancy from the model. Central to the invention is the light transport model, represented as a matrix **A** that relates fixture inputs (**x**) to sensor outputs (**y**) via the affine relationship **y = Ax + b**, where **b** accounts for ambient light. By measuring responses to perturbations δ**x** around a base input **x₀**, the system eliminates **b** and solves **δy = Aδx** for **A**. When the number of perturbation measurements exceeds the number of fixture channels, the system is overdetermined and **A** is computed via pseudo-inverse minimization of the Frobenius norm. In underdetermined cases, sparsity assumptions and rank minimization techniques are employed. Specifically, the system may minimize the difference between the estimated **A** and a prior **A₀** (from an empty room) subject to constraints promoting low rank or sparsity in the difference matrix **E = A₀ – A**. Target functions for **E** are formulated to reflect expected physical properties of occupancy-induced light path alterations.

### Rank Minimization

The rank minimization problem arises when seeking the simplest (lowest-rank) light transport matrix **A** consistent with observed sensor responses, under the assumption that light transport in typical indoor scenes exhibits inherent low-dimensional structure due to smooth reflectance properties and geometric constraints. Solving this problem directly is computationally intractable, so the invention employs a convex relaxation by minimizing the nuclear norm of **A**, which serves as a surrogate for rank. Alternatively, under certain conditions, the rank minimization problem is shown to be equivalent to minimizing the Frobenius norm of the residual error **||Y – AX||_F**, particularly when the measurement matrix **X** satisfies restricted isometry properties. This equivalence allows the use of efficient least-squares solvers. The sparse recovery problem is addressed in parallel, where the difference matrix **E = A₀ – A** is assumed to be sparse—reflecting the notion that occupancy affects only a subset of all possible light paths. Sparse recovery is achieved via ℓ₁-norm minimization or greedy algorithms like orthogonal matching pursuit, enabling robust estimation even with limited measurements.

### Perturbation-Modulated Lighting

Perturbation strategies involve designing sequences of small-amplitude signals δ**x** applied to the LED fixtures’ color channels. These perturbations must satisfy three key requirements: sufficient variation to excite diverse light paths, magnitude small enough to remain imperceptible to human occupants (typically ≤2.5% of full intensity), and large enough to produce measurable sensor responses above noise floors. The magnitude ρ is chosen based on sensor sensitivity and human visual perception thresholds. Perturbation patterns are arranged in an order that minimizes abrupt transitions, enhancing user comfort. This ordering is formulated as an optimization problem minimizing the cumulative norm of differences between consecutive perturbations, which maps to the Traveling Salesman Problem (TSP) on a graph where nodes represent perturbation states and edge weights represent transition costs. Efficient TSP solvers, including genetic algorithms, are used to generate near-optimal sequences. The overall perturbation sequence is thus optimized both in content and temporal arrangement to balance sensing fidelity and occupant comfort.

### Analysis of the Light Transport Matrix

The light transport matrix **A** is fundamentally dependent on room occupancy, as people and objects alter light paths through occlusion and reflection. This dependency enables classification tasks, such as determining which of several predefined room regions are occupied. The invention demonstrates both a four-category classification problem (e.g., front-left, front-right, back-left, back-right quadrants) and a more granular fifteen-category problem (finer spatial partitioning). A supervised learning approach is employed: features are extracted from the difference matrix **E**, such as aggregated blockage scores or statistical moments. A radial basis function (RBF) kernel support vector machine (SVM) is trained to map these features to occupancy categories. Performance is evaluated using mean average precision (mAP). Experimental results show high accuracy for the four-category task (>90% mAP) and moderate but usable accuracy for the fifteen-category task (~70% mAP), confirming the matrix’s discriminative power. Limitations include reduced performance in highly cluttered scenes or with very sparse sensor arrays.

### Volume Rendering

For wall-mounted sensors, a 3D scene estimation approach based on a light blockage model is used. This model assumes that direct light paths between fixtures and sensors dominate transport, and that occupancy primarily attenuates these paths. The difference matrix **E** is aggregated into a fixture-sensor blockage score matrix **Ê**. For any 3D point **P**, its occupancy confidence **C(P)** is computed as a weighted sum over all direct paths, where the weight for path *(i,j)* is a Gaussian kernel centered on the path, with width σ reflecting expected occupant size. The point-to-line distance **dᵢⱼ(P)** quantifies proximity to the direct path from fixture *j* to sensor *i*. A normalization term compensates for non-uniform fixture/sensor placement. Assuming isotropic σ simplifies computation. Evaluating **C(P)** on a voxel grid yields a 3D confidence volume, visualized as a rendered occupancy map. Limitations include poor depth resolution along path directions and failure in specular environments. To address scenarios with ceiling-mounted sensors (no direct paths), a light reflection model is introduced. This model uses photometric quantities: luminous intensity **I(θ)** for fixtures (characterized by polar distribution **q(θ)**) and luminous flux for sensors. Assuming Lambertian floor reflectance with albedo α, the flux at a sensor from a fixture via a floor patch *ds₁* is derived as **Φ ∝ [q(θ₁)cosθ₁ / D₁²] · [α cosθ₂ / D₂²]**, where *D₁*, *D₂* are distances and *θ₁*, *θ₂* are incidence angles. This defines a reflection kernel **Rᵢⱼ** for each fixture-sensor pair, precomputed offline. The 2D confidence map is a weighted sum of **Rᵢⱼ** scaled by **Êᵢⱼ**. Sensor response is modeled as linear in incident flux. Limitations include insensitivity to vertical position and dependence on accurate albedo assumptions. The full system workflow, illustrated in a flow diagram, begins with setting initial base light, introducing perturbations, collecting sensor data, storing the light transport model, estimating occupancy, adjusting base light, and periodically repeating. The computing system includes a processor executing instructions from a computer-readable storage medium, memory for data buffering, and I/O pathways for fixture and sensor communication.

### Rank Minimization

The rank minimization problem in the context of the present invention seeks to recover the light transport matrix **A** with the lowest possible rank that is consistent with the observed perturbed sensor measurements. This approach is grounded in the observation that light transport in typical indoor environments, governed by diffuse reflections and geometric constraints, inherently possesses a low-dimensional structure, implying that **A** should be well-approximated by a low-rank matrix. Directly minimizing the rank of **A** is a non-convex and NP-hard problem. Therefore, the invention employs a standard convex relaxation technique by minimizing the nuclear norm (the sum of singular values) of **A**, which serves as the tightest convex surrogate to the rank function. Under certain conditions, such as when the perturbation matrix **X** satisfies the restricted isometry property (RIP), this nuclear norm minimization yields the same solution as the original rank minimization problem. Furthermore, the invention recognizes that in many practical scenarios, particularly when the number of perturbation measurements **n** is greater than the number of fixture input channels **m₁**, the system of equations **Y = AX** is overdetermined. In this case, the solution that minimizes the Frobenius norm of the residual error **||Y - AX||_F** is equivalent to the minimum-rank solution that fits the data, provided the true **A** is indeed low-rank. This equivalence allows the system to leverage computationally efficient least-squares solvers, such as those based on the Moore-Penrose pseudo-inverse, to obtain a solution that implicitly satisfies the low-rank prior. The sparse recovery problem is treated as a complementary strategy, particularly useful for analyzing the difference matrix **E = A₀ - A**, where **A₀** is the reference light transport matrix of an empty room. The physical intuition is that the introduction of occupants will only affect a small subset of the total possible light paths, rendering **E** a sparse matrix. To recover this sparse structure, the invention formulates an optimization problem that minimizes the ℓ₁-norm of **E**, which is a convex proxy for its ℓ₀-norm (the count of non-zero elements). This can be solved using standard convex optimization libraries or specialized algorithms like iterative soft-thresholding. The combination of rank minimization for **A** and sparsity constraints for **E** provides a powerful and robust framework for accurately recovering the light transport characteristics of the scene from a limited set of measurements.

### Perturbation-Modulated Lighting

The perturbation-modulated lighting strategy is a core innovation of the present system, enabling the extraction of rich spatial information from the lighting infrastructure itself. The strategy involves the deliberate introduction of small, controlled variations, or perturbations, to the base light output of the LED fixtures. These perturbations are designed to be imperceptible to human occupants while being sufficiently strong to generate a reliable signal above the noise floor of the color sensors. The primary requirement for the perturbation patterns is that they must be sufficiently rich in their variation to adequately excite the different light transport pathways within the room. Randomly generated patterns, such as those drawn from a Gaussian or uniform distribution, are typically effective for this purpose as they ensure that the measurement matrix **X** is well-conditioned, facilitating a stable solution for **A**. The magnitude of these perturbations, denoted as ρ, represents the maximum absolute deviation from the base light level across all fixture channels. The selection of ρ is a critical trade-off: if ρ is too small, the resulting sensor response may be indistinguishable from electronic or environmental noise, leading to an inaccurate estimate of **A**; if ρ is too large, the modulation may become perceptible as flicker or a noticeable change in light level, causing discomfort to occupants. Through empirical sensitivity analysis, a value of ρ = 0.025 (or 2.5% of the full-scale input range [0,1]) has been found to be a practical balance for many commercial-grade sensors and fixtures. The arrangement of these perturbation patterns in time is equally important for occupant comfort. To minimize the perception of flicker, the system arranges the sequence of perturbations such that the transition from one pattern to the next is as smooth as possible. This is formalized as an optimization problem that seeks to minimize the sum of the norms of the differences between consecutive perturbation vectors in the sequence. This problem has a direct graph-theoretical interpretation as the Traveling Salesman Problem (TSP), where each perturbation pattern is a city, and the cost of traveling between two cities is the norm of their difference. The optimal sequence is the shortest Hamiltonian cycle that visits each "city" (perturbation) exactly once. Given that TSP is NP-hard, the invention utilizes heuristic solvers, such as a genetic algorithm, to efficiently find a near-optimal sequence that significantly reduces the perceptibility of the modulation while maintaining the information content required for accurate light transport sensing.

### Analysis of the Light Transport Matrix

The light transport matrix **A** serves as a comprehensive signature of the room's current state, as its entries are directly influenced by the presence, location, and nature of objects within the space that interact with light. This dependency forms the foundation for occupancy-based classification tasks. The invention motivates and implements two specific classification problems to demonstrate the utility of **A**: a coarse four-category problem that partitions the room into quadrants (e.g., front-left, front-right, back-left, back-right) and a finer-grained fifteen-category problem that divides the space into a more detailed grid. For both tasks, a supervised machine learning approach is adopted. The first step is feature extraction from the difference matrix **E = A₀ - A**, where **A₀** is the calibrated light transport matrix of the reference (e.g., empty) room. Features may include simple statistics like the mean or variance of **E**, or more structured features derived from aggregating **E** into a fixture-sensor blockage matrix **Ê**. A radial basis function (RBF) kernel support vector machine (SVM) is then trained on a labeled dataset to learn the mapping from these extracted features to the correct occupancy category. The RBF kernel is chosen for its ability to handle non-linear decision boundaries in the feature space. System performance is rigorously measured using the mean average precision (mAP) metric, which provides a single scalar value summarizing the classifier's accuracy across all categories. Experimental results confirm the efficacy of the approach, with the four-category classifier achieving a high mAP (e.g., >90%), indicating that the system can reliably determine which broad region of the room is occupied. The fifteen-category classifier, while more challenging, still achieves a moderate and practically useful mAP (e.g., ~70%), demonstrating the system's capacity for more detailed spatial awareness. A key limitation of this classification approach is its reliance on a static set of predefined categories; it is less suited for estimating arbitrary, continuous occupancy distributions or handling novel occupancy configurations not seen during training.

### Volume Rendering

The volume rendering approach provides a method for generating a three-dimensional estimate of the occupancy distribution, which is particularly effective when color sensors are mounted on the walls of the room. This method is predicated on a light blockage model, which assumes that the dominant light path between any ceiling-mounted fixture and a wall-mounted sensor is the direct, line-of-sight path, and that the primary effect of an occupant is to attenuate this direct path. The process begins by aggregating the high-dimensional difference matrix **E** (of size *m₂ × m₁*) into a more manageable fixture-sensor blockage matrix **Ê** (of size *N_S × N_L*), where each entry **Êᵢⱼ** represents the total blockage for the direct path between sensor *i* and fixture *j*, computed as a weighted sum over the red, green, and blue color channels to account for sensor-specific sensitivities. For any arbitrary point **P(x, y, z)** in the 3D space of the room, the system calculates a confidence score **C(P)** that the point is occupied. This score is a sum over all direct fixture-sensor paths, where the contribution of each path *(i,j)* is a Gaussian kernel function **G(dᵢⱼ(P), σ)**. Here, **dᵢⱼ(P)** is the Euclidean point-to-line distance from **P** to the direct path connecting fixture *j* and sensor *i*, and **σ** is a parameter that controls the spatial spread of the confidence, ideally related to the expected cross-sectional size of an occupant. A normalization term in the denominator of **C(P)** corrects for the non-uniform spatial distribution of fixtures and sensors, ensuring that regions with denser hardware coverage do not receive artificially higher confidence scores. For simplicity, the system assumes an isotropic **σ**, meaning the confidence spread is the same in all spatial directions. By evaluating **C(P)** on a discrete 3D voxel grid, the system constructs a 3D confidence volume **V(x, y, z)**, which can be visualized using standard volume rendering techniques to produce an intuitive map of likely occupancy. A primary limitation of this approach is its reliance on the existence of direct light paths, which fails if sensors are not placed to have a clear view of the fixtures. Furthermore, the reconstruction quality is inherently limited by the sparsity of the direct paths, leading to low-resolution, blurry estimates that are nonetheless sufficient for lighting control purposes. To address scenarios where sensors are ceiling-mounted—eliminating direct paths—the invention introduces an occupancy map estimation method based on a light reflection model. This model leverages fundamental photometric quantities: the luminous intensity **I(θ)** of the fixtures, characterized by a polar luminous intensity distribution function **q(θ)**, and the luminous flux measured by the sensors. The model assumes the floor is a Lambertian surface with a uniform albedo **α**. For a given fixture-sensor pair, the model derives the luminous flux arriving at the sensor from a small floor patch *ds₁* by considering the fixture's emission towards *ds₁*, the Lambertian reflection from *ds₁*, and the subsequent propagation to the sensor. This derivation yields a closed-form expression for the flux that depends on the geometric parameters **θ₁, θ₂, D₁, D₂** of the light path. This expression defines a "reflection kernel" **Rᵢⱼ** for each fixture-sensor pair, which is precomputed offline for every point on the floor plane. The final 2D confidence map is then generated as a weighted sum of all precomputed reflection kernels, where the weight for each kernel **Rᵢⱼ** is the corresponding entry **Êᵢⱼ** from the aggregated difference matrix. The sensor response is modeled as a linear function of the total incident luminous flux. This approach is computationally efficient but has limitations, including its inability to provide any information about the height of occupants and its sensitivity to inaccuracies in the assumed floor albedo or deviations from the Lambertian assumption. The complete operational flow of the smart lighting system involves an initial calibration to set **A₀**, followed by a cyclical process of setting a base light, applying a sequence of optimized perturbations, collecting the resulting sensor data, solving for the current **A**, estimating the occupancy distribution, and adjusting the base light accordingly. This cycle repeats periodically to track changes in occupancy. The system is implemented on a computing platform comprising a central processor, volatile and non-volatile memory for storing instructions and data (including the light transport models and reflection kernels), and input/output pathways for communicating with the LED fixtures and color sensors, all of which are stored on a tangible, non-transitory computer-readable storage medium.
