## BACKGROUND

Trauma remains one of the leading causes of death worldwide, with blunt mechanical impact injuries representing a significant proportion of trauma-related fatalities and long-term disabilities. These injuries arise from diverse contexts including vehicular collisions, military combat exposure, sports-related concussions, and occupational accidents. The pathophysiological consequences of such trauma can manifest acutely, as in cases of blunt liver injury requiring immediate surgical intervention, or chronically, as observed in traumatic brain injury (TBI) and chronic traumatic encephalopathy (CTE), where symptoms may emerge months or years after repeated sub-lethal impacts. Despite extensive research efforts, the fundamental cellular and molecular mechanisms underlying blunt impact injury remain incompletely understood, largely due to limitations in existing experimental models.

Animal models have historically served as the primary platform for studying trauma pathophysiology. However, these models suffer from several critical drawbacks: they lack standardization across laboratories, exhibit high inter-individual variability, raise ethical concerns, and often fail to recapitulate human-specific injury responses due to interspecies differences in anatomy, physiology, and cellular behavior. Moreover, endpoint assays commonly used in animal studies—such as histopathology or immunohistochemistry—provide only static snapshots of damage and cannot capture the dynamic, real-time evolution of cellular responses to mechanical insult. These limitations have motivated a shift toward in vitro platforms that enable controlled, reproducible, and ethically sustainable investigation of injury mechanisms at the cellular level.

Human tissues are routinely subjected to linear accelerations ranging from 80 to 250 g during high-impact events such as football tackles, boxing punches, or blast exposures in military settings. Acceleration is widely used as a surrogate metric for injury severity because it is readily measurable in field settings using wearable sensors. However, acceleration alone does not fully capture the biomechanical insult experienced by cells; rather, it induces transient pressure gradients within biological fluids and soft tissues, which may be the true mediators of cellular damage. Among proposed injury mechanisms, dynamic cavitation—the formation, expansion, and violent collapse of vapor-filled bubbles in response to rapid tensile pressure—has gained increasing attention as a potential driver of tissue disruption in the brain and other organs during high-acceleration events.

Despite theoretical and computational advances, experimental validation of cavitation-mediated injury in live biological systems has been hindered by formidable technical challenges. Conventional in vitro wound healing or scratch assays are ill-suited for studying acute mechanical trauma because they induce non-physiological, localized damage that does not replicate the distributed, transient forces of real-world impact. Similarly, electrical pulse-based methods (e.g., electroporation) introduce confounding thermal effects and non-mechanical stressors that obscure the interpretation of purely mechanical injury pathways. There exists a critical need for an in vitro platform capable of delivering well-characterized, biologically relevant mechanical impacts while maintaining strict environmental control and enabling longitudinal, multiplexed observation of live cell populations.

Current in vitro impact systems often fail to replicate the precise temporal profile (sub-millisecond duration) and amplitude (>150 g) of real-world blunt trauma. Furthermore, they typically lack integration with live-cell imaging, preventing pre- and post-impact comparison of the same cell population—a necessity given the inherent heterogeneity of cellular responses. Without such capabilities, it is impossible to distinguish true injury-induced changes from natural variations in growth, confluency, or morphology. The absence of concurrent pressure and acceleration measurements further limits mechanistic insight, as the relationship between applied acceleration and resulting intracellular or pericellular pressure remains poorly quantified in live cell environments. These gaps underscore the urgent need for a well-controlled, high-fidelity in vitro platform that can isolate and interrogate specific injury mechanisms—particularly the relative contributions of bulk acceleration versus localized cavitation—to advance both fundamental understanding and clinical translation in trauma medicine.

## BRIEF DESCRIPTION

The present invention provides a novel integrated system and method for in vitro characterization of cellular responses to blunt mechanical impact under tightly controlled and physiologically relevant conditions. The invention enables the application of precisely calibrated mechanical impacts—mimicking real-world trauma scenarios such as those encountered in sports, military, or automotive contexts—to live cell cultures while simultaneously monitoring cellular behavior before, during, and after impact. Central to the invention is a drop-tower-based impact delivery mechanism that generates accelerations in the range of 80–1,500 g with sub-millisecond rise times, closely replicating the biomechanical signatures of human blunt trauma.

A key innovation of the disclosed platform is its multiplexed approach, which allows parallel experimentation on multiple identical cell cultures under identical environmental conditions. This capability dramatically enhances statistical power and reproducibility by enabling direct comparison of impacted versus non-impacted cultures derived from the same passage and seeded at the same density. Moreover, the system is fully compatible with standard live-cell imaging incubators, permitting continuous, non-invasive monitoring of cellular confluency, morphology, membrane integrity, and other dynamic parameters over days or weeks without removing cultures from optimal growth conditions. This eliminates confounding perturbations associated with traditional endpoint assays and reveals temporal patterns of injury progression and recovery that would otherwise remain hidden.

The invention offers numerous advantages over existing methodologies. First, it decouples acceleration from pressure by enabling independent measurement and correlation of both parameters during impact, thereby resolving long-standing ambiguities in injury criteria. Second, it incorporates a specially designed cell culture holder that minimizes artifacts from trapped air, shock wave propagation, and environmental fluctuations, ensuring that observed effects are attributable solely to the intended mechanical insult. Third, through integration with high-speed videography and synchronized sensor data acquisition, the system captures transient phenomena such as cavitation bubble dynamics with microsecond resolution, providing unprecedented insight into the spatiotemporal mechanics of cellular injury.

In summary, the in vitro platform of the present invention provides a comprehensive, high-throughput, and environmentally stable system for probing the fundamental mechanisms of blunt impact injury at the cellular level. It supports a wide range of applications, including the identification of injury thresholds, the evaluation of protective interventions, the screening of therapeutic compounds, and the elucidation of cell-type-specific vulnerability to mechanical stress. By bridging the gap between theoretical biomechanics and experimental cell biology, the invention establishes a new standard for in vitro trauma modeling with broad implications for biomedical research, diagnostics, and safety engineering.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

### In Vitro Setup for Blunt Injury Mechanisms

The core of the invention is a drop-tower-based system designed to deliver reproducible, well-characterized mechanical impacts to live cell cultures. The system comprises a vertical drop tower equipped with a release mechanism, a weighted impactor, and a sample holder positioned at the base. A cell culture setup is securely mounted within the holder, which is engineered to transmit controlled acceleration to the culture while isolating it from extraneous vibrations or direct contact forces. The impactor is lifted to a predetermined height and released, striking the top of the holder and inducing a rapid, transient acceleration in the vertical direction—mimicking the inertial loading experienced by internal organs during blunt trauma.

The cell culture setup consists of a standard 35-mm petri dish containing adherent cells grown in a thin layer of culture medium, sealed with a transparent silicone film to prevent spillage during impact. This assembly is sandwiched between rigid aluminum plates and fastened with bolts to ensure structural integrity. Critically, the depth of the culture medium is maintained at approximately 3–5 mm during normal incubation to facilitate adequate gas exchange (particularly CO₂ diffusion for pH stability) but is temporarily increased to ~10 mm immediately prior to impact by adding additional medium. This step serves two essential purposes: first, it ensures sufficient fluid volume to generate physiologically relevant pressure gradients during acceleration; second, it displaces air from the chamber, eliminating trapped gas pockets that could nucleate spurious cavitation or cause non-biological cell damage upon compression.

The holder itself incorporates two layers of soft foam with distinct mechanical properties to tailor the impact profile. A thin (1 mm), stiff foam (4 MPa) at the top prevents direct metal-to-metal contact between the impactor and holder, thereby avoiding the generation of high-frequency shock waves that do not occur in biological tissues protected by skin and soft tissue. A thicker (12 mm), softer foam (0.4 MPa) at the bottom allows controlled rebound and fine-tunes the acceleration pulse duration to fall within the 0.1–10 ms range typical of human blunt trauma. This dual-foam design enables precise replication of injury-relevant acceleration waveforms while maintaining compatibility with standard laboratory cell culture protocols.

### Multiplexed In Vitro Cell Cultures

To address the inherent heterogeneity of cellular responses and ensure statistically robust conclusions, the invention employs a multiplexed live-cell imaging strategy. Multiple petri dishes—typically up to twelve—are prepared in parallel from the same cell stock and seeded at identical densities. These cultures are placed on a motorized stage within a temperature-, humidity-, and CO₂-controlled incubator equipped with an automated microscope. At regular intervals (e.g., every 30 minutes), the system sequentially images predefined regions of interest (ROIs) across all dishes using phase-contrast or fluorescence optics, generating time-lapse datasets of cellular confluency, morphology, and viability.

Local confluency—the fraction of each ROI covered by cells—is computed in real time using validated image segmentation algorithms, enabling continuous tracking of growth kinetics. This approach reveals spatial variations in cell behavior even within a single dish, which are often masked by whole-dish averaging. For instance, edge effects, local nutrient gradients, or stochastic differences in initial seeding can lead to divergent growth trajectories in different ROIs. By monitoring both average and local confluency, the system avoids misleading conclusions that might arise from sampling only a single, potentially unrepresentative area.

When cultures reach a target confluency (e.g., 35–40%, corresponding to mid-logarithmic growth phase), they are removed from the incubator, supplemented with additional medium to achieve the 10-mm depth, and assembled into individual holders. Control cultures undergo identical handling—medium addition, assembly, and temporary storage outside the incubator—except for the actual impact, thereby isolating the effect of mechanical insult from procedural artifacts. Post-impact, all dishes are returned to the incubator for continued imaging, allowing direct comparison of the same cell populations before and after trauma. This longitudinal, within-subject design dramatically increases sensitivity to subtle injury effects and enables quantification of recovery dynamics, such as membrane repair or repopulation of damaged zones.

### Quantifying the Blunt Injury

The invention enables precise quantification of injury thresholds by correlating mechanical input parameters with cellular output metrics. Experiments are conducted across a range of drop heights (e.g., 5–40 cm), generating peak accelerations from ~100 g to >1,300 g as measured by a triaxial accelerometer mounted on the holder. High-speed cameras (operating at ≥50,000 frames per second) simultaneously record the cell culture chamber during impact, capturing transient phenomena such as fluid motion and bubble formation.

Analysis of confluency curves reveals a critical drop height (~30–40 cm, corresponding to ~1,000–1,370 g) above which significant, localized cell loss occurs. Below this threshold, impacted cultures exhibit growth trajectories indistinguishable from controls, demonstrating that high acceleration alone—even exceeding conventional injury criteria by an order of magnitude—does not necessarily cause damage in small-scale systems. This finding underscores the importance of system size (specifically fluid height H) in determining injury susceptibility, consistent with the theoretical relationship pₐ ≈ –ρH·aᵢₙ, where pressure scales linearly with both acceleration and characteristic length.

At supra-threshold impacts (e.g., 40 cm drop), high-speed imaging reveals the nucleation and collapse of cavitation bubbles at the bottom of the petri dish—the site of maximum tensile pressure. These events correlate spatially and temporally with focal zones of cell detachment and death, as confirmed by propidium iodide (PI) staining, which labels cells with compromised plasma membranes. Notably, PI-positive cells show progressive fluorescence decay over 24–48 hours, indicating active membrane repair rather than immediate necrosis, a nuance only detectable through longitudinal imaging.

To directly compare acceleration- and cavitation-induced pressures, transparent plastic tubes (diameter ~12 mm) filled with water and instrumented with miniature pressure sensors are subjected to identical impacts. At 30 mm drop (~140 g), smooth, monotonic pressure profiles matching theoretical predictions are observed. At 40 mm drop (~175 g), however, the pressure trace exhibits high-frequency oscillations superimposed on the baseline, coinciding with cavitation bubble collapse captured by synchronized high-speed video. Quantitative analysis shows that cavitation generates pressure transients with amplitudes exceeding 250 kPa and rates of change (>6,000 kPa/ms) orders of magnitude greater than acceleration-induced pressure (<200 kPa, ~170 kPa/ms). These extreme, localized stresses—not bulk acceleration—are identified as the primary drivers of cellular injury, resolving a key ambiguity in trauma biomechanics and establishing cavitation as a critical injury mechanism in high-acceleration scenarios.
