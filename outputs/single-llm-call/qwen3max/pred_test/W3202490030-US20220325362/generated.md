# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

Infectious diseases represent a persistent and formidable threat to global public health, arising from pathogenic microorganisms such as bacteria, viruses, fungi, and parasites that can be transmitted through air, food, bodily fluids, or direct contact. These diseases have historically caused widespread morbidity and mortality, often triggering socioeconomic disruption on a global scale. Notable historical outbreaks include the 1918 Spanish influenza pandemic, which infected an estimated one-third of the world’s population and resulted in tens of millions of deaths, and the 2009 H1N1 swine flu pandemic, which demonstrated the rapid transmissibility of novel influenza strains across international borders. The human immunodeficiency virus (HIV), responsible for acquired immunodeficiency syndrome (AIDS), emerged in the late twentieth century and continues to affect millions worldwide despite advances in antiretroviral therapy. Other significant viral threats include the Zika virus, associated with congenital neurological disorders; the Ebola virus, known for its high case fatality rate and epidemic potential in resource-limited settings; and coronaviruses such as severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus (SARS-CoV) and Middle East respiratory syndrome coronavirus (MERS-CoV), which caused regional outbreaks with high mortality and highlighted the vulnerability of healthcare systems to emerging zoonotic pathogens.

The most recent and impactful global health crisis has been the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic, caused by severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2). First identified in late 2019, SARS-CoV-2 spread rapidly across continents due to its high transmissibility, asymptomatic shedding, and ability to infect via respiratory droplets and aerosols. The emergence of successive variants—such as Alpha, Delta, and Omicron—further complicated containment efforts by altering transmissibility, immune evasion, and clinical severity. A central challenge throughout this and prior pandemics has been the timely and accurate detection of the causative pathogen to enable effective isolation, contact tracing, and treatment strategies. Traditional diagnostic approaches include antigen-based tests, serological assays detecting host antibodies, and nucleic acid amplification tests (NAATs). While antigen tests offer speed and simplicity, they often lack the sensitivity required for early or low-viral-load infections. Serology is useful for determining past exposure but cannot distinguish active infection. In contrast, nucleic acid detection, particularly via polymerase chain reaction (PCR), provides high sensitivity and specificity but requires sophisticated laboratory infrastructure, trained personnel, and thermal cycling equipment, limiting its utility in point-of-care or resource-constrained settings.

To address these limitations, nucleic acid detection systems leveraging CRISPR-Cas technologies have emerged as promising alternatives. These systems exploit the programmable RNA-guided targeting capabilities of CRISPR-associated (Cas) nucleases to detect specific viral sequences with high fidelity. Among these, Cas13-based methods detect RNA targets by activating collateral RNase activity upon target binding, cleaving reporter RNA probes to generate a fluorescent or colorimetric signal. Similarly, Cas12-based systems detect DNA targets and exhibit collateral DNase activity. Both platforms enable isothermal, instrument-free detection and have been adapted into lateral flow or fluorescence-based point-of-care devices. However, current CRISPR diagnostics face several limitations. Their reliance on single-effector enzymes restricts signal amplification potential, often necessitating pre-amplification steps such as recombinase polymerase amplification (RPA) or loop-mediated isothermal amplification (LAMP) to achieve clinically relevant sensitivity. Additionally, Cas12 requires a protospacer adjacent motif (PAM) near the target site, limiting targetable genomic regions, while Cas13 activity can be inhibited by structured RNA or require extensive optimization for multiplexing. Moreover, the collateral cleavage activity in both systems is transient and stoichiometrically linked to target binding, constraining dynamic range and signal-to-noise ratios.

These challenges underscore the need for next-generation CRISPR-based diagnostics that integrate intrinsic signal amplification, multi-modal detection, and compatibility with both RNA and DNA viruses without stringent sequence constraints. The Type III-A CRISPR-Cas system, also known as Csm, offers a compelling solution. Unlike Class 2 systems (e.g., Cas12, Cas13), Type III systems are multi-subunit ribonucleoprotein complexes that respond to target RNA transcription and possess multiple enzymatic outputs: sequence-specific RNA cleavage, non-specific DNase activity, cyclic oligoadenylate (cOA) synthesis, and cOA-activated ancillary RNase activity. This multilayered response enables disproportionate signal amplification and dual-channel readouts, yet its complexity has historically hindered practical deployment. The present invention overcomes these barriers by engineering a robust, reconstituted Type III-A system optimized for rapid, sensitive, and versatile viral detection—thereby addressing critical gaps in current infectious disease diagnostics.

## BRIEF SUMMARY

The present invention introduces a novel diagnostic platform termed MORIARTY (Multipronged, One-pot, target RNA-Induced, Augmentable, Rapid, Test sYstem), which leverages the unique biochemical properties of the Type III-A CRISPR-Cas system for the detection of viral nucleic acids. MORIARTY exploits the Lactococcus lactis Csm (LlCsm) effector complex and its ancillary nuclease LlCsm6 to simultaneously generate two independent, cumulative signals—DNase and RNase activities—upon recognition of a target viral RNA or transcribed DNA. This dual-output architecture enables enhanced sensitivity, built-in signal amplification via cyclic oligoadenylate (cOA6)-mediated activation of LlCsm6, and flexibility in assay design under varied buffer conditions. The system operates effectively in both amplification-free and pre-amplification-coupled formats, making it suitable for high-sensitivity detection in clinical samples.

The invention provides methods for detecting viral pathogens, particularly RNA viruses such as SARS-CoV-2, by reprogramming the CRISPR RNA (crRNA) guide sequence of the LlCsm complex to target conserved regions of the viral genome, such as the Spike (S) gene. Upon binding to cognate viral RNA, the LlCsm complex activates its HD domain to cleave single-stranded DNA reporters and its GGDD motif to synthesize cOA6, which in turn allosterically activates LlCsm6 to degrade single-stranded RNA reporters. Fluorescently labeled DNA and RNA probes enable real-time, quantitative monitoring of both activities in a single reaction vessel. Key advantages of MORIARTY include its ability to function at a constant temperature (37°C), eliminating the need for thermocyclers; its compatibility with crude clinical samples; its capacity for multiplexed targeting using multiple crRNAs to enhance sensitivity; and its adaptability to transcription-coupled detection when combined with T7 RNA polymerase for DNA virus or amplified DNA detection.

Embodiments of the invention include amplification-free MORIARTY assays capable of detecting femtomolar concentrations of in vitro transcribed SARS-CoV-2 RNA, as well as RT-RPA-coupled MORIARTY workflows achieving attomolar sensitivity—comparable to gold-standard qRT-PCR—when applied to patient-derived nasopharyngeal swabs. The system demonstrates high specificity, distinguishing single- and double-mismatched targets, and shows strong concordance with clinical qPCR results. Furthermore, MORIARTY can be configured with different fluorophore-quencher pairs (e.g., DNA-Alexa, DNA-FAM, RNA-FAM) to enable single- or dual-channel readouts, facilitating integration into portable fluorescence readers or lateral flow devices. In summary, the invention provides a versatile, sensitive, rapid, and equipment-minimal diagnostic platform for infectious disease surveillance, outbreak response, and point-of-care testing.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The MORIARTY system represents a significant advancement in nucleic acid diagnostics by harnessing the multifunctional enzymatic activities of the Type III-A CRISPR-Cas system derived from Lactococcus lactis (LlCsm). Unlike conventional CRISPR diagnostics that rely on single-effector nucleases like Cas12 or Cas13, MORIARTY integrates four coordinated enzymatic functions within a single reaction: (1) sequence-specific cleavage of target RNA by the Csm3 subunit, (2) collateral single-stranded DNase activity by the HD domain of Csm1, (3) synthesis of cyclic oligoadenylate second messengers (primarily cOA6) by the GGDD motif of Csm1, and (4) cOA6-activated non-specific RNase activity by the ancillary protein LlCsm6. This architecture enables a multipronged detection strategy wherein a single target recognition event triggers two parallel, cumulative signal outputs—DNase and RNase—that can be independently or jointly monitored to enhance sensitivity and reliability.

A key motivation for developing MORIARTY stems from the urgent need for rapid, accurate, and deployable diagnostics during infectious disease outbreaks. The system achieves exceptional sensitivity, capable of detecting viral RNA at femtomolar concentrations in amplification-free formats and attomolar levels when coupled with isothermal pre-amplification such as reverse transcription-recombinase polymerase amplification (RT-RPA). This performance rivals or exceeds that of existing CRISPR-based platforms while offering distinct advantages: no requirement for protospacer adjacent motifs (PAMs), activation by either RNA or transcribed DNA, intrinsic signal amplification via cOA6, and operation at a single temperature (37°C), enabling use with simple heating devices like hand warmers.

The core of the MORIARTY system is the LlCsm effector complex, which is produced recombinantly in Escherichia coli from an all-in-one expression plasmid encoding all Csm subunits (Csm1–6) and a customizable crRNA. The crRNA can be reprogrammed to target any viral sequence provided it contains a non-complementary 3′ protospacer flanking sequence (3′ PFS) relative to the crRNA 5′ tag—a minimal constraint satisfied by most viral genomes, including SARS-CoV-2. Upon binding to cognate target RNA, the complex undergoes conformational activation, initiating DNase activity (strictly Mn²⁺-dependent) and cOA6 synthesis (supported by Mg²⁺ or Mn²⁺). The synthesized cOA6 diffuses and activates LlCsm6, which then degrades RNA reporters indiscriminately, generating a secondary, amplified signal.

MORIARTY supports amplification-free detection by directly sensing viral RNA in clinical samples. Under optimized conditions—typically 10 mM MgCl₂, 1.5 mM ATP, and elevated LlCsm6 concentration—the system primarily reports cOA6-driven RNase activity, achieving detection limits of ~5 fM for SARS-CoV-2 S-gene mRNA. Sensitivity is further enhanced through multiplexed targeting using three distinct LlCsm complexes directed against non-overlapping regions of the S gene, lowering the limit of detection to ~2000 copies/μL in patient RNA extracts.

For ultra-sensitive applications, MORIARTY is seamlessly integrated with RT-RPA in a one-pot, transcription-coupled format termed T7-MORIARTY. Here, viral RNA is first reverse-transcribed and amplified into double-stranded DNA containing a T7 promoter. Addition of T7 RNA polymerase drives in situ transcription, producing RNA that activates LlCsm. This configuration allows simultaneous DNase and RNase reporting under mixed Mg²⁺/Mn²⁺ conditions, yielding statistically significant signals down to 62 copies/μL and demonstrating strong concordance with clinical qRT-PCR across patient samples.

System embodiments include variations in probe design (DNA-Alexa for DNase, RNA-FAM for RNase), metal ion composition (Mg²⁺ alone for RNase-dominant readout; Mg²⁺ + Mn²⁺ for dual activity), and crRNA multiplexing. Mutant complexes (e.g., Csm3-D30A for sustained activity; Csm1-H13A/D14N to ablate DNase) enable mechanistic validation and assay tuning. The versatility of MORIARTY extends beyond SARS-CoV-2 to any RNA or DNA virus, positioning it as a universal platform for pandemic preparedness and decentralized diagnostics.

### EXAMPLES

The MORIARTY system was developed and validated through a series of experimental examples demonstrating its design, optimization, and clinical applicability. Initially, the LlCsm effector complex was produced by expressing an all-in-one codon-optimized plasmid in E. coli, followed by Ni-NTA affinity and size-exclusion chromatography to yield active ribonucleoprotein (RNP) complexes. The ancillary nuclease LlCsm6 was expressed and purified separately. The system was reprogrammed by modifying the 29-nucleotide protospacer region of the crRNA to target the SARS-CoV-2 Spike (S) gene at positions 22280–22308 (LlCsm_S0), ensuring a non-complementary 3′ protospacer flanking sequence (3′ PFS) to permit activation.

Fluorescent DNA and RNA probes were designed: DNA-Alexa (5′ Alexa594, 3′ Iowa Black RQ) for DNase activity and RNA-FAM (5′ FAM, 3′ Iowa Black FQ) for RNase activity. In reconstituted assays, addition of cognate target RNA triggered LlCsm1-stimulated DNase cleavage (detectable at 570/630 nm) and cOA6-stimulated LlCsm6-mediated RNase cleavage (480/530 nm). Under Mg²⁺/ATP conditions, only RNA-FAM signal was observed; inclusion of Mn²⁺ enabled dual fluorescence. Mutagenesis of the HD domain (H13A/D14N) abolished DNase signal, while HEPN domain mutation (R355A) in LlCsm6 eliminated RNase activity, confirming signal origins.

One-pot amplification-free MORIARTY reactions contained LlCsm, LlCsm6, ATP, metal ions, and probes in Tris acetate buffer at 37°C. Simultaneous fluorescence rises in both channels confirmed multipronged detection. Augmentability was demonstrated by replacing DNA-Alexa with DNA-FAM, allowing combined 480/530 nm readout with RNA-FAM for enhanced total signal. Optimization revealed that 1.5 mM ATP and 250 nM LlCsm6 maximized cOA6 amplification, while Mg²⁺ alone sufficed for high-sensitivity RNase-only detection.

T7-MORIARTY extended the platform to DNA templates. Reactions included T7 RNA polymerase, rNTPs, and a DNA template with a T7 promoter. Transcription-generated RNA activated LlCsm, yielding RNA-FAM signal; addition of 0.5 mM Mn²⁺ enabled minor DNase contribution. Templates lacking the T7 promoter produced no signal, confirming transcription dependence. Combined DNA-FAM/RNA-FAM probes again augmented total fluorescence. Optimal T7-MORIARTY conditions included 17 mM MgCl₂, 0.5 mM MnCl₂, 0.5 mM rNTPs, and 60 μg/mL T7 polymerase.

Example 2 demonstrated amplification-free SARS-CoV-2 detection. LlCsm_S0_D30A (with Csm3-D30A mutation for sustained activity) detected in vitro transcribed S-gene mRNA down to 5 fM. Multiplexing with LlCsm_S7 and LlCsm_S8 (targeting additional S-gene sites) improved sensitivity 10-fold. Applied to patient RNA, MORIARTY correctly identified high-titer positives (low Ct by qPCR) but missed low-titer cases, indicating a need for pre-amplification in some contexts.

Example 3 implemented RT-RPA-coupled MORIARTY. Viral RNA underwent RT-RPA using S-gene-specific primers (one containing a T7 promoter). The product stimulated T7-MORIARTY, detecting as low as 62 copies/μL of control virus RNA. Testing on 14 patient samples showed 93% concordance with qPCR (7/8 positives, 3/4 negatives correctly identified). Raw fluorescence data showed clear separation between positive and negative samples. Requirement for the T7 promoter was confirmed by negative results with non-T7 primers. End-point and slope-based analyses both supported diagnostic accuracy, establishing MORIARTY as a robust, rapid alternative to PCR.

### Material and Methods

The LlCsm expression plasmid (pACYC backbone) encoded all Csm subunits and a CRISPR array with BbsI cloning sites for crRNA protospacer insertion. Mutations (Csm1-H13A/D14N, Csm3-D30A, Csm6-R355A) were introduced via Q5 site-directed mutagenesis. crRNAs targeting SARS-CoV-2 S-gene regions (22280–22308, 24702–24730, 25061–25089) were cloned using BbsI. Plasmids were verified by sequencing.

LlCsm complexes were expressed in E. coli NiCo21(DE3) induced with 0.3 mM IPTG, purified via Ni-NTA chromatography (exploiting His6-tag on Csm2), and polished by size-exclusion chromatography in storage buffer (20 mM HEPES pH 7.5, 200 mM NaCl, 5 mM MgCl₂, 14 mM β-mercaptoethanol). LlCsm6 and T7 RNA polymerase were purified similarly from dedicated plasmids.

S-gene mRNA was transcribed in vitro from BamHI-linearized pGBW-m4046887 plasmid using T7 RNA polymerase, treated with Turbo DNase, and purified via Monarch RNA Cleanup kit. RNA was quantified and stored at −80°C.

Amplification-free MORIARTY reactions (25 μL) contained 0.1× TAPA buffer, 250 nM LlCsm, 1–250 nM LlCsm6, 0.5–2 μM probes, 0–10 mM MgCl₂/MnCl₂, 0–1.5 mM ATP, and target RNA or water. Fluorescence was read every minute at 480/530 and 570/630 nm. Multiplex reactions used 250 nM each of three LlCsm complexes.

T7-MORIARTY (25–100 μL) included 1× TAPA, 1× transcription buffer, 2 μM each probe, 250 nM LlCsm, 250 nM LlCsm6, 17 mM MgCl₂, 0.5 mM MnCl₂, 0.5 mM rNTPs, 10 mM TCEP, 60 μg/mL T7 polymerase, and DNA template or RT-RPA product. Fluorescence was read every 5 minutes at 480/530 nm.

RT-RPA used TwistAmp Basic kit with 0.5 μM primers, 100 U Protoscript RT, and 14 mM MgOAc, incubated 30 min at 42°C. Patient RNA was extracted from nasopharyngeal swabs using QIAamp kits.

Target sequences, crRNAs, DNA templates, and RPA primers are provided in Supplementary Tables 2–6. All experiments included appropriate negative controls (water, non-cognate RNA, mismatched DNA) and were performed in triplicate. Statistical analysis used ANOVA with Dunnett’s test.
