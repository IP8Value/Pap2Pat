# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates to resonant devices, and more particularly to high-frequency bulk acoustic wave (BAW) gyroscopes that utilize substrate-decoupling structures to minimize environmental dependencies and enhance performance in rotation-rate sensing applications. The invention further pertains to micro-machined vibratory gyroscopes designed for use in consumer electronics, automotive safety systems, industrial navigation, and other applications requiring robustness against shock, vibration, and temperature fluctuations. Specifically, the invention provides a novel mechanical architecture that decouples the resonant element from its substrate, thereby reducing anchor loss, damping coupling, and environmentally induced drift while maintaining high quality factors and mode-matching fidelity essential for precision angular rate detection.

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

Micro-machined vibratory gyroscopes have become indispensable components in modern inertial sensing systems, enabling functionalities ranging from motion-controlled gaming interfaces to advanced vehicle stability control and pedestrian dead reckoning in mobile devices. Among the most prevalent designs are low-frequency flexural tuning-fork gyroscopes (TFGs), which operate by detecting Coriolis-induced motion between two mechanically coupled proof masses. These devices have gained popularity due to their compatibility with standard microfabrication processes and relatively straightforward integration into consumer electronics. However, their operational frequencies—typically below 20 kHz—fall within the spectral range of common environmental disturbances such as mechanical shocks, random vibrations, and even acoustic pressure waves, rendering them susceptible to false signals and long-term output drift.

This vulnerability has motivated significant interest in alternative gyroscope architectures that offer greater immunity to environmental perturbations. Acceleration suppression mechanisms have been implemented in TFGs through redundant proof mass configurations that reject linear accelerations as common-mode signals. While effective in principle, such approaches substantially increase device footprint and often require post-fabrication electromechanical calibration to compensate for inevitable asymmetries introduced during manufacturing, thereby limiting their suitability for high-volume, cost-sensitive applications.

In response to these limitations, bulk acoustic wave (BAW) gyroscopes have emerged as promising alternatives. BAW gyros exploit degenerate vibrational modes of axis-symmetric resonators—such as disks or rings—operating at MHz-range frequencies. This high-frequency operation inherently filters out low-frequency environmental noise, including shock and vibration, while simultaneously enabling high quality factors (Q) under moderate vacuum conditions (1–10 Torr). Moreover, the symmetry of the resonator supports natural mode matching, which is critical for maximizing sensitivity to the Coriolis effect and minimizing zero-rate output (ZRO) errors.

Despite these advantages, conventional BAW gyroscopes remain susceptible to performance degradation caused by damping coupling between degenerate modes. Damping coupling arises when the two nominally identical modes experience unequal energy dissipation due to asymmetries in loss mechanisms, particularly anchor loss—the dissipation of vibrational energy through the physical connection between the resonator and its substrate. In anisotropic materials like (100) single-crystal silicon, the directional dependence of mechanical properties can cause each mode to couple differently to the substrate, leading to environment-dependent ZRO drift that cannot be easily corrected by electronic means. Unlike stiffness-coupling errors, which produce quadrature signals distinguishable via phase-sensitive demodulation, damping-coupling errors manifest in-phase with the true Coriolis signal, making them indistinguishable without complex and power-intensive modal reversal techniques.

Thus, there exists a critical need for an improved gyroscope design that preserves the high-frequency, high-Q benefits of BAW technology while effectively mitigating environmentally induced damping coupling and anchor loss. Such a device must be manufacturable using scalable micromachining processes, maintain robust performance across temperature and mechanical stress, and enable stable, low-drift operation suitable for demanding applications in navigation, robotics, and autonomous systems.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention addresses the aforementioned limitations by introducing a novel substrate-decoupled bulk acoustic wave (SD-BAW) gyroscope that minimizes environmental dependencies through a mechanically engineered decoupling mechanism. Central to this innovation is a stress isolation structure interposed between the resonant element and its anchor point, which significantly attenuates the transmission of strain energy from the resonator into the substrate, thereby reducing anchor loss and ensuring symmetric damping for degenerate vibrational modes.

The invention employs a decoupling mechanism comprising a mirrored arrangement of double-folded fish-hook springs that suspend the resonant annulus from a central anchor. These spring-like flexure members are configured to provide high compliance in the plane of resonance while maintaining structural integrity, effectively isolating the resonator from substrate-borne acoustic waves and thermal-mechanical perturbations. The geometry of the springs—including abrupt angular transitions and symmetric placement around the circumference—ensures that both degenerate modes experience identical mechanical boundary conditions, thereby minimizing damping coupling.

In one embodiment, the resonant apparatus comprises a circular annulus fabricated from a 40-μm-thick single-crystal silicon layer on a silicon-on-insulator (SOI) wafer, with a radius of approximately 420 μm. The annulus supports second-elliptical (n=3) in-plane degenerate modes used for rotation-rate detection. Surrounding the annulus are electrodes with ultra-narrow capacitive gaps (e.g., 270 nm) that enable electrostatic excitation, readout, and frequency tuning. The entire structure is suspended by the substrate-decoupling structure, which connects the inner perimeter of the annulus to a central anchor embedded in the substrate.

The gyroscope apparatus further includes a set of dedicated tuning and decoupling electrodes: VT1 and VT2 for independent frequency adjustment of each mode to achieve perfect mode matching, and VQA and VQB for electrostatic cancellation of stiffness-coupling terms. This enables real-time compensation of fabrication-induced anisoinertia and anisoelasticity, ensuring zero frequency split and minimal quadrature error.

Additionally, the invention encompasses a method of manufacturing the bulk acoustic wave resonator element using a modified high-aspect-ratio poly- and single-crystal silicon (HARPSS™) process. This method includes deep reactive ion etching (DRIE) to define lateral features, sacrificial oxide deposition to establish in-plane and out-of-plane capacitive gaps, polysilicon refill for electrode formation, and hydrofluoric acid (HF) release followed by wafer-level hermetic packaging at a controlled pressure (1–10 Torr). Through-silicon vias (TSVs) and metal routing facilitate electrical connectivity to external interface electronics.

By integrating mechanical decoupling with electrostatic compensation, the SD-BAW gyroscope achieves unprecedented environmental robustness, exhibiting bias instability as low as 3.5°/h, angle-random walk of 0.074°/√h, and vibration sensitivity below 0.012°/s/gRMS—performance metrics that surpass those of conventional TFGs by orders of magnitude.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

An annulus, as used herein, refers to a ring-shaped resonant structure characterized by radial symmetry and capable of supporting degenerate vibrational modes. In the context of rotation-rate gyroscopes, such an annulus serves as the core sensing element, wherein mechanical energy is stored in standing wave patterns that respond to applied angular velocity via the Coriolis effect. The motivation for employing an annular geometry stems from its inherent axisymmetry, which facilitates the existence of two orthogonal, degenerate modes with identical natural frequencies in the absence of imperfections—a prerequisite for high-sensitivity Coriolis detection.

The equations of motion governing a vibratory gyroscope are derived from Lagrangian mechanics and describe two coupled second-order oscillators. When subjected to an angular rate Ω(t) about the axis normal to the plane of vibration, the Coriolis force couples the drive and sense modes, inducing a measurable displacement in the sense mode proportional to Ω(t). Specifically, for a mode-matched system operating at resonance, the sense-mode displacement due to Coriolis acceleration is given by q₂coriolis(t) = (2λQ₂/ω₀₂)Ω(t)q̄₁sin(ω₀₂t), where λ is the angular gain determined by the Bryan factor, Q₂ is the quality factor of the sense mode, ω₀₂ is its resonance frequency, and q̄₁ is the amplitude of the drive-mode oscillation.

A rotation-rate gyroscope operates by maintaining one mode (the drive mode) in sustained self-oscillation while monitoring the orthogonal mode (the sense mode) for Coriolis-induced motion. The drive and sense modes are typically the second-elliptical (n=3) in-plane modes of the annulus, which exhibit four antinodes and four nodes arranged in an elliptical pattern. These modes are excited and sensed via capacitive transduction using electrodes aligned with the antinodes.

The natural frequencies of the degenerate modes are ideally equal but are often split due to fabrication tolerances or material anisotropy. This frequency split degrades sensitivity and increases susceptibility to noise. Energy loss in the system is quantified by the quality factor Q, defined as Q = 2πW/ΔW, where W is the peak energy stored and ΔW is the energy dissipated per cycle. Total energy loss arises from multiple sources: thermoelastic damping (TED), viscous (squeeze-film) damping, surface scattering, intrinsic material losses, and anchor loss. Among these, anchor loss is particularly problematic because it is highly sensitive to substrate interactions and can differ between modes.

Solving for the sense-mode displacement reveals that damping coupling generates an error signal q₂damping(t) = −(Q₂/m₂₂ω₀₂)b₂₁q̄₁sin(ω₀₂t), which is in-phase with the Coriolis signal and thus indistinguishable without additional measures. This underscores the necessity of mechanical symmetry in loss mechanisms.

### Mode-to-Mode Coupling in Vibratory Gyroscopes

Mode-to-mode coupling in vibratory gyroscopes refers to unintended energy exchange between the drive and sense modes due to structural imperfections. In rotation-rate gyroscopes, this manifests as a zero-rate output (ZRO)—a non-zero sense-mode signal in the absence of rotation. ZRO arises from two primary coupling mechanisms: stiffness coupling and damping coupling. Stiffness coupling, represented by off-diagonal terms k₁₂ and k₂₁ in the stiffness matrix, produces a quadrature error signal q₂stiffness(t) = (Q₂/m₂₂ω₀₂²)k₂₁q̄₁cos(ω₀₂t), which is 90° out of phase with the Coriolis signal and can be rejected using I-Q demodulation. Electrostatic forces applied via dedicated decoupling electrodes can cancel this term by introducing compensating stiffness.

In contrast, damping coupling, captured by coefficients b₁₂ and b₂₁, generates an in-phase error that mimics the true signal. The damping coupling force b₂₁ arises from asymmetries in energy dissipation pathways. In a second-order system, the damping ratio ζ = b/(2√(km)) governs decay rate, and differences in ζ between modes lead to b₂₁ ≠ 0. Sources of damping coupling include asymmetric anchor loss, uneven surface roughness, and anisotropic TED. The total energy lost per cycle is the sum of contributions from all loss mechanisms, with anchor loss being dominant in poorly isolated resonators.

Bulk-acoustic wave (BAW) disk gyroscopes leverage MHz-frequency operation to reject environmental noise. Their capacitive transduction uses ultra-narrow gaps (e.g., 270 nm) to achieve high electromechanical coupling. The second elliptical modes are ideal for rate detection due to their high Coriolis sensitivity and manufacturability in (100) silicon, where they maintain degeneracy despite crystal orientation.

Anchor loss in BAW resonators is expressed as Qanchor⁻¹ ∝ ∫σₛεₛdV over the anchor volume, where σₛ and εₛ are stress and strain at the interface. Substrate-decoupled BAW gyroscopes mitigate this by inserting a compliant decoupling structure—comprising mirrored double-folded fish-hook springs—between the annulus and the anchor. This structure attenuates strain transmission, as verified by finite element analysis showing reduced strain energy density at the anchor.

The decoupling mechanism enables the device to function as both a yaw-gyroscope (z-axis) and a pitch/roll gyroscope (x/y-axis) by selecting appropriate mode orientations. During in-plane resonance, the springs undergo lateral deflection, while out-of-plane modes induce minimal motion, preserving isolation. Features enabled include tailored Q-factor, precise frequency control, and immunity to substrate spurious modes.

By ensuring symmetric damping and minimizing anchor loss, the SD-BAW gyroscope achieves stable, high-performance operation across temperature and mechanical stress, fulfilling the stringent requirements of next-generation inertial navigation systems.
