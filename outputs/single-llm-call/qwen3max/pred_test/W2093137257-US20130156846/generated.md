# DESCRIPTION

## INTRODUCTION

Protein translation is a fundamental biological process by which the genetic information encoded in messenger RNA (mRNA) is converted into functional polypeptide chains composed of amino acids. This highly regulated and efficient mechanism ensures that the twenty canonical proteinogenic amino acids are accurately assembled in accordance with the nucleotide sequence of the mRNA template. The fidelity of this process is maintained through multiple layers of molecular proofreading, primarily mediated by aminoacyl-tRNA synthetases, which selectively attach the correct amino acid to its corresponding transfer RNA (tRNA). Despite these safeguards, translational errors do occur, albeit at low frequencies estimated between one in 10³ to one in 10⁴ amino acid incorporations. Such errors can result in mischarged tRNAs, leading to the incorporation of incorrect amino acids into nascent polypeptides, which may subsequently cause protein misfolding, loss of function, or aggregation—events that are often cytotoxic and implicated in various pathological conditions, including neurodegenerative diseases.

The potential for error becomes more pronounced when nonprotein amino acids—structurally similar analogs of canonical amino acids—are present within the cellular milieu. These exogenous or endogenously derived compounds can be mistakenly recognized by aminoacyl-tRNA synthetases and incorporated into proteins during translation. Among such nonprotein amino acids, β-N-methylamino-L-alanine (BMAA) has garnered significant attention due to its environmental prevalence and suspected role in neurodegeneration. BMAA is a neurotoxic compound produced by cyanobacteria and has been detected in various ecosystems, including aquatic food chains and terrestrial plants such as cycads. Early studies suggested that BMAA could be incorporated into proteins, as evidenced by autoradiographic distribution patterns following administration of radiolabeled BMAA in animal models, which mirrored those of standard proteinogenic amino acids. Subsequent research confirmed that BMAA becomes covalently bound within polypeptide chains, requiring harsh chemical or enzymatic hydrolysis for release, thereby indicating peptide bond formation rather than mere adsorption or noncovalent association.

The structural similarity between BMAA and L-serine—a canonical amino acid involved in numerous metabolic and signaling pathways—has led to the hypothesis that BMAA may be misincorporated in place of serine during protein synthesis. This misincorporation can disrupt normal protein structure and function, particularly in proteins where serine residues are critical for post-translational modifications such as phosphorylation, which regulate activity, localization, and stability. Given that many neurodegenerative disorders—including Alzheimer’s disease, amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), Parkinson’s disease, and frontotemporal dementia—are characterized by the accumulation of misfolded and aggregated proteins, the potential for BMAA to induce such pathologies through translational infidelity represents a compelling mechanistic link between environmental exposure and neurological disease.

Protein synthesis fidelity, therefore, is not only a matter of intrinsic enzymatic accuracy but also depends on the relative concentrations of canonical versus noncanonical amino acids within the cell. When nonprotein amino acids like BMAA are present at sufficient levels, they can outcompete their canonical counterparts for binding to aminoacyl-tRNA synthetases, especially if structural mimicry is high. This competitive inhibition underscores the vulnerability of the translational machinery to environmental toxins and highlights the importance of maintaining adequate intracellular pools of canonical amino acids to safeguard proteome integrity. In this context, the discovery that L-serine can effectively block the misincorporation of BMAA into human proteins offers a promising therapeutic avenue for mitigating BMAA-induced neurotoxicity and associated proteinopathies.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides methods and compositions for preventing the misincorporation of β-N-methylamino-L-alanine (BMAA) into proteins by administering L-serine or its pharmacologically acceptable derivatives, precursors, conjugates, or formulations. It has been discovered that BMAA, a nonprotein amino acid produced by cyanobacteria and linked to neurodegenerative diseases such as amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), Alzheimer’s disease, and Parkinsonism-dementia complex, is misincorporated into human proteins in place of L-serine during translation. This misincorporation leads to protein misfolding, aggregation, cellular stress, and ultimately neuronal apoptosis. Critically, the co-administration of L-serine competitively inhibits BMAA incorporation in a concentration-dependent manner, thereby preserving protein homeostasis and preventing downstream neurotoxic effects.

Alzheimer’s disease, a progressive neurodegenerative disorder, is characterized by the accumulation of amyloid-beta (Aβ) plaques and neurofibrillary tangles composed of hyperphosphorylated tau protein. The amyloid precursor protein (APP) undergoes sequential cleavage by β- and γ-secretases to generate Aβ peptides, which aggregate and trigger neuroinflammation, synaptic dysfunction, and neuronal death. While the primary etiology of Alzheimer’s remains multifactorial, environmental exposures—including chronic intake of BMAA—may exacerbate protein misfolding cascades. By blocking BMAA incorporation, L-serine may reduce the burden of aberrant proteins that seed or accelerate Aβ and tau pathology.

L-serine is thus positioned as a novel drug candidate for the prevention and treatment of neurological diseases associated with BMAA exposure or protein misfolding. The invention encompasses methods for preventing BMAA incorporation into nascent polypeptides by elevating intracellular L-serine concentrations, either through direct supplementation or via administration of L-serine precursors such as 3-phosphoglycerate or derivatives that enhance bioavailability. Additionally, the invention includes methods for preventing protein misfolding and aggregation by maintaining translational fidelity, thereby reducing the formation of toxic protein species implicated in neurodegeneration.

The scope of treatable neurological diseases includes, but is not limited to, ALS, Alzheimer’s disease, Parkinson’s disease, Huntington’s disease, frontotemporal dementia, Pick’s disease, and other tauopathies or synucleinopathies. Symptoms ameliorated by the invention include cognitive decline, motor dysfunction, speech and language impairments, mood disturbances, sleep abnormalities, and behavioral changes. Administration routes for L-serine include oral, intravenous, intrathecal, subcutaneous, and intranasal delivery, with dosing regimens tailored to individual patient needs, typically ranging from 0.5 to 30 grams per day in divided doses.

The invention further extends to L-serine derivatives and conjugates designed to improve stability, blood-brain barrier penetration, or metabolic resistance. These include alkylated L-serine, L-serine esters, pegylated L-serine, lipidated L-serine, and L-serine polymers. Salts of L-serine, such as sodium or calcium salts, are also encompassed. Moreover, the invention provides screening methods to identify agents that modulate serine racemase activity or inhibit BMAA incorporation, using cell-free or cell-based assays that measure radiolabeled BMAA integration into proteins or autofluorescence as a proxy for aggregation. Such agents may serve as adjunctive or alternative therapeutics.

Pharmaceutical formulations of L-serine may include carriers, excipients, isotonic agents, absorption-delaying polymers, and controlled-release matrices composed of biodegradable polymeric substances. Kits comprising unit doses of L-serine, instructions for use, and packaging inserts are provided for clinical or prophylactic application. The invention thus offers a comprehensive platform for diagnosing, preventing, stabilizing, and treating neurological disorders rooted in translational infidelity induced by environmental neurotoxins like BMAA.
