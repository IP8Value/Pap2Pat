# DESCRIPTION

The present invention relates to an improved process for the production of high-quality middle distillates, such as kerosene and gas oil, from hydrocarbon feedstocks derived from renewable or synthetic sources, particularly those obtained via the Fischer-Tropsch synthesis. The invention further encompasses a novel bifunctional hydrocracking and hydroisomerisation catalyst specifically engineered to maximize yield and selectivity toward middle distillates while minimizing undesirable side reactions such as overcracking or excessive hydrogenolysis. This catalyst comprises a tailored combination of a hydro-dehydrogenating metal function and a unique acidic support based on a crystallised IZM-2-type solid embedded within an amorphous or poorly crystallised porous inorganic binder. The process according to the invention integrates strategic fractionation, hydrotreatment, selective hydroisomerisation, and controlled hydrocracking steps, optionally coupled with intelligent recycling schemes for unconverted heavy fractions or intermediate cuts, thereby achieving unprecedented efficiency and product quality. The invention addresses long-standing challenges in upgrading Fischer-Tropsch waxes—namely, the broad carbon number distribution inherent to the synthesis and the presence of oxygenated impurities—by employing a robust catalytic system and optimized reaction conditions that maintain ideal bifunctional behavior even under industrially relevant operating parameters.

## PRIOR ART

The Fischer-Tropsch process is a well-established method for converting synthesis gas (a mixture of carbon monoxide and hydrogen) derived from coal, natural gas, or biomass into liquid hydrocarbons. This process yields a complex mixture of linear paraffins, olefins, and minor amounts of oxygenates, with a carbon number distribution governed by the Anderson-Flory-Schulz kinetics, which inherently limits the direct yield of desirable middle distillates (C10–C20) to approximately 40% by weight. To overcome this limitation, post-synthesis upgrading via catalytic hydrocracking or hydroisomerisation is essential. However, conventional hydrocracking processes developed for petroleum-derived feedstocks are suboptimal for Fischer-Tropsch products due to fundamental differences in composition; notably, Fischer-Tropsch waxes are virtually free of sulfur and nitrogen contaminants but may contain significant levels of oxygenated compounds such as alcohols, carboxylic acids, and esters.

Feedstocks for hydrocracking can originate from diverse sources, including renewable oils and fats, coal-derived liquids, and Fischer-Tropsch syncrude. While oils and fats require extensive pretreatment to remove heteroatoms, coal-derived liquids often contain polycyclic aromatics that promote coking. In contrast, Fischer-Tropsch waxes consist predominantly of long-chain n-paraffins, making them highly reactive but also susceptible to deep hydrogenolysis when inappropriate catalysts are used. Hydroisomerisation catalytic reactions, crucial for improving cold flow properties of both fuels and lubricants, rely on bifunctional catalysts that balance a hydrogenating/dehydrogenating function with an acidic function. Bifunctional catalysts typically incorporate metals from Group VIII (e.g., Pt, Pd, Ni, Co) and/or Group VIB (e.g., Mo, W), either in metallic or sulfided form, supported on acidic carriers such as zeolites or amorphous silica-alumina.

The acid function, usually of Brønsted type, facilitates carbocation formation and skeletal rearrangement, while the hydrogenating function ensures rapid (de)hydrogenation equilibria and suppresses coke formation. Achieving the proper balance between these two functions is critical: an overly strong acid function leads to excessive cracking, whereas a weak hydrogenating function results in poor stability and increased hydrogenolysis. Conventional catalysts for hydrocracking include silica-alumina-based systems, which offer moderate acidity and good middle distillate selectivity but suffer from low activity and susceptibility to deactivation. Zeolite-based catalytic systems, such as those employing USY, Beta, or ZSM-5, provide higher activity due to strong acidity but often produce excessive light gases (C3–C4) due to secondary cracking, especially with long-chain paraffins. Moreover, many zeolites lack the shape selectivity required to favor mono- or dibranched isomers over tribranched species prone to β-scission. These drawbacks underscore the need for a new class of catalysts specifically designed for the unique characteristics of Fischer-Tropsch feedstocks.

## Subject-Matter of the Invention

The subject-matter of the present invention is a process for producing middle distillates from a hydrocarbon feedstock comprising paraffinic compounds having at least 20 carbon atoms, wherein said process comprises subjecting at least a portion of said feedstock to hydrocracking and hydroisomerisation in the presence of a novel bifunctional catalyst. This catalyst is characterised by a specific composition that synergistically combines a controlled hydro-dehydrogenating metal function with an acidic support comprising a crystallised IZM-2-type solid dispersed within an amorphous or poorly crystallised porous inorganic binder. The IZM-2 solid provides tailored acidity and pore architecture conducive to selective cracking and isomerisation, while the binder ensures mechanical integrity and optimal diffusion properties. The process is designed to operate under conditions that promote "ideal" bifunctional catalysis, wherein the rate-determining step occurs on the acid sites, thereby maximizing primary cracking and isomerisation selectivity while minimizing secondary reactions.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

The hydrocracking/hydroisomerisation catalyst employed in the present invention is a bifunctional material comprising a hydro-dehydrogenating function and an acidic function. The operating conditions for the hydrocracking/hydroisomerisation step are carefully selected to ensure high conversion of heavy paraffins (C20+) into middle distillates (C10–C20) with minimal formation of light gases (C1–C4). Typical conditions include a temperature range of 280°C to 380°C, a total pressure of 2 MPa to 8 MPa, a hydrogen-to-hydrocarbon molar ratio of 200 to 1000 Nl/l, and a liquid hourly space velocity (LHSV) of 0.1 to 2 h⁻¹. The chemical composition of the catalyst is defined by precise ranges of its constituent elements to achieve optimal performance.

### The Hydrocracking and Hydroisomerisation Catalyst

The hydrocracking and hydroisomerisation catalyst according to the invention is a composite material designed to facilitate both bond scission and skeletal rearrangement of long-chain paraffins. Its composition includes a hydro-dehydrogenating metal component supported on a mixed substrate comprising an oxide-type amorphous or poorly crystallised porous inorganic binder and a crystallised IZM-2-type solid. The hydro-dehydrogenating metal content is derived from Group VIII and/or Group VIB metals. Group VIII metals include noble metals such as platinum and palladium, and non-noble metals such as nickel and cobalt. Group VIB metals include molybdenum and tungsten. Preferred metal combinations involve platinum alone or in combination with palladium, or nickel-molybdenum in reduced (non-sulfided) form to avoid the need for continuous sulfiding agents. The total metal content typically ranges from 0.1 to 5% by weight, with noble metals preferably between 0.1 and 1% and base metals between 1 and 5%.

The substrate composition consists of an amorphous or poorly crystallised porous inorganic binder, such as alumina, silica, or silica-alumina, which provides mechanical strength and macroporosity, and a crystallised IZM-2-type solid, which imparts controlled microporosity and acidity. The IZM-2 solid content in the final catalyst ranges from 5 to 90% by weight, preferably 20 to 70%. The hydro-dehydrogenating function is primarily provided by Group VIII noble metals (Pt, Pd), Group VIII non-noble metals (Ni, Co), or Group VIB metals (Mo, W), with preferred combinations being Pt alone, Pt-Pd, or Ni-Mo. The metal content is optimized to satisfy Weisz’s intimacy criterion, ensuring rapid (de)hydrogenation equilibria relative to acid-catalyzed steps.

The crystallised IZM-2 solid exhibits a distinct X-ray diffraction pattern characterized by specific dhkl values and relative intensities, indicative of its unique framework topology. Its chemical composition follows the general formula:  
XO₂·bY₂O₃·cM₂/ₙO·dR·eH₂O,  
where X is silicon, germanium, or tin; Y is aluminum, gallium, iron, or boron; M is an alkali or alkaline earth metal or ammonium; R is an organic structure-directing agent; and a, b, c, d, e are molar coefficients with typical ranges: a = 1, b = 0.001–0.5, c = 0–0.5, d = 0.01–1, e = 0–10. Preferred elements are X = Si, Y = Al, and M = Na or NH₄. The IZM-2 solid in crude synthesis form contains the organic template R, which is subsequently removed via calcination. Preparation involves mixing sources of X (e.g., tetraethyl orthosilicate), Y (e.g., sodium aluminate), and M (e.g., sodium hydroxide) with a hydroxide source (e.g., NaOH) and the organic template (e.g., 1,6-bis(methylpiperidinium)hexane dibromide), followed by hydrothermal treatment at 100–200°C for 1–10 days, optionally with seeding. The solid is then filtered, washed, dried, and calcined at 500–600°C to remove the template, yielding the ammonium or acid form after ion exchange.

The substrate is prepared by combining the IZM-2 solid with an alumina source, such as fine or ultrafine boehmite, which is processed into aqueous dispersions. Additives like peptizing agents (e.g., nitric acid) and extrusion aids (e.g., cellulose) are incorporated before shaping via extrusion. The shaped substrate is dried at 80–150°C and calcined at 400–600°C. Post-synthesis treatments may include hydrothermal steaming or autoclaving to modify porosity. The hydro-dehydrogenating function is deposited via incipient wetness impregnation using metal salts (e.g., H₂PtCl₆, Ni(NO₃)₂, (NH₄)₆Mo₇O₂₄), followed by drying and calcination. Promoters such as boron, silicon, or phosphorus may be co-impregnated to fine-tune acidity or metal dispersion.

### The Embodiments According to the Invention

The process for producing middle distillates involves multiple integrated steps. In a first embodiment, the Fischer-Tropsch effluent is fractionated into light (C10–) and heavy (C20+) fractions. The heavy fraction undergoes hydrotreatment over a catalyst such as NiMo/Al₂O₃ at 200–300°C and 3–5 MPa to saturate olefins and decompose oxygenates. The hydrotreated effluent is then fractionated into light (C10–), intermediate (C10–C20), and heavy (C20+) cuts. The intermediate fraction is passed over a hydroisomerising catalyst (e.g., Pt/ZSM-22) at 250–350°C to improve cold flow, while the heavy fraction is hydrocracked/hydroisomerised over the IZM-2-based catalyst. The resulting effluents are distilled to recover kerosene and gas oil, with the residual C370+ fraction recycled.

A second embodiment omits intermediate fraction hydroisomerisation and instead focuses on direct hydrocracking of the entire heavy fraction after hydrotreatment and water removal. A third embodiment integrates all steps—fractionation, hydrotreatment, water removal, hydroisomerisation of intermediates, hydrocracking of heavies, and distillation—with optional recycling of kerosene or gas oil to further optimize branching distribution. A fourth embodiment allows flexibility in recycling strategies, including residual fraction recycle to increase overall conversion or kerosene/gas oil recycle to enhance isomerisation depth. These embodiments are non-limiting and highlight the adaptability of the invention to various operational goals.

## The Products Obtained

The middle distillates obtained exhibit exceptional quality: cetane numbers exceeding 70, near-zero sulfur and aromatics content, and excellent cold flow properties due to high degrees of isomerisation (typically >30% branched isomers in the C18 cut). Gas oil and kerosene fractions meet stringent environmental specifications while maintaining high energy density.

### Example 1

Catalyst C1 was prepared by impregnating a commercial silica-alumina support with platinum, serving as a comparative reference.

### Example 2

1,6-Bis(methylpiperidinium)hexane dibromide was synthesized and used as a template to prepare IZM-2 solid via hydrothermal crystallisation. The solid was combined with boehmite, extruded, and calcined to form a substrate. After ammonium exchange and calcination, platinum was deposited via incipient wetness. The final catalyst contained 0.5 wt% Pt with a dispersion of 60%, and a Pt distribution coefficient of 0.85, indicating uniform metal placement.

### Example 3

A Fischer-Tropsch wax was separated into C10– and C10+ fractions. The C10+ fraction was hydrotreated over catalyst C1, then the C370+ cut was hydrocracked over catalyst C4 (IZM-2-based). Distillation of the effluent yielded 78 wt% middle distillates (C10–C20), with only 5 wt% naphtha (C5–C9) and 2 wt% LPG (C3–C4), demonstrating superior selectivity over conventional catalysts.
