# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

Videoconferencing technology has become an increasingly prevalent means of facilitating remote communication, enabling individuals and groups to interact across distances through real-time audio and video transmission. Despite significant advances in bandwidth, resolution, and latency reduction, current videoconferencing systems remain limited in their ability to replicate the nuanced social dynamics inherent in face-to-face interactions. In particular, such systems often fail to preserve or convey critical nonverbal cues—such as gaze direction, body orientation, and spatial awareness—that are essential for establishing mutual understanding, managing conversational turn-taking, and maintaining a shared sense of presence among participants. These limitations are especially pronounced in asymmetric meeting configurations, such as hub-and-satellite arrangements, where a single remote participant (the satellite) interacts with a group of collocated individuals (the hub). In such scenarios, the satellite is typically represented by a static proxy device comprising a display screen, camera, microphone, and speaker, which lacks the physical expressiveness necessary to project attentional focus or engage in naturalistic social signaling.

One of the most persistent challenges in videoconferencing environments is the difficulty of determining directionality—specifically, identifying whom a remote participant is addressing or attending to at any given moment. In co-located settings, individuals rely on subtle visual and spatial cues, including head orientation, eye gaze, and torso alignment, to infer others’ loci of attention. These cues operate both consciously and subconsciously, supporting fluid coordination and reducing ambiguity during multi-party conversations. However, in mediated settings, these cues are either absent, distorted, or misaligned due to technical constraints such as fixed camera positions, narrow fields of view, and the decoupling of visual input and output devices. As a result, hub participants often experience confusion regarding the satellite’s intended addressee, leading to conversational breakdowns, missed contributions, and a diminished sense of inclusion—a phenomenon sometimes referred to as the “skip-over effect.” Similarly, the satellite may struggle to accurately signal attentional intent, further exacerbating asymmetries in social presence and engagement.

These challenges are not unique to videoconferencing but extend to other mediated communication environments where spatial and directional awareness is compromised. For example, telepresence robots, virtual reality avatars, and augmented reality interfaces all grapple with similar issues in conveying attentional states and fostering reciprocal awareness between remote and local participants. In each case, the absence of reliable mechanisms for projecting and interpreting directionality undermines the natural flow of interaction and reduces the effectiveness of collaborative tasks. Consequently, there exists a long-felt but unmet need for communication systems that can bridge this gap by providing intuitive, responsive, and socially meaningful indicators of attentional focus, thereby restoring a more equitable and engaging experience for all participants regardless of their physical location.

## SUMMARY

The present invention introduces a novel communication system designed to enhance directional awareness and social presence in mediated group interactions, particularly in hub-and-satellite meeting configurations. The system receives both explicit and implicit control inputs from a remote participant and processes these inputs to generate outputs that indicate the participant’s locus of attention within the local environment. Based on a predefined selection criterion, the system selects an appropriate output mechanism and adjusts the position or state of an indicator accordingly, thereby providing real-time, physically embodied feedback to collocated participants about whom the remote individual is addressing or attending to.

Explicit input mechanisms include user-initiated actions such as cursor positioning, touchscreen gestures, voice commands, or dedicated hardware controls, wherein the remote participant deliberately specifies a target location or individual within the shared space. Implicit input mechanisms, by contrast, derive attentional intent from passive behavioral signals, such as head pose, eye gaze direction, torso orientation, or patterns of speech, without requiring conscious intervention from the user. These inputs are captured via integrated sensors—including cameras, microphones, inertial measurement units, or depth-sensing devices—and processed using machine learning models or geometric inference algorithms to estimate the user’s current focus of attention.

The system employs a modular architecture comprising an attention determination module, an explicit control processing module, an implicit control processing module, and a mode selection module. The attention determination module continuously analyzes sensor data to infer the remote participant’s attentional state. The explicit and implicit control processing modules translate user inputs into actionable commands, while the mode selection module evaluates contextual factors—such as task type, conversation dynamics, user preference, or system reliability—to determine which control mode should govern the output at any given time. The selected output is then rendered through one or more indicator mechanisms, which may include mechanical vanes, motorized pointers, solid-state displays, rotating screens, holographic projections, or graphical overlays on handheld or room-scale displays. By dynamically aligning the indicator’s orientation or state with the inferred direction of attention, the system enables collocated participants to intuitively understand whom the remote user is engaging with, thereby improving conversational fluidity, reducing ambiguity, and enhancing overall sense of presence.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The following disclosure is organized to provide a comprehensive and enabling description of the invention, including its structural components, functional modules, operational logic, and illustrative embodiments. The invention is best understood in the context of a hub-and-satellite meeting environment, wherein a single remote participant (the satellite) communicates with a group of collocated individuals (the hub) through a proxy device situated within the shared physical space. However, the principles described herein are equally applicable to other multi-party communication scenarios involving distributed participants.

An illustrative communication system 100 includes a proxy device positioned at the hub site, equipped with a display screen showing live video of the satellite, a wide-field-of-view camera capturing the entire meeting room, speakers, microphones, and an actuated indicator mechanism. The satellite interacts with the system via a client application running on a computing device, which presents a panoramic view of the hub environment derived from the wide-angle camera feed. Terminology used herein includes “explicit control input” to denote intentional user actions aimed at directing attention, and “implicit control input” to refer to automatically inferred behavioral cues. “Output mechanism” encompasses any physical or visual means of indicating directionality, while “selection criterion” refers to rules or heuristics governing the choice between explicit and implicit modes.

The system’s core logic components include an attention determination module, which processes raw sensor data to estimate the satellite’s focus; explicit and implicit control processing modules, which map user inputs to spatial coordinates; and a mode selection module, which arbitrates between control strategies based on confidence scores, user settings, or environmental context. Optional features may include calibration routines, privacy filters, haptic feedback, or integration with calendar or agenda systems to anticipate likely addressees.

In operation, the satellite views the hub through a full-width panoramic window on their display. When they move their mouse cursor over a specific person in this view, the explicit control input is transmitted to the hub-side proxy, causing a mechanical pointer—such as a motorized vane or rotating arm—to swivel toward that individual. Alternatively, if implicit mode is active, a head-tracking algorithm analyzes the satellite’s webcam feed to detect horizontal head rotation, and the pointer automatically aligns with the inferred direction of gaze. The mode selection module may default to explicit control during structured turn-taking tasks but switch to implicit during brainstorming sessions to reduce cognitive load.

Output mechanisms vary in form and fidelity. Mechanical vane mechanisms use servomotors to rotate a physical pointer, offering strong spatial affordance but limited angular resolution. Solid-state vane mechanisms employ arrays of LEDs or e-ink segments to simulate directional cues without moving parts. Display-related mechanisms may overlay arrows, highlights, or translucent cones on shared screens or augmented reality glasses. In implementation 200, the system extends to multiple satellite participants, each with their own proxy and indicator, coordinated through a central conferencing service that manages coupling between devices and synchronizes attentional states across sites.

Data stores maintain user profiles, calibration parameters, and historical interaction logs to personalize behavior over time. Functions are mapped across hub conferencing devices (e.g., smart tables, ceiling-mounted projectors) and satellite conferencing devices (e.g., laptops, tablets), ensuring seamless interoperability. In a typical meeting room setup, a motor-driven mechanical pointer mounted atop the proxy display rotates ±90 degrees to indicate attentional focus. This design provides visceral, glanceable feedback that mitigates the “newscaster effect” and reduces skip-over incidents.

Alternative mechanical pointers include non-movable indicators with segmented lighting, or handheld computing devices displaying dynamic compass-like arrows that adjust based on the satellite’s head pose. Solid-state output mechanisms may utilize curved surface displays—cylindrical or spherical—to present omnidirectional attention cues, or holographic representations that project a 3D arrow into the room. Visual information output mechanisms can render globe-based, overhead, or panoramic graphical representations showing the satellite’s current focus relative to all hub participants.

The system also incorporates a position adjustment module that translates the satellite’s head motion into normalized room coordinates, accounting for display size, camera field of view, and seating geometry. Predefined positions corresponding to known participant locations may be used to snap the indicator to stable orientations, reducing jitter. Automatic detection of hub participants via facial recognition or RFID tags further enhances accuracy.

Illustrative processes include receiving control input (explicit or implicit), determining the active control mode via the mode selection module, and driving the indicator to the computed orientation. Electrical data processing functionality is implemented using standard computing hardware: processing devices (CPUs/GPUs), memory (RAM/ROM), media devices (cameras/mics), input/output modules (USB/Wi-Fi), presentation modules (displays/speakers), and communication buses (PCIe/USB-C). These components collectively enable real-time, low-latency operation essential for natural interaction.

In conclusion, the disclosed system represents a significant advancement in mediated communication by reintroducing physically grounded attentional cues into videoconferencing environments. Through a combination of explicit and implicit control, adaptive mode selection, and diverse output mechanisms, it restores a critical dimension of social awareness that has long been missing from remote collaboration tools.
