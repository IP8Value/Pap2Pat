# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD OF THE DISCLOSURE

The present disclosure relates to the field of forensic science, and more specifically to a method and system for dating blood pools found at crime scenes or other locations where the time of deposition of biological fluid is unknown. The term “dating blood pools” as used herein refers to the determination of the elapsed time between the initial formation of a blood pool—i.e., the moment when liquid blood was first deposited onto a surface—and a subsequent observation time, such as the time of forensic investigation. This technique enables investigators to estimate the time of a bloodshed event in the absence of a body or other traditional time-of-death indicators, thereby providing a critical temporal reference point for reconstructing the sequence of events at a scene. The disclosed method leverages physical and morphological changes that occur during the drying process of blood, particularly the evolution of the wet area and the propagation of the drying front, in conjunction with environmental parameters and intrinsic blood properties, to calculate an elapsed time with a high degree of accuracy. The invention is particularly applicable to forensic contexts where rapid, non-invasive, and reliable estimation of blood pool age is required, and it represents a novel integration of fluid dynamics, image analysis, and empirical modeling tailored specifically to the complex behavior of human blood under ambient conditions.

## TECHNICAL BACKGROUND

Current methods for estimating the time of death or the timing of events at a crime scene are heavily reliant on the presence of a human body and include techniques such as measurement of body temperature (algor mortis), assessment of muscle rigidity (rigor mortis), livor mortis, and forensic entomology. However, in cases where no body is present—such as in hit-and-run incidents, dismemberment scenarios, or staged crime scenes—these methods are inapplicable, leaving a significant gap in investigative capabilities. While bloodstain pattern analysis (BPA) is routinely employed to infer the direction, force, and origin of blood spatter, it has not historically provided a means to determine the absolute time since a blood pool was formed. Prior attempts to date blood have focused on biochemical degradation markers, such as RNA decay or protein denaturation, but these approaches suffer from high variability due to environmental fluctuations, require laboratory infrastructure, and often lack the precision needed for forensic timelines.

Spectroscopy-based techniques, including Raman spectroscopy and infrared spectroscopy, have been explored for blood age estimation by monitoring chemical changes in hemoglobin and other biomolecules over time. While these methods can distinguish between fresh and aged blood, they typically offer only broad time categories (e.g., hours vs. days) and are sensitive to substrate interference, lighting conditions, and sample contamination. Moreover, they often require direct contact with the stain or specialized equipment not readily available at crime scenes.

Hyperspectral imaging has emerged as a non-contact alternative that captures spectral signatures across a wide range of wavelengths, enabling the detection of subtle chemical changes in drying blood. Although promising, this technology remains expensive, computationally intensive, and dependent on extensive calibration datasets. Its practical deployment in routine forensic investigations is limited by the need for controlled lighting and the complexity of data interpretation.

The article by Laan et al. examined the evaporation dynamics of single blood droplets, identifying distinct drying stages and correlating them with hematocrit levels and environmental humidity. However, their model is restricted to microliter-scale volumes and does not account for the complex fluid mechanics of larger blood pools, which exhibit non-uniform drying fronts and gel-like behavior due to coagulation. Similarly, Choi et al. investigated the use of colorimetric changes in bloodstains to estimate age, but their approach is highly subjective and influenced by surface reflectance and ambient light. Thanakiatkrai et al. proposed a DNA degradation-based method, which, while scientifically valid, requires destructive sampling and laboratory processing, rendering it unsuitable for rapid on-site estimation.

Collectively, these prior approaches fail to address the unique challenges posed by blood pools—volumes significantly larger than droplets—that undergo multi-stage drying involving coagulation, gelation, and desiccation. The absence of a robust, physics-based, and image-driven method for dating blood pools has remained a critical unmet need in forensic science, which the present disclosure now fulfills.

## SUMMARY OF THE DISCLOSURE

As used herein, the term “comprise” is to be interpreted as specifying the presence of the stated features, integers, steps, or components, but not excluding the presence or addition of one or more other features, integers, steps, components, or groups thereof. The term “about” is understood to mean within ±10% of the stated value, unless otherwise specified by context.

The objective of the present disclosure is to provide a method and system for accurately estimating the time elapsed since the formation of a blood pool, using non-invasive imaging and environmental measurements. The method is based on the discovery that the drying dynamics of blood pools follow predictable physical laws once key parameters—including wet area, perimeter, environmental conditions, and blood’s diffusion coefficient—are accounted for.

A blood pool, as defined herein, refers to a contiguous deposit of liquid human blood on a surface, having an initial mass greater than 0.3 grams, which undergoes a multi-stage drying process characterized by coagulation, gelation, and progressive desiccation from the edges inward. The method is limited to blood pools that have progressed beyond the initial coagulation stage but are not yet fully desiccated, as the presence of a discernible drying front—the boundary between wet and dry regions—is essential for parameter extraction.

The method finds application in forensic investigations, disaster victim identification, and historical crime scene re-evaluation, where establishing a timeline of events is crucial. The drying process of blood involves the evaporation of water from the plasma phase, followed by diffusion through a porous fibrin network, and culminates in shrinkage and cracking. This process is modeled using a drying model that correlates the time elapsed with the blood’s effective diffusion coefficient, which is influenced by environmental and geometric factors.

A database of environmental and initial conditions is maintained, containing pre-measured values of diffusion coefficients, shape factors, and evaporation rates for various combinations of temperature, relative humidity, substrate type, and initial blood pool geometry. Environmental conditions at the scene—specifically ambient temperature and relative humidity—are determined using a thermometer and hygrometer integrated into the system. The initial area of the blood pool is estimated from the total area observed in a digital image, combined with an average height derived from empirical data for the given substrate.

The determined environmental conditions and initial area are compared against the database to select the most appropriate set of reference parameters. Blood pool parameters—including wet perimeter, wet area, and shape factor—are then measured from the image. The wet perimeter is the length of the boundary between the wet and dry regions, while the wet area is the surface area still containing liquid blood. These are extracted using image processing software that distinguishes red (wet) from dark brown/black (dry) regions based on color thresholds.

Pictures of the blood pool are taken using a camera, preferably a smartphone with known focal length and resolution, and optionally with a scale reference for accurate metric conversion. Image processing involves segmentation, edge detection, and area/perimeter calculation. The drying stages of the blood pool—coagulation, gelation, rim desiccation, center desiccation, and final desiccation—are identified to ensure the pool is in a suitable stage for analysis.

Mass variation is correlated with wet area through a normalized function: m/mi = 1 − α[1 − (A/Ai)]^β, where α and β are empirically derived correlation coefficients. The statistical value of the blood pool’s height is obtained from a distribution of measured heights for similar substrates, and an average value (e.g., 1.44 mm for tile) is used in calculations. The correlation coefficients are measured from controlled experiments and fitted to a curve that best describes the relationship between mass loss and wet area reduction.

The blood pool dating method begins by determining the drying front from the image, then calculating the time elapsed between the observation time and the initial deposition time. A system for implementing this method includes a camera, thermometer, hygrometer, image processing software, calculation software, and a database of pre-measured parameters. The calculation software uses the drying model equation, which incorporates the blood’s diffusion coefficient equation, the coefficient of transfer equation, the evaporation rate equation, the Knudsen layer equation, and the shape factor equation.

The Knudsen layer is defined as the thin vapor layer adjacent to the liquid surface, with thickness Lk = kT/(πd²Pa). The shape factor L* is defined as A/(hP), capturing the geometric influence on evaporation. The method is repeated multiple times—by taking successive images—to obtain an average elapsed time, improving accuracy. The system is designed for field use, with all components integrated into a portable device or smartphone application, enabling real-time estimation at crime scenes.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF EMBODIMENTS

The present disclosure encompasses several embodiments, all aimed at providing a practical, accurate, and field-deployable method for dating blood pools. In a non-limiting example, a system for dating a blood pool comprises a smartphone equipped with a high-resolution camera, image processing software, calculation software, a database of measured blood pool parameters, and external sensors including a thermometer and hygrometer. The image processing software is configured to receive a digital photograph of the blood pool, segment the image to identify the wet region, and compute the wet area and wet perimeter using pixel-to-metric conversion based on a reference scale or known camera parameters. The calculation software implements the drying model, receiving inputs from the image processing software and the environmental sensors, and outputs an estimated elapsed time.

The database contains entries for various combinations of substrate (e.g., tile, linoleum, wood), temperature (e.g., 20–30°C), relative humidity (e.g., 20–60%), and initial blood pool characteristics. Each entry includes pre-determined values for the diffusion coefficient, shape factor, and correlation coefficients α and β. During operation, the user uploads a picture of the blood pool and inputs or automatically transmits environmental data. The system then retrieves the closest matching database entry.

The method proceeds by determining the drying front, which is visually identifiable as the transition between red (wet) and dark (dry) regions. The wet area (Ax) and total area (Ai) are measured, and the initial mass (mi) is estimated as mi = hAiρ, where h is the average height for the substrate and ρ is blood density. The current mass (mx) is derived from the normalized mass equation using Ax/Ai. The evaporation rate J* is calculated using the diffusion coefficient Dblood ≈ 1×10⁻⁹ m²/s, adjusted for environmental conditions via the Knudsen layer and shape factor.

In a validation test, a blood pool of 8.75 g was deposited on a white tile at 22.5°C and 20% humidity. A photograph was taken after 8 hours 37 minutes. The system calculated an elapsed time of 8 hours 18 minutes, yielding a margin of error of less than 4%. Repeating the process with multiple images further reduced uncertainty. Numerical values used include α = 0.78, β = 0.16, h = 1.44 mm for tile, and Dblood = 1×10⁻⁹ m²/s under standard conditions.

The system supports automated repetition: images taken at intervals are processed sequentially, and the resulting time estimates are averaged. The accuracy of the method is directly dependent on the richness and granularity of the database, which must be populated through extensive controlled experiments across diverse conditions. A key diagram correlates the dimensionless group KiLkL*⁰·⁵ with the percentage of water remaining, demonstrating the universality of the scaling law. A table provides blood’s diffusion coefficients for different humidities (e.g., 1.2×10⁻⁹ m²/s at 20% RH, 0.8×10⁻⁹ m²/s at 40% RH) and substrates, enabling precise environmental correction.

This embodiment represents a complete, self-contained forensic tool that transforms a simple photograph into a quantitative time estimate, fulfilling a long-standing need in criminal investigation.
