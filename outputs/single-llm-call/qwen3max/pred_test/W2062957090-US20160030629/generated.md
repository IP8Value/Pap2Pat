# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates to peptide-albumin hydrogels, which represent a novel class of biomaterials engineered for advanced three-dimensional (3D) cell culture applications. Hydrogels have long been recognized as promising scaffolds for mimicking the extracellular matrix (ECM), offering a hydrated, porous, and mechanically supportive environment conducive to cellular growth, migration, and function. Among various hydrogel systems, peptide-based hydrogels have garnered significant attention due to their biocompatibility, structural similarity to natural ECM components, and tunable physicochemical properties. However, despite these advantages, conventional peptide hydrogels suffer from critical limitations that hinder their practical utility in biological research and therapeutic development. A primary limitation lies in the requirement for non-physiological triggers—such as extreme pH adjustments, temperature shifts, or chemical cross-linking agents—to initiate gelation. These conditions are often cytotoxic, necessitating complex procedural workarounds that compromise cell viability during encapsulation. Furthermore, once formed, many peptide hydrogels are irreversible or require harsh enzymatic or chemical treatments for degradation, making the recovery of embedded cells for downstream analysis both inefficient and damaging. Natural hydrogels derived from collagen, fibrin, or Matrigel offer inherent bioactivity but suffer from batch-to-batch variability, undefined composition, and limited mechanical control. Synthetic polymer hydrogels, while reproducible, often lack the nanoscale architecture and biochemical cues necessary to support authentic cell behavior. Thus, there remains an unmet need for a hydrogel system that combines the structural fidelity and biocompatibility of peptide materials with the convenience of physiological gelation, reversible mechanics, and gentle cell isolation—all within standard cell culture media containing serum proteins such as albumin.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention defines peptide-albumin hydrogels as self-assembled, reversible, and shear-thinning hydrogel matrices formed through the interaction of specifically designed linear self-assembling peptides with albumin present in standard cell culture media, such as Minimum Essential Medium (MEM) supplemented with fetal bovine serum (FBS). These hydrogels form spontaneously at neutral pH and ambient or physiological temperatures upon simple mixing of a peptide solution with albumin-containing medium, eliminating the need for external triggers or cytotoxic buffers. The invention further describes methods of forming such hydrogels by combining an aqueous solution of a self-assembling peptide—comprising defined hydrophobic, hydrophilic, and turn-inducing regions—with a biologically compatible medium containing albumin, typically at concentrations ranging from 0.1 mM to 5 mM peptide and 0.1% to 10% (v/v) albumin. Gelation occurs rapidly, within minutes, yielding a nanofibrous network that entraps cells homogeneously during formation without compromising viability. Critically, the resulting hydrogel exhibits dynamic mechanical properties: it is shear-thinning under applied stress (e.g., pipetting) and rapidly reassembles into a gel upon cessation of stress, enabling repeated handling and injection. Moreover, the hydrogel can be reversibly liquefied through dilution and maintained in solution at low temperatures, facilitating high-yield, non-destructive cell isolation via standard centrifugation. This combination of mild formation, tunable mechanics, and reversible sol-gel transitions distinguishes the invention from prior art and establishes a robust platform for 3D cell culture, drug screening, and regenerative medicine.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

Self-assembled hydrogels of the present invention are formed through non-covalent interactions between synthetic peptides and endogenous albumin in culture media, resulting in a physically cross-linked, water-rich matrix. The hydrogel matrix network consists of entangled peptide nanofibers that associate with albumin molecules, creating a percolating scaffold with uniform internetwork morphology. This network is characterized by its reversibility: the hydrogel undergoes sol-gel transitions in response to mechanical shear, dilution, and temperature changes, without requiring chemical modification. Applications of these hydrogels span 3D cell culture, tissue engineering, controlled drug delivery, and high-throughput screening, owing to their biocompatibility and ease of use. The uniform internetwork morphology ensures consistent pore distribution, with an average cell size of the hydrogel matrix—referring to the void spaces between fibers—ranging from 10 to 100 micrometers, suitable for multicellular spheroid formation. The average pore size, defined as the interstitial spaces between nanofibers, is between 20 and 200 nanometers, permitting diffusion of nutrients, oxygen, and therapeutic agents. Peptide nanofibers constitute the primary structural element, with an average diameter of 15 to 25 nanometers and an average length exceeding 1 micrometer, providing high surface area for cell adhesion and signaling. The storage modulus (G′) of the hydrogels ranges from 50 to 1000 Pascals, depending on peptide concentration, and the tunable strength allows customization for specific cell types—from soft gels for neural cells to stiffer matrices for epithelial or cancer models. The reversible property enables repeated liquefaction and re-gelation; hydrogels recover up to 100% of their original storage modulus within minutes after shear-induced breakdown, with typical recovery rates of 70–100% observed after 1–10 minutes of rest. Shear thinning occurs under strain exceeding 100%, reducing viscosity dramatically, followed by rapid re-hydrogelation upon strain removal. Dilution of the destroyed gel below a critical peptide concentration (e.g., <0.5 mM) prevents reassembly, maintaining a low-viscosity state ideal for cell isolation. The hydrogels exhibit excellent water solubility in their pre-gel state and maintain stability across a temperature range of 4°C to 50°C, with reversible stiffening at higher temperatures. Preparation involves dissolving lyophilized peptide in a mild buffer (e.g., 100 mM sodium bicarbonate, pH ~8) at 1–10 mM, then mixing with albumin-containing medium (e.g., MEM + 10% FBS) to achieve final peptide concentrations of 1–3 mM and albumin at ~0.6 mM (from 10% FBS). The peptide-albumin solution remains fluid initially but gels within 15 minutes at room temperature. Advantages include elimination of pH/temperature manipulation, direct cell encapsulation in nutrient-rich medium, and non-enzymatic cell recovery. Uses include culturing adherent and suspension cells in 3D, with demonstrated success in breast cancer (MCF-7) and stem cell models. Hydrogel cell culture proceeds by mixing cell suspensions directly with peptide-medium solutions, allowing homogeneous embedding. Detailed hydrogel formation exploits albumin-triggered self-assembly of linear peptides. These peptides feature a modular structure: a central hydrophobic region (e.g., VVAG, IVA, or similar aliphatic sequences) flanked by hydrophilic residues (e.g., E, K, R) and a β-turn-promoting segment (e.g., D-Pro-Gly or Asn-Gly). The hydrophobic region drives self-association via van der Waals forces, while hydrophilic termini ensure solubility and electrostatic repulsion control. The turn region facilitates conformational switching to β-sheet structures upon albumin binding. Peptide length typically spans 8–12 amino acids, with molecular weights between 800–1500 Da. Peptides are prepared via solid-phase Fmoc chemistry, purified by HPLC, and stored lyophilized at −20°C. Preferred designs balance hydrophobicity and charge to optimize albumin responsiveness. Advantages include reproducibility, scalability, and compatibility with standard lab workflows. The phrase “and/or” denotes inclusive logical disjunction. Numerical ranges (e.g., 1–3 mM) include endpoints and intermediates. Hydrogel application in cell isolation leverages dilution-induced liquefaction. Peptide-albumin solutions support cell viability during encapsulation. Cell culturing occurs at 37°C, 5% CO₂, with medium changes every 48 hours. Hydrogel reversibility enables repeated mechanical disruption. In summary, the peptides are short, amphiphilic, albumin-responsive, and form reversible, nanofibrous hydrogels under physiological conditions.

### EXAMPLES

The following examples illustrate embodiments of the invention without limiting its scope. Example I introduces a representative peptide, h9e, and demonstrates albumin-triggered hydrogel formation. Peptide synthesis employed Fmoc-SPPS on Rink amide resin, yielding >95% purity by HPLC. Hydrogel preparation involved mixing 10 mM h9e in bicarbonate buffer with MEM + 10% FBS to 1–3 mM final peptide concentration. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) revealed 20-nm nanofibers binding albumin, confirming co-assembly. Rheology showed G′ values of 100–700 Pa at 37°C, increasing with concentration. Hydrogel properties included rapid formation (<15 min), shear-thinning, and >85% recovery after four cycles. TEM images confirmed nanofiber-albumin complexes. Rheological testing established concentration-dependent mechanics. The peptide-albumin relationship was synergistic: no gel formed in albumin-free media. Mechanical strength and gelation rate scaled with peptide dose. Hydrogels formed identically in DMEM, RPMI, and other media containing serum. Example II addresses the need for advanced 3D models bridging 2D cultures and animal studies. Hydrogel scaffolds overcome limitations of natural (variable) and synthetic (non-biomimetic) gels. Peptide-based systems enable safe cell encapsulation and isolation—unlike Puramatrix or Matrigel, which require acidic/cold pre-gel states. The invention introduces peptide-medium hydrogels using cancer cell media. h9e synthesis and hydrogelation proceeded as above. MCF-7 cells formed tumor-like clusters in 3D, with high viability. Morphology was spherical vs. flat in 2D; proliferation was slower but sustained. Post-isolation, cells retained bioactivity. The hydrogel matrix exhibited deformability and reassembly, enabling pipette-based handling. Thus, peptide-medium hydrogels are ideal for 3D oncology models. Additional data: h9e was synthesized and dissolved at 10 mM. Hydrogelation in MEM occurred within 15 min. Rheological tests confirmed G′ ~700 Pa (3 mM). SEM showed dense nanofiber networks. In MEM, gelation kinetics were optimal. Nanofiber networks were homogeneous. Dynamic rheology confirmed rapid recovery. Deformability allowed injection. Amplitude sweeps showed full recovery after 10-min rest. Temperature tests (4–50°C) revealed reversible stiffening. For stem cell applications, h9e formed gels in MSC medium. Rheology showed G′ ~500 Pa. Shear-thinning and self-healing were preserved. Cell recovery exceeded 95%. In MSC medium, h9e gelled reliably. Rheological properties matched those in MEM. Shear recovery was >90%. Cell isolation yielded viable MSCs. Similarly, in 2i medium (for pluripotent stem cells), h9e formed stable hydrogels. Rheology indicated G′ ~400 Pa. Self-healing occurred within 5 min. Cells were recovered intact. Stability tests showed h9e-2i hydrogels remained intact for >14 days at 37°C. h9e solutions (pre-gel) were stable for months at 4°C. Stability results confirm shelf-life and operational robustness. In summary, h9e hydrogels are versatile, reversible, and compatible with diverse cell types and media, enabling next-generation 3D culture and drug testing.
