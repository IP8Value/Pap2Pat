# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD

The present invention relates generally to the field of natural language processing (NLP), and more specifically to systems and methods for word sense disambiguation (WSD). NLP encompasses a broad range of computational techniques designed to enable machines to understand, interpret, generate, and respond to human language in a meaningful way. Within this domain, WSD constitutes a fundamental subtask that addresses the inherent ambiguity of natural language by determining the correct meaning—or “sense”—of a polysemous word based on its surrounding linguistic context. Accurate WSD is critical for enhancing the performance of numerous downstream NLP applications, including but not limited to machine translation, information retrieval, question answering, text summarization, and semantic parsing. The invention disclosed herein introduces a novel approach to WSD that leverages lexical knowledge from multiple heterogeneous word sense inventories through an automated gloss alignment mechanism, thereby enabling robust semantic equivalence recognition across diverse linguistic resources without reliance on extensive task-specific annotated data.

## BACKGROUND

Word sense disambiguation has long been recognized as a cornerstone challenge in computational linguistics due to the pervasive polysemy of words in human languages. Traditional approaches to WSD have largely relied on supervised learning paradigms that require large volumes of manually annotated training data linking context sentences to predefined word senses—typically drawn from structured lexical databases such as WordNet. While recent advances in deep learning, particularly those involving fine-tuning of pre-trained transformer-based language models like BERT and RoBERTa, have significantly improved WSD performance on standard benchmarks, these models remain fundamentally constrained by their dependence on inventory-specific annotations and limited generalizability to rare or unseen word senses. Specifically, when trained exclusively on datasets like SemCor—which annotate only a subset of common word senses—such models exhibit substantial performance degradation on low-shot or zero-shot senses that lack sufficient exemplars in the training corpus. Moreover, because existing supervised WSD systems are typically optimized against a single word sense inventory (e.g., WordNet), they cannot easily incorporate complementary semantic knowledge encoded in alternative dictionaries or lexical resources, thereby missing opportunities to enrich sense representations with broader, cross-inventory perspectives. These limitations hinder the development of truly general-purpose WSD systems capable of robustly handling the full spectrum of lexical ambiguity encountered in real-world language use.

## SUMMARY

The present invention overcomes the aforementioned limitations by introducing a gloss alignment algorithm that systematically integrates lexical knowledge from multiple word sense inventories to construct a unified semantic equivalence framework. This algorithm is motivated by the empirical observation that different expert-compiled dictionaries often describe the same underlying meanings of a word using distinct yet semantically equivalent glosses (i.e., definition sentences). By aligning these parallel glosses across inventories according to their semantic content, the invention generates high-quality training signals for a general-purpose semantic equivalence recognizer. In one embodiment, the method comprises generating aligned word sense inventories by applying a maximum weighted bipartite matching optimization over textual similarity scores between gloss pairs from two or more dictionaries. The invention further provides a process for obtaining a word-in-context sentence and determining semantic equivalence scores between that sentence and candidate glosses from aligned inventories. Based on these scores, the correct sense of the target word is predicted. To train the underlying model, the invention generates positive and negative gloss pairs: positive pairs consist of glosses aligned across inventories or within the same inventory that correspond to the same meaning, while negative pairs comprise unaligned or mismatched glosses. A secondary pre-trained model, such as SBERT, is employed to compute sentence textual similarity scores that serve as edge weights in the bipartite matching formulation. The resulting aligned data is then used to train a gloss classifier—implemented via a transformer architecture—that learns to distinguish semantically equivalent from non-equivalent gloss-context pairs. This trained model functions either as a general-purpose WSD system or as a foundation for fine-tuned expert models tailored to specific evaluation benchmarks.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention introduces a word sense predicting model grounded in the principle of semantic equivalence between a target word in context and its defining gloss. Central to this model is the gloss alignment of word sense inventories, which enables the aggregation of lexical knowledge from multiple authoritative dictionaries—including WordNet, Oxford Advanced Learner’s Dictionary, Merriam-Webster’s Advanced Learner’s Dictionary, Collins COBUILD Advanced Dictionary, Cambridge Advanced Learner’s Dictionary, and Longman Dictionary of Contemporary English—into a coherent, interoperable resource. The alignment process begins by generating pairs of glosses: for each common keyword shared across two inventories, all possible gloss combinations are enumerated. These pairs are then labeled as positive if they express the same meaning (as determined by the alignment algorithm) or negative otherwise. Importantly, positive and negative pairs are also generated using glosses within each individual word sense inventory by pairing example sentences with their corresponding glosses (positive) or with glosses of other senses of the same word (negative). Once the training data is constructed, a context sentence containing the target word is obtained, and the model is trained using this augmented dataset. The architecture employs pre-trained transformers—such as BERT Base or RoBERTa Large—as semantic encoders to derive contextualized representations of both the target word in its sentence and the candidate gloss sentences. The model outputs a probability score indicating the likelihood that the gloss is semantically equivalent to the word in context, thereby enabling prediction of the correct word sense.

Evaluation of the word sense predicting model is conducted using established WSD datasets, including the all-words benchmark suite standardized by Raganato et al. and the FEWS dataset focused on low-shot scenarios. Positive and negative pairs of glosses are derived not only from built-in training data but also from the aligned multi-inventory corpus, which substantially expands coverage of rare senses. Aligned inventories are generated using dictionary-based word sense inventories, wherein parallel glosses from multiple sources are aligned via an optimization setup that maximizes overall sentence textual similarity. This alignment is formalized as a Maximum Weighted Bipartite Matching problem, solved using linear programming techniques to ensure one-to-one correspondence between glosses of equivalent meaning. The matching function is configured to maximize the aggregate cosine similarity between sentence embeddings produced by a secondary pre-trained model such as SBERT. An example setup involves constructing a weighted bipartite graph where nodes represent glosses from two inventories and edge weights reflect SBERT-derived similarity scores; the optimal matching is then computed to yield high-confidence alignments.

The semantic equivalence recognizer model is trained using gloss examples extracted from these aligned inventories, leveraging both intra-inventory and inter-inventory contrastive pairs. Augmented training data—comprising millions of automatically generated gloss-context instances—enables robust pre-training of a general model that captures universal principles of semantic equivalence independent of any single inventory. This general model may be directly applied to WSD tasks or further fine-tuned on task-specific data to produce an expert model. During inference, the model produces output probabilities for each candidate gloss, selecting the one most likely to be semantically equivalent to the target word in context. Process 400 embodies this workflow: it begins by generating aligned inventories, obtains a word-in-context sentence, computes semantic equivalence scores against all candidate glosses, and predicts the correct sense based on the highest-scoring gloss. Process 500 details the alignment mechanism: it collects glosses from a first and second word sense inventory, determines the best match between them using Maximum Weighted Bipartite Matching, computes sentence textual similarity scores via SBERT, configures the matching function accordingly, and generates both positive and negative gloss pairs for training. Process 600 describes inference using the semantic equivalence recognizer: it inputs the context sentence and gloss pairs into the model, identifies glosses associated with the target word, applies the trained gloss classifier, and generates a probability score for each gloss. Similarly, Process 700 reiterates this inference pipeline, emphasizing the dual input of context and gloss pairs into the recognizer model to yield final sense predictions. Experimental results demonstrate that the general model achieves a 13.1% improvement over baselines on zero-shot senses without any task-specific fine-tuning, while the expert model sets new state-of-the-art results—surpassing prior art by 1.2% on all-words WSD and 4.3% on low-shot WSD. Furthermore, transfer learning on the FEWS dataset with augmented training data yields significant gains, and application to the Word-in-Context task improves RoBERTa Large accuracy by 6%, outperforming much larger models such as T5-3B. These outcomes validate the efficacy of leveraging multiple aligned word sense inventories to build scalable, generalizable, and high-performing WSD systems.
