# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

Nanofluidic techniques represent a class of advanced microscale fluid manipulation technologies that enable precise control and analysis of minute volumes of biological samples, typically in the picoliter to nanoliter range. These techniques have found widespread application in molecular biology, genomics, diagnostics, and high-throughput screening due to their ability to miniaturize and parallelize biochemical reactions while maintaining high sensitivity and specificity. Central to many nanofluidic platforms is the integration of microfabricated channels, valves, and reaction chambers that allow for automated partitioning, mixing, and thermal cycling of reagents with minimal sample consumption. Such systems are particularly valuable in applications requiring absolute quantification of nucleic acids, such as viral load measurement, rare mutation detection, and copy number variation (CNV) analysis.

Digital polymerase chain reaction (digital PCR or dPCR) is a powerful quantitative technique that leverages the principles of limiting dilution and Poisson statistics to achieve absolute quantification of target DNA molecules without reliance on standard curves. In conventional digital PCR, a DNA sample is serially diluted and distributed across numerous individual reaction vessels—historically microwells or droplets—such that a significant fraction of reactions contain either zero or one target molecule. Following amplification via PCR, positive reactions (those containing at least one target molecule) are counted, and the original concentration of target molecules is inferred using statistical models. This approach provides high precision and sensitivity, especially for low-abundance targets, and is less susceptible to amplification efficiency variations than real-time quantitative PCR (qPCR).

Despite its advantages, conventional digital PCR suffers from several limitations. First, manual or semi-automated partitioning methods often introduce pipetting errors and variability between replicates. Second, the use of discrete tubes or plates limits scalability and throughput. Third, traditional platforms may lack integrated fluidic control, requiring external handling steps that increase contamination risk and reduce reproducibility. Moreover, the statistical frameworks used to interpret digital PCR data have historically been simplistic or based on assumptions that do not fully account for the stochastic nature of molecular partitioning or the uncertainty in concentration estimates, particularly when estimating ratios of concentrations as required in CNV studies.

Copy number variation (CNV) refers to structural genomic alterations involving gains or losses of DNA segments larger than 500 base pairs, which can encompass entire genes or regulatory regions. CNVs are now recognized as a major source of genetic diversity in human populations and have been implicated in numerous diseases, including cancer, neurodevelopmental disorders, and autoimmune conditions. Accurate determination of CNVs is therefore critical for both research and clinical diagnostics. However, existing whole-genome scanning technologies—such as array-based comparative genomic hybridization (array-CGH) and high-density single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) microarrays—while capable of genome-wide profiling, suffer from limited resolution, poor sensitivity for small CNVs, and difficulty in detecting low-level mosaicism. Furthermore, these methods often require large amounts of high-quality DNA and complex bioinformatic pipelines.

Digital array-based CNV determination offers a promising alternative by combining the precision of digital PCR with multiplexed, sequence-specific amplification in a highly parallelized nanofluidic format. In this approach, a DNA sample is partitioned into hundreds or thousands of nanoliter-scale reaction chambers on an integrated biochip, where co-amplification of a target gene and a reference gene (e.g., a single-copy endogenous control) occurs simultaneously. The ratio of positive chambers for the two genes provides a direct estimate of the relative copy number. However, prior art lacks robust statistical methods to compute accurate confidence intervals for both individual concentrations and their ratios, especially when dealing with non-normal sampling distributions or asymmetric uncertainties. This gap undermines the reliability of CNV calls, particularly near decision thresholds (e.g., distinguishing diploid from triploid states).

There remains a clear and pressing need for improved methods and systems that integrate advanced nanofluidic partitioning with rigorous statistical inference to enable accurate, reproducible, and statistically sound CNV analysis. Such innovations must address the limitations of current digital PCR platforms by providing not only point estimates of concentration ratios but also well-calibrated confidence intervals that reflect the true uncertainty inherent in molecular partitioning and detection processes. The present invention fulfills this need through novel computational frameworks and system architectures designed specifically for high-confidence CNV determination in digital array platforms.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention introduces advanced nanofluidic systems and associated computational methods for the accurate determination of copy number variation (CNV) in biological samples. At the core of the invention is a method and system that partitions a DNA sample into a plurality of nanoliter-scale reaction chambers on a digital array biochip, enabling digital PCR to be performed in a massively parallel and highly controlled manner. From the observed number of positive reaction chambers—those containing one or more target DNA molecules—the system computes statistically rigorous estimates of the true molecular concentrations of both a target gene and a reference gene, along with confidence intervals that quantify the uncertainty of these estimates.

In one aspect, the invention provides a method for estimating the concentration of DNA molecules in a biological sample. This method begins by storing in a memory device the total number of reaction sites (chambers) available in the digital array panel, denoted as C. The biological sample, mixed with appropriate PCR reagents, is then distributed among these C reaction sites such that DNA molecules are randomly partitioned according to Poisson statistics. After thermal cycling and fluorescence imaging, the number of reaction sites containing amplified product—i.e., those that were initially occupied by at least one DNA molecule—is determined, yielding a count H. The portion of positive reaction sites, denoted as p̂ = H/C, serves as an unbiased estimator of the probability p that a given chamber contains at least one molecule. Using the established relationship p = 1 − e^−λ, where λ is the true mean number of molecules per chamber (i.e., concentration scaled to chamber volume), the system computes an estimated concentration λ̂ = −ln(1 − p̂). Critically, the invention further computes a confidence interval around λ̂ by first deriving the sampling distribution of p̂, approximating it as normal for large C, calculating its standard deviation σ_p̂ = √[p̂(1 − p̂)/C], and then propagating this uncertainty through the nonlinear transformation to obtain asymmetric confidence bounds on λ.

In another aspect, the invention provides a refined method for estimating the concentration of DNA molecules that explicitly constructs probability density functions (PDFs) to capture the full uncertainty distribution. This involves forming a first PDF for the estimated probability p̂ based on binomial sampling theory, computing a corresponding confidence interval for p, and then transforming this interval into a second PDF for λ using the change-of-variable technique. The resulting PDF for λ enables more nuanced statistical inference, particularly when integrating over multiple panels or comparing across samples.

A key innovation of the invention lies in its method for estimating the ratio of concentrations between two DNA sequences—such as a test gene and a reference gene—and computing a statistically valid confidence interval for this ratio. The method distributes a first biological sample (or a multiplexed reaction containing both targets) into a set of reaction chambers and independently determines the estimated concentration λ̂₁ and its confidence interval for the first target. Similarly, it determines λ̂₂ and its confidence interval for the second target. Rather than relying on simplistic error propagation, the invention employs a numerical algorithm based on a generalized interpretation of Fieller’s Theorem. This algorithm constructs the joint sampling distribution of λ̂₁ and λ̂₂, integrates over angular wedges in the two-dimensional parameter space to form a ratio PDF q(r̂), and derives confidence limits [r_Low, r_High] such that the integral of q(r̂) over this interval equals the desired confidence level (e.g., 95%). This approach accommodates arbitrary, potentially asymmetric sampling distributions and avoids the pitfalls of assuming normality in ratio estimation.

The invention further enhances applicability by supporting pooled analysis across multiple digital array panels, thereby increasing effective chamber count and improving statistical power. It is broadly applicable not only to CNV analysis but also to any scenario requiring precise ratio estimation of biomolecules, including allelic imbalance, gene expression normalization, and pathogen load quantification relative to host DNA. By integrating nanofluidic hardware with sophisticated statistical computation, the present invention delivers a robust, accurate, and user-friendly platform for high-confidence molecular quantification.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF SPECIFIC EMBODIMENTS

Copy number variation (CNV) represents a fundamental class of genomic structural variation wherein segments of DNA, ranging from 500 base pairs to several megabases, are duplicated or deleted relative to a reference genome. Accurate detection and quantification of CNVs are essential for understanding genetic disease mechanisms, cancer genomics, and population genetics. Digital PCR (dPCR) has emerged as a gold-standard technique for CNV analysis due to its ability to provide absolute quantification without calibration curves. The present invention builds upon dPCR by implementing it on a nanofluidic digital array chip, which partitions DNA samples into hundreds of nanoliter-volume reaction chambers, enabling high-precision molecular counting.

The digital array chip, a central component of the invention, is a microfluidic biochip fabricated from elastomeric materials such as polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS). It features an array of 765 reaction chambers per panel, each with a volume of approximately 6 nanoliters, resulting in a total reaction volume of 4.59 microliters per panel. Integrated microvalves, actuated pneumatically or electromechanically, control the flow of sample and reagents through a network of microchannels, allowing precise loading of PCR mix into each chamber. The chip is compatible with commercial platforms such as Fluidigm’s BioMark system, which provides integrated thermal cycling and fluorescence imaging capabilities. During operation, a DNA sample mixed with TaqMan master mix, target-specific primers and probes (e.g., FAM-labeled for the test gene), and reference gene assay (e.g., VIC-labeled for RNase P) is loaded onto the chip. Valves are actuated to isolate each reaction chamber, and the chip undergoes thermocycling: 95°C for 10 minutes followed by 40 cycles of 95°C for 15 seconds and 60°C for 1 minute. Post-amplification, an optical imaging system captures fluorescence signals from all chambers, and software identifies positive chambers based on signal intensity thresholds.

The mathematical framework underpinning the invention addresses the core problem of CNV analysis: given observed counts h₁ and h₂ of positive chambers for a target and reference gene, estimate the ratio r = λ₁/λ₂ of their true concentrations and compute a 95% confidence interval [r_Low, r_High]. This problem is decomposed into two parts: (1) estimating individual concentrations λ₁ and λ₂ from h₁ and h₂, and (2) estimating the ratio and its confidence interval. Unlike Bayesian approaches that assume prior distributions over λ, the present invention treats λ as a fixed but unknown parameter and uses frequentist statistical inference.

Each reaction chamber is modeled as a Bernoulli trial: success (positive) if it contains ≥1 molecule, failure otherwise. If the true concentration is λ molecules per chamber, the probability of success is p = 1 − e^−λ, derived from the Poisson distribution of molecule counts per chamber. Given C chambers and H positives, the sample proportion p̂ = H/C is an unbiased estimator of p with standard deviation σ_p̂ = √[p̂(1 − p̂)/C]. For large C (e.g., C = 765 or multiples thereof), the sampling distribution of p̂ is approximately normal. The estimated concentration is λ̂ = −ln(1 − p̂). To compute a confidence interval for λ, the invention applies the delta method or exact change-of-variable transformation: the 95% confidence limits for p are p̂ ± 1.96σ_p̂, which are mapped to λ_Low = −ln(1 − p_Upper) and λ_High = −ln(1 − p_Lower), yielding an asymmetric interval that correctly reflects the nonlinear relationship.

Computer simulations validate this approach. In one experiment, 70,000 panels were simulated with λ = 0.523 (corresponding to ~400 molecules per panel). The histogram of observed H values matched the theoretical binomial distribution, and 95% of computed confidence intervals contained the true λ, confirming coverage accuracy.

For ratio estimation, the invention implements a numerical algorithm inspired by the geometric interpretation of Fieller’s Theorem. Even though λ̂₁ and λ̂₂ are not normally distributed, their joint sampling distribution is constructed via simulation or analytical approximation. The ratio PDF q(r̂) is formed by integrating the joint PDF over lines of constant slope r̂ = λ̂₁/λ̂₂ in the (λ̂₁, λ̂₂) plane. Confidence limits are derived from the cumulative distribution of q(r̂). As an approximation, direct formulas based on asymmetric confidence ellipses can be used: if [λ₁_L, λ₁_U] and [λ₂_L, λ₂_U] are the individual 95% intervals, then r_Low = λ₁_L / λ₂_U and r_High = λ₁_U / λ₂_L, though the numerical method is preferred for accuracy.

Experimental validation used a spike-in design: synthetic RPP30 DNA was added to human genomic DNA (NA10860) at known ratios (1 to 3.5 copies per diploid genome, normalized to single-copy RNase P). Five panels per condition yielded >95% of true ratios within computed 95% confidence intervals. With three or more panels, confidence intervals narrowed sufficiently to resolve adjacent ratios (e.g., 2 vs. 2.5).

The optical imaging system comprises an excitation light source (e.g., LED or laser), an excitation filter wheel to select wavelengths matching FAM and VIC fluorophores, an excitation shutter for timing control, a fiber bundle or lens system to illuminate the microfluidic device, and an emission path with an emission filter wheel, emission shutter, and a CCD or CMOS detector array. The system is designed as a 1:1 magnification optical train with low numerical aperture (NA) to maximize depth of field and ensure uniform focus across all chambers. Pixel size is matched to chamber dimensions (~50–100 µm) to enable single-chamber resolution. Temperature control is achieved via a thermal transfer device in contact with the chip, regulated by a thermocouple or resistance-based sensor, with vacuum or mechanical force ensuring good thermal contact. Fiducial marks on the chip enable automatic alignment during imaging.

Alternative embodiments include blind-channel devices, matrix-type arrays, and chips with varying chamber counts (e.g., 480, 1024). Detection methodologies extend beyond fluorescence to chemiluminescence or absorbance. Intercalation dyes like SYBR Green may replace probe-based detection. The computational methods are implemented in software running on a computer with database storage, enabling high-throughput analysis of multiple samples and panels.

In summary, the invention provides a complete, integrated solution for CNV analysis that combines nanofluidic sample partitioning, robust thermal and optical control, and statistically rigorous concentration and ratio estimation. By delivering accurate point estimates with well-calibrated confidence intervals, it enables confident clinical and research decisions based on digital PCR data.
