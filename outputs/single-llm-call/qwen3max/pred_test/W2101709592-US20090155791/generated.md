The present invention is directed to methods and compositions for the detection of methylated CpG-containing nucleic acids, particularly in the context of epigenetic diagnostics. The disclosure incorporates by reference all publications, patents, and patent applications cited herein, including but not limited to U.S. Patent Nos. 5,786,146; 6,258,540; and international publications WO 2004/092398 and WO 2007/038835, which describe foundational techniques in DNA methylation analysis, bisulfite conversion, and methylation-specific amplification. ## FIELD OF INVENTION

The present invention relates to a method for detecting methylated CpG-containing nucleic acids in a biological sample. More specifically, the invention provides an improved approach for designing methylation-independent oligonucleotide primers that enable proportional amplification of both methylated and unmethylated DNA templates following bisulfite modification. This method facilitates accurate post-amplification assessment of DNA methylation status at specific genomic loci, thereby enhancing the reliability of epigenetic diagnostics in research and clinical settings.

## BACKGROUND OF INVENTION

### Importance of DNA Methylation

DNA methylation is a covalent biochemical modification involving the addition of a methyl group to the carbon-5 position of cytosine residues, predominantly occurring at CpG dinucleotides in mammalian genomes. This epigenetic mark plays a pivotal role in regulating gene expression without altering the underlying DNA sequence. DNA methylation is essential for normal development, genomic imprinting, X-chromosome inactivation, and suppression of transposable elements. The enzymatic reaction is catalyzed by DNA methyltransferases (DNMTs), which transfer a methyl group from S-adenosylmethionine to cytosine, forming 5-methylcytosine. CpG islands—regions of the genome enriched in CpG dinucleotides—are frequently located in promoter regions of genes. Under physiological conditions, these islands remain unmethylated in actively transcribed genes, whereas methylation of CpG islands is associated with transcriptional silencing. Aberrant DNA methylation patterns, including hypermethylation of tumor suppressor gene promoters and global hypomethylation, are hallmark features of cancer and other diseases. Hypermethylation of CpG islands in promoter regions leads to stable gene silencing, contributing to oncogenesis when it affects genes involved in cell cycle control, DNA repair, or apoptosis. Different tumor types exhibit distinct methylation signatures, making DNA methylation a valuable biomarker for early detection, prognosis, and therapeutic monitoring. Consequently, there is a strong motivation to develop robust, sensitive, and quantitative methods for detecting DNA methylation. Existing methodologies include methylation-specific PCR (MSP), bisulfite sequencing, pyrosequencing, and methylation-sensitive high-resolution melting (MS-HRM). However, many of these approaches suffer from significant limitations, particularly PCR amplification bias that preferentially amplifies unmethylated DNA over methylated DNA, leading to underestimation or complete failure to detect biologically relevant methylation levels. This bias compromises the accuracy and reproducibility of methylation analysis, especially in heterogeneous clinical samples where methylated alleles may be present at low abundance.

## SUMMARY OF INVENTION

The present invention provides a method for detecting methylated CpG-containing nucleic acids that overcomes the limitations of prior art techniques by employing specially designed methylation-independent primers that incorporate a limited number of CpG dinucleotides at strategic positions. These primers, when used in conjunction with optimized annealing temperatures during PCR amplification, enable proportional amplification of both methylated and unmethylated DNA templates following bisulfite conversion. The invention encompasses the primer design principles, amplification protocols, and analytical methods—such as high-resolution melting curve analysis—that together allow for accurate, sensitive, and quantitative determination of DNA methylation status at specific genomic loci. Key aspects include the inclusion of one to three CpG sites per primer, preferably distal to the 3' end, salt-adjusted melting temperatures near 65°C, and empirical optimization of annealing stringency to counteract inherent PCR bias favoring unmethylated sequences.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

### DEFINITIONS

Amplification refers to the enzymatic process of generating multiple copies of a target nucleic acid sequence from a single or few initial template molecules. This is typically achieved through repeated cycles of denaturation, annealing, and extension in a polymerase chain reaction (PCR). A double-stranded polynucleotide consists of two complementary strands of nucleotides held together by hydrogen bonds between base pairs. A gene is a segment of DNA that contains the instructions for synthesizing a functional product, such as a protein or RNA molecule. A nucleotide is the basic structural unit of nucleic acids, comprising a nitrogenous base (adenine, thymine/uracil, guanine, or cytosine), a five-carbon sugar (deoxyribose in DNA, ribose in RNA), and one or more phosphate groups. Nucleotides link via phosphodiester bonds to form polynucleotide chains. Base pairing follows Watson-Crick rules: adenine pairs with thymine (or uracil in RNA) via two hydrogen bonds, and guanine pairs with cytosine via three hydrogen bonds. An oligonucleotide is a short single-stranded polymer of nucleotides, typically 15 to 50 bases in length, used as a primer or probe in molecular biology. A polynucleotide is a longer chain of nucleotides, which may be single- or double-stranded. A dinucleotide is a molecule consisting of two nucleotides joined by a phosphodiester bond; the CpG dinucleotide comprises a cytosine followed by a guanine on the same DNA strand. Methylation status refers to whether a cytosine residue within a CpG dinucleotide is methylated (5-methylcytosine) or unmethylated. Detection of methylation status involves distinguishing between these two states, often after chemical modification such as bisulfite treatment. PCR bias denotes the non-uniform amplification efficiency of methylated versus unmethylated DNA templates during PCR, typically resulting in preferential amplification of unmethylated sequences due to their lower melting temperature after bisulfite conversion.

### Samples

Biological samples suitable for use in the present invention include any source containing genomic DNA, such as tissue biopsies, blood, saliva, urine, cerebrospinal fluid, fine needle aspirates, formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissues, and cultured cells. Preferred sample sources include tumor tissues, peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs), and bodily fluids known to contain circulating tumor DNA. Sample selection is guided by the clinical or research question, with emphasis on obtaining sufficient quantity and quality of DNA. Specific sample sources encompass colorectal cancer specimens, breast cancer biopsies, leukemia cell lines, and cervical swabs. Nucleic acid extraction can be performed using standard commercial kits or phenol-chloroform methods, yielding purified genomic DNA. In some embodiments, crude lysates may be used directly without purification, though this is less preferred. The target nucleic acid is genomic DNA containing CpG-rich regions of interest, such as promoter-associated CpG islands. Nucleic acids may exist as pure preparations or as mixtures containing both methylated and unmethylated alleles. Genomic nucleic acid preparation typically involves cell lysis, protein removal, and DNA precipitation or column-based purification. The amount of nucleic acid required varies by assay sensitivity but generally ranges from 10 ng to 1 µg per reaction.

### Modification of DNA

A critical step in the method involves chemical modification of DNA to distinguish methylated from unmethylated cytosines. This is accomplished by treating the DNA sample with sodium bisulfite, which selectively deaminates unmethylated cytosine residues to uracil, while leaving 5-methylcytosine intact. The reaction proceeds under acidic conditions with high concentrations of sodium bisulfite, typically involving denaturation of DNA, incubation with bisulfite reagent at 50–55°C for several hours, followed by desulfonation and purification. After modification, unmethylated cytosines are converted to uracils, which are subsequently amplified as thymines during PCR, whereas methylated cytosines remain as cytosines. This conversion creates sequence differences between originally methylated and unmethylated DNA strands, enabling discrimination during downstream analysis. PCR amplification of the modified nucleic acid is then performed using methylation-independent primers designed according to the principles of the invention.

### Methylation-Independent Primer

A methylation-independent primer, as defined herein, is an oligonucleotide designed to amplify a target region regardless of its methylation status, yet engineered to mitigate PCR bias through strategic inclusion of CpG dinucleotides. These primers hybridize to bisulfite-converted DNA and are capable of binding both methylated (C-containing) and unmethylated (T-containing) templates, but with tunable affinity based on annealing temperature. The presence of CpG dinucleotides in the primer sequence is central to the invention: unlike conventional MIP primers that exclude all CpGs, the primers of the present invention incorporate a limited number—typically one, and up to three—CpG sites per primer. The location of these CpGs is critical; they must be positioned away from the 3' end to avoid absolute specificity for methylated templates, which would convert the primer into an MSP-type reagent. Empirical testing determines optimal placement, often in the 5' half of the primer. Primer length is generally between 18 and 30 nucleotides, with examples including 22-mers and 25-mers. The method for determining methylation status involves amplifying bisulfite-modified DNA with these primers under controlled annealing conditions, followed by analysis of the amplicon (e.g., by melting curve analysis). Hybridization to CpG islands is enabled by the primer’s ability to tolerate C/T variation at CpG sites. Specific hybridization requirements include sufficient complementarity to ensure specific binding, with mismatches at CpG sites being permissible due to controlled stringency. Primer identity to the target template is approximately 85–100%, with deliberate mismatches at non-CpG cytosines converted to thymine. Mismatches at the 3' end, particularly involving non-CpG-derived Ts, enhance specificity for bisulfite-converted DNA. Annealing temperature modulation is a key feature: lower temperatures reduce discrimination, while higher temperatures increase selectivity for methylated templates. Stringency is further modulated by buffer composition, particularly salt concentration (e.g., KCl, (NH₄)₂SO₄), with lower salt increasing stringency. Temperature modulation is preferred over chemical modifiers for fine-tuning. Primers are selected from specific sequences designated as SEQ ID NO: 128–151. Preferred pairs include SEQ ID NO: 138 and 139, 142 and 143, and 144–151 combinations. Additional preferred pairs are SEQ ID NO: 128/129, 130/131, 132/133, 134/135, 136/137, and 140/141. Target genes include MGMT, BNIP3, CDKN2A, RASSF1A, APC, and MLH1. In specific embodiments, primers target MGMT (SEQ ID NO: 128–129), BNIP3 (130–131), CDKN2A (132–133), RASSF1A (134–135), APC (136–137), and MLH1 (138–139). The MGMT gene is of particular significance, as its promoter methylation is a predictive biomarker for response to temozolomide chemotherapy in glioblastoma and other cancers.

### Amplifying Step

The amplifying step involves PCR-mediated replication of the bisulfite-modified target region using methylation-independent oligonucleotide primers. The polymerization reaction is catalyzed by a thermostable DNA polymerase, such as Taq, Hot Start Taq, or the Stoffel fragment, in the presence of deoxynucleotide triphosphates (dNTPs), buffer, and magnesium ions. PCR is the preferred amplification method due to its sensitivity, speed, and compatibility with downstream analyses. The process begins with a melting step, wherein double-stranded DNA is denatured at 94–98°C for 15–60 seconds. This is followed by an annealing step at a precisely controlled temperature—typically between 55°C and 65°C—for 15–60 seconds, during which primers hybridize to the template. The elongation step occurs at 72°C for 15–60 seconds per kilobase, allowing the polymerase to synthesize new DNA strands. These steps are repeated for 30–50 cycles in a thermal cycler. Real-time PCR variants employ fluorescent dyes (e.g., SYBR Green, EvaGreen) that intercalate into double-stranded DNA, enabling quantification during amplification (qPCR). Multiplex PCR, wherein multiple targets are amplified simultaneously, is also feasible with appropriate primer design. Other PCR variants, including nested PCR and touchdown PCR, may be employed to enhance specificity or sensitivity. The amplifying step concludes with a final extension and optional hold at 4–10°C.

### Analysis of Amplified CpG-Containing Nucleic Acids

Following amplification, the methylation status of the target region is determined by analyzing the sequence composition of the PCR product. Due to bisulfite conversion, unmethylated cytosines appear as thymines, while methylated cytosines remain as cytosines, resulting in amplicons with differing GC content and melting behavior. Analysis methods include melting curve analysis, high-resolution melting (HRM), sequencing, restriction digestion, and hybridization. Melting curve analysis is particularly advantageous due to its closed-tube format, speed, and cost-effectiveness. In HRM, the PCR product is slowly heated while fluorescence is continuously monitored; as the DNA melts, the dye is released, causing a drop in fluorescence. The resulting melting curve is normalized and analyzed for shape and peak melting temperature (Tm). Methylated amplicons, richer in GC content, melt at higher temperatures than unmethylated ones. Platforms such as the LightCycler 480, Rotor-Gene Q, and QuantStudio support HRM. The in-tube methylation assay estimates the relative amount of methylated DNA by comparing sample melting profiles to standard curves generated from known mixtures of methylated and unmethylated DNA (e.g., 0%, 10%, 25%, 50%, 100% methylated). Determination of methylation status is based on the Tm peak: a single peak at high Tm indicates full methylation, a low Tm peak indicates no methylation, and intermediate or broad peaks suggest partial methylation. Normalization involves aligning pre- and post-melt baselines, and derivative plots (-dF/dT vs. temperature) are used to identify Tm peaks accurately.

### Nucleic Acid Sequencing

Nucleic acid sequencing provides base-resolution methylation data. The dideoxy or Sanger method involves chain termination using dideoxynucleotides (ddNTPs) that lack a 3' hydroxyl group, halting DNA synthesis at specific bases. Four reactions (each with a different ddNTP) generate fragments of varying lengths, which are separated by capillary electrophoresis to determine the sequence. Applied to bisulfite-converted DNA, cytosines in the sequence correspond to originally methylated sites, while thymines indicate unmethylated cytosines.

### Primer Extension

Primer extension involves annealing a primer adjacent to the region of interest and extending it with a DNA polymerase in the presence of labeled or unlabeled nucleotides. The length or composition of the extension product reveals the methylation status at specific CpG sites, particularly when combined with mass spectrometry (e.g., MALDI-TOF) or fluorescence detection.

### Restriction Enzyme Digestion

Restriction enzyme digestion exploits the fact that some enzymes (e.g., HpaII, BstUI) cannot cleave methylated recognition sites. After bisulfite conversion and PCR, the amplicon is digested with methylation-sensitive restriction enzymes. Unmethylated sequences are cut, while methylated ones remain intact. Analysis via gel electrophoresis reveals digestion patterns: complete digestion indicates no methylation, partial digestion suggests heterogeneity, and no digestion implies full methylation. Enzymes like HhaI, AciI, and HinP1I are also used. Modified and amplified nucleic acids are resolved on agarose or polyacrylamide gels, with band intensity correlating to methylation levels.

## Disorders

Aberrant CpG methylation is indicative of numerous disorders, particularly cancers. Hypermethylation of tumor suppressor gene promoters (e.g., CDKN2A, BRCA1) increases cancer risk by silencing protective genes. Conversely, demethylation of transposable elements can lead to genomic instability and oncogene activation. Specific disorders include breast cancer, bladder cancer, ovarian cancer, melanoma, prostate cancer, lung cancer, colon cancer, endometrial cancer, leukaemia, gastric cancer, cervical cancer, and imprinting disorders such as Beckwith-Wiedemann and Angelman syndromes.

Specific embodiments include primers targeting MGMT or BNIP3 with corresponding control DNAs.

## Use

The methylation-independent oligonucleotide primers are used for detecting the methylation status of CpG-containing nucleic acids in a sample, enabling diagnosis, prognosis, or monitoring of methylation-associated diseases.

## EXAMPLES

### Example 1

Genomic DNA containing CpG sites was modified with sodium bisulfite, converting unmethylated cytosines to uracils. Initial PCR amplification using conventional MIP primers (lacking CpGs) showed strong bias toward unmethylated alleles, even in samples with 50% methylated DNA. Primers were redesigned to include a single CpG dinucleotide positioned away from the 3' end. Testing across annealing temperatures revealed that at 60°C, amplification became proportional. Melting profiles of PCR products from mixed templates showed distinct, resolvable curves corresponding to methylation levels, demonstrating improved assay sensitivity and accuracy.

### Example 2

Four redesigned primer assays were validated using defined mixtures of methylated and unmethylated DNA (0–100%). All assays successfully amplified the methylated allele even at 1% abundance, whereas conventional primers failed below 10%. The modified MS-MCA methodology exhibited superior linearity and dynamic range. Key advantages included reduced false negatives, quantitative capability, and compatibility with high-throughput platforms.

### Example 3

Methylation-sensitive high-resolution melting (MS-HRM) was developed as a sensitive, high-throughput method. DNA from colorectal cancers and cell lines was extracted and bisulfite-modified. Controls included CpGenome Universal Methylated DNA (positive) and PBMC DNA (negative). Dilution series (0–100% methylated) were amplified using MGMT-targeted primers. HRM analysis normalized melting curves and estimated methylation levels. Redesigned primers (MGMT MS-HRM2/3) amplified shorter fragments (80–100 bp), enhancing sensitivity to 0.1%. Validation against MethylLight confirmed accuracy. The assay was applied to 8 cell lines and 19 clinical specimens, showing consistent results. BNIP3 MS-HRM similarly performed well. Compared to sequencing (low throughput), pyrosequencing (costly), and MSP (prone to false positives), MS-HRM offered balanced strengths.

### Example 4

Melting curve assays were motivated by the need for closed-tube, quantitative methylation analysis. Bisulfite modification involved DNA denaturation, sodium bisulfite incubation (55°C, 16h), and purification. PCR amplification used primers designed with limited CpGs and T-residues at the 3' end for bisulfite specificity. New MIP primer guidelines emphasized CpG inclusion, Tm matching (~65°C), and avoidance of secondary structures. High-resolution melting used saturating dyes (EvaGreen) on LightCycler 480. Precise fluorescence acquisition and normalization enabled methylation estimation. Critical factors included Mg²⁺ optimization, hot-start polymerases, and stopping PCR pre-plateau to avoid re-annealing artifacts. Storage of bisulfite DNA at –80°C preserved integrity. Standards were essential for quantification, and PCR bias remained a key consideration in sensitivity.

### Example 5

An MS-HRM protocol was established: genomic DNA was extracted, bisulfite-modified (EZ DNA Methylation Kit), and amplified using primers with one CpG each. Reagents included Hot Start Taq, EvaGreen, and Mg²⁺-optimized buffer. Equipment included a Rotor-Gene Q fluorimeter. Methylated/unmethylated references were diluted to create standards. Procedures involved 45-cycle PCR followed by HRM (65–95°C, 0.1°C/s). Data analysis used derivative peak identification and curve normalization. Troubleshooting addressed issues like poor conversion (validate with non-CpG cytosines) and primer dimers (optimize Mg²⁺). Anticipated results included distinct melting profiles for 0%, 10%, and 100% methylated samples, enabling semi-quantitative assessment in clinical specimens.
