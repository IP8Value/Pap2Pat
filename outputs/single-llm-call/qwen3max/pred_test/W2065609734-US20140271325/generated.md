# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

Conventional sintering techniques for refractory metals such as tungsten have long been hindered by intrinsic limitations that impede the production of fully dense, fine-grained bulk materials. Tungsten, in particular, exhibits extremely high melting and self-diffusion temperatures, which necessitate sintering at temperatures exceeding 2000 °C to achieve appreciable densification through solid-state diffusion mechanisms. Even under such extreme conditions, conventional pressureless sintering of tungsten typically yields components with significant residual porosity and coarse microstructures, thereby compromising mechanical performance and limiting industrial applicability. Alternative approaches, including liquid phase sintering and activated sintering, have been employed to lower sintering temperatures; however, these methods often introduce secondary phases that degrade high-temperature stability or require precise compositional control to avoid deleterious intermetallic formation. Moreover, severe plastic deformation and other top-down processing routes, while capable of producing nanocrystalline structures, suffer from geometric constraints, scalability issues, and an inherent inability to eliminate internal defects such as voids and dislocation tangles. Bottom-up powder consolidation strategies offer a promising pathway to bulk nanocrystalline alloys but are frequently thwarted by concurrent grain coarsening and sluggish densification kinetics during thermal processing. The thermodynamic instability of nanocrystalline microstructures—driven by the high interfacial energy associated with grain boundaries—further exacerbates this challenge, as grain growth readily occurs at temperatures well below those required for full densification. Consequently, existing sintering methodologies fail to simultaneously achieve high relative density, nanoscale grain size, and compositional homogeneity in bulk refractory alloys without recourse to external pressure, electric fields, or complex multi-stage thermal cycles. These limitations underscore the need for a novel sintering paradigm that decouples densification kinetics from grain growth, enabling the fabrication of fully dense nanocrystalline components through simple, scalable, and pressureless thermal processing.

## SUMMARY

The present invention is motivated by the urgent need for nanocrystalline alloys that combine exceptional mechanical properties with thermal stability and manufacturability. Nanocrystalline metals, characterized by grain sizes below 100 nanometers, exhibit significantly enhanced strength, hardness, and wear resistance compared to their coarse-grained counterparts, yet their practical deployment has been stymied by microstructural instability during consolidation. To address this, the invention introduces a novel sintering method—termed nanophase separation sintering—that leverages the controlled decomposition of a metastable, supersaturated nanocrystalline solid solution to accelerate densification while preserving fine grain structure. In a key embodiment, the sintering method comprises mechanically alloying a refractory metal such as tungsten with a solute element like chromium to form a nanocrystalline powder in which the solute is fully dissolved despite negligible equilibrium solubility, compacting the powder into a green body, and then heating the compact to a temperature at which the supersaturated phase separates into a refractory-rich matrix and a solute-rich secondary phase that preferentially precipitates at particle surfaces and interparticle necks. This secondary phase acts as a rapid diffusion conduit, facilitating mass transport and enabling near-full densification at temperatures hundreds of degrees below those required for conventional sintering. Another embodiment of the invention is the sintered nanocrystalline alloy itself, which exhibits a relative density greater than 95%, a grain size less than 200 nanometers, and a dual-phase microstructure comprising a continuous refractory metal matrix and a dispersed network of solute-rich regions at grain boundaries and triple junctions. The invention further encompasses combinations wherein multiple additive elements are employed—one serving as a stabilizer to inhibit grain growth and another as an activator to promote phase separation and diffusion—thereby enabling tunable control over sintering kinetics, final density, and microstructural refinement. Collectively, these embodiments provide a robust, pressureless route to bulk nanocrystalline alloys with unprecedented combinations of density, grain size, and thermal stability.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

Nanocrystalline metals represent a class of advanced structural materials wherein the average grain size is reduced to the nanometer scale, typically below 100 nanometers. This extreme refinement of the microstructure confers a host of desirable properties, including dramatically increased yield strength in accordance with the Hall-Petch relationship, enhanced hardness, improved wear and corrosion resistance, and unique deformation mechanisms such as grain boundary sliding and rotation. Mechanical test data on nanocrystalline Ni—W alloys, for instance, demonstrate hardness values exceeding 8 GPa, far surpassing those of conventional alloys. The grain size effect on hardness is particularly pronounced in the nanocrystalline regime, where further reductions in grain size yield diminishing returns and may even lead to softening due to the dominance of grain boundary-mediated processes. Similarly, activation volume measurements reveal a transition from dislocation-based to interface-controlled plasticity as grain size decreases below a critical threshold. These observations motivate precise control over grain size during material synthesis and processing to optimize mechanical performance. For the purposes of this invention, nanocrystalline materials are defined as polycrystalline solids with an average grain diameter of less than 100 nanometers, encompassing both ultra-fine grain (50–100 nm) and true nanocrystalline (<50 nm) regimes. Grain geometry in these materials is typically equiaxed, though elongated or faceted morphologies may arise depending on processing conditions. The particulate shape of precursor powders is generally spherical or irregular micron-scale agglomerates, each comprising numerous nanograins—a critical distinction from nanopowders wherein each particle is a single nanocrystal. The nanocrystalline alloy composition of the invention comprises a primary refractory metal such as tungsten, optionally combined with other transition metals, and incorporates specific additive elements selected based on their ability to form supersaturated solid solutions during mechanical alloying and to phase-separate upon heating. Metal element selection is guided by considerations of atomic size mismatch, enthalpy of mixing, and diffusivity. Activator elements, such as chromium or nickel, are chosen for their capacity to dissolve in the host metal during high-energy milling and to precipitate as a fast-diffusing secondary phase during sintering. Stabilizer elements, such as titanium, are selected for their strong segregation to grain boundaries, thereby reducing grain boundary energy and inhibiting coarsening. The resulting sintered material achieves a relative density of at least 90%, preferably greater than 95%, and is classified as a bulk nanocrystalline alloy when it maintains nanoscale grains throughout its macroscopic volume. The inherent instability of nanocrystalline microstructures arises from the excess Gibbs free energy associated with grain boundaries, which drives grain growth to minimize total interfacial area. This can be expressed via the Gibbs free energy equation ΔG = γ·ΔA, where γ is the grain boundary energy and ΔA is the change in interfacial area. Limiting grain growth thus requires either kinetic suppression—through pinning by second-phase particles—or thermodynamic stabilization via reduction of γ. While kinetic approaches are limited by the thermal instability of pinning agents, the thermodynamic approach leverages the Gibbs adsorption equation to reduce γ through solute segregation. Grain boundary energy reduction is achieved when additive elements with negative enthalpies of segregation (ΔHseg) preferentially occupy boundary sites. The magnitude of ΔHseg is influenced by atomic radius mismatch; greater mismatch enhances segregation strength by inducing local strain fields that favor solute enrichment at interfaces. Thermal stability is determined by the balance between driving force for coarsening and resistance provided by segregated species. In the context of tungsten, activated sintering traditionally relies on additives like nickel or iron to form transient liquid phases or enhance diffusion, but these often compromise high-temperature performance. The present invention instead exploits solid-state phase separation in nanocrystalline W—Cr alloys, where Cr precipitation under high-rate loading enables localized shearing and accelerated densification without melting. Bottom-up methodologies, such as mechanical alloying followed by sintering, are contrasted with top-down severe plastic deformation (SPD) techniques like equal-channel angular pressing; while SPD produces nanocrystalline surfaces, it cannot eliminate internal porosity or scale to bulk components. The two-step bottom-up method of the invention—mechanical alloying to create supersaturated nanocrystalline powder followed by pressureless sintering—avoids volume defects inherent in SPD. Additive elements are introduced not only to activate sintering but also to stabilize the nanocrystalline state. The importance of ΔHseg is paramount: elements with highly negative ΔHseg strongly segregate, lowering γ and raising the onset temperature for grain growth. Atomic radius mismatch beyond 10% typically enhances segregation strength. Thermal stability is experimentally verified by monitoring grain size evolution during isothermal holds. Activated sintering of tungsten is redefined herein as a process wherein additive elements modify sintering kinetics not through liquid formation but via solid-state phase separation that creates rapid diffusion pathways. The additive element effect on sintering kinetics is profound: Cr in W reduces the effective activation energy from ~600 kJ/mol (self-diffusion in W) to ~370 kJ/mol (diffusion of W in Cr). This is attributed not to dislocation climb but to an electron transfer mechanism that enhances grain boundary diffusivity. Sintering kinetics are governed by a generalized sintering equation wherein the instantaneous densification rate depends on surface energy, atomic volume, and diffusion coefficients for volume and grain-boundary paths. Rearrangement of this equation permits estimation of activation energy via the master sintering curve method. Thermodynamic stabilization of tungsten alloys is achieved by co-addition of stabilizer and activator elements. Stabilizer elements, such as Ti, possess high melting points, strong W bonding, and large atomic mismatch, and are added in amounts of 5–40 atomic percent. Activator elements, such as Cr or Ni, have lower melting points, moderate solubility in W, and negative ΔHseg, and are added in amounts of 5–25 atomic percent. Selection criteria include mutual immiscibility with W at equilibrium, positive heat of mixing to enable supersaturation, and compatibility in phase diagrams to avoid brittle intermetallics. The role of activators is to phase-separate during heating, forming Cr-rich necks that dissolve and transport W atoms. Interaction between activator and stabilizer must be managed to prevent compound formation; phase diagram analysis ensures that ternary systems like W-Ti-Cr remain within single-phase or benign two-phase fields. The amount of additives is critical: too little fails to activate sintering, while too much induces excessive second-phase formation or liquid phases. Embodiments include W-15Cr, W-35Ti-10Cr, and Cr-15Ni. Liquid phase sintering is avoided; conditions are maintained below eutectic temperatures. However, if employed, liquid phase sintering would require additives that form transient liquids below 1500°C, offering advantages of rapid pore filling but risking microstructural coarsening, which stabilizers mitigate. Detection of liquid phase is unnecessary here, as sintering proceeds entirely in the solid state. The pore filling mechanism involves W diffusion through Cr-rich necks, driven by capillary forces. Sintered nanocrystalline alloys are produced by ball-milling elemental powders for 15–30 hours to form nanocrystalline particulates with grain sizes of 10–20 nm, compacting into green bodies, and sintering at 950–1500°C in reducing atmospheres. The non-equilibrium phase formed during milling decomposes on heating, yielding a W-rich matrix and Cr-rich precipitates. This second metal material enhances sintering kinetics by providing short-circuit diffusion paths. Final properties include >98% relative density and grain sizes <100 nm. Sintering conditions involve heating rates of 5–20°C/min in Ar/H₂. Ball-milling produces contamination <3 wt% WC, which is tolerated. Average grain size is calculated via XRD peak broadening using Scherrer’s equation. Polycrystalline particulates, not single crystals, ensure internal diffusion paths. The supersaturated phase sinters via solid-state neck formation, not liquid flow. The master sintering curve integrates instantaneous linear shrinkage rate, incorporating surface energy, atomic volume, and diffusion parameters to extract activation energy, which for W-15Cr is 373 kJ/mol—consistent with W diffusion in Cr.

### Non-Limiting Working Examples

Tungsten powder with an average particle size of 1–5 μm and 99.9% purity was used as the base material. The ball milling process was conducted in a SPEX 8000 high-energy mill using tungsten carbide media at a ball-to-powder ratio of 5:1, with 1 wt% stearic acid as a process control agent, for durations ranging from 15 to 30 hours to form nanocrystalline tungsten alloy powders. Cylindrical green compacts of 6 mm diameter and 3–4 mm height were formed by cold uniaxial pressing at 360 MPa. Dimensional changes during sintering were measured using a thermodilatometer (TMA) under flowing high-purity argon gas containing 3% hydrogen. Hydrogen-containing atmospheres were found to effectively reduce native oxides on powder surfaces, thereby enhancing sinterability, whereas vacuum atmospheres led to incomplete oxide removal and argon-only atmospheres resulted in sluggish kinetics, supporting a volatile vapor phase oxide hydrate theory wherein H₂O(g) desorbs from reduced surfaces. Non-isothermal heating techniques, particularly constant rate of heating at 5, 10, 15, and 20°C/min, were employed to probe sintering kinetics, complemented by isothermal holds for microstructural analysis. In Example 1, tungsten powder was ball milled with 20 at% Cr for 20 hours. XRD peaks showed complete dissolution of Cr into the W lattice, evidenced by a single body-centered cubic phase with shifted lattice parameter. Grain size was measured at approximately 13 nm by TEM. Sintering onset occurred at ~950°C, achieving 91% densification by 1300°C. Post-sintering microstructure revealed Cr-rich precipitates at necks, and activation energy was determined as 373 kJ/mol. Example 2 used 15 at% Cr, showing similar XRD peak shifts and Cr dissolution, with 98% densification at 1500°C and comparable activation energy. Example 3 employed 20 at% Ti, which exhibited inferior sintering due to Ti’s strong oxide formation and lack of beneficial phase separation. Example 4 varied Cr content from 5 to 25 at%, identifying 15–20 at% as optimal for densification. Example 5 ball milled W-Ti-Cr (35Ti-10Cr), yielding a nanostructured alloy with 100 nm grains and >98% density after sintering. Example 6 demonstrated Cr-rich phase precipitation and neck formation in W-20Cr via STEM-EDS. Example 7 applied the method to Cr-15Ni, achieving accelerated densification with activation energy of 258 kJ/mol, consistent with Cr diffusion in Ni, and showing Ni segregation at necks.
