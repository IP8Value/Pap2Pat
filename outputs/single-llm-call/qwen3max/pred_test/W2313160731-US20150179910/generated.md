# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNICAL FIELD

The present invention relates to the field of energy conversion, and more particularly to systems and methods for converting thermal energy into electrical energy using solar radiation as a primary heat source. The invention encompasses integrated configurations that combine solar concentration technologies with thermoelectric generators, optionally in conjunction with steam-based power generation, to produce electricity efficiently and sustainably. The disclosed systems are especially suited for deployment in marine or aquatic environments, including onshore, offshore, and artificial island platforms, and are designed to operate with enhanced thermal efficiency through the use of advanced heat transfer fluids and optimized heat exchange geometries.

## BACKGROUND ART

Thermoelectric generators (TEGs) are solid-state devices that convert thermal energy directly into electrical energy through the Seebeck effect. These devices typically consist of semiconductor materials arranged in p-n junctions that generate a voltage when subjected to a temperature gradient. While TEGs offer advantages such as reliability, lack of moving parts, and scalability, their widespread adoption has been limited by relatively low conversion efficiencies, which are highly dependent on maintaining a significant and stable temperature differential across the device.

Solar energy conversion systems have traditionally relied on photovoltaic cells or concentrated solar power (CSP) plants that use mirrors or lenses to focus sunlight onto a receiver, heating a working fluid that drives a turbine connected to a generator. Conventional CSP systems often employ synthetic oils, molten salts, or pressurized water/steam as heat transfer fluids (HTFs). However, these systems suffer from several limitations. Many organic HTFs degrade at elevated temperatures, exhibit high viscosity, low thermal conductivity, or narrow operational temperature windows, necessitating high pumping power and complex containment systems. Moreover, traditional CSP installations require substantial land use, are sensitive to environmental conditions, and involve high capital and maintenance costs due to mechanical complexity.

Prior art systems that attempt to integrate solar thermal collection with thermoelectric generation often fail to address the critical challenge of maintaining an optimal and sustained thermal gradient. Submerging TEGs in ambient water to provide a cold sink is known, but without precise control over the hot-side temperature and efficient heat delivery via a stable HTF, performance remains suboptimal. Furthermore, existing designs lack adaptive orientation mechanisms to track the sun’s position, reducing energy capture efficiency throughout the day. There remains a need for an integrated, robust, and scalable system that overcomes these limitations through novel fluid chemistry, geometric optimization, and intelligent system architecture.

## DISCLOSURE OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a system for converting thermal energy into electrical energy that overcomes the deficiencies of prior art by integrating a solar concentrator, a specialized heat transfer fluid, and a submerged thermoelectric generator within a closed-loop configuration. The system comprises a solar concentrator configured to direct and focus incident sunlight onto a focal region containing a vacuum-sealed tubing assembly through which a heat transfer fluid flows. This fluid, heated to elevated temperatures by concentrated solar radiation, is conveyed via insulated ducting to a thermoelectric generator that is fully submerged in a body of water, such as a lake, ocean, or reservoir, thereby utilizing the water as a natural heat sink to maintain a large temperature differential across the thermoelectric modules.

The heat transfer fluid is recycled through a feedback loop that returns the cooled fluid back to the solar concentrator after it has passed through the thermoelectric generator, enabling continuous operation. In an alternative embodiment, the system further includes a steam generator and turbine, wherein a portion of the thermal energy from the heat transfer fluid is used to produce steam that drives a turbine coupled to an electrical generator, while the residual heat is directed to a thermoelectric generator integrated with a steam condensing unit, thereby maximizing overall energy recovery.

The thermoelectric generator is configured with one or more thermoelectric panels arranged to maximize surface contact with the heat transfer fluid on the hot side and with the surrounding water on the cold side. An array of fins is disposed on the outer surface of the thermoelectric generator to enhance convective heat dissipation into the water, improving the thermal gradient. The solar concentrator is equipped with an automatic orientation adjustment mechanism, such as a motorized tracking system, that continuously aligns the concentrator with the sun’s azimuth and elevation to optimize solar flux capture throughout the day.

In a further embodiment, the entire system is mounted on an artificial island comprising a buoyant ring supporting a platform upon which the solar concentrators and associated storage tanks are installed. This floating configuration enables deployment in open water bodies without requiring fixed infrastructure, and may be designed to rotate about a central axis to facilitate solar tracking. The artificial island embodiment enhances scalability and environmental compatibility, particularly in regions where land availability is constrained.

## MODE(S) FOR CARRYING OUT THE INVENTION

In a first embodiment, the system is deployed onshore, with parabolic solar concentrators arranged in parallel rows to maximize land use efficiency. Each concentrator focuses sunlight onto a vacuum tubing assembly positioned at its focal point, through which the heat transfer fluid—comprising a thermally stable aryl ether compound synthesized via a one-pot solvent-minimized protocol—flows. The heated fluid is conveyed through an insulated inner duct to a submerged thermoelectric generator located offshore or in a nearby reservoir. The generator is housed in a waterproof casing and features an outer surface studded with an array of aluminum or copper fins to accelerate heat dissipation into the surrounding water.

A feedback loop connects the outlet of the thermoelectric generator to a storage tank via an outer duct, completing the circulation path. A control valve regulates the flow rate of the heat transfer fluid based on real-time temperature differentials measured across the thermoelectric modules, ensuring optimal performance under varying solar irradiance. The storage tank acts as a buffer to stabilize fluid supply and accommodate transient fluctuations in solar input.

In an offshore embodiment, the solar concentrators and storage tank are mounted on a floating installation supported by pontoons or a rigid buoyant structure anchored to the seabed. This configuration eliminates land use constraints and leverages the high heat capacity of seawater as a consistent cold sink. Electrical wiring encased in waterproof conduits transfers the generated electricity to shore via subsea cables.

In the artificial island embodiment, a circular buoyant ring constructed from corrosion-resistant composite materials supports a central platform. Solar concentrators are mounted on the platform in a radial or concentric arrangement. Holders affixed to the underside of the platform secure multiple thermoelectric generator units, each connected in series to increase output voltage. The island may be designed to rotate slowly about a vertical axis, driven by a solar-tracking actuator, to maintain optimal alignment with the sun without reorienting individual concentrators.

The thermoelectric generator units feature modular ducting: an inner duct with a triangular cross-sectional area facilitates turbulent flow of the heat transfer fluid to enhance convective heat transfer, while an outer duct of matching geometry provides structural support and thermal insulation. Adjacent duct segments are coupled via interlocking engagement regions and sealed outlet tubes to ensure leak-proof continuity. Thermal interface material, such as graphite-based paste or phase-change composites, is applied between the duct walls and thermoelectric modules to minimize interfacial thermal resistance.

Electrical wiring interconnects the thermoelectric modules in series or parallel configurations depending on desired voltage and current output. Alternative cross-sectional geometries—including circular and rectangular profiles—are employed based on hydrodynamic and thermal optimization requirements. In hybrid embodiments, excess thermal energy not utilized by the thermoelectric generator is diverted to a steam generator containing water, producing steam that drives a turbine. The exhaust steam is then condensed in a condensing unit thermally coupled to a secondary thermoelectric generator, with molten salt serving as a high-capacity heat retention medium in a dedicated heat exchanger for nighttime or cloudy-day operation. Additional conveyance lines enable selective routing of the heat transfer fluid between the thermoelectric and steam subsystems, allowing dynamic load balancing and energy storage integration.
