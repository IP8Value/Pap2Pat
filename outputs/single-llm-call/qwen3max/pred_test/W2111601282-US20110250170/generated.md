# DESCRIPTION

Cancer remains one of the most formidable challenges in modern medicine, with malignant tumours exhibiting high rates of morbidity and mortality despite advances in therapeutic strategies. Conventional cancer therapies—including surgery, radiation, and systemic chemotherapy—often fall short in achieving durable remissions, particularly in aggressive malignancies such as glioblastoma multiforme. These limitations stem from a combination of factors, including poor drug penetration into tumour tissue, systemic toxicity that restricts dose escalation, and intrinsic or acquired resistance mechanisms within tumour cells. In light of these shortcomings, there has been a concerted effort to develop more selective and effective treatment modalities that can specifically target tumour-associated structures while sparing healthy tissues.

A promising strategy to overcome the limitations of conventional therapies involves the selective delivery of bioactive agents to the tumour microenvironment using monoclonal antibodies or their derivatives. Antibodies can be engineered to recognize antigens that are either uniquely expressed or significantly overexpressed in tumours compared to normal tissues. By conjugating such antibodies to cytotoxic drugs, cytokines, or radionuclides, it becomes possible to concentrate therapeutic payloads at the tumour site, thereby enhancing efficacy while minimizing off-target effects. This approach, often referred to as targeted therapy or antibody-directed delivery, leverages the exquisite specificity of the immune system for precise molecular recognition.

Central to this strategy is the identification of suitable target antigens that fulfill several key criteria: they must be accessible to circulating antibodies, exhibit minimal expression in vital normal tissues, and be abundantly present in the tumour stroma or on tumour cells. Among the most compelling targets identified to date are components of the extracellular matrix (ECM) that undergo structural alterations or are re-expressed during tumour progression. One such antigen is tenascin-C, a large hexameric glycoprotein that plays a role in tissue remodeling during embryogenesis and wound healing but is largely absent in healthy adult tissues. However, tenascin-C is markedly upregulated in a wide range of solid tumours, where it contributes to tumour cell migration, invasion, and angiogenesis.

Tenascin-C exists in multiple splice variants, and certain domains, such as the A1 domain, are selectively expressed in pathological conditions like cancer. Monoclonal antibodies have been developed that specifically recognize these tumour-associated epitopes. Of particular relevance is the human monoclonal antibody F16, which binds with high affinity to the A1 domain of tenascin-C. Unlike murine antibodies, which can elicit human anti-mouse antibody (HAMA) responses, F16 is fully human, thereby reducing immunogenicity and enabling repeated administration in clinical settings. Moreover, F16 and its derivatives have demonstrated excellent tumour-targeting properties in preclinical models, accumulating preferentially in tumour stroma while showing minimal uptake in normal organs.

The utility of F16 extends beyond simple targeting; it serves as an ideal scaffold for the construction of immunocytokines—fusion proteins comprising an antibody moiety linked to a cytokine. Interleukin-2 (IL-2), a potent immunostimulatory cytokine that activates natural killer (NK) cells and T lymphocytes, has been fused to F16 to create the immunocytokine F16–IL2. This molecule combines the tumour-localizing capacity of F19 with the immune-activating potential of IL-2, thereby concentrating immune stimulation directly within the tumour microenvironment. Importantly, because the A1 domain of tenascin-C is predominantly expressed in the subendothelial extracellular matrix of tumour vasculature and stroma, F16–IL2 delivers IL-2 precisely where it can exert maximal antitumor effects without inducing systemic cytokine toxicity.

In parallel, other ECM-targeting antibodies have been developed, such as L19, which recognizes the extradomain B (ED-B) of fibronectin—a domain similarly absent in most adult tissues but highly expressed in tumour neovasculature. The ED-B domain arises from alternative splicing of the fibronectin pre-mRNA and is a hallmark of angiogenic blood vessels. L19-based immunocytokines, including L19–IL2 and L19–IL12, have shown promising results in preclinical and early clinical trials. Similarly, the F16 antibody has been used to construct not only F16–IL2 but also radioimmunoconjugates and other fusion proteins tailored for specific therapeutic applications.

Glioblastoma, the most common and aggressive primary brain tumour in adults, exemplifies the urgent need for improved therapeutic approaches. The current standard of care includes maximal surgical resection followed by concurrent radiotherapy and temozolomide chemotherapy, yet median survival remains under 15 months. Glioblastomas are characterized by extensive microvascular proliferation, necrosis, and diffuse infiltration into surrounding brain parenchyma. Critically, both the ED-B domain of fibronectin and the A1 domain of tenascin-C are overexpressed in high-grade astrocytomas, including glioblastoma, making them attractive targets for antibody-based therapy.

Preclinical studies have demonstrated that monoclonal antibodies like F16 can effectively target intracranial glioblastoma xenografts, penetrating the disrupted blood–brain barrier associated with tumour vasculature. Radioimmunotherapy using radiolabeled anti-tenascin antibodies has shown some clinical activity, but efficacy has been limited by suboptimal tumour-to-background ratios and radiation toxicity. In contrast, immunocytokines offer a non-radioactive, immune-mediated mechanism of action that may circumvent these issues.

A pivotal discovery underlying the present invention is the synergistic therapeutic effect observed when combining the alkylating agent temozolomide with the immunocytokine F16–IL2. While temozolomide exerts direct cytotoxic effects through DNA alkylation, F16–IL2 recruits and activates immune effector cells—particularly NK cells and macrophages—within the tumour. This dual mechanism results in enhanced tumour cell killing, suppression of proliferation, and induction of apoptosis, leading to complete tumour eradication in preclinical models where monotherapies fail to achieve durable responses.

The first aspect of the invention thus provides a method of treating a tumour in a subject, comprising administering a therapeutically effective amount of a chemotherapeutic agent, particularly an alkylating agent such as temozolomide, in combination with an immunocytokine comprising an antibody moiety specific for a tumour-associated antigen—preferably the A1 domain of tenascin-C—and a cytokine moiety, preferably interleukin-2. The second aspect relates to the use of such a combination for the manufacture of a medicament for the treatment of cancer, especially glioblastoma. The third aspect encompasses pharmaceutical compositions containing both agents, formulated for co-administration or sequential administration, with defined dosing regimens that maximize synergy while minimizing toxicity.

# DETAILED DESCRIPTION

Chemotherapeutic agents encompass a broad class of compounds that inhibit cell division or induce cell death in rapidly proliferating cells, including cancer cells. Among these, alkylating agents represent one of the oldest and most widely used categories. Alkylating agents function by transferring alkyl groups to DNA, leading to cross-linking of DNA strands, mispairing of nucleotides, and ultimately triggering apoptosis. Examples of alkylating agents include nitrogen mustards (e.g., cyclophosphamide, chlorambucil), nitrosoureas (e.g., carmustine, lomustine), alkyl sulfonates (e.g., busulfan), triazines (e.g., dacarbazine, temozolomide), and platinum-containing compounds (e.g., cisplatin, carboplatin). Prodrugs, which are pharmacologically inactive compounds that are metabolized in vivo to active forms, are also included within this definition; temozolomide is a prime example, as it is converted at physiological pH to the active alkylating agent MTIC (5-(3-methyltriazen-1-yl)imidazole-4-carboxamide).

Temozolomide is particularly relevant in the context of central nervous system tumours due to its ability to cross the blood–brain barrier and its established role in the standard treatment of glioblastoma. The tumours amenable to treatment according to the present invention include, but are not limited to, glioblastoma multiforme, anaplastic astrocytoma, and other high-grade gliomas, as well as any solid tumour that overexpresses the A1 domain of tenascin-C or other targetable ECM antigens.

The antibody moiety of the immunocytokine is designed to bind specifically to a tumour-associated antigen. Preferred target antigens include splice variants of extracellular matrix proteins that are selectively expressed in tumour stroma, such as the A1 domain of tenascin-C and the ED-B domain of fibronectin. The preferred antibody is a human monoclonal antibody or fragment thereof that exhibits high affinity and specificity for the A1 domain of tenascin-C. One such antibody is designated 4A1-F16, which has been extensively characterized for its binding properties and tumour-targeting capabilities.

Variants of the 4A1-F16 antibody may include amino acid substitutions, deletions, or insertions that do not substantially impair antigen binding but may enhance stability, solubility, or pharmacokinetic properties. The antibody molecule may be presented in various formats, including full-length IgG, single-chain variable fragments (scFv), small immunoproteins (SIP), diabodies, or minibodies. The SIP format, for instance, consists of a dimeric structure formed by two polypeptide chains, each comprising a variable heavy (VH) domain, a variable light (VL) domain, and a constant domain (e.g., CH4), connected by a flexible peptide linker.

The VH domain of the 4A1-F16 antibody contains complementarity-determining regions (CDRs) that mediate antigen recognition. Specifically, the VH CDR1 has the sequence GYTFTSYW (SEQ ID NO: 1), VH CDR2 is ISPYNGGT (SEQ ID NO: 2), and VH CDR3 is ARDGYAMDY (SEQ ID NO: 3). The VL domain includes VL CDR1: RSSQSLLHSNGNTYLH (SEQ ID NO: 4), VL CDR2: KVSNRFS (SEQ ID NO: 5), and VL CDR3: SQSTHVPPT (SEQ ID NO: 10). Variants of these domains may be generated through site-directed mutagenesis or phage display techniques, provided that binding affinity for the A1 domain is retained. Alterations may include conservative amino acid substitutions or modifications to framework regions to improve expression or stability.

A suitable antibody for use in the invention is one that maintains the antigen-binding specificity of 4A1-F16 while being compatible with fusion to a cytokine moiety. The scFv format, wherein VH and VL domains are linked by a peptide linker such as (GGGGS)3 (SEQ ID NO: 6), is particularly amenable to genetic fusion. The SIP homodimer is formed via disulfide bonding mediated by the CH4 domain, which also contributes to serum half-life extension. The peptide linker connecting the antibody moiety to the cytokine moiety is critical for maintaining the structural integrity and functional independence of both domains. A preferred linker sequence is (GGGGS)2 (SEQ ID NO: 7), though other flexible or rigid linkers may be employed.

The immunocytokine comprises interleukin-2 (IL-2) as the cytokine moiety. Human IL-2 is a 133-amino acid protein with the sequence MAPIPGEPPKWEFEDFFLQLLSAGQASQEPAPKPEASAGPGAPRRPSPSPGESSTAASSSLPLPSPRLPALPESSGPSSGSASDGPRGP (SEQ ID NO: 8). Variants of IL-2 may include muteins with altered receptor binding profiles, such as those with reduced affinity for the IL-2Rα chain to minimize regulatory T-cell activation. The IL-2 moiety is fused genetically to the C-terminus of the antibody moiety via the peptide linker, resulting in a single polypeptide chain that self-assembles into a functional dimeric immunocytokine.

Administration of the immunocytokine is typically intravenous, although other routes may be considered depending on the tumour location. The individual to be treated is a mammal, preferably a human, diagnosed with a tumour that expresses the target antigen. Treatment involves repeated dosing of both the immunocytokine and the chemotherapeutic agent according to a schedule that maximizes synergistic interaction—for example, concurrent administration on multiple days over a treatment cycle.

# TERMINOLOGY

### Antibody Molecule

An antibody molecule refers to an immunoglobulin or derivative thereof capable of specifically binding to an antigen. Immunoglobulins are glycoproteins composed of two identical heavy chains and two identical light chains, each containing variable and constant regions. Antibody fragments include Fab, Fab′, F(ab′)2, Fv, scFv, diabodies, triabodies, and minibodies, all of which retain antigen-binding capability. Chimeric molecules may be constructed by fusing antibody fragments to heterologous proteins such as cytokines, enzymes, or toxins. Human or humanized antibody molecules are engineered to reduce immunogenicity in human subjects, with humanized versions containing only the CDRs from a non-human source grafted onto a human framework.

Binding fragments encompass any portion of an antibody that retains the ability to bind antigen, including single-domain antibodies (dAbs). dAbs derived from camelid heavy-chain-only antibodies (VHH domains) are particularly stable and soluble. Functional antibody fragments may also be produced by proteolytic digestion (e.g., papain or pepsin cleavage) or by recombinant DNA technology and peptide synthesis. Bispecific or bifunctional antibodies, such as those generated using BiTE (bispecific T-cell engager) technology, contain two distinct antigen-binding sites and can simultaneously engage two different targets, such as a tumour antigen and a T-cell receptor.

### Antigen-Binding Site

The antigen-binding site is the region of an antibody molecule responsible for recognizing and binding to a specific epitope on an antigen. In conventional antibodies, this site is formed by the juxtaposition of the VH and VL domains, with the CDRs making direct contact with the antigen. Antigen-binding sites can be engineered through mutagenesis or combinatorial library screening to alter specificity, affinity, or cross-reactivity. Multiple antigen-binding sites may be incorporated into a single molecule to enhance avidity or enable simultaneous targeting of multiple antigens.

# EXPERIMENTS

### Summary

The therapeutic properties of temozolomide in combination with F16–IL2 were evaluated in preclinical models of human glioblastoma. The combination demonstrated superior antitumour efficacy compared to either agent alone, resulting in complete tumour eradication in subcutaneous models and significant tumour growth inhibition in orthotopic models, without increased toxicity.

### Immunohistochemistry on Human Glioblastoma Samples and on Glioblastoma Xenografts

The F16 antibody, specific for the A1 domain of tenascin-C, was used in biotinylated SIP format to assess antigen expression in human glioblastoma specimens and U87MG xenografts. Immunohistochemical staining revealed strong and selective expression of the A1 domain in both human and murine tumours, with a perivascular distribution pattern comparable to that observed with the L19 antibody, which targets the ED-B domain of fibronectin. This confirmed the suitability of tenascin-C A1 as a target for antibody-based therapy in glioblastoma.

### Cell Lines and Animals

Human U87MG glioblastoma cells were cultured under standard conditions and implanted subcutaneously or intracranially into immunodeficient BALB/c nude mice to generate xenograft models. Female mice were used for subcutaneous studies, while male mice were used for orthotopic experiments, in accordance with institutional animal care guidelines.

### Antibodies and Therapeutic Agents

The F16 antibody and its fusion protein F16–IL2 were prepared as previously described, with F16–IL2 consisting of the SIP(F16) antibody moiety fused to human IL-2 via a peptide linker. Temozolomide was dissolved in saline containing 10% DMSO for intraperitoneal administration.

### Biodistribution Experiments

Quantitative biodistribution analysis following intravenous injection of radioiodinated F16–IL2 in tumour-bearing mice demonstrated preferential accumulation in tumour tissue (2.3% ID/g) with a tumour-to-blood ratio of 11.5, confirming efficient in vivo targeting.

### Subcutaneous Glioma Mouse Models

Mice bearing subcutaneous U87MG tumours were treated with F16–IL2 (20 μg i.v.), temozolomide (0.525 mg i.p.), the combination, or vehicle control, administered five times every three days. Tumour volumes were measured regularly, and animals were monitored for weight loss and signs of toxicity. The combination group exhibited complete and durable tumour regression, whereas monotherapies showed only transient effects.

### Immunofluorescence Assessment of Tumour-Infiltrating Cells and of Microvascular Density in Subcutaneous Glioma Xenografts

Immunofluorescent staining of tumour sections revealed a marked increase in infiltration of NK cells (asialo GM1+) and macrophages (F4/80+) in the combination therapy group, localized around CD31+ vascular structures. Microvascular density was not significantly altered, indicating that the primary mechanism was immune-mediated rather than anti-angiogenic. Ex vivo detection confirmed selective accumulation of F16–IL2 in tumour stroma.

### Statistical Analysis

Biodistribution data were expressed as %ID/g. Tumour volumes were compared using Student’s t-test, with significance set at P<0.05. Kaplan–Meier survival curves were generated and compared using the log-rank test. The combination therapy group showed significantly reduced tumour growth, higher rates of tumour eradication, stable body weights, and prolonged survival compared to all other groups, confirming the synergistic therapeutic benefit of F16–IL2 and temozolomide.
