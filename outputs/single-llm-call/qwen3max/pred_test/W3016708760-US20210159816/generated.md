# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD

The present invention relates generally to the field of energy harvesting, and more particularly to systems and methods for harvesting electrical energy from ambient mechanical vibrations. With the proliferation of low-power electronic devices—especially in the domains of wireless sensor networks, structural health monitoring (SHM), and the Internet of Things (IoT)—there is a growing demand for autonomous, maintenance-free power sources that eliminate reliance on conventional batteries. Energy harvesting technologies aim to convert otherwise wasted ambient energy into usable electrical power. Among the various forms of ambient energy available in operational environments—including thermal gradients, light, electromagnetic radiation, and fluid flow—mechanical vibrations are especially prevalent in industrial machinery, civil infrastructure, transportation systems, and even biological organisms. The invention disclosed herein specifically addresses the challenge of efficiently capturing vibrational energy across a range of frequencies by introducing an actively tunable piezoelectric vibration energy harvester (PVEH) that dynamically adjusts its resonant frequency to match ambient excitation conditions. This adaptive capability significantly enhances energy conversion efficiency compared to conventional fixed-resonance harvesters, thereby enabling reliable and sustained operation of microelectronic systems without external power intervention.

## BACKGROUND

Piezoelectric vibration energy harvesting has emerged as a leading approach for powering distributed microsystems due to the high electromechanical coupling efficiency of piezoelectric materials and their compatibility with miniaturized device architectures. Traditional PVEHs typically employ a cantilever beam structure with a piezoelectric element bonded to its surface; when subjected to base excitation at or near the beam’s natural resonant frequency, large mechanical deflections induce significant strain in the piezoelectric layer, generating a usable electrical output via the direct piezoelectric effect. However, a fundamental limitation of such resonant harvesters is their narrow operational bandwidth: they exhibit peak power output only within a very limited frequency range centered around their fixed resonant frequency. In real-world environments, ambient vibrations are often non-stationary, broadband, or subject to drift due to changes in operating conditions, rendering fixed-frequency harvesters inefficient or entirely ineffective outside their narrow resonance window. To address this issue, numerous strategies have been explored to broaden the effective bandwidth or enable tunability of the harvester’s resonance. Passive approaches—such as incorporating nonlinear elements (e.g., magnetic repulsion/attraction), multi-modal cantilevers, or arrays of beams with staggered resonances—can extend bandwidth but lack adaptability to unpredictable or time-varying excitation spectra. Active tuning methods, which dynamically alter the harvester’s mechanical properties during operation, offer greater flexibility but often require auxiliary power sources that may exceed the harvested energy, negating net gains. Prior active techniques have employed piezoelectric actuators, shape memory alloys, or electromagnetic mechanisms to adjust stiffness or mass, yet these solutions suffer from high actuation power demands, slow response times, or complex integration requirements. Consequently, there remains a critical need for an energy-efficient, rapidly responsive, and integrable active tuning mechanism that consumes minimal power—ideally less than the incremental energy gained through tuning—thereby enabling truly autonomous, self-optimizing energy harvesters. The present invention fulfills this need through the novel integration of Ionic Polymer Metal Composites (IPMCs) as low-power electroactive tuning elements within a piezoelectric cantilever architecture.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides a tunable piezoelectric vibration energy harvesting (PVEH) device and associated methods that overcome the narrow bandwidth limitations of conventional resonant harvesters by enabling real-time adjustment of the system’s resonant frequency to match ambient vibration conditions. Central to the invention is the incorporation of one or more Ionic Polymer Metal Composite (IPMC) actuators as active tuning members that apply controlled mechanical loads to a piezoelectric cantilever beam, thereby altering its effective stiffness and shifting its resonant frequency. PVEH devices according to the invention comprise a cantilever structure formed of a substrate beam with a piezoelectric transducer—preferably a Macro Fiber Composite (MFC)—bonded thereto in a unimorph configuration. The IPMC actuator, positioned adjacent to the cantilever, is configured to bend upon application of a low-voltage electrical signal (typically 0.5 V to 4 V), bringing it into contact with the beam and exerting a compressive or impact load that modifies the beam’s dynamic response. The magnitude of this load, and thus the degree of frequency shift, is precisely controllable via the input voltage to the IPMC, enabling fine-tuned resonance matching. Critically, the actuation force generated by the IPMC is sufficient to induce meaningful frequency shifts while consuming minimal electrical power—demonstrated experimentally to be substantially less than the incremental power harvested due to improved resonance alignment. In certain embodiments, the IPMC is implemented as a stacked or dual-strip configuration to enhance force output and stability. The harvested AC electrical output from the piezoelectric transducer is conditioned through a sensor circuit, typically including a full-wave bridge (FWB) rectifier, to produce a DC output suitable for charging storage elements such as capacitors or thin-film batteries. The invention further encompasses closed-loop energy harvesting systems wherein a portion of the harvested power is used to drive the IPMC actuator under the control of a low-power microcontroller that monitors ambient vibration frequency—via analysis of the harvester’s own output—and automatically adjusts the IPMC voltage to maintain optimal resonance. Methods of energy harvesting according to the invention include steps of detecting ambient vibration frequency, applying a control signal to an IPMC tuning member to shift the resonant frequency of the harvester toward the detected ambient frequency, and converting mechanical vibration into electrical energy via the tuned piezoelectric transducer, thereby achieving enhanced net power output over a broad and variable frequency spectrum.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

### Introduction

The present invention discloses piezoelectric vibration energy harvesting (PVEH) devices and associated methods that integrate smart materials to achieve active, low-power tuning of resonant frequency. Conventional PVEHs suffer from inherently narrow bandwidths, limiting their utility in real-world environments where vibration spectra are dynamic. The disclosed systems overcome this by leveraging the unique properties of two advanced composite materials: Macro Fiber Composites (MFCs) as the primary piezoelectric transducer and Ionic Polymer Metal Composites (IPMCs) as the active tuning actuator. MFCs offer significant advantages over monolithic piezoceramics, including superior flexibility, robustness under high strain, ease of bonding to curved or irregular surfaces, and efficient energy conversion in cantilever configurations due to their interdigitated electrode design that facilitates in-plane poling and minimizes interfacial voltage drop. These characteristics make MFCs particularly well-suited for applications involving large deflections, such as low-frequency vibration harvesting. Complementing the MFC is the IPMC, an electroactive polymer that functions as a soft actuator capable of generating measurable mechanical force and displacement in response to very low driving voltages (1–4 V). IPMCs consist of an ion-exchange polymer membrane—typically Nafion—plated with noble metal electrodes (e.g., platinum or gold) on both surfaces. Upon application of an electric field, hydrated cations within the membrane migrate toward the cathode, dragging water molecules with them and creating an osmotic pressure gradient that results in macroscopic bending. This actuation mechanism consumes minimal current, enabling the IPMC to be powered directly from the harvester’s own output without compromising net energy gain. The synergy between MFC and IPMC allows the creation of a self-sustaining, adaptive energy harvester: the MFC converts ambient vibrations into electricity, while a fraction of that electricity powers the IPMC to tune the system’s resonance, maximizing future energy capture. This closed-loop autonomy represents a significant advancement over prior art, which either lacked tunability or required external power for active adjustment.

### Example 1

Referring now to PVEH device 100, a first exemplary embodiment comprises a bimodal aluminum cantilever beam consisting of a thicker root section (0.64 mm thick, 15 cm × 3.5 cm) and a thinner extension (0.37 mm thick, 10.5 cm × 2 cm) with a 3 cm overlap region to provide graded stiffness. Bonded to the upper surface of the root section in a unimorph configuration is an MFC transducer (Model M8514 P2), featuring an active area of 85 mm × 14 mm, a free strain of 630 ppm, and a blocking force of 76 N. An IPMC tuning member, formed of two rectangular strips (each 30 mm × 5 mm × 0.2 mm) joined at their tips in a diamond-shaped stack, is mounted perpendicular to the beam width at a location 14 cm from the fixed end. In its resting state (0 V applied), the IPMC remains disengaged from the cantilever. When a voltage (e.g., 1.5 V) is applied across the IPMC electrodes, the strips bend inward, contacting the beam and applying a localized compressive load. This load effectively increases the beam’s axial stiffness, thereby raising its resonant frequency. The magnitude of the frequency shift is proportional to the applied IPMC voltage, allowing precise tuning. Experimental validation demonstrated that with no IPMC actuation, device 100 resonated at 5.9 Hz with a power output of 29 µW. Application of 1.5 V to the IPMC shifted resonance to 7.57 Hz and increased power output to 52.03 µW, while consuming only 0.765 µW—yielding a net gain of over 22 µW. Higher voltages (up to 2.1 V) produced further shifts up to 8.0 Hz with correspondingly higher net power, confirming the efficacy of the tuning mechanism.

### Example 2

PVEH device 200 represents a second embodiment designed to investigate the influence of actuator placement geometry on tuning performance. Device 200 employs a different MFC transducer (Model M8507 P2; 85 mm × 7 mm active area, 605 ppm free strain, 38 N blocking force) bonded to a similar aluminum cantilever substrate. The key distinction lies in the mounting arrangement of the IPMC actuator: rather than a fixed contact point, the IPMC is affixed to an adjustable fixture that varies the initial gap distance (denoted “h”) between the IPMC tip and the cantilever surface, ranging from 10 mm to 30 mm. During operation, a constant 1.5 V is applied to the IPMC, causing it to bend and strike the beam upon sufficient deflection. Experiments revealed that smaller gap distances (e.g., h = 10 mm) resulted in earlier and more forceful contact during beam oscillation, producing larger resonant frequency shifts—from 8.4 Hz (no actuation) to 9.9 Hz at h = 10 mm—compared to minimal shifts at h = 30 mm (8.6 Hz). Although power output slightly decreased at smaller gaps due to damping effects, the net harvested energy remained positive across all tested configurations, demonstrating that spatial positioning of the IPMC provides an additional design parameter for tailoring the tuning range and sensitivity of the harvester.

### Example 3

Energy harvesting system 300 illustrates a fully integrated, autonomous implementation of the invention. System 300 includes a PVEH device equipped with an MFC transducer and an IPMC tuning member, coupled to a control circuit comprising a full-wave bridge (FWB) rectifier built from four IN4001 diodes and a 220 µF smoothing capacitor. The rectified DC output is directed to both an energy storage unit (e.g., a rechargeable thin-film battery or supercapacitor) and a low-power microcontroller. The microcontroller continuously monitors the AC voltage waveform from the MFC to infer the ambient vibration frequency—using either peak detection or fast Fourier transform algorithms—and consults an embedded lookup table correlating frequency with optimal IPMC drive voltage. Based on this analysis, the controller adjusts the voltage supplied to the IPMC via a digital-to-analog converter or pulse-width modulation driver, thereby dynamically tuning the harvester’s resonance. In this configuration, the IPMC strips function as a tunable load member whose effective stiffness is modulated by the control signal. Because the IPMC’s power consumption is consistently lower than the incremental harvesting gain, system 300 achieves positive net energy accumulation even during active tuning, enabling indefinite operation without external intervention. This closed-loop architecture is particularly advantageous for structural health monitoring nodes deployed on bridges, aircraft, or industrial equipment where vibration characteristics evolve over time.

### Example 4

Illustrative dimensions and geometries of PVEH devices according to the invention are provided to guide practical implementation. In one representative configuration, the cantilever beam measures between 20 cm and 30 cm in total length, with cross-sectional widths of 2–4 cm and thicknesses graded from 0.3 mm to 0.7 mm to optimize low-frequency response. The MFC transducer occupies 40–60% of the beam length near the fixed end, with active areas ranging from 70 mm × 7 mm to 85 mm × 14 mm depending on power requirements. IPMC actuators are fabricated as thin strips (0.1–0.3 mm thick) with lengths of 20–40 mm and widths of 3–6 mm, allowing easy integration into compact housings. Stacked IPMC configurations—where two or more strips are connected mechanically in series or parallel—can be employed to amplify displacement or force output without increasing drive voltage. Electrode connections to both MFC and IPMC are made via flexible printed circuits or wire bonds to accommodate motion. These dimensional parameters are not limiting but serve as exemplary values that have demonstrated effective operation in laboratory prototypes targeting ambient vibrations in the 5–15 Hz range, common in civil infrastructure and rotating machinery.

### Example 5

Resonance frequency tuning in PVEH devices is achieved through controlled modification of the cantilever’s effective stiffness via IPMC-induced loading. When the IPMC is actuated, it applies a force that introduces a compressive preload or impact load at a specific location along the beam. According to Euler-Bernoulli beam theory, such a load alters the boundary conditions and internal stress state, thereby changing the natural frequency. Experimental data from Configuration 1 show a near-linear relationship between IPMC drive voltage and resonant frequency shift: from 5.9 Hz at 0 V to 8.0 Hz at 2.1 V, a span of 2.1 Hz achieved with less than 6 µW of actuation power. Similarly, in Configuration 2, varying the IPMC-beam gap distance “h” from 30 mm to 10 mm shifted resonance from 8.6 Hz to 9.9 Hz under constant 1.5 V drive. These results confirm that both electrical (voltage) and mechanical (position) parameters can be exploited to achieve continuous, reversible tuning across a useful bandwidth. The tuning speed is limited only by the IPMC’s hydration-dependent ion transport kinetics, typically on the order of hundreds of milliseconds—sufficient for most quasi-static environmental changes. Importantly, the frequency shift is repeatable and stable over multiple actuation cycles, provided the IPMC remains adequately hydrated, a condition easily maintained in sealed or humidified enclosures.

### Example 6

A method of energy harvesting according to the invention comprises the following steps: (a) providing a piezoelectric vibration energy harvester comprising a cantilever beam with a piezoelectric transducer and an IPMC tuning member disposed adjacent thereto; (b) exposing the harvester to ambient mechanical vibrations; (c) measuring the electrical output of the piezoelectric transducer to determine the dominant frequency of the ambient vibrations; (d) applying a control voltage to the IPMC tuning member based on the measured frequency, wherein the control voltage causes the IPMC to deform and apply a mechanical load to the cantilever beam, thereby shifting the resonant frequency of the harvester toward the ambient vibration frequency; and (e) converting the mechanical energy of the tuned vibrations into electrical energy via the piezoelectric transducer. The control voltage is selected such that the power consumed by the IPMC is less than the incremental power gained by improved resonance matching, ensuring positive net energy yield. In an autonomous implementation, steps (c) through (d) are executed continuously by an onboard microcontroller powered by the harvester itself, enabling real-time adaptation to changing environmental conditions without user intervention.
