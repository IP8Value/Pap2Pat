# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

### Field of the Invention

The present invention relates generally to the field of medical device testing, and more particularly to methods and systems for evaluating the mechanical and geometric properties of elastomeric medical devices such as breast implants under compressive loading. Specifically, the invention provides a simplified, reproducible, and accurate methodology for determining key engineering parameters—including implant geometry, multi-dimensional strain, and tangent moduli—based on load-displacement data obtained during quasi-equilibrium compression between parallel plates. The invention further enables comprehensive characterization of shape stability, which correlates with clinical performance metrics such as capsular contracture rates, rupture resistance, and fold flaw susceptibility.

### Description of Related Art

Current breast implant testing protocols, as recommended by regulatory bodies such as the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA), rely heavily on standardized mechanical evaluations derived from ASTM and ISO standards. These include tensile strength, ultimate elongation, tear resistance, joint integrity, cyclic fatigue endurance, and ultimate strength assessments. While these tests provide valuable baseline data for comparing implant designs and evaluating explanted devices, they do not capture the full mechanical response of an implant under compressive loads that simulate in vivo conditions. In particular, existing methodologies fail to quantify the overall geometric deformation—or shape change—of the entire implant when subjected to compression, despite evidence suggesting that such deformation influences critical clinical outcomes including capsular contracture, shell fatigue, and long-term durability.

Conventional compression testing of breast implants typically involves placing the implant between two flat, parallel platens and applying a controlled load, often in air and at room temperature. This approach is widely used for cyclic fatigue and ultimate strength testing and is amenable to laboratory implementation. However, prior art methods treat the resulting load-displacement curves as empirical endpoints without deriving intrinsic geometric or mechanical properties of the implant itself. Consequently, comparisons between implant types remain qualitative or limited to failure thresholds, rather than providing insight into how an implant deforms, stabilizes, or interacts with surrounding tissue under load.

A significant limitation of current methods is their inability to decouple implant-specific mechanical behavior from experimental artifacts such as platen-implant friction. Most testing is conducted with dry, polished stainless-steel plates, which may introduce artificial resistance to lateral expansion during compression. This frictional constraint can inflate apparent stiffness and mask true implant compliance, leading to non-physiological characterizations. Moreover, no standardized technique exists to compute real-time geometric parameters—such as diameter, surface area, or contact profile—from continuous load and displacement measurements during dynamic compression.

There remains a clear need for a simplified, yet comprehensive, testing methodology that bridges the gap between empirical load-displacement data and meaningful engineering descriptors of implant behavior. Such a method should enable calculation of geometric properties and mechanical moduli without requiring complex imaging, internal pressure sensors, or post-test dissection. It should also accommodate both static and dynamic loading regimes while validating the assumption that dynamic compression approximates a series of equilibrium states—a so-called quasi-equilibrium process. Furthermore, the method must be applicable across diverse implant architectures, including single-lumen saline, cohesive silicone gel, and multi-lumen structured designs like the IDEAL IMPLANT.

The potential benefits of such an innovation are substantial. By quantifying shape stability through multi-dimensional strains and tangent moduli, manufacturers and regulators could better predict clinical performance, optimize implant design, and correlate mechanical behavior with adverse events such as capsular contracture or fold-induced shell failure. Additionally, the ability to test under both dry and lubricated conditions would allow simulation of varying degrees of interfacial slip, mimicking the range of in vivo environments—from adherent capsules to fluid-lubricated pockets—thereby enhancing the physiological relevance of laboratory data.

## OBJECTS OF THE INVENTION

It is an object of the present invention to provide a simplified means to determine the geometric properties of a breast implant undergoing compressive loading, including but not limited to its diameter, surface area, and projection, using only experimentally measurable parameters such as plate spacing and applied load. It is another object to provide a method for determining the engineering mechanical properties of a breast implant, specifically multi-dimensional strains and corresponding tangent moduli, which serve as quantitative indicators of shape stability under load. A further object is to establish and validate a quasi-equilibrium assumption that permits the use of dynamic compression data—collected continuously during automated testing—to accurately infer instantaneous geometric and mechanical states of the implant, thereby eliminating the need for time-consuming manual measurements at discrete load steps.

Yet another object is to provide a means to assess the impact of design changes—such as variations in lumen structure, fill material, or shell thickness—on overall implant mechanics through comparative analysis of strain and modulus profiles. Additionally, it is an object to enable comprehensive evaluation of breast implant properties across a spectrum of interfacial conditions, including both dry and lubricated platen surfaces, to better approximate the variable frictional environments encountered in vivo. Collectively, these objects aim to transform conventional compression testing from a purely empirical durability assessment into a rich source of biomechanical insight relevant to both product development and clinical safety.

## BRIEF SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention introduces a novel methodology for characterizing the geometric and mechanical response of breast implants and other elastomeric medical devices subjected to compressive loading between parallel plates. Central to the invention is the adoption of a quasi-equilibrium assumption, which posits that during slow, continuous compression, the implant passes through a sequence of near-equilibrium states, allowing dynamic load-displacement data to be treated as equivalent to static measurements for the purpose of geometric and mechanical analysis.

The method begins with the measurement of load and plate spacing (i.e., implant projection) during a controlled compression test, typically performed using a computerized load frame such as an Instron system. From these primary measurements, and using a known or pre-measured implant volume, the invention computes key geometric properties—including implant diameter and external surface area—via a validated composite geometric model that approximates the compressed implant as a combination of a central cylindrical “puck” and an outer half-torus. This model yields closed-form equations that express diameter and surface area solely as functions of plate spacing and volume, enabling real-time calculation during dynamic testing.

Using the computed geometry, the invention then derives engineering strains in three dimensions: projection strain (change in height), diametric strain (change in width), and areal strain (change in total external surface area). These strains provide a complete description of implant shape change under load. Furthermore, by normalizing load with respect to the initial planform area, the invention defines an engineering compressive stress, which, when paired with strain, allows computation of local tangent moduli—the instantaneous slope of the stress-strain curve—as quantitative measures of shape stability.

The invention is particularly suited for application to breast implants, including saline-filled, silicone gel-filled, and multi-lumen structured designs, but is equally applicable to any elastomeric medical device that undergoes compressive deformation, such as tissue expanders, testicular prostheses, or soft tissue fillers. A key benefit of the invention is its simplicity: it requires only standard compression equipment and eliminates the need for auxiliary instrumentation. Despite this simplicity, the method achieves high accuracy, with geometric predictions validated to within ±2% of manual measurements for loads above 5 N, and excellent reproducibility, demonstrated by relative standard deviations below 0.6%.

In summary, the invention provides a robust, scalable, and physiologically informative framework for evaluating how elastomeric implants deform under load, offering unprecedented insight into shape stability—a property increasingly linked to clinical outcomes such as capsular contracture, rupture resistance, and patient satisfaction.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates to breast implants and other elastomeric medical devices that undergo dynamic compression between parallel plates, simulating mechanical challenges encountered in vivo. Breast implants suitable for evaluation by the disclosed method include those constructed from biocompatible elastomers such as cross-linked polydimethylsiloxane (silicone), filled with either saline, silicone gel, or structured multi-lumen configurations containing baffles or nested shells. The invention employs a load frame apparatus, such as an Instron 5500R equipped with a load cell (e.g., 1 kN capacity) and automated data acquisition system, to apply controlled compressive forces while simultaneously recording displacement.

The upper and lower platens are typically fabricated from polished stainless steel with a surface roughness (Ra) of approximately 0.18 micrometers, though alternative materials and finishes may be employed. Plate spacing, defined as the distance between the opposing platens and equivalent to the implant’s projection (H), is precisely controlled via cross-head movement. A dynamic load program is implemented by moving the cross-head at a constant speed—typically 25.4 cm/minute—to apply a continuously increasing load from an initial preload (e.g., 0.445 N) to a maximum force (e.g., 534 N), covering the range of physiological and supra-physiological loads experienced during activities such as mammography, CPR, or vigorous exercise.

The force applied to the implant is measured in real time by a load cell, and both force (F) and plate spacing (H) are recorded digitally throughout the test. The process begins with calibration of the load frame (step 10) to ensure measurement accuracy. A sterilized, commercial-grade breast implant is then manufactured and loaded according to standard protocols (step 12), and its intrinsic properties—including fill volume, mass, and average shell thickness—are recorded (step 14). The implant is placed centrally between the platens, and the upper platen is lowered until a minimal preload of 0.445 N is achieved, establishing the initial geometric state (step 16). Optionally, the platen surfaces may be lubricated with a thin layer of silicone oil (e.g., DPDM 400) to reduce interfacial friction (step 18).

The dynamic load program is then executed (step 20), and F and H are continuously recorded (step 22). Using these data and the known implant volume (V), the invention computes the implant’s instantaneous geometry and mechanical properties (step 24). The geometric model assumes the compressed implant consists of a central cylindrical region (36) of height H and diameter d, combined with an outer half-torus (38) representing the curved perimeter. Volume conservation yields a quadratic equation in d, solved to give:

d = (1/2)[−(πH)/2 + √((16/π)(V/H) + ((π²/4) − (8/3))H²)]

From this, the total implant diameter is calculated as D = H + d, under the assumption that the perimeter curvature approximates a semicircle of diameter H. Substituting d into the expression for surface area gives:

A = (πd²)/2 + (π²dH)/2 + πH²

which simplifies to a function of D and H, and ultimately of H alone via the diameter equation.

Engineering strains are then computed as percentage changes from initial values: εH = −(H − Ho)/Ho × 100%, εD = (D − Do)/Do × 100%, and εA = (A − Ao)/Ao × 100%. Compressive stress is defined as S = F/Apo, where Apo = πDo²/4 is the initial planform area. Tangent moduli are derived as local slopes: ESH = ΔS/ΔεH, ESD = ΔS/ΔεD, and ESA = ΔS/ΔεA.

Validation studies confirm that the quasi-equilibrium assumption holds, as dynamic H(F) curves closely match static measurements (average differences <1%). The geometric model predicts D within ±2% for loads >5 N. Shape stability is quantified by lower strains and higher moduli; for example, the IDEAL IMPLANT exhibits 6–42% lower diametric strain than gel implants at 100–500 N, depending on lubrication.

### First Invention Embodiment

In a first embodiment, load-displacement measurements were obtained for three implant types—IDEAL IMPLANT, Allergan Natrelle INSPIRA cohesive gel, and Allergan Natrelle INSPIRA gel—under dry platen conditions. Static and dynamic H(F) curves showed excellent agreement, validating the quasi-equilibrium assumption. Implant #1 (IDEAL IMPLANT, 425 cc) and Implant #2 (cohesive gel, 420 cc) exhibited similar projections up to 100 N, beyond which the IDEAL IMPLANT maintained greater height, indicating superior resistance to flattening.

### Second Invention Embodiment

In a second embodiment, implant diameter was calculated from dynamic H measurements using the geometric model. Quasi-equilibrium was again validated by comparing calculated D to static measurements. For Implant #1 (IDEAL IMPLANT) and Implant #2 (gel implant, 410 cc), calculated diameters matched measured values within 2.5% and 1.1%, respectively, over 22–534 N. The IDEAL IMPLANT consistently displayed smaller diameters at equivalent loads, confirming enhanced shape retention.

### Third Invention Embodiment

A third embodiment focused on diametric strain. Three implants—Implant #1 (IDEAL IMPLANT), Implant #2 (cohesive gel), and Implant #3 (gel)—were tested. At 300 N with lubricated platens, εD was 18% for the IDEAL IMPLANT versus 28% and 26% for the gel implants, demonstrating significantly lower lateral expansion and thus greater shape stability.

### Fourth Invention Embodiment

In a fourth embodiment, planform stress (S = F/Apo) was computed. Given nearly identical initial planform areas (~108 cm²), stress profiles mirrored load trends. However, when plotted against strain, stress revealed intrinsic differences: the IDEAL IMPLANT required higher stress to achieve the same strain as gel implants, confirming greater mechanical resistance to deformation.

### Fifth Invention Embodiment

A fifth embodiment determined engineering moduli. For Implant #1, #2, and #3, tangent moduli diverged above 10–25% strain, with the IDEAL IMPLANT exhibiting up to 130% higher areal modulus at 40% strain under lubricated conditions. This quantifies the transition to a stiffer response at higher deformations, a hallmark of structural reinforcement in multi-lumen designs.

### Additional Embodiments

The invention contemplates application to other elastomeric devices, including tissue expanders and penile prostheses. Multi-lumen structures with nested shells and baffles are explicitly supported. The method is adaptable to cyclic fatigue testing at frequencies of 0.1–5 Hz and cross-head speeds of 1–100 mm/min. Data acquisition systems sample at ≥10 Hz over force ranges of 0.1–1000 N. Contoured platens may replace flat ones to simulate anatomical loading. Software integration automates computation of geometry and moduli. Testing may be performed with or without lubrication to assess friction sensitivity. Anatomically shaped (teardrop) implants are characterized by adapting the geometric model to asymmetric profiles. Platen materials include aluminum, titanium, or polymers, with surface finishes from Ra = 0.05 to 2.0 μm. Elastomeric devices may be fabricated from silicone, polyurethane, hydrogels, or thermoplastic elastomers.
