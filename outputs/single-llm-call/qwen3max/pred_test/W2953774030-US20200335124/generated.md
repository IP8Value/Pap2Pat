# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

Chatbots, or conversational systems, have become increasingly prevalent across a wide range of industries, serving as automated interfaces for customer support, sales, service delivery, and internal enterprise operations. These systems simulate human-like dialogue by interpreting user inputs—typically in the form of natural language—and generating contextually appropriate responses based on predefined logic or learned models. As organizations scale their deployment of such systems, often managing hundreds or thousands of distinct chatbots simultaneously, the need arises not only to monitor individual bot performance but also to understand broader behavioral patterns across the entire ecosystem of deployed bots. Traditional approaches to chatbot evaluation focus on isolated metrics such as response accuracy, latency, or user satisfaction scores derived from post-interaction surveys. However, these methods often fail to capture systemic issues related to bot design, structural inefficiencies, or conversational failures that may recur across multiple bots or domains. Moreover, distinguishing between bots actively engaged with real users versus those used solely for testing or development remains a non-trivial challenge, particularly when test conversations mimic realistic user behavior. Similarly, identifying problematic or “egregious” conversations—those in which the bot fails so severely that human intervention would be necessary—is critical for targeted quality improvement but difficult to automate without a robust representation of both bot structure and conversational dynamics. Consequently, there exists a pressing need for analytical frameworks capable of generating high-level, generalizable representations of chatbots that encode both their architectural characteristics and their interactive behavior during real-world usage. Such representations would enable scalable classification tasks, including the detection of production-ready bots and the identification of conversations requiring urgent remediation, thereby facilitating data-driven optimization of conversational AI platforms at scale.

## SUMMARY

The present invention introduces a novel framework for analyzing conversational systems through the generation and application of bot embeddings, referred to herein as BOT2VEC. In various embodiments, the invention provides methods and systems for constructing content-based and structure-based representations of chatbots, which are then used to train neural network models capable of performing classification tasks relevant to bot management and quality assurance. The motivation for bot analysis stems from the operational complexity introduced by large-scale deployments of heterogeneous chatbots across commercial platforms, where manual inspection is infeasible and conventional metrics lack sufficient granularity. Bot embeddings, as defined herein, are vectorized representations that encapsulate either the linguistic content of conversations handled by a bot (content-based) or the dynamic traversal patterns observed during interactions with users relative to the bot’s underlying graph structure (structure-based). These embeddings enable the detection of real, production-deployed bots by distinguishing them from bots used exclusively for testing or debugging, even when simulated conversations closely resemble authentic user interactions. Additionally, the invention facilitates the detection of problematic or egregious conversations—those exhibiting severe failures in intent recognition, dialog coherence, or task completion—by leveraging the learned bot representations to contextualize individual conversation instances within the broader behavioral profile of the bot. To achieve this, a neural network model is trained using conversation-level inputs mapped to bot identifiers, such that the resulting output embedding layer yields a fixed-dimensional vector for each bot that reflects its functional and structural properties. The system embodiment includes a computing infrastructure configured to receive a chatbot graph, analyze said graph to extract structural features, generate both content-based and structure-based representations of conversations occurring within the bot, and apply trained classifiers to perform diagnostic analytics. A computer program product is further disclosed, comprising computer-readable program instructions stored on a tangible medium that, when executed, cause a processor to carry out the steps of receiving a chatbot graph, generating representations, training a neural network, and performing classification tasks. In summary, the method encompasses: receiving a directed graph representation of a chatbot; modeling user utterances and bot responses to create a content-based vector; mapping conversation paths onto a standardized bin vector to produce a structure-based representation; training a neural network using these representations to learn bot-specific embeddings; and applying the trained model to classify bots as production or non-production and conversations as egregious or non-egregious.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

Conversational systems, commonly known as chatbots, are software applications designed to engage in dialogue with human users through text or voice interfaces. These systems are widely employed in customer service, technical support, e-commerce, healthcare triage, and internal enterprise workflows, replacing or augmenting human agents in routine interactions. As the adoption of such systems grows, so does the necessity for systematic analysis of their behavior, not only to ensure individual bot efficacy but also to derive insights applicable across entire bot portfolios managed on commercial platforms. The analysis of conversational systems is motivated by the need to identify underperforming bots, detect anomalous usage patterns, prioritize improvement efforts, and validate deployment status—tasks that become increasingly complex as the number of bots scales into the thousands. Central to this analysis is the representation of bot structure, which in most commercial platforms takes the form of a directed graph encoding the logical flow of conversation. This graph-based paradigm allows developers to define intents, responses, and decision pathways, making it a natural substrate for computational analysis. The classification task for bot analysis involves determining whether a given bot is actively serving real users (a “production” bot) or is confined to development/testing environments, as well as assessing whether specific conversations exhibit critical failures warranting immediate attention.  

The system overview encompasses a voice response system integrated with backend analytics modules, operating on a general-purpose computer system equipped with memory, processing units, and network connectivity. Within this architecture, bots are implemented as executable graph structures governed by voice response software that interprets user inputs and navigates the graph accordingly. Each bot is defined by a set of nodes and edges, where nodes represent potential user intents paired with optional bot replies, and edges dictate control flow based on classification outcomes. The bot overview reveals that these graphs consist of root nodes initiating conversations, intermediate nodes evaluating user utterances against predefined intents, and special sink nodes handling unrecognized inputs. Graph components include user intent classifiers, positive and negative edges indicating successful or failed intent matches, and jump nodes enabling non-linear navigation. Node structure comprises an intent definition, an optional response template, and outgoing edge references. Positive edges activate upon correct intent classification, advancing the conversation along specialized sub-dialogs, while negative edges route control to alternative intent checks at the same decision level. Graph execution begins at the root node and proceeds via sequential evaluation of user utterances, with each turn generating a path through the graph based on classifier outputs.  

A representative conversation example illustrates how a user query about a headset issue traverses nodes for payment, account operations, and store information before matching the “Technical problem” intent, leading to a follow-up question about the headset model. Node classification determines whether an utterance aligns with a node’s intent, while node traversal follows positive or negative edges accordingly. Special sink nodes, such as “Anything Else,” serve as fallback handlers for out-of-scope queries, triggering default recovery messages. Graph notations standardize node coordinates using depth (number of positive edges from root) and level (set of nodes at a given depth), with width defined as the maximum number of vertically connected nodes at any level. The depth and width of the graph influence its capacity to handle complex, multi-turn dialogues versus simple Q&A scenarios. Bot behavior is thus intrinsically linked to graph topology: shallow, wide graphs suit broad but shallow interactions, whereas deep, narrow graphs support intricate transactional flows. Characteristics of the bot graph—including node count, depth distribution, jump frequency, and sink utilization—collectively encode the bot’s functional scope and interaction style.  

The process of operation begins with the generation of representations of the bot graph. To create a content-based representation, a vocabulary is built from all terms across conversations, excluding identity-revealing tokens like URLs or bot names, and retaining only the top-k most frequent terms. A vector template is defined using term frequency (TF) weighting, and separate vectors are computed for user utterances and bot responses within a conversation, which are then concatenated to form the final content-based input. For the structure-based representation, a common bin vector is constructed with sections corresponding to graph levels and bins accommodating nodes within those levels, sized according to average bot graph dimensions across the dataset. Nodes are mapped to bin vectors via an algorithm that assigns coordinates to standardized bins. Utterance modeling distinguishes between success nodes (last non-sink node in a path), failure nodes (sink nodes), regular nodes (intermediate path nodes), and uninvolved nodes (not traversed). Each bin maintains four counters tracking occurrences of these node types per utterance. Conversation modeling aggregates bin vectors across all utterances in a conversation by summing counters, thereby capturing patterns such as successful resolution frequency, failure concentration, and traversal diversity. An example shows how multiple turns contribute to a cumulative bin vector reflecting the conversation’s structural footprint.  

Neural network model training leverages these representations to learn discriminative bot embeddings. The content-based model processes concatenated TF vectors through fully connected layers, augmented with interaction features like squared differences and element-wise products. The structure-based model ingests the 616-dimensional bin vector (154 bins × 4 counters) through two hidden layers. Both architectures use ReLU activations, dropout regularization (rate 0.5), and Adam optimization (learning rate 0.001), with output layers sized to the number of bots. Training uses cross-entropy loss to predict bot identity from conversation inputs, yielding bot-specific embedding vectors from the output weight matrix. These embeddings support two primary analytics tasks: detecting real (production) bots and detecting egregious conversations. Ground truth for production status is established via expert annotation of sampled conversations, with consensus labeling yielding 40 production bots out of 92. A baseline SVM model using 17 handcrafted features (e.g., unique utterances, turn statistics) achieves moderate performance, while BOT2VEC-S improves F1-score by 18.6%. For egregious conversation detection, ground truth comes from annotating 1,200 conversations across 12 production bots, with inter-annotator agreement (κ=0.93). Extending the state-of-the-art EGR model with BOT2VEC embeddings, BOT2VEC-S delivers a 16.4% relative F1 improvement. Structure-based analysis further reveals domain clustering: bots in IT, HR, and banking exhibit lower average cosine distances (0.614) within domains than across domains (0.694), validating semantic coherence in embeddings.  

The computer system architecture includes input/output circuitry for user and network interaction, a network adapter for data ingestion from bot platforms, and memory storing program instructions and datasets. Neural network training routines orchestrate data loading, forward/backward passes, and parameter updates. Content-based representation routines handle vocabulary construction, masking, and vectorization, while structure-based routines manage graph parsing, coordinate mapping, and bin vector aggregation. Classification routines implement inference for real bot detection and egregious conversation identification. Neural network models are instantiated with specified layer dimensions and activation functions. A database management system stores bot graphs, conversation logs, and embeddings, enhanced with data skipping indexes for efficient querying. Filters and routines preprocess inputs and post-process outputs. The operating system supports multi-processor, multi-tasking, and multi-process computing to parallelize representation generation and model training. The computer program product comprises a computer-readable storage medium encoded with instructions that, when executed, perform all disclosed methods. These instructions may exist as assembler code, instruction-set-architecture commands, machine instructions, microcode, firmware, or state-setting data, enabling deployment across diverse hardware environments via network transmission or local installation.
