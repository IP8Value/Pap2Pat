# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

Human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) is a retrovirus that causes acquired immunodeficiency syndrome (AIDS), a condition characterized by progressive failure of the immune system and susceptibility to life-threatening opportunistic infections and cancers. Since its emergence in the early 1980s, HIV has infected over 75 million people globally, with approximately 38 million individuals living with HIV as of recent estimates. The advent of combination antiretroviral therapy (cART) has transformed HIV/AIDS from a uniformly fatal disease into a manageable chronic condition. cART typically involves a regimen of at least three antiretroviral agents from two or more drug classes, including nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitors (NRTIs), non-nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitors (NNRTIs), protease inhibitors (PIs), integrase strand transfer inhibitors (INSTIs), and entry inhibitors. When administered consistently and effectively, cART suppresses viral replication to undetectable levels, restores immune function, reduces AIDS-related morbidity and mortality, and prevents transmission of HIV to others. Despite these remarkable advances, challenges remain, including the emergence of drug resistance mutations, long-term toxicities, adherence burdens, and the inability of current regimens to eradicate the latent viral reservoir. Consequently, there is a persistent need for novel antivirals with improved pharmacokinetic profiles, higher barriers to resistance, and activity against multidrug-resistant strains.

The HIV-1 capsid protein (CA) has emerged as a compelling target for next-generation antiretroviral therapeutics. CA is a structural protein derived from the proteolytic cleavage of the Gag polyprotein during viral maturation. It consists of two independently folded domains: an N-terminal domain (CA-NTD) comprising seven α-helices and a C-terminal domain (CA-CTD) containing four α-helices and a short 310-helix, connected by a flexible linker of approximately five amino acid residues. In the mature infectious virion, CA self-assembles into a conical capsid core composed of approximately 250 hexameric and 12 pentameric subunits, forming a protective shell around the viral RNA genome and associated enzymes. This capsid core is not merely a passive container but plays active roles throughout the viral lifecycle. Following fusion of the viral and host cell membranes, the intact or partially intact capsid core is released into the cytoplasm, where it undergoes a tightly regulated process of disassembly known as uncoating. Uncoating is temporally linked to reverse transcription and is essential for the formation of the preintegration complex (PIC). The capsid core also facilitates nuclear entry of the PIC in non-dividing cells by interacting with components of the nuclear pore complex, such as nucleoporin 153 (NUP153) and cleavage and polyadenylation specificity factor 6 (CPSF6). Moreover, evidence suggests that the capsid remains associated with the viral DNA during nuclear import and may influence the site selection for integration into the host genome. During the late phase of replication, newly synthesized Gag polyproteins assemble at the plasma membrane, and subsequent proteolytic processing triggers capsid core formation, a process termed maturation. Disruption of any of these CA-dependent steps—uncoating, nuclear entry, integration, or assembly—can potently inhibit viral replication.

Given its multifaceted roles, CA represents a high-value target for antiviral intervention. Small molecule inhibitors that bind CA can either prevent proper capsid assembly (assembly inhibitors) or destabilize the mature capsid prematurely (disassembly accelerators). Several classes of CA inhibitors have been reported, including benzodiazepines (BD), benzimidazoles (BM), pyrrolopyrazolones (e.g., BI-2), CAP-1, PF74, and the recently disclosed GS-CA1 and GS-6207. These compounds bind to distinct or overlapping pockets on the CA surface, often at inter-protomer interfaces. For example, PF74, BI-2, CPSF6, and NUP153 all bind to a pocket formed between the NTD and CTD of adjacent CA monomers within the hexamer. Resistance mutations mapping to this pocket (e.g., Q67H, N74D, A105S) validate its functional importance. Other inhibitors, such as CAP-1 and the BD/BM series, bind to a site primarily within the NTD, while Ebselen targets cysteine residues in the CTD. Despite promising in vitro activity, many early CA inhibitors suffered from poor pharmacokinetics, low potency, or rapid resistance development. The discovery of GS-CA1 and its clinical analog GS-6207 (Lenacapavir) marked a significant breakthrough, demonstrating picomolar antiviral potency, a high barrier to resistance, and potential for ultra-long-acting dosing (e.g., twice-yearly subcutaneous injection). However, the emergence of resistance, even if rare, underscores the need for additional therapeutic options targeting CA, particularly those with novel chemotypes and mechanisms of action. Peptide-based inhibitors offer a promising alternative, potentially achieving higher specificity and affinity through multivalent interactions with the CA surface. Thus, there remains a critical and unmet need for new antiviral agents that bind HIV-1 capsid with high affinity and disrupt its essential functions in the viral lifecycle.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides novel cyclic or linear peptides that bind specifically and with high affinity to the HIV-1 capsid protein, thereby inhibiting viral replication. These peptides are designed based on structural motifs common to known small-molecule inhibitors of capsid (such as GS-CA1, GS-6207, and PF74) and host-derived peptide ligands (such as fragments of CPSF6 and NUP153) that interact with the same binding pocket on the capsid hexamer. The peptides of the invention comprise a defined sequence of amino acids, wherein specific positions are occupied by hydrophobic or polar uncharged residues that mimic the key pharmacophoric elements of the aforementioned inhibitors. In certain embodiments, the peptide is cyclic, formed by a covalent linkage between the N- and C-termini or between side chains, which confers enhanced stability and binding affinity compared to linear counterparts. The invention further encompasses variants of the core peptide sequence, including conservative substitutions that maintain the overall physicochemical properties and binding capability. Additionally, the peptides may be extended with additional amino acids at the N- or C-terminus to facilitate conjugation, solubilization, or cellular uptake. In other embodiments, the peptides are conjugated to detectable labels (e.g., fluorescent dyes), chelators for radiometal complexation, or radionuclides for diagnostic or therapeutic applications. The peptides bind to a well-defined pocket on the HIV-1 capsid protein, located at the interface between the N-terminal and C-terminal domains of adjacent protomers within the hexameric lattice. This binding pocket is lined by residues including L56, M66, Q67, N74, K70, T107, Y130, R173, and A105, among others. The peptides engage these residues through a combination of hydrophobic interactions, hydrogen bonding, and van der Waals contacts, mimicking the binding mode of natural host factors and synthetic inhibitors. The binding affinity of the peptides for the HIV-1 capsid protein is in the nanomolar range, with certain cyclic peptides exhibiting affinities significantly superior to reference compounds such as PF74. The peptides are typically 7 to 20 amino acids in length and adopt a constrained conformation that optimally presents key side chains for interaction with the capsid surface. By occupying this critical pocket, the peptides of the invention interfere with essential capsid functions, including proper uncoating, nuclear import, and/or viral assembly, leading to potent inhibition of HIV-1 replication.

### Overview

Structural insights into capsid inhibitor binding have been pivotal in the rational design of antivirals. The crystal structure of the HIV-1 capsid hexamer bound to GS-CA1, though not publicly available, has been reported to reveal a binding site overlapping with that of PF74, CPSF6, and NUP153. Computational modeling using the PF74-bound CA hexamer structure (PDB 4XFZ) as a template confirms that GS-CA1 occupies a pocket at the NTD-CTD interface, engaging residues L56, M66, Q67, N74, and A105—mutations at these sites confer resistance to GS-CA1. This binding pocket is highly conserved across HIV-1 subtypes, explaining the broad activity of GS-CA1. GS-6207, a clinical analog of GS-CA1, differs by three fluorine-containing modifications and exhibits slightly improved binding affinity in docking simulations, attributed in part to a reoriented cyclopenta-pyrazole ring and a new hydrogen bond between its amide group and the K70 side chain. Superposition of GS-CA1, PF74, and BI-2 reveals a common binding mode, with aromatic rings occupying equivalent spatial positions, underscoring the importance of this pharmacophore. Similarly, the phenylalanine residues of CPSF6 (F321) and NUP153 (F1417) superpose precisely with the difluorobenzyl moiety of GS-CA1, indicating convergent evolution of binding motifs. In contrast, CAP-1 binds to a distinct site within the NTD, though it shares interactions with M66 and L69. BD and BM inhibitors bind near the CAP-1 site but do not overlap with the GS-CA1 pocket. Coumermycin A1 was predicted to bind at the dimer interface, but docking failed to place it near resistance residues N74 and A105, casting doubt on this model. Other inhibitors like compound 34 bind near P38 and R173—residues also contacted by GS-CA1—while C1 binds at the CypA loop, and Ebselen targets CTD cysteines. Leveraging these structural comparisons, we designed a cyclic peptide, Pep-2-cyclic, incorporating key hydrophobic and polar elements from GS-CA1, PF74, and host peptides. Docking placed Pep-2-cyclic in the canonical NTD-CTD pocket, and experimental validation by microscale thermophoresis confirmed its binding affinity for CA hexamers was approximately 7-fold greater than that of PF74.

### Cyclic Peptides

The cyclic peptides of the invention are characterized by a specific amino acid sequence designed to mimic the binding epitope of capsid inhibitors. The core sequence is defined as X1-X2-X3, where X1 is a hydrophobic amino acid (e.g., Leu, Phe, Trp), X2 is a polar uncharged amino acid (e.g., Gln, Asn, Ser), and X3 is a hydrophobic amino acid (e.g., Met, Leu, Ile). Alternative sequences may include conservative substitutions, such as replacing Leu with Ile or Val, or Gln with Asn. The peptide may be extended with additional amino acids at the N- or C-terminus, such as glycine-serine linkers or cell-penetrating sequences (e.g., TAT). Modifications to the sequence include N-terminal acetylation, C-terminal amidation, or incorporation of non-natural amino acids to enhance stability or affinity. The peptide length typically ranges from 7 to 15 amino acids. Additional components may include polyethylene glycol (PEG) chains for improved pharmacokinetics or lipid tails for membrane anchoring. These cyclic peptides bind specifically to the HIV-1 capsid protein, particularly to the pocket formed at the interface of the N-terminal and C-terminal domains within the hexameric lattice. The binding pocket comprises residues L56, M66, Q67, N74, K70, T107, Y130, R173, and A105. Binding affinity, as measured by microscale thermophoresis, is in the range of 10–100 nM, with preferred embodiments exhibiting Kd values below 50 nM. The peptides exhibit inhibitory activity against HIV-1 replication in cell culture, with EC50 values in the low nanomolar range. The peptides are produced by solid-phase peptide synthesis, followed by cyclization in solution via native chemical ligation or lactam bridge formation. A linear peptide precursor is first synthesized, then cyclized under controlled conditions. Additional modifications, such as fluorescent labeling or biotinylation, may be introduced post-synthesis. Pharmaceutical compositions comprising the cyclic peptide and a pharmaceutically acceptable carrier are provided for administration to subjects. Methods of treatment involve administering an effective amount of the cyclic peptide to a subject infected with HIV-1 to inhibit viral replication.

### Linear Peptides

The linear peptides of the invention share the same core sequence motif as the cyclic peptides: X1-X2-X3, where X1 and X3 are hydrophobic amino acids and X2 is a polar uncharged amino acid. Alternative sequences include conservative substitutions that preserve binding function. These peptides may also be extended with additional amino acids at either terminus for functionalization. Sequence modifications encompass N-terminal acetylation, C-terminal amidation, or use of D-amino acids to resist proteolysis. The typical length is 8 to 20 amino acids. Additional components may include solubilizing tags (e.g., poly-Arg) or targeting moieties. Linear peptides bind to the same HIV-1 capsid pocket as their cyclic counterparts, engaging residues L56, M66, Q67, N74, and others. Binding affinity is generally lower than cyclic versions, ranging from 50 to 500 nM, but optimized sequences can achieve sub-100 nM Kd. They demonstrate antiviral activity in cellular assays, though often requiring higher concentrations than cyclic peptides. Production is via standard solid-phase peptide synthesis. C-terminal modifications include addition of carboxylic acid, amide, or ester groups. Additional modifications may involve phosphorylation or glycosylation. Pharmaceutical compositions are formulated for systemic or localized delivery. Methods of treatment entail administering the linear peptide to an HIV-1-infected subject to suppress viral load. Also provided is an isolated polynucleotide encoding the linear peptide, which can be inserted into a vector for recombinant expression. Suitable vectors include plasmids or viral vectors (e.g., lentiviral, AAV). Host cells for expression include bacterial (E. coli), yeast, insect, or mammalian cells (e.g., HEK293).

## EXAMPLES

### Example 1

The HIV-1 capsid protein structure was prepared for molecular docking using the X-ray crystal structure of the CA hexamer in complex with PF74 (PDB entry 4XFZ). The protein structure was processed using the PrepWizard module of the Schrödinger Suite, which added hydrogen atoms, assigned bond orders, generated probable ionization states of titratable residues at physiological pH, and optimized the orientation of water molecules. The flexible linker region between the N-terminal and C-terminal domains was retained in its native conformation. The resulting structure was energy-minimized using the OPLS3 force field to relieve steric clashes while preserving the overall fold. This prepared structure served as the receptor for all subsequent docking simulations.

### Example 2

Docking simulations of GS-CA1, GS-6207, and Coumermycin A1 were performed using the Induced-Fit Docking (IFD) protocol in the Schrödinger Suite. Ligand structures were generated using ChemSketch and minimized with MacroModel before being processed with LigPrep to generate low-energy conformers and correct ionization states. A docking grid of 36 Å × 36 Å × 36 Å was centered on the PF74 binding site in the prepared CA hexamer structure. The IFD protocol involved initial rigid-receptor docking with Glide, followed by side-chain sampling and backbone refinement in Prime, and final rescoring. The pose with the best IFD score was selected for analysis. GS-CA1 and GS-6207 docked into the canonical NTD-CTD pocket with high scores, while Coumermycin A1 poses did not consistently engage known resistance residues.

### Example 3

The designed cyclic peptide Pep-2-Cyclic was docked into the CA hexamer structure using the same IFD protocol. The peptide structure was generated and minimized using Prime with the MM/GBSA method. Docking was performed with the grid centered on the PF74 site. The best-scoring pose placed the peptide deep within the NTD-CTD pocket, with hydrophobic side chains oriented toward L56, M66, and A105, and polar groups forming hydrogen bonds with Q67 and N74. Independent docking with PatchDock yielded a similar binding mode, confirming the robustness of the prediction.

### Example 4

Peptide Pep-2-cyclic was synthesized by solid-phase peptide synthesis and purified by HPLC. Binding affinities for HIV-1 CA hexamers were determined by microscale thermophoresis (MST). CA hexamers were labeled with Alexa Fluor 647 dye and mixed with increasing concentrations of Pep-2-cyclic or PF74 (1–2000 nM). Reaction mixtures in 2× MST buffer were loaded into capillaries, and thermophoresis was monitored using a Monolith NT.115 instrument. Data were analyzed using MO. Affinity software by fitting to a quadratic binding equation: ΔF = A * ([P] + [CA] + Kd - sqrt(([P] + [CA] + Kd)^2 - 4*[P]*[CA])) / (2*[CA0]), where A is an arbitrary amplitude parameter, Kd is the dissociation constant, [P] and [CA] are free ligand and protein concentrations, and [P0] and [CA0] are total concentrations. The Kd for Pep-2-cyclic was 32 ± 3 nM, while PF74 exhibited a Kd of 212 ± 7 nM, demonstrating a 6.6-fold higher affinity for the designed peptide.
