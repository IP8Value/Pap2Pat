# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

Amyloid diseases constitute a class of debilitating and often fatal neurodegenerative disorders characterized by the accumulation of misfolded proteins that form insoluble aggregates in various tissues, particularly in the brain. These aggregates, commonly referred to as amyloid plaques, are composed of fibrillar protein structures that disrupt normal cellular function and lead to progressive neuronal loss. Among the most well-known and extensively studied amyloid diseases is Alzheimer’s disease (AD), which represents a leading cause of dementia worldwide and imposes a significant burden on healthcare systems and families alike. The pathological hallmarks of AD include extracellular deposits of amyloid-beta (Aβ) peptides and intracellular neurofibrillary tangles composed of hyperphosphorylated tau protein. Historically, the focus of therapeutic intervention has centered on the removal or prevention of amyloid plaque formation, based on the long-standing amyloid cascade hypothesis.

Alzheimer’s disease is a complex and multifactorial disorder whose etiology involves genetic, environmental, and age-related factors. The disease typically manifests in late adulthood and progresses insidiously, with early symptoms including memory impairment, cognitive decline, and behavioral changes that gradually worsen over time. At the molecular level, AD is associated with the abnormal proteolytic processing of the amyloid precursor protein (APP), which results in the production of Aβ peptides of varying lengths. Of these, the 42-amino acid isoform, known as Aβ42, is particularly prone to aggregation and is considered the primary component of amyloid plaques found in the brains of AD patients. While Aβ42 can exist in monomeric form, it readily undergoes conformational changes that promote its self-assembly into oligomeric and fibrillar structures.

The role of the Aβ42 peptide in the pathogenesis of Alzheimer’s disease has been the subject of intense investigation. Initially, it was believed that the large, insoluble amyloid fibrils were the primary toxic species responsible for neuronal dysfunction and death. However, more recent evidence has challenged this view, suggesting instead that smaller, soluble oligomers of Aβ42—particularly low-n assemblies such as dimers, trimers, and tetramers—are the principal neurotoxic agents. These oligomers are capable of disrupting synaptic function, inducing oxidative stress, and triggering inflammatory responses even before the formation of visible plaques. Notably, certain oligomeric forms, such as the 56 kDa dodecamer, have been shown to correlate strongly with cognitive impairment in animal models. This paradigm shift has redirected research efforts toward understanding and targeting the earliest stages of Aβ42 oligomerization, as inhibiting this process may prevent the formation of toxic intermediates and halt disease progression.

Despite significant advances in our understanding of Aβ42 biology, current research faces several limitations. Many existing screening platforms for anti-amyloid compounds rely on in vitro assays that monitor fibril formation rather than the initial oligomerization events. Such approaches may inadvertently identify compounds that stabilize toxic oligomeric intermediates or fail to address the biologically relevant conformations of Aβ42 present in living cells. Moreover, mammalian cell-based models, while physiologically relevant, are often complex, costly, and difficult to scale for high-throughput screening. There is therefore a pressing need for robust, genetically tractable model systems that recapitulate the early stages of Aβ42 oligomerization in a cellular context and provide a functional readout amenable to both chemical and genetic screening.

To address this need, researchers have turned to the yeast Saccharomyces cerevisiae as a powerful model organism for studying protein misfolding and aggregation. Yeast offers numerous advantages, including rapid growth, ease of genetic manipulation, and conservation of fundamental cellular processes such as protein quality control and chaperone-mediated disaggregation. Importantly, yeast naturally harbors prion proteins—self-propagating amyloid aggregates—that serve as excellent models for human amyloidogenic proteins. One such prion is formed by the translational termination factor Sup35p, which underlies the [PSI+] phenotype. The Sup35p protein consists of three domains: an N-terminal prion domain (N), a middle domain (M) of unclear function, and a C-terminal release factor domain (RF) essential for viability. In its prion state ([PSI+]), Sup35p aggregates into amyloid fibers, impairing its ability to terminate translation at stop codons—a phenomenon known as nonsense suppression.

The prion properties of Sup35p are critically dependent on the yeast chaperone Hsp104, a member of the AAA+ ATPase family. Hsp104 functions by fragmenting large Sup35p aggregates into smaller, transmissible seeds, thereby ensuring their propagation to daughter cells during division. Inhibition of Hsp104’s ATPase activity—achieved pharmacologically by guanidine hydrochloride—blocks this fragmentation process, leading to the accumulation of large, non-infectious aggregates and eventual "curing" of the [PSI+] prion. This well-characterized system provides a foundation for modeling other amyloidogenic processes in yeast.

Previous attempts to model Aβ42 aggregation in yeast have yielded mixed results, often failing to capture the formation of soluble, low-n oligomers or lacking a quantitative functional readout. Existing models either focused on late-stage fibril formation or did not integrate the oligomerization event with a measurable cellular phenotype. These limitations hinder the development of effective screening platforms for therapeutics targeting early Aβ42 assembly.

In response to these challenges, the present invention introduces a novel yeast-based model system specifically designed to recapitulate the initial oligomerization of Aβ42. This system leverages the functional sensitivity of the Sup35p RF domain to aggregation-induced inactivation, thereby linking Aβ42 oligomerization to a clear phenotypic output. Central to this model is the creation of a chimeric fusion protein, termed AβMRF, in which the Aβ42 peptide is fused to the M and RF domains of Sup35p (lacking the N-terminal prion domain). Expression of AβMRF in a sup35Δ yeast strain renders the cells dependent on this fusion for viability, while its oligomerization impairs translational termination, resulting in a detectable growth phenotype.

This new model incorporates a sensitive oligomerization assay based on the ability of AβMRF to form SDS-stable low-n oligomers—dimers, trimers, and tetramers—that mirror the toxic species observed in human AD. The system is further refined through the use of point mutations in Aβ42 that disrupt key aggregation interfaces, providing internal controls for specificity. Critically, the model reveals unexpected interactions between Aβ42 oligomers and the chaperone Hsp104, demonstrating that Hsp104 binds to AβMRF and modulates its stability and oligomerization state—an insight not previously appreciated in mammalian systems.

The advantages of this new model are manifold. It is genetically tractable, scalable, and provides a direct link between Aβ42 oligomerization and a quantifiable cellular phenotype (growth on adenine-deficient media and colony color). It enables both chemical and genetic screens for agents that inhibit early Aβ42 assembly without promoting toxic intermediates. Furthermore, the system can be adapted to study other amyloidogenic proteins by substituting the Aβ42 sequence with alternative disease-associated peptides.

Potential applications of this invention extend beyond basic research. It serves as a platform for drug discovery, allowing high-throughput identification of small molecules, peptides, or genetic modifiers that suppress Aβ42 oligomerization. It may also facilitate the study of chaperone networks involved in handling toxic oligomers, offering insights into endogenous neuroprotective mechanisms. Ultimately, this model bridges a critical gap between in vitro biochemistry and in vivo pathology, providing a physiologically relevant yet experimentally accessible system for combating amyloid diseases.

In summary, the background of the invention encompasses the growing recognition of Aβ42 oligomers as key neurotoxic agents in Alzheimer’s disease, the limitations of current research models in capturing early oligomerization events, and the strategic exploitation of yeast prion biology to create a novel, functional assay system that directly links Aβ42 self-assembly to a measurable cellular phenotype, thereby enabling innovative approaches to therapeutic discovery and mechanistic investigation.

## BRIEF SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a novel yeast-based model system and associated methods for studying the oligomerization of the amyloid-beta 42 (Aβ42) peptide and for identifying agents that interfere with this process. At the core of the invention is a genetically engineered yeast strain in which the essential translational termination factor Sup35p is replaced by a chimeric fusion protein, termed AβMRF, comprising the Aβ42 peptide fused to the middle (M) and release factor (RF) domains of Sup35p. This system exploits the sensitivity of the RF domain’s activity to conformational changes induced by Aβ42 oligomerization, thereby converting a molecular aggregation event into a readily observable cellular phenotype.

The yeast model system is constructed in a sup35Δ strain carrying a nonsense mutation in the ADE1 gene (ade1-14), which renders the cells adenine auxotrophs unless translational termination is impaired. When the AβMRF fusion protein oligomerizes via its Aβ42 moiety, its RF domain becomes functionally compromised, leading to increased readthrough of the premature stop codon in ade1-14. This results in two easily scored phenotypes: the ability of cells to grow on medium lacking adenine (-Ade) and the formation of white or pink colonies on rich medium (YPD), as opposed to the red colonies characteristic of cells with fully functional termination activity. Thus, the degree of Aβ42 oligomerization is directly correlated with a quantifiable growth and colorimetric readout.

A critical feature of the invention is the incorporation of the MRF domain—the M and RF domains of Sup35p without the N-terminal prion domain—which ensures that any observed aggregation and phenotypic change is solely attributable to the fused Aβ42 sequence and not to endogenous prion behavior. The MRF domain retains full translational termination activity when monomeric but becomes impaired upon oligomerization of the attached Aβ42 peptide. This design provides a clean and specific reporter for Aβ42 self-assembly.

The invention further includes an oligomerization assay based on the detection of SDS-stable low-n oligomers (dimers, trimers, tetramers) of AβMRF by immunoblotting. These oligomers resist dissociation in SDS at room temperature but disaggregate upon boiling, mirroring the biophysical properties of pathogenic Aβ42 oligomers in human disease. The assay is validated using point mutations in Aβ42—such as F19T/F20T (Aβm1MRF) and F19T/F20T/I31P (Aβm2MRF)—that disrupt key aggregation interfaces and correspondingly reduce oligomer formation and restore RF activity, confirming the specificity of the system.

The invention also discloses the unexpected role of the yeast chaperone Hsp104 in modulating AβMRF stability and oligomerization. Contrary to its role in propagating Sup35p prions, Hsp104 binds to AβMRF and appears to protect it from degradation and disaggregation by other cellular factors. Deletion of HSP104 reduces total AβMRF levels and shifts the equilibrium toward monomers, while treatment with guanidine hydrochloride—a known Hsp104 inhibitor—paradoxically increases AβMRF oligomerization, suggesting that guanidine acts on alternative cellular targets involved in Aβ42 clearance. This insight reveals a novel protective function of Hsp104 in the context of Aβ42 toxicity.

Based on this model, the invention provides robust screening methods for identifying therapeutic agents that inhibit Aβ42 oligomerization. Candidate compounds—including small molecules, peptides, antibodies, antisense oligonucleotides, and genetic modifiers—are tested for their ability to restore red colony color and adenine auxotrophy in AβMRF-expressing yeast, indicating reduced oligomerization and restored RF activity. The system is amenable to high-throughput formats and can be used to screen diverse libraries, including combinatorial chemical libraries, natural product extracts, and genomic or cDNA expression libraries.

The invention contemplates the identification of both proteinaceous and non-proteinaceous compounds that target Aβ42 directly or modulate cellular pathways involved in its handling, such as chaperone networks, proteostasis regulators, or degradation machinery. Potential targets include the Aβ42 peptide itself, its aggregation interfaces, or host factors that influence its folding and stability.

Alternative screening methods include direct biochemical assessment of oligomer levels via immunoblotting or co-immunoprecipitation of interacting partners, such as Hsp104. The system can also be adapted to study other disease-associated aggregated proteins—such as α-synuclein, huntingtin, or TDP-43—by substituting the Aβ42 sequence with the relevant amyloidogenic domain, thereby broadening its applicability to multiple neurodegenerative conditions.

Therapeutic benefits of agents identified by this system include the prevention of toxic oligomer formation, reduction of synaptic dysfunction, and potential slowing or halting of disease progression in amyloid-related disorders. The invention thus provides a pathway to develop disease-modifying therapies rather than merely symptomatic treatments.

Additionally, the invention encompasses specialized yeast strains engineered to express AβMRF or its variants under inducible promoters (e.g., CUP1), facilitating controlled expression and minimizing basal toxicity. These strains may carry additional mutations to enhance drug permeability or reduce metabolic interference, improving the fidelity of compound screening.

Finally, the invention includes kits for practicing the disclosed methods, comprising the engineered yeast strains, growth media, detection reagents, and instructional materials. These kits enable researchers and pharmaceutical developers to implement the screening platform without extensive genetic engineering, accelerating the discovery of novel anti-amyloid therapeutics.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides novel compositions and methods for screening agents that interfere with the oligomerization of the amyloid-beta 42 (Aβ42) peptide, a key pathogenic process in Alzheimer’s disease and related amyloid disorders. The therapeutic benefits of agents identified by these screening methods are substantial, as they hold the potential to prevent the formation of neurotoxic soluble oligomers, thereby preserving neuronal function and slowing disease progression. The invention is grounded in a yeast cell-based assay that uniquely links Aβ42 oligomerization to a functional readout of translational termination efficiency, enabling both qualitative and quantitative assessment of candidate compounds.

The methods of screening for therapeutic agents described herein are specifically designed for diseases associated with protein aggregation, with a primary focus on Alzheimer’s disease but extendable to other conditions such as Parkinson’s disease, Huntington’s disease, and amyotrophic lateral sclerosis. The core of the invention is a yeast cell-based assay that utilizes a genetically modified strain of Saccharomyces cerevisiae in which the endogenous SUP35 gene has been deleted and replaced by a plasmid-encoded chimeric protein, AβMRF, consisting of the Aβ42 peptide fused to the M and RF domains of Sup35p. This assay capitalizes on the fact that oligomerization of Aβ42 impairs the activity of the RF domain, leading to nonsense suppression of the ade1-14 allele and a corresponding change in growth and pigmentation phenotypes.

The components of the yeast cell-based assay include a sup35Δ yeast strain carrying the ade1-14 nonsense mutation, a plasmid expressing the AβMRF fusion protein under the control of an inducible promoter (such as CUP1), and appropriate selective media. The role of Sup35p in this assay is pivotal: its RF domain is essential for viability and for accurate termination of translation at stop codons. When fused to Aβ42 and expressed in the absence of endogenous Sup35p, the functionality of this domain becomes a sensitive reporter for Aβ42 conformational state.

The use of the ade1-14 and ura3-14 alleles as markers provides a dual-selection system. The ade1-14 allele allows phenotypic scoring based on adenine prototrophy and colony color, while ura3-14 (or other auxotrophic markers) can be used for plasmid maintenance or counter-selection in strain construction. Yeast strains with mutations in SUP35 and HSP104 genes are employed to dissect the roles of these factors in Aβ42 handling; for example, hsp104Δ strains reveal the protective role of Hsp104 against AβMRF degradation.

Various yeast strains can be used in the assay, including derivatives of 74D-694, which is well-characterized for prion studies. Limitations of using yeast cells—such as differences in membrane composition affecting drug permeability or divergent metabolic pathways—can be mitigated by introducing mutations that enhance compound uptake or reduce efflux, or by using permeabilizing agents in controlled experiments.

Kits containing the engineered yeast strains, along with detailed protocols and reagents, are provided to facilitate widespread adoption of the screening platform. These kits may include strains expressing AβMRF, Aβm1MRF, and Aβm2MRF as positive and negative controls.

The types of candidate compounds that can be screened are diverse and include small organic molecules, peptides, peptidomimetics, antibodies, nucleic acid-based agents (such as antisense oligonucleotides, ribozymes, and siRNAs), and natural product extracts. Genetic agents, such as cDNA libraries or CRISPR guide RNAs, can also be screened to identify host factors that modulate Aβ42 oligomerization.

Proteins that can be screened include chaperones, proteases, and other interactors that may stabilize monomers or promote degradation of oligomers. Combinatorial libraries—both chemical and biological—are particularly valuable for unbiased discovery. Chemical classes amenable to screening include heterocycles, saccharides, steroids, triterpenoids, and other bioactive scaffolds. Structural identification of hit compounds can guide medicinal chemistry efforts to generate optimized derivatives with improved potency and pharmacokinetic properties.

Small organic compounds with molecular weights less than 2,500 daltons are preferred for drug-like properties. Peptides may be stabilized through the incorporation of unnatural amino acids or by functionalization of the amino or carboxylic terminus via acetylation, amidification, or esterification to enhance metabolic stability and cell permeability.

Natural and synthetically produced libraries, including those derived from microbial fermentation or plant extracts, offer rich sources of bioactive compounds. Combinatorial approaches, such as phage display or mRNA display, can be used to evolve high-affinity binders against Aβ42 oligomers.

The invention thus provides a comprehensive, flexible, and scalable platform for the discovery of therapeutics targeting the earliest and most toxic stages of Aβ42 aggregation, with broad implications for the treatment of neurodegenerative diseases.

### Model of Aβ42 Oligomerization

The activity of Sup35p and its assay in vivo are central to the model of Aβ42 oligomerization disclosed herein. The translational termination function of the RF domain is conveniently monitored using the ade1-14 nonsense allele, which introduces a premature stop codon in the ADE1 gene. In yeast strains expressing fully functional Sup35p (or MRF), translation terminates at this codon, resulting in a truncated, nonfunctional Ade1p enzyme. Consequently, cells cannot synthesize adenine and accumulate a red pigment due to the buildup of an intermediate in the adenine biosynthesis pathway. These cells are adenine auxotrophs (Ade-) and form red colonies on rich medium. However, when the termination activity is compromised—as occurs when Sup35p is in the aggregated [PSI+] prion state or when the AβMRF fusion oligomerizes—readthrough of the stop codon allows production of full-length Ade1p, enabling adenine synthesis. Such cells are adenine prototrophs (Ade+) and form white or pink colonies.

The construction of the AβMRF fusion protein involved fusing the DNA sequence encoding the Aβ42 peptide to the 5' end of the MRF coding sequence, with an HA epitope tag inserted between the M and RF domains for detection and immunoprecipitation. Mutations were introduced into the Aβ42 portion to generate control constructs: Aβm1MRF contains double substitutions (F19T, F20T) disrupting the first aggregation interface, while Aβm2MRF contains an additional I31P substitution, disrupting a second interface. When these constructs were expressed in a sup35Δ ade1-14 strain, AβMRF-expressing cells were white and Ade+, indicating impaired termination activity due to oligomerization. In contrast, Aβm1MRF and Aβm2MRF cells were dark pink and Ade-, demonstrating that the mutations restored RF function by inhibiting oligomerization. These results establish a direct correlation between Aβ42 sequence integrity, oligomerization propensity, and phenotypic output.

### Example 2

Analysis of yeast lysates by immunoblotting confirmed the formation of SDS-stable oligomers of AβMRF. Lysates were treated with 1% SDS at room temperature to preserve non-covalent oligomeric complexes, then resolved by SDS-PAGE or agarose gel electrophoresis. Immunoblotting with anti-Aβ antibodies (e.g., 6E10) revealed bands corresponding to monomers, dimers, trimers, and tetramers of AβMRF, consistent with the predicted molecular weights. These oligomers were stable in 2% SDS at room temperature but dissociated into monomers upon boiling, indicating that their assembly is mediated by non-covalent interactions typical of amyloid oligomers. Notably, the efficiency of anti-Aβ antibody recognition decreased with increasing oligomer size, likely due to epitope masking within the aggregated structure—a phenomenon also observed with other amyloid proteins. Nevertheless, the oligomers were reliably detected, validating the biochemical basis of the model.

### Example 3

Point mutants of AβMRF were analyzed by SDS-electrophoresis and immunoblotting to assess the impact of specific amino acid substitutions on oligomerization. Aβm1MRF showed a marked reduction in higher-order oligomers compared to AβMRF, with a predominance of monomeric and dimeric species. Aβm2MRF exhibited even less oligomerization, with only trace amounts of dimers detectable. This graded reduction in oligomer formation directly paralleled the restoration of translational termination activity, as evidenced by the increasingly red colony color and reduced growth on -Ade medium. The persistence of dimers in Aβm2MRF suggests that the remaining intact aggregation interfaces (residues 35–37 and 40–42) retain some self-association capacity. These results underscore the specificity of the system and its ability to discriminate subtle differences in oligomerization propensity.

### Example 4

The effect of guanidine hydrochloride on AβMRF oligomers was investigated to explore the role of chaperone activity. Treatment with 6.3 mM guanidine dramatically increased the levels of AβMRF and Aβm1MRF oligomers while depleting the monomeric pool, as shown by immunoblotting. This effect was absent in Aβm2MRF, confirming its dependence on Aβ42 aggregation competence. Surprisingly, deletion of HSP104 had the opposite effect: it decreased AβMRF oligomer levels and reduced total protein abundance by ~40%. This indicates that Hsp104 is not the target of guanidine in this context and suggests that guanidine inhibits other ATP-dependent disaggregases or degradation factors. The contrasting effects of guanidine and HSP104 deletion highlight the complexity of cellular proteostasis networks in handling Aβ42 oligomers.

### Example 5

Co-immunoprecipitation experiments demonstrated a direct interaction between Hsp104 and AβMRF. Using HA-tagged AβMRF, Hsp104 was specifically pulled down, whereas no interaction was observed with MRF alone. This binding was mapped to the Aβ42 portion of the fusion, implying that Hsp104 recognizes structural features of the oligomerizing peptide. Based on these findings, it is hypothesized that Hsp104 binding shields AβMRF from degradation and disaggregation by other cellular factors. Consistent with this, HSP104 deletion led to reduced AβMRF protein levels and a shift toward monomers, supporting a protective role for Hsp104. These results reveal a novel chaperone function distinct from Hsp104’s canonical role in prion propagation and suggest that chaperone networks may play dual roles in amyloid diseases—both promoting and inhibiting toxicity depending on context.

### A Suppressible Allele

The translational readthrough of the ade1-14 nonsense mutation provides a powerful suppressible allele for screening. Compounds that reduce Aβ42 oligomerization will restore proper termination at the ade1-14 stop codon, resulting in adenine auxotrophy and red colony color. This simple, visual readout enables rapid identification of hits in large-scale screens without requiring specialized equipment. The system can be automated for high-throughput applications using robotic colony picking and image analysis.

### Example 7

The yeast strains and media used in the invention are derived from 74D-694, with genomic deletions of SUP35 and/or HSP104 maintained by plasmid-borne SUP35. Plasmid construction began with p1071, which encodes full-length Sup35p with an HA tag. The Aβ42 fragment was PCR-amplified using primers designed to match the peptide sequence and flanked by restriction sites (BamHI/BglII). This fragment was inserted into p1071 to replace the NM domain, yielding p1300. The native SUP35 promoter was then replaced with the copper-inducible CUP1 promoter from p984 using XhoI/BamHI, generating p1301. An HA-tagged M domain was PCR-amplified and inserted into the linker region to create the final AβMRF construct (p1364). Site-directed mutagenesis introduced F19T/F20T (Aβm1MRF, p1397) and I31P (Aβm2MRF, p1541) substitutions. These fusions were shuffled into pRS313 (HIS3 marker) for strain transformation. All plasmids were verified by restriction analysis and sequencing, and protein expression was confirmed by immunoblotting.

Yeast growth assays involved spotting equal cell densities on YPD and -Ade plates, with color development enhanced by cold incubation. Immunoblotting procedures included cell lysis with glass beads, protein quantification by Bradford assay, and SDS treatment at room temperature to preserve oligomers. Samples were resolved on polyacrylamide or agarose gels—agarose providing better resolution for high-molecular-weight complexes—and transferred to PVDF membranes. Detection used monoclonal antibodies against Aβ (6E10), RF (BE4), or Hsp104, with chemiluminescent visualization. Recombinant Aβ42 was polymerized in vitro and analyzed similarly to confirm oligomer migration patterns. Immunoprecipitation used anti-HA beads to pull down AβMRF complexes, followed by immunoblotting for co-precipitating partners like Hsp104. These detailed methods ensure reproducibility and reliability of the screening platform.
