# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD

The present invention relates to electrochemical exfoliation. More particularly, the invention pertains to systems and methods for the electrochemical exfoliation of layered carbonaceous materials, especially petroleum-derived needle coke, to produce high-quality graphene-family nanomaterials. The invention further encompasses novel reactor configurations that enable efficient, scalable, and continuous electrochemical exfoliation under controlled mechanical compression, thereby maintaining electrical contact throughout the exfoliation process and facilitating the production of exfoliated nanosheets with desirable lateral dimensions, thicknesses, and aspect ratios suitable for advanced applications in energy storage, composites, and electronics.

## BACKGROUND

Electrochemical exfoliation has emerged as a promising route for the production of two-dimensional nanomaterials, particularly graphene, due to its relative simplicity, scalability, and compatibility with aqueous or mild chemical environments compared to harsh oxidative routes such as Hummers’ method. Traditional electrochemical exfoliation techniques typically employ bulk graphite electrodes, wherein an applied voltage drives ion intercalation between graphitic layers, generating gas evolution or electrostatic repulsion that forces layer separation. However, these conventional approaches suffer from significant limitations. As exfoliation proceeds, the structural integrity of the monolithic graphite electrode degrades, leading to loss of electrical contact, incomplete exfoliation, and inconsistent product quality. Moreover, the use of natural graphite as a feedstock is constrained by finite global reserves, impurity content, and variability in crystallinity, which collectively hinder reproducibility and large-scale deployment. Petroleum coke, a by-product of oil refining, represents an abundant and underutilized carbon source. Among its grades, needle coke exhibits high aromaticity, long-range graphitic order, and a layered microstructure, making it a compelling alternative precursor. Yet, prior attempts to exfoliate coke have yielded poorly defined products, often requiring aggressive post-processing or resulting in quantum dots rather than extended nanosheets. These shortcomings underscore the need for an integrated approach that combines tailored feedstock selection, optimized pre-treatment, controlled electrochemical conditions, and effective separation protocols—all within a reactor architecture designed to sustain electrical connectivity during expansion. The present invention addresses these challenges by introducing a compression-based electrochemical exfoliation reactor and associated methodologies that enable the consistent, high-yield conversion of needle coke into electrochemically exfoliated graphene (EEG) with properties rivaling those derived from natural graphite.

## SUMMARY

The invention discloses a reactor for electrochemical exfoliation configured to maintain electrical contact with a bed of particulate parent material during exfoliation-induced expansion through the application of compressive force. The reactor comprises a container, a porous chamber disposed within the container to hold the parent material, a working electrode in electrical communication with the parent material, at least one counter electrode, an electrolyte solution, and a pressure source operable to apply uniaxial or biaxial compression to the porous chamber. Additionally, the invention discloses a method of electrochemical exfoliation comprising the steps of preparing a particulate parent material derived from petroleum coke, particularly needle coke; pre-treating the material to enhance wettability and intercalation; placing the material within a porous chamber under compressive load; immersing the assembly in an electrolyte solution; applying a direct current voltage between the working and counter electrodes to drive intercalation and layer separation; and subsequently separating the exfoliated nanosheets from unreacted and non-graphitic components via multi-stage centrifugation. The method further includes optional post-treatment steps such as thermal annealing to enhance electrical conductivity. This integrated system and process enable the scalable production of high-aspect-ratio graphene nanosheets from an industrial by-product stream, thereby transforming low-value coke into a high-value functional nanomaterial.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

Electrochemical exfoliation is a process by which layered materials are delaminated into individual or few-layer nanosheets through the insertion of ions or molecules between atomic planes under an applied electrical potential. The invention relates to processes and systems for electrochemical exfoliation specifically engineered for carbon-rich, layered precursors derived from petroleum refining streams. Current techniques for producing graphene often rely on natural graphite, which is geologically limited and frequently contaminated with silicates and metals, or on chemical vapor deposition, which is costly and not amenable to bulk powder production. Alternative approaches using coke have been hampered by poor control over exfoliation kinetics, inadequate separation of desired nanosheets from by-products, and reactor designs that fail to accommodate volumetric expansion without losing electrical continuity. These limitations motivate the development of a robust, compression-enabled electrochemical exfoliation platform.

The advantages of electrochemical exfoliation according to the present invention include high material throughput, minimal use of hazardous reagents, preservation of sp² carbon networks, and compatibility with industrial waste streams. Central to this approach is the compression reactor, which applies controlled pressure to a bed of parent material during electrolysis, preventing disintegration and ensuring uniform current distribution. The parent material is a carbonaceous substance possessing a layered planar structure—defined as an arrangement of fused aromatic rings stacked in parallel planes with weak van der Waals interactions between layers. Illustrative examples include highly ordered needle coke, which exhibits elongated, fibrous domains with crystalline alignment conducive to intercalation.

Exfoliation of the parent material occurs when anodic or cathodic polarization induces electrolyte species (e.g., sulfate, ammonium) to intercalate between layers, generating internal pressure that overcomes interlayer binding forces. The resulting product consists of nanosheets—two-dimensional structures with a thickness of less than 10 nanometers and a largest lateral dimension ranging from sub-micron to several micrometers. Suitable parent materials are predominantly petroleum cokes, classified into fuel coke, anode coke, and needle coke based on production conditions and end-use. Fuel coke, derived from low-grade residues, is highly disordered and rich in impurities, rendering it unsuitable. Anode coke exhibits moderate crystallinity but lacks the long-range order needed for efficient exfoliation. Needle coke, produced from aromatic feedstocks like fluid catalytic cracker decant oil, displays pronounced graphitic alignment and is the preferred embodiment.

The particulate form of the parent material is critical; particles are typically milled or sieved to achieve sizes between 1 and 50 micrometers. Smaller particles may be removed via centrifugation prior to reaction, as excessive fines hinder compaction and reduce exfoliation efficiency. Crystalline grain size directly influences exfoliation: larger, well-ordered domains facilitate uniform intercalation and yield larger nanosheets. The exfoliated material is a heterogeneous mixture comprising unexfoliated coke, amorphous carbon fragments, and desired graphene-family nanosheets. Separation into distinct products is achieved through sequential centrifugation: a low-speed spin removes coarse unreacted material, while a high-speed spin pellets the nanosheets, leaving small impurities in the supernatant.

Exfoliated nanosheets exhibit a largest lateral dimension predominantly in the 1–3 µm range, with thicknesses corresponding to 1–5 atomic layers. The aspect ratio (lateral dimension divided by thickness) exceeds 100 in many cases, a key metric for percolation in conductive networks. Compositions enriched in exfoliated material can contain greater than 80% nanosheets by mass after purification. Electrochemical exfoliation methods employ voltages between 8 and 15 V, with 12 V found optimal for needle coke; lower voltages yield insufficient intercalation, while higher voltages risk membrane degradation or parasitic reactions. Pre-treatment with nitric acid enhances surface hydrophilicity, promoting electrolyte penetration. Solvent washing with dichloromethane removes organic residues, and thermal treatment at 100–120°C ensures dryness without graphitization.

Parent material properties are refined by centrifugation at 4000 rpm to isolate particles with optimal size and morphology. Feed preparation involves mixing pre-treated coke with water, followed by sedimentation to collect the reactive fraction. The exfoliation step occurs in a compressed porous chamber submerged in 0.1 M ammonium sulfate electrolyte. Post-reaction, the mixture is washed, dispersed in ethanol-water, and subjected to two-stage centrifuging. Unexfoliated particulates may be recycled into subsequent batches without re-pre-treatment. Post-treatment includes dispersion stabilization, drying under vacuum or freeze-drying, and size classification via additional centrifugation or filtration. Annealing at 500–1100°C under inert atmosphere significantly boosts electrical conductivity by removing adsorbed species and improving inter-sheet contact.

The reactor comprises a rigid container housing a porous chamber made of cellulose dialysis membrane or sintered metal with pore sizes of 1–10 µm, selected to retain parent particles while allowing ion transport. The chamber is supported by a frame and sealed with clamps or O-rings. A bed of parent material, typically 1–5 cm in height and diameter, is loaded into the chamber. Compression is applied via a spring-loaded plate, pneumatic actuator, or dead weight, exerting 0.1–10 MPa. The electrical system includes a platinum or graphite working electrode inserted into the bed, copper mesh counter electrodes surrounding the chamber, and a DC power supply capable of delivering constant voltage or current. Current flows radially or axially through the compacted bed, ensuring uniform exfoliation.

In operation, the porous chamber is filled with pre-treated coke, sealed, and compressed. Electrolyte is introduced, and voltage is applied for 1–4 hours. Exfoliated material swells but remains electrically connected due to compression. Alternative embodiments position counter electrodes internally or use multiple working electrodes. In one embodiment, a spring applies adjustable pressure. In another, a fixture supports the chamber vertically. Six specific reactor embodiments vary in electrode geometry, pressure mechanism, and chamber orientation. Three method embodiments detail variations in pre-treatment, voltage profile, and annealing protocol.

## EXAMPLES

### Example 1

Needle coke fines (CK-1) were pre-treated by washing with dichloromethane, followed by reflux in 6 M nitric acid at 120°C for 4 hours. The acid-treated material was neutralized, dried, and centrifuged at 4000 rpm for 10 minutes to remove fines, yielding CK-1b. This material was packed into a dialysis bag (2.5 cm × 16 cm), compressed under 0.75 kg, and immersed in 0.1 M ammonium sulfate. A 12 V potential was applied between a platinum wire (working electrode) and copper mesh (counter electrode) for 2 hours. The product (CK-1c) was washed, dispersed in ethanol-water (1:1), and subjected to two-stage centrifugation: first at 2000 rpm for 10 min to discard large particles, then at 5000 rpm for 20 min to pellet the nanosheets (CK-1d). Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) revealed sheet-like morphologies 1–3 µm in lateral size. Atomic force microscopy (AFM) confirmed thicknesses of 1–3 nm. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) showed hexagonal lattice fringes with 0.34 nm spacing, indicative of graphitic stacking. Raman spectroscopy exhibited a prominent 2D peak and an I_D/I_G ratio of 0.85, confirming few-layer graphene character.

### Example 2

Electrical conductivity of pre-treated coke samples (CK-1a, CK-2a, CK-3a) was measured via four-point probe on vacuum-filtered buckypapers. CK-1a exhibited 0.07 S/m, CK-2a 0.23 S/m, and CK-3a 0.38 S/m, reflecting differences in particle morphology and crystallinity. After exfoliation and separation, only CK-1d showed a dramatic increase to 56.9 S/m, consistent with its graphene-like structure, whereas CK-2d and CK-3d remained below 5 S/m due to lack of exfoliated sheets.

### Example 3

Dry CK-1d powder was annealed under argon at 500°C (12 h and 24 h), 900°C (12 h), and 1100°C (12 h). Conductivity increased from 56.9 S/m (as-produced) to 250 S/m (500°C/12 h), 258 S/m (500°C/24 h), 345 S/m (900°C), and 474 S/m (1100°C). The marginal gain from extended time versus significant improvement with temperature indicates that thermal restructuring—not merely oxygen removal—enhances charge transport. These values meet the 10–1000 S/m range required for lithium-ion battery anodes, demonstrating the suitability of annealed coke-derived graphene for energy storage applications.
