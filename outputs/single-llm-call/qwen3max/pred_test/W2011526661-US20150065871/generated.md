# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

The blood-brain barrier (BBB) presents a formidable physiological obstacle to the systemic delivery of therapeutic agents to the central nervous system. Composed of tightly joined endothelial cells lining cerebral microvessels, the BBB restricts the passage of more than 98% of small-molecule drugs and nearly all large-molecule therapeutics, thereby severely limiting treatment options for neurological and neuropsychiatric disorders. While various strategies have been explored—including intracranial injections, chemical modification of drugs to exploit endogenous transport systems, or co-administration with BBB-permeabilizing agents—these approaches suffer from significant drawbacks such as invasiveness, lack of spatial specificity, or global disruption of the BBB, which increases the risk of neurotoxicity. These limitations are especially problematic when targeting deep subcortical structures like the basal ganglia, where invasive techniques may cause collateral damage to overlying cortical and white matter tracts. Consequently, there remains an urgent clinical and scientific need for a non-invasive, spatially precise method capable of transiently and reversibly opening the BBB in predefined brain regions without compromising surrounding tissue.

Focused ultrasound (FUS), when combined with intravenously administered microbubbles, has emerged as a promising technique for achieving localized BBB disruption. The oscillation of microbubbles within the ultrasound focus generates mechanical stresses on capillary walls, temporarily increasing vascular permeability and enabling passive diffusion of otherwise excluded molecules. This approach is inherently non-invasive, as it operates through the intact skull and scalp, and its spatial precision is governed by the focal properties of the ultrasound beam. However, the clinical translation of FUS-mediated BBB opening faces two principal challenges: accurate transcranial targeting and real-time monitoring of treatment safety and efficacy. The human and non-human primate skull introduces significant acoustic aberrations due to its heterogeneous composition, variable thickness, and mismatch in speed of sound relative to brain tissue. These aberrations distort the ultrasound wavefront, leading to focal shifts, beam broadening, and reduced peak pressure at the intended target—effects that worsen at higher frequencies. Although lower frequencies mitigate aberration-induced defocusing, they concurrently enlarge the focal volume and increase the risk of inertial cavitation, which can cause irreversible tissue damage. Thus, selecting an optimal ultrasound frequency involves balancing targeting accuracy against safety margins.

Moreover, current clinical FUS systems often rely on magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) for both targeting and post-procedural verification of BBB opening, necessitating integration within expensive, immobile MRI-guided platforms. This dependency restricts accessibility and scalability, particularly in outpatient or resource-limited settings. A standalone, MRI-independent system would greatly enhance the utility of FUS for chronic or repeated treatments, such as those required in neurodegenerative diseases. To this end, real-time monitoring of cavitation activity—the physical interaction between microbubbles and ultrasound—is essential to ensure that only stable, non-damaging oscillations occur during sonication. Passive cavitation detection (PCD), which analyzes the acoustic emissions from oscillating microbubbles, offers a potential solution by distinguishing between harmonic signatures of stable cavitation and broadband noise indicative of inertial collapse. However, reliable transcranial PCD remains technically challenging due to skull-induced signal attenuation and distortion. Therefore, there exists a critical unmet need for an integrated, portable FUS system that combines precise stereotactic targeting with robust real-time acoustic monitoring to enable safe, effective, and MRI-independent BBB opening in deep brain structures.

## SUMMARY

The present invention introduces a transcranial monitoring system designed for real-time, non-invasive, and MRI-independent blood-brain barrier (BBB) opening with enhanced safety and targeting accuracy. This system enables localized, reversible BBB disruption in deep subcortical brain regions—such as the caudate nucleus and putamen—without requiring concurrent MRI guidance, thereby expanding the potential for outpatient and chronic therapeutic applications. The core innovation lies in the integration of a single-element focused ultrasound transducer with a coaxially aligned passive cavitation detector (PCD), mounted on a stereotactic manipulator, and coupled with a software-driven targeting and monitoring framework that compensates for known sources of error while providing immediate feedback on cavitation behavior.

The system comprises several key components: a 500-kHz spherically focused ultrasound transducer configured to deliver brief, low-pressure pulses; a confocally aligned hydrophone serving as a PCD to capture backscattered acoustic emissions from microbubbles in real time; a stereotactic frame for precise subject immobilization and coordinate-based targeting; a degassing and acoustic coupling subsystem to ensure consistent energy transmission through the scalp and skull; and a computing platform running specialized software for treatment planning, real-time signal analysis, and post-sonication verification. The ultrasound transducer operates at an intermediate frequency of 500 kHz, which represents a strategic compromise between minimizing skull-induced beam aberrations and maintaining a sufficiently small focal volume for sub-milliliter targeting precision in non-human primates and, by extension, humans.

A real-time monitoring component processes the PCD signal to compute two primary metrics: Harmonic Energy Increase (HEI) and Broadband Energy Increase (BEI). HEI quantifies the enhancement in harmonic and sub-harmonic frequencies resulting from stable microbubble oscillations, serving as an indicator of successful BBB opening. BEI measures the rise in broadband acoustic noise associated with inertial cavitation, which correlates with potential tissue damage. By continuously evaluating these metrics during sonication, the system provides immediate feedback to the operator, allowing for dynamic adjustment or termination of treatment to ensure safety. Furthermore, the invention encompasses computer program products and integrated systems that automate the conversion between stereotactic manipulator settings and three-dimensional brain coordinates, enabling individualized targeting based on pre-acquired structural MRI scans without requiring real-time imaging during the procedure.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The invention provides a comprehensive system for real-time transcranial monitoring of safe and targeted blood-brain barrier (BBB) opening using focused ultrasound (FUS) in combination with intravenously administered microbubbles. The system is engineered to operate independently of magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) during the sonication procedure, relying instead on stereotactic coordinates derived from a one-time pre-procedural MRI scan and real-time acoustic feedback to ensure treatment accuracy and safety. The ultrasound transducer, a single-element spherically focused device operating at a center frequency of 500 kHz, is mounted on a stereotactic manipulator that allows precise positioning in three-dimensional space relative to the subject’s brain anatomy. This transducer is paired with a coaxial hydrophone positioned through its central aperture, ensuring that both devices share a common focal region for accurate passive cavitation detection (PCD).

System 100, as embodied in the invention, integrates all hardware and software components necessary for end-to-end BBB opening. Prior to sonication, the subject—typically a non-human primate or human patient—is anesthetized and secured within a stereotaxic frame to minimize motion artifacts. Anesthesia is induced via ketamine and maintained with isoflurane, ensuring stable physiological conditions throughout the procedure. The ultrasound transducer is then attached to the stereotaxic manipulator, and its position is calibrated using a custom software suite that maps manipulator settings to stereotactic brain coordinates. This software accounts for nine degrees of freedom, including medio-lateral, anterior-posterior, and dorso-ventral drives, as well as angular adjustments (azimuth and elevation), manipulator arm selection, and transducer attachment geometry. Before microbubble administration, negative control sonications are performed in the absence of microbubbles to establish baseline acoustic emission levels.

Monodispersed microbubbles, sized between 4 and 5 micrometers and prepared via differential centrifugation, are then injected intravenously. These microbubbles serve as acoustic amplifiers, enhancing the mechanical bioeffects of ultrasound at the capillary level. The sonication procedure lasts approximately two minutes, with focal maximum pressures maintained between 0.20 and 0.30 MPa—parameters empirically determined to induce stable cavitation without inertial collapse. Post-sonication positive controls are conducted immediately after treatment to characterize the pressure-response relationship of cavitation emissions.

To compensate for energy loss through biological tissues, the emission amplitude is adjusted upward by approximately 7.15 dB (a factor of 2.28) relative to water-based calibrations, accounting for estimated attenuations of −0.9 dB/cm in the 0.5-cm-thick scalp, −5.7 dB through the skull, and −0.5 dB/cm in 2 cm of brain tissue. A degassing system ensures that dissolved gases in the coupling medium do not interfere with acoustic transmission, while acoustic gel provides consistent coupling between the transducer and the scalp. The transducer-hydrophone assembly is rigidly mounted to prevent misalignment during prolonged procedures.

Individualized targeting begins with the acquisition of T1-weighted, stereotactically aligned structural MRI images. Using proprietary software (e.g., stereotax.R), clinicians specify a target brain region and desired approach angle—typically near-perpendicular to the skull surface to minimize refraction. The software computes up to eight possible manipulator configurations that achieve the specified target and angle, allowing selection of the optimal path based on anatomical constraints. The predicted focal point and axis are projected onto the subject’s MRI for visual confirmation. After sonication, BBB opening is verified using contrast-enhanced MRI: T1-weighted images are acquired before and 30 minutes after intravenous injection of gadodiamide. Regions of increased T1 signal indicate successful BBB disruption. Additional sequences, including T2-weighted, T2 FLAIR, and susceptibility-weighted imaging (SWI), are used to assess safety by detecting edema, hemorrhage, or other signs of tissue injury.

Image registration aligns pre- and post-contrast T1 images to the reference stereotactic scan using algorithms such as FSL’s FLIRT. Gadodiamide concentration is estimated by computing the post/pre signal ratio, and symmetric vascular contributions are removed via image flipping and division. Targeting accuracy is quantified by transforming the enhancement map into a coordinate frame centered on the predicted focus, with axes aligned to the ultrasound beam. Voxels exhibiting >10% T1 enhancement are classified as “opened,” and the center of mass of the opened region defines the observed focal location. Errors are computed in lateral (x-y) and axial (z) dimensions, with total targeting error typically averaging 3.1 ± 1.3 mm in non-human primates.

Real-time monitoring relies on spectral analysis of the PCD signal. The frequency spectrum is decomposed into harmonic (stable cavitation) and broadband (inertial cavitation) components. HEI and BEI are calculated by comparing energy levels during sonication to negative control baselines. A HEI increase of ≥15 dB confirms microbubble activity, while BEI remaining below a 6 dB threshold indicates absence of damaging inertial events. Cavitation energy is computed by integrating spectral amplitudes over defined bandwidths (0.6–5.2 MHz), with harmonic bands excluded to isolate broadband content. This real-time feedback loop enables immediate assessment of treatment success and safety, forming the cornerstone of the invention’s MRI-independent operation.

### Example 1

Experimental validation of the system was conducted through a series of 17 sonications targeting the caudate nucleus (6) and putamen (11) in two male rhesus monkeys. Each sonication followed a standardized timeline: anesthesia induction, stereotactic positioning, negative control, microbubble injection, 2-minute treatment sonication, positive controls, and post-procedural MRI. Targeting accuracy was assessed by comparing the predicted geometric focus—adjusted for a 5-mm skull-induced focal shift—to the observed center of BBB opening derived from contrast-enhanced T1 imaging. Results demonstrated a mean total targeting error of 3.1 ± 1.3 mm, with lateral and axial errors of 2.5 ± 1.2 mm and 1.5 ± 1.3 mm, respectively. Systematic errors, averaged across targets and animals, totaled 2.7 mm, while random day-to-day fluctuations contributed a mean error of 1.5 ± 0.7 mm. Notably, putamen sonications exhibited a consistent ventral offset, likely due to gravitational drift in angled approaches.

BBB opening volumes averaged 115 ± 44 mm³, scaling with applied pressure: larger openings occurred at 0.30 MPa, moderate at 0.20–0.25 MPa, with two failures at lower pressures. Real-time HEI and BEI monitoring confirmed stable cavitation in all but one sonication, with HEI increases of 15–25 dB and BEI consistently below the 6 dB damage threshold. The dynamic range of these metrics was characterized via pressure-response curves, revealing HEI onset at 0.15 MPa and saturation above 0.25 MPa, while BEI remained flat up to 0.35 MPa—defining a safe operating window. Although HEI presence strongly predicted BBB opening success (94% concordance), its magnitude did not correlate with opening volume within the narrow pressure range tested.

Safety was confirmed by absence of abnormalities in T2-weighted and SWI images, indicating no edema or hemorrhage. Behavioral observations post-procedure revealed no deficits. A preliminary closing-timeline experiment showed BBB restoration within two days following a single sonication, consistent with murine data. Error source analysis identified three contributors: geometric errors from manipulator calibration (1–2 mm), analysis errors from image registration and noise (~1 mm), and ultrasound aberration errors from skull-induced focal shifts. The observed 6.5-mm axial shift closely matched in vitro predictions (5 mm), suggesting minimal additional in vivo distortion. Stereotactic manipulator calibration using a metal rod mimicking transducer geometry confirmed mechanical precision. The invention thus demonstrates that accurate, safe, and monitorable BBB opening is achievable without real-time MRI, paving the way for portable, clinic-based neuromodulation and drug delivery.

### Example 2

Further experimental results reinforced the system’s reliability in targeting deep brain nuclei. Caudate and putamen sonications achieved consistent BBB opening with high spatial fidelity. Analysis confirmed that PCD-derived HEI serves as a robust binary indicator of treatment success, though not a quantitative predictor of opening volume under fixed microbubble and pressure conditions. Safety assessments across all procedures showed no evidence of tissue damage via MRI or clinical observation. An exploratory study of BBB closing duration indicated near-complete restoration by 48 hours post-sonication.

In vitro validation employed desiccated macaque and human skulls to quantify transcranial acoustic transmission. Skulls were degassed to eliminate air pockets and mounted in a water tank with the transducer-hydrophone assembly. Pressure calibration established baseline emissions in water, which were then compared to signals transmitted through skull samples. In-house microbubbles (4–5 µm) were size-isolated and injected during in vivo experiments. PCD and B-mode ultrasound provided complementary monitoring: PCD for spectral cavitation analysis, B-mode for gross bubble visualization.

In vivo techniques mirrored clinical workflow: subjects were anesthetized, positioned stereotactically, and sonicated after microbubble injection. MRI analysis included image re-alignment, enhancement evaluation via post/pre ratios, and volume calculation of opened regions. PCD signals were processed to compute Stable Cavitation Dose (SCD), Inertial Cavitation Dose (ICD), and Cavitation Signal-to-Noise Ratio (SNR)—defined as the ratio of cavitation energy to background noise. Statistical comparisons between in vitro and in vivo data confirmed that 100- and 5000-cycle pulses yielded detectable cavitation SNR through skull, with thresholds established for reliable transcranial detection.

Real-time PCD monitoring successfully distinguished safe (stable-only) from potentially harmful (inertial) regimes. Sensitivity tests showed that pulse length influenced cavitation dose detection limits, with longer pulses enhancing SNR. Skull attenuation was measured at −5.7 dB at 500 kHz, consistent across specimens. The system reliably detected cavitation through intact skull, enabling in situ estimation of bubble activity strength. Nonlinear scattering effects were accounted for in spectral filtering. Ultimately, the invention achieved safe BBB opening with controlled volume variation, and ICD remained below damage thresholds, confirming procedural safety across all tested parameters.
