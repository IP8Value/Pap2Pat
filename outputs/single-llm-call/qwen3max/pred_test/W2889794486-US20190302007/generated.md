## BACKGROUND

Optical extinction and absorption spectroscopy have long served as foundational analytical tools for determining the molecular composition of complex media. For over a century and a half, these techniques have enabled scientists to probe the interaction of light with matter, providing critical insights into the chemical and physical properties of substances across diverse scientific disciplines. Among these methods, ultraviolet-visible (UV-VIS) spectrophotometry has emerged as a standard laboratory technique since the 1940s, widely employed in fields ranging from materials science and physical chemistry to molecular biology due to its relative simplicity and broad applicability.

UV-VIS spectrophotometry operates by measuring the attenuation of light as it passes through a sample, with the assumption that any reduction in transmitted intensity is attributable solely to molecular absorption. This approach yields what is commonly referred to as an extinction spectrum, which is interpreted as a direct reflection of the sample’s absorptive characteristics. However, this interpretation is fundamentally flawed when applied to complex or heterogeneous media, as it fails to account for another significant contributor to light attenuation: scattering. Scattering arises from refractive index mismatches at internal interfaces within a sample, causing photons to deviate from their original trajectory without being absorbed. Consequently, a portion of the incident light never reaches the detector, leading conventional spectrophotometers to erroneously attribute this loss to absorption.

The distinction between absorption and scattering is crucial: absorption is an intrinsic molecular property, directly related to electronic transitions and thus serving as a reliable “molecular fingerprint,” whereas scattering is an extrinsic phenomenon dictated by the physical structure, morphology, and heterogeneity of the medium. In simple, homogeneous samples—such as pure liquids or dilute solutions—absorption dominates, and UV-VIS spectra provide accurate and interpretable data. However, in more complex systems—such as biological tissues, blood, colloidal suspensions, polymer films, plasmonic nanostructures, and metamaterials—scattering can be substantial, often overwhelming the true absorption signal and rendering conventional UV-VIS spectra misleading or uninterpretable.

To address this challenge, researchers have explored various strategies to isolate the absorption component from the total extinction. One such method involves the use of an integrating sphere, which surrounds the sample and captures nearly all scattered light, thereby enabling more accurate determination of total transmittance and reflectance. While effective in principle, this technique is highly sensitive to sample geometry, positioning, and chamber design, and requires extensive calibration across the wavelength range of interest. Moreover, it is ill-suited for routine use in standard spectrophotometric setups, particularly those employing microplate or cuvette-based formats.

Alternative approaches have leveraged ultra-short laser pulses to analyze the temporal spreading of light as it propagates through a scattering medium, theoretically allowing separation of absorption and scattering contributions. However, these methods demand picosecond or femtosecond pulsed lasers that are tunable over broad spectral ranges—a capability not commercially available—and rely on complex time-resolved detection schemes. Furthermore, they are only applicable to thick, diffusively scattering samples, limiting their utility in general analytical contexts.

Plasmonic nanoparticles, particularly gold nanoparticles (GNPs), exemplify the challenges posed by combined absorption and scattering. Due to their localized surface plasmon resonance, GNPs exhibit strong, size- and shape-dependent optical responses that include both significant absorption and intense scattering. These properties underpin their widespread use in biomedical sensing, imaging, and therapy. Yet, precisely because their extinction spectra are composites of absorption and scattering, conventional UV-VIS measurements cannot reliably inform applications that depend critically on the absorption component—such as photothermal therapy, where heat generation is proportional only to absorbed, not scattered, light.

Photothermal spectroscopy offers a partial solution by detecting the thermal lensing or beam deflection caused by light absorption, thereby isolating the absorptive contribution. However, this method remains sensitive to variations in local laser fluence induced by scattering and is typically limited to very low concentrations or single-particle studies. It also fails when nanoparticles are embedded in highly scattering environments, such as within biological tissues or cells.

Thus, despite decades of advancement, there remains a critical unmet need for a robust, non-contact, and broadly applicable spectrophotometric method capable of accurately measuring true optical absorption independent of scattering—particularly in complex, heterogeneous, and biologically relevant media.

## SUMMARY

The present invention introduces a novel non-contact photoacoustic spectrophotometry system designed to overcome the limitations of conventional optical absorption measurement techniques in scattering media. This system enables the accurate and quantitative determination of the true optical absorption spectrum of a sample, irrespective of the presence or magnitude of light scattering, by leveraging the photoacoustic effect in a non-invasive, reflection-mode configuration.

The system comprises a tunable time-modulated light source configured to deliver pulses of light at selectable wavelengths across a desired spectral range, such as the near-infrared or visible spectrum. The light source is operable to irradiate a sample contained within a measurement chamber, inducing transient thermal expansion in regions where light is absorbed, thereby generating ultrasonic (photoacoustic) waves. Critically, the amplitude of these photoacoustic signals is proportional to the local optical absorption coefficient and is largely insensitive to scattering, as scattering does not contribute to heat deposition.

A non-contact detector is positioned to receive the photoacoustic waves generated at the interface of the sample chamber without requiring physical or acoustic coupling to the sample. In one embodiment, this detector comprises an interferometer—specifically, a fiber-optic Sagnac interferometer—configured to detect minute displacements of the chamber’s surface caused by the photoacoustic pressure waves. The interferometer utilizes a low-power, continuous-wave probe laser beam reflected from a coated surface of the chamber, with phase shifts in the returning beam encoding the amplitude of the acoustic signal.

The system further includes a processor in communication with the detector, configured to digitize, amplify, and analyze the detected signals to construct an absorption spectrum. The processor correlates the photoacoustic signal amplitude at each wavelength with the corresponding optical absorption coefficient, optionally applying calibration factors derived from reference standards.

In a specific embodiment, the tunable time-modulated light source is a diode-pumped, nanosecond-pulsed laser with rapid wavelength-switching capability (e.g., <1 ms) and high pulse repetition rates (e.g., ≥1 kHz), enabling fast spectral acquisition. The interferometer is implemented as a common-path fiber-optic Sagnac loop, providing high sensitivity and immunity to environmental vibrations. The overall system is integrated into a compact platform compatible with standard laboratory sample formats, such as cuvettes or microplate wells.

The method for constructing an absorption spectrum according to the invention involves irradiating the sample with wavelength-tunable pulsed light, detecting the resulting photoacoustic signals via a non-contact optical interferometer, and processing the signal amplitudes to yield a quantitative absorption spectrum. This method is scattering-insensitive, works in reflection mode, and is applicable to optically dense, turbid, or heterogeneous samples—including those containing plasmonic nanoparticles or living cells.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The photoacoustic effect, first described by Alexander Graham Bell in 1880, refers to the generation of acoustic waves following the absorption of modulated or pulsed light by a material. When a sample absorbs light, a fraction of the photon energy is converted into heat, causing rapid thermoelastic expansion and launching a pressure wave—i.e., ultrasound. The amplitude of this photoacoustic (PA) signal is directly proportional to the amount of absorbed light, making it an ideal proxy for the optical absorption coefficient, independent of scattering.

The present invention exploits this principle in a non-contact photoacoustic spectrophotometry configuration. Unlike conventional PA systems that require piezoelectric transducers in direct contact with the sample, the disclosed system uses an all-optical, remote detection scheme. This eliminates the need for acoustic coupling gels or physical contact, preserving sample integrity and enabling compatibility with standard spectrophotometric workflows.

Conventional photoacoustic spectroscopy, while powerful, has been limited by its reliance on contact-based ultrasound detection, which complicates integration with existing optical instrumentation and restricts applicability to liquid or soft samples. Moreover, in highly scattering media, the distribution of laser fluence can be altered, potentially biasing PA signal amplitude. The present invention overcomes these limitations through a combination of narrow-beam excitation and surface-displacement detection.

The extinction A, commonly used in radiometry, is defined as A = log₁₀(I₀/I), where I₀ is the incident light intensity and I is the transmitted intensity. In non-scattering media, A is related to the absorption coefficient μₐ via the Bouguer-Lambert-Beer law: μₐ = (ln(10) · A)/d, where d is the path length. However, this law fails in scattering media, as scattering redistributes light without absorption, invalidating the assumption of exponential attenuation along a single path. Consequently, UV-VIS extinction in turbid samples does not reflect true absorption and cannot be reliably decomposed into absorption and scattering components.

The non-contact PA method circumvents this issue by measuring only the absorbed energy. The PA signal P is given by P ∝ μₐ · Γ · F, where Γ is the Grüneisen parameter (efficiency of thermoelastic conversion) and F is the local laser fluence. By using a tightly focused excitation beam with a diameter smaller than the transport mean free path of photons in the medium, the fluence F becomes nearly independent of scattering, rendering P directly proportional to μₐ.

In operation, the sample—such as a solution of gold nanorods or cells internalizing nanoparticles—is placed between two quartz plates, with the top plate coated with a reflective layer optimized for the probe laser wavelength (e.g., 1550 nm). A tunable pulsed laser (e.g., 700–900 nm) irradiates the sample through the bottom plate, generating PA waves that propagate to the top surface. A continuous-wave probe laser, part of a fiber-optic Sagnac interferometer, reflects off the coated surface; surface displacements induced by PA waves modulate the phase of the reflected beam, which is detected as an interference signal.

The system may include an XY translation stage synchronized with laser firing to raster-scan the sample, ensuring homogeneous probing and mitigating sedimentation effects. Pulse energy is controlled via neutral-density filters to avoid nonlinear thermal effects, and repetition rates are set to enable rapid spectral acquisition (e.g., 21 wavelengths in ~10 minutes).

Calibration is performed using a reference absorber with known μₐ, such as cupric sulfate, establishing a linear relationship between PA signal amplitude and absorption coefficient. This allows absolute quantification of μₐ across the spectrum.

The advantages of this method are manifold: it is non-contact, scattering-insensitive, applicable to dense or cellular samples, and provides true absorption spectra essential for optimizing nanoparticle-based diagnostics and therapies. Potential applications extend to flow cytometry, point-of-care pathology, spatially resolved molecular imaging, and real-time monitoring of dynamic chemical processes.
