# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

### Field of the Invention

Microfluidic devices are engineered systems that manipulate small volumes of fluids, typically in the range of microliters to picoliters, within networks of channels having dimensions on the order of tens to hundreds of micrometers. These devices are widely used in biological, chemical, and medical applications due to their ability to precisely control fluid flow, enable high-throughput screening, reduce reagent consumption, and mimic physiological microenvironments. In particular, microfluidic platforms have become foundational in the development of organ-on-a-chip technologies, which aim to replicate the structural and functional characteristics of human tissues and organs for drug testing, disease modeling, and fundamental biological research.

### Description of Related Art

The utility of microfluidic devices in cell culture has been significantly enhanced by the incorporation of dynamic fluid flow, which more accurately emulates the in vivo conditions experienced by cells within living tissues. Fluid shear stress—the mechanical force exerted by flowing fluid on adherent cells—has been shown to profoundly influence cellular behavior, including proliferation, differentiation, migration, and gene expression, particularly in epithelial and endothelial cell types. Consequently, the ability to apply controlled and physiologically relevant fluid shear stress is a critical requirement for advanced in vitro models such as organ-on-a-chip systems.

Traditionally, fluid flow in microfluidic organ-on-a-chip devices has been achieved using external pumps connected to the device via tubing. While effective in generating precise flow rates, this approach presents several limitations that hinder widespread adoption. First, the integration of external pumps and tubing introduces complexity, making the setup less accessible to laboratories without specialized microfluidics expertise. Second, the presence of tubing increases the risk of bubble formation, leakage, and contamination, all of which can compromise experimental integrity and reproducibility. Third, external pumping systems often require significant capital investment and dedicated space, limiting their feasibility for resource-constrained settings. Finally, in applications involving biohazardous agents such as pathogens or viruses, the open nature of tubing connections poses biosafety concerns.

To address these challenges, researchers have explored alternative strategies for driving fluid flow in microfluidic systems. One such approach involves the use of hydrostatic pressure gradients created by height differences between fluid reservoirs. However, this method offers limited flexibility in modulating flow rates and does not support rapid medium exchange, which is essential for drug exposure studies. Another widely adopted solution employs rocking platforms that tilt the entire device to induce bidirectional gravity-driven flow. While eliminating the need for external pumps, rocker-based systems generate complex, non-uniform flow profiles with oscillating shear stresses that may not accurately reflect physiological conditions. Moreover, commercial rockers remain costly and are not universally compatible with standard incubator environments.

The concept of organ-on-a-chip itself has gained considerable traction as a promising alternative to animal models in preclinical research. These systems integrate living human cells within microengineered environments that recapitulate key aspects of organ-level physiology, such as tissue-tissue interfaces, mechanical cues, and dynamic perfusion. Despite their potential, the reliance on external fluidic infrastructure continues to impede the democratization of organ-on-a-chip technology across diverse research communities. There remains a pressing need for simple, robust, and equipment-free methods to achieve controlled, unidirectional fluid flow in microfluidic cell culture devices—particularly those that can be operated by non-specialist users under standard cell culture conditions without compromising sterility, reproducibility, or physiological relevance.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides an apparatus for generating controlled fluid flow in microfluidic devices without the need for external pumps, power sources, or tubing. The apparatus comprises a fluid reservoir in fluid communication with a microfluidic channel and an evaporation reservoir positioned at the outlet of the channel. Critically, a wetting, wicking, or hydrophilic structure—such as a superhydrophilic thread—is placed in contact with the outlet of the microfluidic channel and extends into the evaporation reservoir. This structure passively draws fluid from the microfluidic channel via capillary action, and the continuous evaporation of the fluid from the exposed surface of the structure in the controlled environment of a cell culture incubator generates a sustained driving force for unidirectional flow. The flow rate can be precisely tuned by adjusting the physical dimensions—such as length and diameter—of the hydrophilic structure, thereby enabling control over the fluid shear stress experienced by cultured cells. The invention further encompasses a method for fluid flow in a microfluidic device, comprising the steps of providing a microfluidic channel bounded by a solid substrate, introducing a fluid into an inlet reservoir in fluid communication with the channel, positioning a hydrophilic wicking structure in contact with the outlet of the channel, and allowing evaporation of the fluid from the wicking structure to drive continuous flow through the channel over extended periods, typically up to 24 hours or more.

## DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

The present invention introduces a novel microfluidic apparatus designed to enable autonomous, pumpless perfusion of cell cultures, particularly for organ-on-a-chip applications. The apparatus is fundamentally simple yet highly effective, leveraging the natural phenomena of capillary wicking and controlled evaporation to generate reliable fluid flow. The core components of the apparatus include a microfluidic channel formed within or on a solid substrate, an inlet fluid reservoir in fluid communication with one end of the channel, and an outlet connected to a hydrophilic wicking structure that extends into an evaporation zone. This wicking structure—preferably a superhydrophilic thread composed of materials such as rayon and polyester—acts as a passive pump by drawing fluid from the channel and exposing it to the humidified atmosphere of a standard cell culture incubator, where gradual evaporation sustains a continuous flow gradient.

The mechanism of fluid flow is driven by the interplay between capillary forces within the porous matrix of the wicking structure and the evaporation rate at its exposed surface. In a typical cell culture incubator maintained at 37°C, 5% CO₂, and approximately 90% relative humidity, the evaporation of aqueous culture medium from the thread is slow but steady, creating a consistent negative pressure that pulls fluid through the microchannel. This process eliminates the need for any external mechanical or electrical components, rendering the system entirely equipment-free. The absence of tubing and pumps not only simplifies operation but also enhances biosafety and sterility, as the entire device can be enclosed within a standard sterile Petri dish during incubation.

The invention is broadly applicable across a wide range of microfluidic applications, but it is particularly advantageous for organ-on-a-chip devices, which require stable, long-term perfusion to maintain differentiated cellular phenotypes. Organ-on-a-chip technology is experiencing rapid market growth due to its potential to improve drug development pipelines and reduce reliance on animal testing. However, its broader adoption has been hampered by the technical complexity of traditional perfusion systems. The present invention directly addresses this barrier by providing a user-friendly, low-cost, and robust alternative that delivers precise fluidic conditions essential for mimicking in vivo physiology. Dynamic culture environments are known to be critical for inducing proper cell polarization, tight junction formation, and functional maturation—processes that are highly sensitive to the magnitude and uniformity of fluid shear stress.

The pumpless apparatus of the invention excels in providing such controlled conditions. By selecting threads of specific diameters (e.g., 0.5 mm to 2 mm) and lengths (e.g., 1 mm to 28 mm), users can achieve flow rates ranging from 0.15 µL min⁻¹ to 6 µL min⁻¹, corresponding to physiologically relevant shear stresses between 0.002 and 0.15 dyn cm⁻²—ranges suitable for intestinal, lung, and other epithelial cell types. This tunability is crucial, as different cell types exhibit distinct shear stress optima; for example, Caco-2 intestinal cells differentiate optimally under low shear (≈0.02 dyn cm⁻²), while A549 lung cells undergo epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) at higher shear levels (above ≈0.03 dyn cm⁻²). The invention thus enables precise mechanobiological studies without complex instrumentation.

Moreover, the apparatus is highly accessible to non-specialist users. No training in microfluidics or pump operation is required; setup involves merely inserting a thread into the outlet port and placing the device in an incubator. The solid substrate of the microfluidic device is typically fabricated from polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) bonded to a glass coverslip, though other biocompatible materials such as thermoplastics or hydrogels may also be used. Fabrication employs standard soft lithography: a master mold is created using photolithography (e.g., with SU-8 photoresist), PDMS is cast and cured, and inlet/outlet ports are punched. The microfluidic channel may incorporate additional features such as valves, pillars, or multi-layer architectures, though the basic embodiment requires only a single channel connecting two reservoirs.

The fluid reservoir is typically a larger cavity (e.g., 8 mm diameter) at the inlet to hold sufficient medium for multi-day experiments, while the outlet features a smaller aperture (e.g., 1.5 mm) to facilitate secure placement of the wicking thread. Critically, the thread is positioned so that it does not contact the bottom of the channel, preventing complete drainage and maintaining a constant fluid volume over time. The wicking structure may be a twisted fiber thread, a bundle of nanofibers, or a micropillar array engineered to exhibit strong capillary action. Surface tension and material hydrophilicity are key parameters governing performance; plasma treatment or chemical modification can enhance wettability if needed.

Environmental factors such as temperature, humidity, and air circulation within the incubator influence evaporation rate and thus flow stability. However, under standard incubator conditions, flow remains remarkably constant for at least 24 hours, and threads can function reliably for up to five days—sufficient for full cellular differentiation. In some embodiments, a dead volume reservoir is added downstream of the wicking structure to buffer against evaporation fluctuations. Multichannel configurations enable multiplexed experiments, with each channel independently controlled by a thread of unique dimensions, allowing parallel testing of multiple shear stress conditions on a single chip. Co-culture arrangements are also feasible, with separate channels or compartments supporting different cell types under shared or distinct flow regimes.

The advantages of the apparatus are numerous: it is cost-effective, scalable, sterile, bubble-free, and compatible with standard microscopy and cell biology workflows. It supports both constant-width channels for uniform shear and gradient channels (e.g., Hele-Shaw designs) for spatially varying conditions. The invention thus represents a significant advancement in microfluidic cell culture, lowering the barrier to entry for organ-on-a-chip research while maintaining scientific rigor and physiological relevance.

### Examples

The invention was validated using organ-on-a-chip configurations tailored for intestinal and lung epithelial models. Master molds were fabricated using soft lithography: SU-8 50 photoresist was spin-coated onto silicon wafers at 2000 rpm to yield 150 µm thick layers, patterned via maskless lithography using a Dilase 650 writer, and thermally hardened. PDMS prepolymer (10:1 base-to-curing agent ratio) was cast onto the molds, cured at 65°C for 2 hours, and peeled off. Inlet holes (8 mm diameter) and outlet holes (1.5 mm diameter) were punched into the PDMS slabs to form reservoirs. The PDMS layers were then oxygen plasma treated and permanently bonded to No. 1 glass coverslips by incubation at 65°C for 10 minutes.

Three device geometries were employed: a constant-width channel (1 mm wide, 150 µm high), a Hele-Shaw channel with linearly varying width to generate a shear stress gradient, and a multiplexed device with five parallel channels. Prior to cell seeding, channels were sterilized with 70% ethanol and rinsed with cold PBS. Surfaces were coated with 1% Matrigel diluted in serum-free medium, incubated for 1 hour at 37°C, and aspirated. Human intestinal epithelial Caco-2 cells and human lung epithelial A549 cells (including a vimentin-RFP reporter line) were cultured in standard media and seeded into devices at 2 × 10⁵ cells cm⁻² via the outlet to minimize cell loss.

Superhydrophilic Spunlace threads (70% rayon, 30% polyester) were characterized by scanning electron microscopy (SEM), revealing smooth fibers of ≈15 µm diameter and a porous, twisted architecture conducive to capillary flow. Capillary properties were confirmed by measuring dye wicking heights over time in threads of 0.5–2 mm diameter. Flow behavior in microchannels was quantified by tracking 200 nm fluorescent polystyrene beads using time-lapse microscopy; velocities were converted to flow rates using channel cross-sectional area. Threads generated stable flow for 24 hours, with rates tunable from 0.15 to 6 µL min⁻¹ by adjusting thread length and diameter.

Caco-2 monolayers cultured under 0.02 dyn cm⁻² shear stress for 5 days exhibited dense F-actin networks, high ZO-1 expression at tight junctions, and abundant active mitochondria—phenotypes indistinguishable from those achieved with external pumps. A549 VIM RFP cells exposed to shear stresses above 0.03 dyn cm⁻² showed significantly elevated vimentin expression and spindle-like morphology, confirming shear-induced EMT. In multiplexed devices, five parallel channels driven by threads of varying lengths produced distinct flow rates, enabling simultaneous assessment of shear-dependent responses.

The scope of the invention encompasses all microfluidic devices utilizing evaporation-driven flow via hydrophilic wicking structures, including variations in thread material (e.g., cellulose, nylon, or synthetic hydrogels), channel geometry, substrate composition, and application to primary cells, stem cells, or organoids.
