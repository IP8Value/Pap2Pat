# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND OF INVENTION

Optogenetics has emerged as a revolutionary technique in neuroscience, enabling precise control over neuronal activity through the use of light-sensitive proteins known as rhodopsins. Since its inception, this method has provided profound insights into the neural mechanisms underlying complex behaviors and neurological disorders such as Parkinson’s disease, epilepsy, and depression. The core principle of optogenetics involves the genetic expression of microbial opsins—such as channelrhodopsin-2 (ChR2), halorhodopsin (NpHR), and archaerhodopsin (Arch)—in specific neuronal populations, followed by illumination with visible light to modulate membrane potentials via ion flux. Despite its transformative potential, conventional optogenetics remains constrained by significant limitations that hinder its broader application, particularly in clinical settings. Chief among these is the requirement for invasive surgical implantation of optical fibers or waveguides to deliver light deep into brain tissue, which not only introduces physical trauma but also restricts the spatial scope of intervention due to limited light penetration in biological media. Additionally, existing light sources—primarily lasers and light-emitting diodes (LEDs)—suffer from trade-offs between cost, beam collimation, and depth accessibility. Lasers offer high spatial precision but are expensive and difficult to scale for multi-site stimulation, whereas LEDs are more economical but produce divergent beams that lack the necessary focus for targeted neuromodulation. These constraints have motivated an urgent need for non-invasive, deeply penetrating alternatives that preserve the temporal and cellular specificity of optogenetics while eliminating the necessity for cranial surgery. The present invention addresses this critical gap by introducing two novel modalities—X-ray optogenetics (X-optogenetics) and ultrasound optogenetics (U-optogenetics)—that leverage advanced nanomaterials and physical phenomena to achieve remote, non-invasive activation of light-sensitive ion channels without implanted hardware.

## BRIEF SUMMARY

The present invention provides systems and methods for non-invasive optogenetic neuromodulation using either X-ray or ultrasound energy as the primary stimulus, thereby overcoming the depth and invasiveness limitations of conventional optogenetics. In X-optogenetics, X-rays—capable of deep tissue penetration—are used to excite X-ray-excitable nanophosphors that have been functionalized to bind specifically to rhodopsin-expressing neurons. Upon X-ray irradiation, these nanophosphors emit visible light locally, activating nearby rhodopsins and modulating neuronal membrane potentials. In U-optogenetics, ultrasonic waves induce sonoluminescence—light emission from collapsing microbubbles—potentially enhanced by chemiluminescent agents such as fluoresceinyl Cypridina luminescent analog (FCLA), which are similarly targeted to rhodopsins. Both approaches eliminate the need for implanted optical fibers. Current optogenetic techniques are fundamentally limited by the shallow penetration of visible light in tissue (typically less than 1–2 mm) and the requirement for craniotomy, rendering them unsuitable for deep-brain or human applications. While near-infrared upconversion nanoparticles have offered incremental improvements, their efficacy is still constrained by limited skull and tissue transmittance. The disclosed invention overcomes these barriers by utilizing X-rays, which penetrate the entire human brain with minimal attenuation, or ultrasound, which offers radiation-free stimulation with moderate depth capability. The invention thus fulfills a long-felt but unmet need for a truly non-invasive, deep-tissue-compatible optogenetic platform that retains millisecond temporal precision and cellular specificity, paving the way for both preclinical research and future therapeutic interventions in humans.

## DETAILED DISCLOSURE

Optogenetics is a biotechnological method that enables the control of cellular activity—particularly in neurons—through the expression of light-sensitive transmembrane proteins known as rhodopsins. These include channelrhodopsins, which are cation-permeable ion channels activated by blue light to induce depolarization, and ion pumps such as halorhodopsin and archaerhodopsin, which hyperpolarize neurons in response to yellow or green light, respectively. The technique conventionally requires the delivery of visible light (typically 450–590 nm) via surgically implanted optical fibers connected to external lasers or LEDs. However, this approach is inherently invasive and suffers from poor spatial resolution at depth due to scattering and absorption of light in biological tissues. To address these shortcomings, the present invention introduces two complementary non-invasive strategies: X-ray optogenetics and ultrasound optogenetics.

In X-ray optogenetics, the key innovation lies in the use of X-ray-excitable light-emitting particles—specifically nanophosphors—that convert high-energy X-ray photons into visible light locally at the site of rhodopsin expression. Rhodopsins, such as ChR2, NpHR, or Arch, are first genetically introduced into target neurons via viral vectors or transgenic methods. Concurrently, nanophosphors are administered systemically or locally and functionalized with targeting moieties—such as antibodies or peptides—that bind specifically to the expressed rhodopsins, ensuring close proximity (<1 µm) between the light source and the light-sensitive protein. This proximity is critical because visible light intensity decays exponentially in tissue, with >90% loss over just 1 mm. The X-ray source, preferably a carbon nanotube (CNT)-based field-emission device capable of rapid pulsing (5–100 ms), delivers controlled bursts of soft X-rays (0.5–60 keV) that penetrate the skull and brain with minimal attenuation. These X-rays are absorbed by the nanophosphors, which then emit visible photons tuned to the activation spectrum of the target rhodopsin.

A central component of the X-optogenetic system is the X-ray focusing element, which enhances spatial precision and reduces off-target radiation dose. Suitable focusing elements include polycapillary lenses, Fresnel zone plates, or diffraction gratings. A polycapillary lens comprises an array of hollow glass capillaries that guide X-rays via total external reflection to a focal spot as small as 5–500 µm, enabling single-neuron or small-population targeting. Alternatively, a Fresnel zone plate—a circular diffraction grating with radially decreasing line spacing—focuses X-rays through constructive interference at a defined focal length, though it is typically optimized for synchrotron-based monochromatic beams in the 5–8 keV range. The use of such focusing optics ensures that X-ray energy is concentrated only on regions containing functionalized nanophosphors, minimizing unnecessary exposure.

The nanophosphors themselves are engineered for high X-ray-to-visible-light conversion efficiency, biocompatibility, and appropriate size for blood-brain barrier (BBB) penetration. Preferred compositions include gadolinium oxysulfide (Gd₂O₂S) or lithium gallate (LiGa₅O₈) doped with activator ions such as Cr³⁺, Eu³⁺, or Tb³⁺, which enable strong luminescence under X-ray excitation. Particle diameters are optimized between 10–150 nm to balance BBB transit (maximal for <100 nm) and photon yield (scaling with volume). Surface coatings such as polyethylene glycol (PEG), poly(acrylic acid) (PAA), or DSPE-PEG-COOH enhance aqueous solubility, colloidal stability, and biocompatibility while providing functional groups for conjugation to targeting ligands. Conversion efficiencies as high as 60,000 visible photons per MeV of absorbed X-ray energy have been reported, and theoretical modeling indicates that under a safe effective radiation dose of ≤10 mSv—comparable to a diagnostic CT scan—sufficient photons can be generated to activate rhodopsins, especially when particle size and quantum yield are optimized.

Ultrasound optogenetics (U-optogenetics) offers a radiation-free alternative based on sonoluminescence—the emission of light from gas-filled microbubbles undergoing inertial cavitation under ultrasonic pressure waves. When exposed to ultrasound (typically 100 kHz–1 MHz), microbubbles in tissue collapse violently, generating transient high temperatures and pressures that produce broadband light emission via thermal bremsstrahlung. This effect is significantly amplified by co-administered chemiluminescent agents such as FCLA, which react with reactive oxygen species generated during cavitation to emit intense, narrowband visible light (e.g., 532 nm peak), ideal for ChR2 activation. Like nanophosphors, FCLA molecules are functionalized to bind rhodopsins, ensuring localized light delivery. Ultrasound frequencies of 100 kHz achieve penetration depths of up to 3 cm through bone, sufficient for cortical access in humans, especially when combined with emerging acoustic metamaterials that correct for skull-induced wavefront distortion.

Both X- and U-optogenetics provide millisecond temporal control: CNT X-ray sources enable rapid on/off switching, while ultrasound pulses can be precisely gated. Computer-readable media may store executable code for controlling X-ray or ultrasound parameters (pulse duration, frequency, intensity) in coordination with physiological feedback. The following exemplified embodiments further illustrate the invention without limitation.

### Embodiment 1

An optogenetic method is provided wherein neurons expressing rhodopsins are modulated by administering X-ray-excitable light-emitting particles that bind to said rhodopsins, followed by non-invasive irradiation with X-rays, causing the particles to emit visible light that activates the rhodopsins and alters neuronal membrane potential.

### Embodiment 2

The light-emitting particles of Embodiment 1 are nanoparticles having a diameter between 10 and 200 nanometers.

### Embodiment 3

The nanoparticles of Embodiment 2 are nanophosphors comprising a host matrix doped with luminescent ions selected from Cr³⁺, Eu³⁺, and Tb³⁺.

### Embodiment 4

The nanophosphors of Embodiment 3 emit visible light upon X-ray excitation, with peak emission wavelengths between 450 and 700 nm.

### Embodiment 5

The X-ray source used in Embodiment 1 is a carbon nanotube field-emission X-ray source.

### Embodiment 6

The carbon nanotube X-ray source of Embodiment 5 comprises an array of vertically aligned carbon nanotubes serving as electron emitters, gated to produce pulsed X-ray emission with pulse durations of 1–200 milliseconds.

### Embodiment 7

The sample subjected to the method of Embodiment 1 is an animal brain.

### Embodiment 8

The animal brain of Embodiment 7 is a mammal brain.

### Embodiment 9

The mammal brain of Embodiment 8 is a human brain. The method further employs a focusing element for concentrating X-rays onto a target region of the brain. The focusing element is selected from a lens, a zone plate, and a grating. When a lens is used, it is a polycapillary lens. When a zone plate is used, it is a Fresnel zone plate. An X-ray stop or detector may be included to monitor beam alignment or dose. Neurons are transfected with DNA encoding a rhodopsin, which may be channelrhodopsin-2 (ChR2), halorhodopsin (NpHR), or archaerhodopsin (Arch). The light-emitting particles are functionalized to bind specifically to the expressed rhodopsin. Activation of the rhodopsin changes the neuron’s membrane potential, leading to depolarization or hyperpolarization. In an alternative configuration, chemiluminescent agents such as FCLA molecules are used instead of nanophosphors; these agents emit visible light upon ultrasonic stimulation and are functionalized to bind rhodopsins. Ultrasonic waves are applied at frequencies of kHz or MHz. The sample may be an animal brain, a mammal brain, or a human brain.

## Example 1

The feasibility of X-optogenetics was evaluated based on radiation safety and photon yield requirements. A maximum permissible effective radiation dose of 10 mSv—equivalent to a standard diagnostic CT scan—was adopted as the upper limit for a single procedure. At this dose, 1 microgram of brain tissue absorbs 10⁻⁸ J (10 nJ) of energy. Given that 1 J equals 6.24 × 10¹² MeV, this corresponds to 62.4 MeV per microgram. Using Gd₂O₂S nanophosphors with a conversion efficiency of 60,000 visible photons per MeV, approximately 3.74 × 10⁶ photons are generated per microgram of tissue. Assuming a particle density of 3.25 × 10⁷ particles per microgram (for 50 nm diameter particles), this yields ~0.11 photons per particle—insufficient for rhodopsin activation, which requires 1.5–3 absorbed photons per molecule due to retinal’s 30–70% quantum efficiency. However, increasing particle diameter to 150 nm reduces particle count by 27-fold (volume scaling), raising photon yield to ~3 photons per particle. Further enhancement to 50% quantum efficiency (theoretically possible) yields >10 photons per particle, well above the activation threshold. These modifications demonstrate that X-optogenetics is feasible within safe radiation limits. It is understood that such particle engineering falls within the scope of routine optimization, and all referenced literature on nanophosphor synthesis and functionalization is hereby incorporated by reference.
