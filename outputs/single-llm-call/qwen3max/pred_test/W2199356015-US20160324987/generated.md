## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention resides in the field of medicine, specifically within ophthalmology and molecular therapeutics for inherited retinal degenerative diseases. More particularly, the invention relates to methods and compositions for the treatment of autosomal dominant retinitis pigmentosa (adRP) through allele-specific genomic ablation using CRISPR/Cas9-based gene editing technologies. The invention provides a targeted therapeutic approach that selectively disrupts disease-causing mutant alleles while preserving the function of wild-type alleles, thereby halting or reversing photoreceptor degeneration and preserving visual function.

## BACKGROUND

Retinitis pigmentosa (RP) is a group of inherited retinal dystrophies characterized by progressive degeneration of photoreceptor cells, leading to vision loss and eventual blindness. RP affects approximately 1 in 4,000 individuals worldwide and manifests clinically with night blindness, peripheral vision loss, and eventual central vision impairment. Diagnosis typically involves electroretinography (ERG), visual field testing, optical coherence tomography (OCT), and genetic screening to identify causative mutations. The disease exhibits significant genetic heterogeneity, with over 80 genes implicated in its pathogenesis, including both autosomal recessive and autosomal dominant forms.

Autosomal dominant RP (adRP) accounts for 30–40% of all RP cases and is primarily caused by gain-of-function or dominant-negative mutations in genes critical for photoreceptor structure and function. Among these, mutations in the rhodopsin (RHO) gene represent the most common cause of adRP, accounting for up to 25% of cases. Unlike recessive forms, where loss-of-function mutations can be addressed by gene replacement strategies, adRP presents a unique therapeutic challenge because the presence of a single mutant allele is sufficient to drive disease pathology, often through toxic protein accumulation or aberrant signaling.

Conventional therapeutic approaches for adRP have included RNA interference (RNAi) to silence mutant transcripts or transcriptional suppression of both alleles followed by exogenous supplementation of wild-type protein. However, these strategies are limited by incomplete allele discrimination, off-target effects, and the logistical complexity of delivering both silencing and replacement components simultaneously. Moreover, long-term expression of exogenous transgenes raises concerns regarding immunogenicity, insertional mutagenesis, and regulatory burden.

The advent of CRISPR (Clustered Regularly Interspaced Short Palindromic Repeat)/Cas9 genome editing technology has revolutionized the potential for precise correction of genetic disorders. CRISPR/Cas9 enables site-specific double-strand breaks (DSBs) in genomic DNA guided by a programmable RNA molecule, facilitating either non-homologous end joining (NHEJ)-mediated gene disruption or homology-directed repair (HDR)-based correction. In the context of adRP, CRISPR offers a compelling strategy for allele-specific ablation—selectively inactivating the mutant allele while leaving the wild-type allele intact—thereby leveraging the endogenous regulatory machinery and avoiding the need for transgene supplementation.

Motivation for in vivo CRISPR therapy in adRP stems from the monogenic nature of many forms of the disease, the accessibility of the retina for localized delivery, and the demonstrated tolerance of photoreceptors to partial reductions in wild-type rhodopsin expression. Indeed, studies have shown that as little as 10% of normal rhodopsin levels can sustain functional phototransduction, and haploinsufficiency of RHO does not result in clinical pathology. These findings support the feasibility of therapeutic strategies that selectively eliminate mutant alleles without requiring concurrent gene replacement.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a method for treating autosomal dominant retinitis pigmentosa (adRP) in a subject in need thereof, comprising administering to the retina of said subject a therapeutically effective amount of a composition comprising a CRISPR-associated (Cas) protein and a guide RNA (gRNA) specifically designed to target a mutant allele of the rhodopsin (RHO) gene associated with adRP. The gRNA is complementary to a target polynucleotide sequence within the mutant RHO allele and is configured to direct Cas-mediated cleavage exclusively at the mutant locus, exploiting a single-nucleotide difference between the mutant and wild-type alleles that alters the protospacer adjacent motif (PAM) sequence required for Cas9 activity.

In one embodiment, the disease is caused by a dominant-negative mutation in the RHO gene, such as the S334ter mutation, which results in a truncated rhodopsin protein lacking critical C-terminal residues necessary for proper trafficking and deactivation. The therapeutic vector encodes a Cas9 nuclease—preferably Staphylococcus aureus Cas9 (SaCas9) for compatibility with adeno-associated virus (AAV) packaging—and a gRNA targeting a PAM sequence unique to the mutant allele (e.g., 5′-TGG-3′ in RhoS334 versus 5′-TGC-3′ in RhoWT). The composition is administered via subretinal injection, enabling localized delivery to photoreceptor cells.

The genomic editing method involves in vivo generation of a double-strand break (DSB) at the mutant RHO locus, followed by error-prone NHEJ repair that introduces insertions or deletions (indels), thereby disrupting the open reading frame and preventing production of the toxic mutant protein. Critically, the wild-type RHO allele remains unedited due to the absence of a compatible PAM sequence, allowing continued expression of functional rhodopsin sufficient to maintain photoreceptor viability and visual function. This approach obviates the need for exogenous gene supplementation and minimizes off-target effects through stringent allele discrimination.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

All publications, patents, and patent applications cited herein are hereby incorporated by reference in their entirety for all purposes. As used herein, the term “administering” refers to the introduction of a pharmaceutical composition into a subject by any suitable route, including but not limited to intravitreal, subretinal, intravenous, or topical ocular delivery. “Modulation” encompasses any alteration in the level, activity, localization, or function of a target gene or protein, including upregulation, downregulation, activation, inhibition, or ablation. A “pharmaceutically acceptable carrier” includes any excipient, diluent, or vehicle that does not interfere with the biological activity of the active ingredients and is compatible with ocular tissues.

The term “promote” as used herein means to enhance, facilitate, or induce a biological process, such as photoreceptor survival or visual function preservation. A “subject” refers to a human or non-human mammal, preferably a human patient diagnosed with or at risk for adRP. A “therapeutically effective amount” is the quantity of the CRISPR composition sufficient to produce a measurable improvement in disease symptoms, such as reduced photoreceptor loss or improved visual acuity, without causing unacceptable toxicity. “Treat” includes prophylactic, palliative, and curative interventions aimed at preventing, slowing, halting, or reversing disease progression.

Retinitis pigmentosa, particularly the autosomal dominant form, is driven by mutations that confer toxic properties to the encoded protein. Current mutation-focused approaches, such as allele-nonspecific knockdown, require co-delivery of a replacement transgene, increasing vector size and regulatory complexity. Gene editing tools based on CRISPR offer a streamlined alternative. In dominant-negative adRP caused by RHO or peripherin (RDS) mutations, the mutant protein misfolds, accumulates in the endoplasmic reticulum, and triggers apoptosis. For example, the P23H mutation in RHO—a common adRP variant—causes protein misfolding and retention in the cell body. Similarly, peripherin mutations disrupt disc morphogenesis in photoreceptor outer segments.

Gene replacement strategies, while successful in recessive disease models, are inadequate for adRP because they do not address the ongoing toxicity of the mutant protein. Allele-specific genomic ablation using CRISPR/Cas9 circumvents this limitation. The S334ter-line-3 rat model expresses a transgenic mouse Rho allele truncated by 15 C-terminal residues due to a premature stop codon at position 334. This RhoS334 protein lacks phosphorylation sites needed for deactivation and a trafficking signal for outer segment localization, resulting in constitutive signaling and proteotoxic stress that drives rapid photoreceptor apoptosis.

We hypothesized that selective ablation of RhoS334 would eliminate this toxicity while permitting endogenous RhoWT to support photoreceptor function. CRISPR/Cas9 was engineered to exploit a single-nucleotide difference in the PAM sequence adjacent to the target site: RhoS334 contains 5′-TGG-3′, whereas RhoWT has 5′-TGC-3′. Since Cas9 requires a canonical PAM (e.g., 5′-NGG-3′ for Streptococcus pyogenes Cas9), only the mutant allele is cleaved. This design ensures unabated function of RhoWT, which is sufficient for phenotypic rescue given the wide therapeutic window of rhodopsin expression (50–200% of normal is asymptomatic).

### Genomic Editing.

Genomic editing refers to the targeted modification of chromosomal DNA sequences in living cells. Site-specific chromosomal integration can be achieved using engineered nucleases that create double-strand breaks (DSBs), which are repaired by cellular mechanisms. Therapeutic gene cassettes may be inserted via homology-directed repair (HDR), but for dominant disorders, disruption of the mutant allele via non-homologous end joining (NHEJ) is often preferable. Earlier tools like zinc finger nucleases (ZFNs) and transcription activator-like effector nucleases (TALENs) enabled targeted cleavage but suffered from complex design, low efficiency, and high cost.

The CRISPR-Cas system, derived from bacterial adaptive immunity, uses a guide RNA (gRNA) to direct the Cas9 nuclease to a complementary DNA sequence adjacent to a protospacer adjacent motif (PAM). Upon binding, Cas9 induces a DSB, which is repaired by NHEJ (introducing indels that disrupt gene function) or HDR (if a donor template is provided). CRISPR offers superior simplicity, multiplexing capability, and efficiency. Multiple gRNAs can be co-expressed for simultaneous editing of several loci.

For in vivo retinal delivery, adeno-associated virus (AAV) vectors are preferred due to their safety profile and tropism for photoreceptors. However, the large size of SpCas9 (~4.2 kb) exceeds AAV’s packaging capacity (~4.7 kb including promoters and regulatory elements). Staphylococcus aureus Cas9 (SaCas9, ~3.3 kb) is a compact ortholog compatible with AAV, especially when paired with short promoters like tRNA or synthetic minimal promoters.

In the context of RhoS334ter transgene ablation, CRISPR/Cas9 enables prophylactic prevention of RP by eliminating the source of toxicity before irreversible degeneration occurs. The method involves subretinal administration of an AAV vector encoding SaCas9 and a gRNA targeting the mutant-specific PAM. In vivo DSB generation leads to NHEJ-mediated frameshift mutations that abolish RhoS334 expression. Because the wild-type allele lacks the PAM, it remains intact, preserving physiological rhodopsin levels.

This approach is applicable to any dominant disorder where a single-nucleotide variant creates a unique PAM. For recessive mutations, HDR-based correction may be employed, but for dominant-negative conditions, ablation is sufficient. The composition may include purified Cas9 protein pre-complexed with gRNA (ribonucleoprotein, RNP) to limit exposure time and reduce off-target effects. In vitro methods involve editing patient-derived cells followed by transplantation, but in vivo editing is preferred for post-mitotic tissues like the retina.

### Animal Model

The S334ter-line-3 rat model is a well-established transgenic model of autosomal dominant retinitis pigmentosa. These rats carry a mouse Rho transgene harboring the S334ter mutation, which introduces a premature stop codon at codon 334, resulting in a truncated rhodopsin protein missing the C-terminal 15 amino acids. This model recapitulates key features of human class-I RHO mutations, including mislocalization of rhodopsin to the photoreceptor cell body, absence of outer segment formation, rapid photoreceptor degeneration beginning at postnatal day 11, and complete loss of the outer nuclear layer by postnatal day 28. The model is ideal for testing allele-specific ablation strategies due to the clear distinction between mutant and wild-type alleles and the cell-autonomous nature of the degeneration.

### CRISPR/Cas9 Constructs, Generally

CRISPR/Cas9 is uniquely suited for targeting RHO mutations in adRP because it can discriminate between alleles differing by a single nucleotide, particularly when that difference alters the PAM sequence essential for Cas9 binding and cleavage. The mechanism relies on the gRNA hybridizing to a 20-nucleotide target sequence immediately upstream of a PAM (5′-NGG-3′ for SpCas9). If the PAM is absent or non-canonical in the wild-type allele, cleavage is restricted to the mutant locus. This precision enables selective ablation without affecting normal gene function, a critical advantage over RNAi or antisense oligonucleotides, which often exhibit incomplete allele selectivity.

### CRISPR/Cas9 Constructs, Specifically

A gRNA was designed to target exon 1 of the RhoS334 allele, complementary to a sequence immediately upstream of a PAM unique to the mutant (5′-TGG-3′). The gRNA sequence differs from the wild-type target by a single mismatch at position 10, but more importantly, the wild-type locus contains 5′-TGC-3′, which is not recognized by SpCas9. This ensures exclusive cleavage of RhoS334. Alternative PAMs in exon 4 were avoided to prevent generation of alternative truncated isoforms, and the 5′-most PAM was excluded due to risks of polymerase skipping restoring the reading frame.

### Methods

CRISPR/Cas9/guide-RNA constructs were cloned into plasmid vectors under the control of cytomegalovirus (CMV) or chicken β-actin promoters, with mCherry as a reporter. Subretinal injection was performed in postnatal day 0 S334ter-3 rats, followed by electroporation to enhance plasmid uptake in dividing photoreceptor precursors. mCherry reporter expression allowed identification of transfected cells. Retinal cells were dissociated and sorted by fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) for mCherry-positive populations, and genomic DNA was extracted for Sanger sequencing to confirm indel formation at the target locus.

Immunohistochemical evaluation using N-terminal and C-terminal RHO antibodies demonstrated mislocalization of RHO S334 to cell bodies in untreated retinas, whereas treated retinas showed exclusive RHO WT localization to outer segments and absence of RHO S334. Photoreceptor preservation was quantified by counting nuclei in the outer nuclear layer, revealing up to eight layers in treated regions versus one in controls. Visual function was assessed by optokinetic response, showing significantly higher spatial acuity in gRNATRGT-treated eyes.

Cas9 cleavage efficiency was determined by clonal sequencing of PCR amplicons, revealing indel frequencies of 33–36% at the mutant locus with no detectable editing at the wild-type allele or predicted off-target sites. Histological rescue was confirmed by cresyl violet staining, and synaptic integrity was preserved as evidenced by maintained dendritic arborization of rod-bipolar cells and synaptic density between photoreceptors and second-order neurons.

Results demonstrated that RhoS334 ablation led to elimination of toxic protein, restoration of RHO WT trafficking, outer segment formation, and long-term photoreceptor survival. Rescue was strictly confined to mCherry-positive regions, confirming cell-autonomous efficacy. Statistical analysis confirmed significant improvements in photoreceptor density and visual acuity (P ≤ 0.01).

Future investigations will explore dual AAV vectors for SaCas9 and gRNA delivery, PR-specific Eosomes, peptide-mediated ocular delivery (POD), and liposome-mediated Cas9 protein delivery to enhance transfection efficiency and reduce immunogenicity. This work represents the first in vivo functional correction of a dominant mutation using CRISPR, establishing a paradigm for treating adRP and other dominant disorders by exploiting monoallelic PAM differences. Engineering Cas variants with altered PAM specificities or using non-canonical Cas enzymes could expand the range of targetable mutations, broadening therapeutic applicability across diverse genetic backgrounds.
