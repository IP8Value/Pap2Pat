# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNOLOGICAL FIELD

The present invention pertains to the field of ophthalmology, specifically to methods and devices for the noninvasive diagnosis and treatment of ocular disorders involving particulate matter within the eye. More particularly, the invention relates to an acoustic manipulation system configured to generate controlled acoustic fields within the anterior chamber of the human eye to influence the movement, aggregation, and spatial distribution of intraocular particles. The technology leverages principles of acoustic radiation forces to safely and effectively manage pathological or therapeutic particulates without surgical intervention, thereby offering a novel approach to prevent secondary complications such as visual axis obstruction, corneal endothelial deposition, and secondary open-angle glaucoma. This invention bridges the gap between diagnostic ultrasound and therapeutic particle control, introducing a paradigm shift in the management of intraocular particulate conditions through precise, externally applied acoustic energy.

## BACKGROUND

Ophthalmic disorders encompass a broad spectrum of conditions affecting vision and ocular health, many of which involve the presence of particulate matter within the aqueous humor of the anterior chamber. Such particulates may arise from various etiologies, including inflammatory processes (e.g., uveitis), hemorrhagic events (e.g., hyphema), degenerative pigment dispersion syndromes (e.g., pigmentary glaucoma), pseudoexfoliation material, or even exogenous therapeutic agents such as intravitreal corticosteroid suspensions like triamcinolone acetonide. These particles, varying in size from submicron to tens of microns, circulate within the low-viscosity aqueous humor and can sediment over time due to gravity. While initially dispersed, their accumulation poses significant clinical risks. Particulate matter may obstruct the trabecular meshwork—the primary drainage pathway for aqueous humor—leading to elevated intraocular pressure and secondary open-angle glaucoma. Additionally, deposition on the corneal endothelium can result in structural and functional compromise, manifesting as Krukenberg’s spindle or chronic corneal edema. Despite the prevalence and morbidity associated with these conditions, current clinical approaches remain largely reactive, focusing on managing complications after they arise rather than proactively mitigating particle-related pathology. Available treatments include medical therapy to reduce inflammation or intraocular pressure, and surgical interventions such as anterior chamber washout or trabeculectomy. However, these options are invasive, carry inherent risks, and do not address the root issue of uncontrolled particle distribution. There exists a critical unmet need for a noninvasive, preventive strategy capable of modulating intraocular particle behavior to preserve visual function and prevent irreversible tissue damage.

## GENERAL DESCRIPTION

The presence of intraocular particulate matter represents a significant source of both short- and long-term ocular morbidity. In the acute phase, particles suspended in the visual axis cause light scattering, glare, and reduced visual acuity. Over time, their interaction with delicate intraocular tissues—particularly the trabecular meshwork and corneal endothelium—can lead to chronic dysfunction. Particle accumulation in the anterior chamber is not merely passive; it is influenced by aqueous humor dynamics, gravitational forces, and Brownian motion, yet remains unmoderated by any endogenous clearance mechanism beyond slow outflow through Schlemm’s canal. This lack of active regulation underscores the necessity for external intervention to moderate particle distribution. The present invention addresses this need by introducing acoustic field-based diagnosis and manipulation as a means to control intraocular particles. Specifically, the invention utilizes acoustic radiation forces generated by precisely tuned ultrasonic transducers to induce predictable movement of particles within the aqueous humor. These forces arise from the interaction between sound waves and particles that differ in density and compressibility from the surrounding fluid—an almost universal condition for biological and synthetic particulates in aqueous environments. When exposed to a standing acoustic wave, such particles migrate toward nodal regions where acoustic pressure is minimal, resulting in controlled aggregation. This phenomenon enables not only the concentration of particles away from critical optical and drainage pathways but also their strategic relocation to less vulnerable regions of the anterior chamber angle. Furthermore, the same acoustic fields can be employed diagnostically: by observing particle response to specific acoustic parameters, clinicians may infer particle size, density, and composition, aiding in differential diagnosis. The invention thus provides a dual-function platform: therapeutic particle management and diagnostic characterization. Preventive treatment is achieved by hastening the controlled movement of particles before they cause obstruction or tissue interaction, thereby preempting secondary complications such as glaucoma or corneal decompensation. Central to this approach is an acoustic device comprising one or more transducers configured to deliver directional acoustic energy into the eye. The selection of acoustic parameters—including frequency, amplitude, waveform (standing or traveling), and beam geometry—is tailored to the physical properties of the target particles and the anatomical constraints of the eye. The acoustic contrast factor, which governs particle responsiveness, is inherently favorable for most intraocular particulates due to their higher density and lower compressibility relative to aqueous humor. Particle size directly influences the magnitude of the acoustic force, with larger particles experiencing greater force, though even submicron particles can be manipulated given sufficient exposure time. The acoustic device may be configured as a handheld probe, a wearable apparatus, or an integrated component of an imaging system. Its radiation pattern is engineered to create defined nodal and antinodal regions within the anterior chamber. A controller regulates transducer operation, enabling dynamic adjustment of acoustic parameters to achieve desired particle trajectories. Acoustic trapping and manipulation are facilitated through phased arrays or mechanical motion stages that shift the acoustic field in real time, allowing particles to be steered to predetermined locations. In some embodiments, a collimating lens or acoustic hologram shapes the wavefront to generate focused beams or complex interference patterns, such as corneal-standing waves, enhancing spatial precision. Acoustic streaming—a steady fluid flow induced by momentum transfer from sound waves—may also be harnessed to assist particle transport. The device housing includes a coupling layer of biocompatible material (e.g., polydimethylsiloxane) with acoustic impedance matched to ocular tissues to ensure efficient energy transmission while preventing thermal or mechanical injury. Through this integrated system, the invention offers a safe, noninvasive solution to a longstanding clinical challenge.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF EMBODIMENTS

The scope of the present invention encompasses a range of embodiments designed for the noninvasive manipulation of intraocular particulate matter using acoustic radiation forces. To contextualize these embodiments, reference is made to the anatomy of the human eyeball. The eye comprises outer features including the cornea, sclera, and conjunctiva, and internal structures such as the iris, lens, ciliary body, and retina. Of particular relevance is the anterior chamber, bounded anteriorly by the cornea and posteriorly by the iris and lens, filled with aqueous humor. Posterior to the lens lies the vitreous chamber, containing the gel-like vitreous humor. An enlarged cross-sectional view reveals the trabecular meshwork located at the iridocorneal angle, adjacent to Schlemm’s canal, which together form the primary aqueous drainage system. In healthy eyes, aqueous humor flows from the posterior chamber through the pupil into the anterior chamber and exits via the trabecular meshwork into Schlemm’s canal, maintaining stable intraocular pressure. Blockage of this outflow pathway by accumulated particles—such as pigment granules, red blood cells, or exfoliative material—leads to elevated pressure and glaucomatous damage. The anomalous presence of such particles in the anterior chamber is the target of the present invention. The ocular acoustic device comprises an acoustic transducer and a controller. The transducer, typically a piezoelectric element such as lead zirconate titanate (PZT), generates ultrasonic waves upon electrical excitation. The controller, which may include a signal generator and power amplifier, regulates the frequency, amplitude, and duration of the acoustic signal. In operation, the device is positioned adjacent to the cornea, with a coupling layer ensuring acoustic continuity. Upon activation, the transducer produces an acoustic field within the anterior chamber, inducing particle movement via radiation forces. The acoustic pattern may be moved dynamically by adjusting transducer position via a motion stage or by electronically steering a phased array. Various transducer configurations are contemplated, including cylindrical resonators for radial standing waves or planar arrays for linear patterns. The controller executes algorithms to generate specific waveforms—standing waves, traveling waves, or holographically shaped fields—based on desired clinical outcomes. An acoustic hologram may be employed to sculpt complex three-dimensional pressure fields, enabling precise particle organization. Similarly, an acoustic lens can focus energy to specific depths. Controller functionality extends to real-time feedback, potentially integrating data from an imaging module (e.g., OCT or ultrasound biomicroscopy) to adapt acoustic parameters during treatment. The controller may be implemented in dedicated hardware or as software executed on a general-purpose computing system, including networked environments for remote operation or data sharing. Storage media may retain treatment protocols or patient-specific calibration data. The ocular acoustic device may further incorporate an imaging module to visualize particle distribution before, during, and after manipulation. Illustrative embodiments depict the device as a handheld probe with integrated transducer, controller, and coupling interface. The transducer operates in transverse vibration modes at frequencies typically between 0.5 and 5 MHz, with resonance frequencies around 1.1 MHz proving effective in experimental models. At these frequencies, standing waves form with nodes spaced approximately 0.75 mm apart in aqueous media, creating periodic trapping zones. The coupling layer, composed of silicone elastomer (~70 µm thick), ensures acoustic impedance matching between the transducer and cornea, minimizing reflection and maximizing energy transfer while providing thermal and mechanical protection. The standing wave pattern exerts forces on particles proportional to their volume and acoustic contrast, driving them to nodal planes. Over time, particles agglomerate into dense rings or clusters, increasing local thickness. A motion stage allows manual or automated repositioning of the transducer relative to the eye, enabling controlled drift and relocation of particle aggregates. Phased array transducers offer electronic beam steering without physical movement, enhancing clinical practicality. In alternative embodiments, planar acoustic waves or traveling waves may be used to induce directional transport rather than static trapping. The method for controlling particle movement involves generating acoustic waves via the transducer, producing a defined acoustic field in the anterior chamber, and modulating field parameters to displace particulate matter to designated locations. This process is nondestructive, reversible, and adaptable to diverse particle types. The scope of the invention includes all such configurations and methods that achieve safe, effective, noninvasive intraocular particle manipulation through acoustic means.
