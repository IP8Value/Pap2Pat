## FIELD

The present invention relates generally to the field of microfabricated electronic devices, and more specifically to three-dimensional (3D) multielectrode arrays for neural interfacing applications. The invention encompasses novel methods for fabricating high-aspect-ratio 3D electrode structures using a combination of two-photon direct laser writing lithography and conventional thin-film photolithographic processes. These 3D multielectrode arrays are particularly suited for chronic implantation in biological tissues, including the central and peripheral nervous systems, for purposes of electrophysiological recording and electrical stimulation. The disclosed technology enables the creation of customizable, high-resolution electrode geometries—including conical, pyramidal, porous, and biomimetic spike-shaped structures—that overcome limitations inherent in traditional planar electrode arrays. The invention further provides integrated fabrication workflows compatible with flexible polymer substrates, such as polyimide and Parylene-C, thereby facilitating the production of conformable neural interfaces suitable for both acute and chronic in vivo use.

## BACKGROUND

Conventional multielectrode arrays used in neuroscience and bioelectronic medicine are predominantly fabricated using planar thin-film lithography techniques. These methods produce flat, two-dimensional electrode sites patterned on rigid or flexible substrates. While such devices have enabled significant advances in neural recording and stimulation, they suffer from several critical limitations that impede their performance in complex biological environments. First, planar electrodes exhibit poor mechanical compatibility with soft, heterogeneous neural tissue, often leading to micromotion-induced inflammation, glial scarring, and eventual signal degradation over time. Second, the surface area-to-footprint ratio of planar electrodes is inherently low, which constrains charge injection capacity for stimulation and increases impedance for recording, thereby limiting signal-to-noise ratio and power efficiency. Third, the fixed geometry of lithographically defined planar arrays offers minimal adaptability to the three-dimensional architecture of target neural structures—such as cortical layers, retinal cell strata, or peripheral nerve fascicles—resulting in suboptimal spatial resolution and functional specificity. Attempts to create raised electrode features using silicon micromachining or post-processing etching have yielded limited success due to material rigidity, lack of geometric flexibility, and incompatibility with flexible substrates required for chronic biocompatibility. Moreover, existing approaches to increasing electrode surface area—such as roughening via iridium oxide deposition or nanostructuring—are constrained to the plane of the substrate and cannot achieve true volumetric tissue integration. Consequently, there remains a pressing need for a scalable, high-resolution fabrication method capable of producing customizable 3D electrode architectures that enhance tissue-electrode intimacy, reduce immune response, improve electrical performance, and enable chronic stability in neural interfacing applications.

## SUMMARY

The present invention introduces a novel class of 3D multielectrode arrays comprising individually addressable, high-aspect-ratio electrode structures fabricated through an integrated process combining two-photon direct laser writing and conventional photolithography. Each 3D electrode comprises a base portion anchored to a flexible substrate, a shaft portion extending vertically from the substrate, and a tip portion configured for electrical contact with biological tissue. The 3D electrodes are formed from a photopolymer material, such as OrmoComp®, which is selectively polymerized via raster-scanning direct laser writing using femtosecond infrared pulses focused through a high-numerical-aperture immersion objective. Following 3D printing, the entire structure is coated with a conductive metal layer—typically titanium and platinum—via non-directional sputter deposition, rendering the printed polymer electrically active. Electrical connection between each 3D electrode and external circuitry is established through underlying metal traces patterned on the substrate during an initial photolithographic step. A key feature of the invention is the conical or spike-like geometry of the 3D electrodes, which facilitates low-force tissue penetration while resisting buckling during insertion, particularly in delicate or dura-covered brain regions. The aspect ratio of these 3D electrodes—defined as the ratio of height to minimum lateral dimension at the tip—exceeds 10:1 and can be tailored to specific anatomical targets, with typical heights ranging from 200 µm to over 1 mm. Critically, only the tip portion of each electrode is left exposed after application of an insulating layer, such as Parylene-C, which is selectively removed via femtosecond laser ablation to define precise recording or stimulation sites. The spacing between adjacent 3D electrodes is controllable down to 90 µm or less, enabling high-density arrays without inducing a “bed-of-nails” insertion artifact. The method of forming the 3D multielectrode array begins with defining electrical contacts and traces on a flexible substrate using standard photolithography. A sacrificial metal layer, typically chromium, is then deposited and patterned to serve as a lift-off mask. After 3D printing of the electrode structures directly onto the substrate-aligned print locations, a conformal metal layer is applied. Subsequent removal of the sacrificial metal layer lifts off excess metal, leaving only the desired traces and metallized 3D electrodes. An insulating layer is then deposited over the entire device, followed by precision laser milling to expose only the electrode tips. This process supports a wide range of substrate materials, including polyimide, Parylene-C, and silicon dioxide, and is compatible with both rigid and flexible platforms. The metal layers may include adhesion-promoting underlayers such as titanium and biocompatible conductors such as platinum, iridium, or gold, selected based on electrochemical requirements.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

Multielectrode arrays are essential tools in modern neuroscience, neuroengineering, and bioelectronic medicine, enabling simultaneous recording and stimulation of neural activity across multiple sites. Traditional implementations rely on planar thin-film lithography to define metal electrode pads on flat substrates. However, these planar arrays suffer from inadequate tissue integration, limited surface area, and mechanical mismatch with soft biological tissues, leading to chronic inflammatory responses and signal degradation. In contrast, the present invention discloses 3D multielectrode arrays featuring raised electrode structures that project vertically from the substrate, thereby achieving superior spatial resolution, enhanced charge transfer, and improved mechanical compliance. The fabrication process integrates high-resolution 3D printing—specifically raster-scanning direct laser writing (rDLW) based on two-photon polymerization—with conventional semiconductor manufacturing techniques. Photolithography, as used herein, refers to the process of patterning light-sensitive materials (photoresists) using ultraviolet or visible light through a photomask to define microscale features. A 3D electronic device, in this context, denotes any microfabricated component wherein at least one functional element extends significantly out of the plane of the substrate, exhibiting a height-to-width ratio greater than unity. The surface area-to-footprint ratio is defined as the total electrochemically active surface area of an electrode divided by its projected area on the substrate; higher ratios correlate with lower impedance and greater charge injection capacity. Aspect ratio, as applied to 3D electrodes, is the quotient of electrode height and the smallest lateral dimension at its narrowest point—typically the tip. The inventive process begins with a prepared substrate, such as a silicon wafer coated with a flexible polymer like polyimide. Standard photolithography is employed to pattern metal traces and contact pads using a sacrificial chromium layer that survives subsequent 3D printing development steps where conventional photoresists would dissolve. Windows are opened in an insulating layer to expose regions for electrical connection to future 3D structures. The rDLW printer, equipped with a resonant raster scanner and galvanometer-controlled mirrors, uses a femtosecond laser beam focused through an immersion objective to polymerize a biocompatible photopolymer (e.g., OrmoComp®) at sub-micron resolution. Real-time fluorescence imaging ensures micron-level alignment between the 3D print and the underlying substrate features. After development and UV curing, the wafer undergoes plasma cleaning and sputter deposition of Ti/Pt. Lift-off of the Cr layer defines the final metal traces and metallized 3D electrodes. A conformal Parylene-C insulation layer is then applied, and femtosecond laser ablation selectively removes insulation only from the electrode tips, creating well-defined electroactive sites without thermal damage or redeposition. Alternative embodiments include porous 3D electrodes with internal channels or mesh-like architectures to promote tissue ingrowth and vascularization, conical spikes with barbed extension members for anchoring, and pyramidal structures with tunable pore sizes. Customization is achieved by modifying the CAD model prior to printing, allowing adaptation to specific brain regions or nerve morphologies. Electrodes may be further functionalized via iridium oxide electrodeposition or plasma roughening to enhance surface area. The resulting 3D multielectrode arrays enable chronic, high-fidelity neural recordings and efficient stimulation with reduced power consumption, making them ideal for applications ranging from basic neuroscience research to clinical neural prostheses.
