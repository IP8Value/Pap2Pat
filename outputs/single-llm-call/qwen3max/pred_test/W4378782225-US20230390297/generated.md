# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

The Mixed-Lineage Leukemia 3 (MLL3) and Mixed-Lineage Leukemia 4 (MLL4) proteins, encoded by the human genes KMT2C and KMT2D respectively, are core catalytic subunits of the COMPASS (COMplex of Proteins Associated with Set1) family of histone methyltransferase complexes. These enzymes function predominantly as monomethyltransferases for histone H3 at lysine 4 (H3K4me1), a chromatin mark strongly associated with active and primed enhancer elements across the genome. MLL3 and MLL4 are not only enzymatically active but also serve as scaffolding platforms that recruit additional chromatin-modifying cofactors such as the H3K27 demethylase UTX (KDM6A) and the histone acetyltransferase p300 to enhancer regions. Through these dual roles—catalytic deposition of H3K4me1 and non-catalytic recruitment of transcriptional coactivators—MLL3 and MLL4 orchestrate enhancer activation, thereby regulating the expression of genes critical for cellular identity, differentiation, and developmental progression.

During mammalian development, MLL3 and MLL4 exhibit stage-specific and partially redundant functions. MLL4 is essential for post-implantation embryogenesis and its activity demonstrates dosage sensitivity, whereas MLL3 becomes indispensable during late gestation, particularly for organ maturation. In mouse embryonic stem cells (mESCs), although MLL4 is dispensable for maintaining pluripotency and self-renewal under standard culture conditions, it is required for the timely exit from the pluripotent state and for proper lineage commitment. This underscores the role of MLL3/4 in facilitating cell fate transitions rather than sustaining undifferentiated states. The functional importance of these proteins extends beyond development into disease pathogenesis. Large-scale genomic analyses, notably by the Catalogue Of Somatic Mutations In Cancer (COSMIC) consortium, have revealed that KMT2C and KMT2D are among the most frequently mutated epigenetic regulators across a wide spectrum of human cancers, including hematological malignancies and solid tumors such as breast, bladder, colorectal, and prostate cancers. These mutations are predominantly loss-of-function variants—including frameshifts, nonsense mutations, and splice-site alterations—that truncate the protein and abolish catalytic SET domain activity or disrupt complex integrity. Moreover, germline heterozygous loss-of-function mutations in KMT2D cause Kabuki syndrome, a multisystem congenital disorder characterized by intellectual disability, skeletal anomalies, and immune dysfunction, further highlighting the non-redundant physiological roles of MLL4.

Despite the well-documented prevalence of MLL3/4 mutations in cancer and developmental disorders, the molecular consequences of their loss—and the resulting cellular vulnerabilities—have remained poorly understood. While it is established that MLL3/4 deficiency leads to reduced H3K4me1 and H3K27ac at enhancers and consequent downregulation of lineage-specifying genes, the downstream metabolic and signaling adaptations that arise in response to this epigenetic perturbation are not fully elucidated. Recent evidence suggests that epigenetic dysregulation can rewire cellular metabolism, creating context-specific dependencies that may be therapeutically exploitable. However, prior to the present invention, no systematic link had been established between MLL3/4 loss and altered nucleotide metabolism, nor had any synthetic lethal interaction been identified that could be leveraged for targeted therapy in MLL3/4-deficient cancers.

## SUMMARY

The present invention discloses a novel therapeutic strategy for treating cancers harboring loss-of-function mutations in MLL3 and/or MLL4 (collectively referred to as MLL3/4 COMPASS-deficient cancers) through the administration of nucleotide synthesis inhibitors. Specifically, the invention is based on the discovery that genetic ablation or functional impairment of MLL3 and MLL4 induces a synthetic lethal dependency on de novo nucleotide biosynthesis pathways, rendering affected cells exquisitely sensitive to pharmacological inhibition of purine and pyrimidine production. This vulnerability arises due to compensatory upregulation of nucleotide metabolic enzymes and increased metabolic flux through de novo purine synthesis, a phenotype consistently observed in both murine embryonic stem cell models and human cancer cell lines bearing MLL3/4 mutations.

The method for treating MLL3/4-deficient cancer comprises administering to a subject in need thereof an effective amount of a nucleotide synthesis inhibitor. In preferred embodiments, the inhibitor targets de novo purine synthesis. Exemplary de novo purine synthesis inhibitors include lometrexol (LTX), which inhibits glycinamide ribonucleotide formyltransferase (GART); pelitrexol, another GART inhibitor; and methotrexate (MTX), which inhibits dihydrofolate reductase (DHFR), thereby depleting tetrahydrofolate cofactors essential for purine ring assembly. Additional purine pathway inhibitors encompass compounds targeting phosphoribosylamine-glycine ligase (PPAT), phosphoribosylformylglycinamidine synthase (PFAS), phosphoribosylaminoimidazole-succinocarboxamide synthase (PAICS), adenylosuccinate lyase (ADSL), adenylosuccinate synthetase (AdSS), and guanosine monophosphate synthetase (GMPS).

For de novo pyrimidine synthesis inhibition, the invention contemplates the use of CAD (carbamoyl-phosphate synthetase 2, aspartate transcarbamylase, and dihydroorotase) inhibitors, dihydroorotate dehydrogenase (DHODH) inhibitors such as brequinar and leflunomide, and LIMPS (likely referring to upstream regulators or alternative nomenclature for pyrimidine enzymes, interpreted herein as encompassing known pyrimidine synthesis modulators). Combinations of purine and pyrimidine synthesis inhibitors may also be employed to enhance therapeutic efficacy or overcome potential resistance mechanisms.

The method is applicable to a broad range of cancers characterized by MLL3/4 loss-of-function mutations, including but not limited to colorectal cancer, breast cancer, bladder cancer, non-Hodgkin’s lymphoma, medulloblastoma, and prostate cancer. A companion diagnostic aspect of the invention involves testing a biological sample from the subject for the presence of a biomarker indicative of MLL3/4 COMPASS deficiency—such as truncating mutations in KMT2C or KMT2D, reduced protein expression of MLL3/4 or associated COMPASS subunits (e.g., NCOA6, UTX), or a gene expression signature reflective of enhancer dysfunction—and administering the nucleotide synthesis inhibitor only if the biomarker is detected, thereby enabling precision oncology.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present patent disclosure provides methods and compositions for the treatment of cancers deficient in MLL3 and/or MLL4 COMPASS complex activity. The invention arises from the unexpected discovery that loss of MLL3/4 function creates a metabolic dependency on de novo nucleotide synthesis, establishing a synthetic lethal interaction that can be therapeutically exploited. As used herein, “MLL3/4 COMPASS deficient cancer” refers to a neoplasm in which at least one allele of KMT2C and/or KMT2D is inactivated by mutation, deletion, epigenetic silencing, or other mechanism leading to reduced or absent functional protein, resulting in impaired H3K4 mono-methylation at enhancers and dysregulation of enhancer-driven gene expression programs.

The method for treating such cancers comprises administering to a subject in need of treatment a nucleotide synthesis inhibitor in an effective amount. A “subject in need of treatment” is a human or non-human mammal diagnosed with or suspected of having an MLL3/4-deficient cancer, as confirmed by biomarker analysis. MLL3 and MLL4 are members of the COMPASS family of H3K4 methyltransferases, which also includes SET1A, SET1B, MLL1, and MLL2. Unlike MLL1/2, which primarily catalyze H3K4 trimethylation at promoters, MLL3/4 function predominantly at distal enhancers to deposit H3K4me1, facilitating enhancer activation through recruitment of p300 and UTX. Loss-of-function mutations in MLL3/4—particularly truncations that eliminate the C-terminal SET catalytic domain—are common in human cancers and lead to global reduction of H3K4me1 at enhancers, downregulation of differentiation-associated genes, and acquisition of proliferative or stem-like properties.

De novo nucleotide synthesis is the metabolic pathway by which cells generate purine and pyrimidine nucleotides from simple precursors, including amino acids (glutamine, glycine, aspartate), carbon dioxide, and folate-derived one-carbon units. This pathway is distinct from the salvage pathway, which recycles preformed nucleobases. Nucleotide synthesis inhibitors are compounds that block key enzymatic steps in these pathways. De novo purine synthesis inhibitors include lometrexol (a potent and selective GART inhibitor), pelitrexol (also targeting GART), and methotrexate (which indirectly inhibits purine synthesis by depleting reduced folate pools via DHFR inhibition). Other targets include PPAT, PFAS, PAICS, ADSL, AdSS, and GMPS, each catalyzing sequential steps in purine ring assembly. De novo pyrimidine synthesis inhibitors include CAD inhibitors (targeting the multifunctional enzyme initiating pyrimidine synthesis), DHODH inhibitors such as brequinar and leflunomide (blocking conversion of dihydroorotate to orotate), and other agents affecting pyrimidine biosynthesis.

Pharmaceutical compositions of the invention comprise an effective amount of a nucleotide synthesis inhibitor together with a pharmaceutically acceptable excipient, carrier, or diluent. Suitable carriers include water-containing liquid carriers (e.g., saline, buffered aqueous solutions), solid carriers (e.g., lactose, starch, cellulose derivatives), buffering agents (e.g., phosphate, citrate, acetate buffers) to maintain pH in the range of approximately 4 to 8, antioxidants (e.g., ascorbic acid, tocopherols), and stabilizing delivery vehicles (e.g., liposomes, nanoparticles). The term “effective amount” refers to a quantity sufficient to inhibit tumor growth or induce tumor regression in an MLL3/4-deficient cancer without causing unacceptable toxicity. The terms “about,” “approximately,” “substantially,” and “significantly” denote variations within ±10% of a stated value unless otherwise defined. ### EXAMPLES

The invention was developed using a synthetic lethal screening approach in mouse embryonic stem cells (mESCs). An Mll3/4 double-knockout (KO) mESC line was generated by CRISPR/Cas9-mediated deletion of exons 8–9 in Kmt2c (Mll3) and exons 16–22 in Kmt2d (Mll4). Knockout was confirmed by Western blot using antibodies against N- and C-terminal regions of MLL3 and MLL4, as well as COMPASS subunits NCOA6, UTX, and PTIP, all of which were diminished in KO cells. ChIP-seq for H3K4me1 and H3K27Ac revealed significant reduction of these active enhancer marks at MLL4-bound regions, particularly in a subset termed Group2, which correlated with downregulation of nearby genes involved in cytoskeleton and junction organization. Pluripotency markers (Pou5f1, Nanog) and baseline growth rates were unchanged, confirming that MLL3/4 are dispensable for self-renewal but essential for enhancer-mediated differentiation programs.

A genome-wide dropout screen using the Brie CRISPR knockout library in WT versus MLL3/4-KO mESCs identified 300 genes whose loss selectively impaired viability in KO cells. Enrichment analysis revealed strong representation of de novo purine and pyrimidine synthesis enzymes (e.g., Ppat, Mthfd1, Gart, Paics). β-score analysis confirmed KO-specific essentiality. Validation via competitive co-culture of GFP-labeled WT and mCherry-labeled KO cells infected with individual sgRNAs demonstrated preferential depletion of KO cells upon knockdown of nucleotide synthesis genes.

Metabolomic profiling by LC-MS/MS showed altered steady-state levels of nucleotide intermediates in KO cells. Isotope tracing with 15N-glutamine revealed increased flux through de novo purine—but not pyrimidine—synthesis, while 13C-hypoxanthine tracing indicated modest upregulation of the purine salvage pathway. Consistent with this metabolic rewiring, MLL3/4-KO cells exhibited heightened sensitivity to lometrexol (LTX), a GART inhibitor, which was fully rescued by hypoxanthine supplementation. Sensitivity extended to methotrexate and SHIN1 (a serine hydroxymethyltransferase inhibitor), confirming dependence on folate-mediated one-carbon metabolism.

RNA-seq after LTX treatment revealed genotype-specific transcriptional responses: KO cells showed exaggerated downregulation of cytoskeletal genes and upregulation of stress-response pathways. Notably, LTX sensitivity was preserved in full KO but not in ΔSET catalytic-dead mutants, indicating that non-catalytic scaffolding functions of MLL3/4 are primarily responsible for the observed vulnerability. TMT proteomics identified upregulation of 59 purine metabolism proteins in KO cells, corroborating transcriptomic and metabolic data.

Mechanistically, loss of MLL3/4 triggered compensatory binding of MLL1/COMPASS at promoters of purine metabolism genes, increasing H3K4me3 and driving their expression. Inhibition of MLL1-WDR5 interaction (but not Menin-MLL1/2) selectively reduced viability of KO cells, confirming MLL1 compensation as a key driver. Hi-C analysis revealed higher-order chromatin changes, including increased A-compartmentalization and B-to-A shifts at loci encoding upregulated genes, suggesting structural contributions to transcriptional reprogramming.

In human cancers, MLL4-mutant colorectal cell lines (e.g., HCT116, RKO) displayed elevated de novo purine flux and selective LTX sensitivity compared to MLL4-WT lines (e.g., HT55, Caco2). Knockdown of GART or PAICS impaired growth only in mutant lines. RNA-seq after LTX treatment in mutant cells uncovered a conserved downregulation of mitotic genes (e.g., PLK1, AURKA), defining a LTX-responsive mitotic signature. In vivo, LTX (25 mg/kg) significantly suppressed HCT116 xenograft growth and extended survival, with minimal effect on HT55 tumors, validating MLL4 status as a predictive biomarker.

These findings demonstrate that MLL3/4 deficiency creates a targetable metabolic dependency, providing a rationale for clinical evaluation of nucleotide synthesis inhibitors—particularly lometrexol—in patients with MLL3/4-mutant cancers. The integration of epigenetic and metabolic profiling enables patient stratification and personalized therapeutic intervention.
