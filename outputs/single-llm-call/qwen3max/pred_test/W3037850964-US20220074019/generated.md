# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates to a novel and environmentally sustainable process for the extraction of rare earth elements (REEs) from REE-bearing geological materials, particularly clay-rich sedimentary deposits associated with coal seams and coal utilization byproducts. The invention specifically encompasses a method employing an organic acid-based solution (OAS), comprising at least one organic acid in combination with a salt, to selectively leach REEs under mild ambient conditions of temperature and pH. This process is designed to efficiently liberate ion-exchangeable and weakly bound REEs from aluminosilicate-rich clay matrices while minimizing the co-dissolution of gangue elements and radioactive contaminants, thereby providing a cleaner, more cost-effective, and socially acceptable alternative to conventional mineral acid leaching or high-temperature pyrometallurgical methods for domestic REE production.

## BACKGROUND

The global transition toward low-carbon and renewable energy technologies has created an unprecedented demand for rare earth elements (REEs), which are critical components in permanent magnets, batteries, catalysts, and numerous high-tech applications. Despite possessing significant domestic resources, the United States remains heavily reliant on foreign sources, primarily China, for its REE supply, creating a strategic vulnerability in its energy and manufacturing sectors. Consequently, there is an urgent national imperative to develop secure, domestic sources of these critical minerals. A promising avenue identified by the U.S. Department of Energy is the recovery of REEs from domestic coal and coal utilization byproducts (CUB), such as mining waste rock and preparation plant refuse, which often contain clay-rich horizons known as underclays that can be enriched in REEs.

The current global REE market is dominated by Chinese production, which leverages unique geologic resources known as ion-adsorption clays. These deposits, formed by the intense weathering of granitic rocks, host a substantial portion of their REE content as ions adsorbed onto clay mineral surfaces, making them amenable to in-situ solution mining using lixiviants like ammonium sulfate. However, conventional extraction methods for most other REE ores, including those from hard rock sources like bastnaesite, typically rely on aggressive inorganic acids such as hydrochloric or sulfuric acid. These processes are not only highly corrosive and energy-intensive but also generate large volumes of hazardous waste, including acidic tailings and co-extracted radioactive thorium and uranium, posing significant environmental and remediation challenges.

These environmental concerns, coupled with a strong societal aversion to traditional mining activities due to their ecological footprint, have spurred the search for greener extraction technologies. While the Chinese ion-adsorption clay model offers a template for low-impact recovery, similar high-grade deposits are not widespread in the United States. Therefore, a method is needed that can effectively target the REE fraction in more common, lower-grade domestic resources like coal-associated clays. In-situ solution mining, a technique used for uranium and potash, presents a potentially less invasive approach, but its application to REEs has been limited by the inefficacy and environmental hazards of traditional inorganic acid lixiviants. Some research has explored bioleaching using fungi or bacteria that produce organic acids, but these biological systems are often slow, difficult to control at scale, and sensitive to process conditions. Thus, there remains a critical, unmet need for a robust, controllable, and environmentally benign chemical process that can selectively and efficiently extract REEs from a variety of domestic, clay-rich feedstocks under mild operating conditions.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides a novel and efficient process for the extraction of rare earth elements (REEs) and other critical metals from REE-bearing materials, particularly clay-rich sedimentary deposits such as underclays associated with coal seams and various coal utilization byproducts. The core of this process is the use of an Organic Acid-based Solution (OAS) that operates effectively at ambient temperatures and within a near-neutral to mildly acidic pH range. The OAS is specifically formulated to comprise at least one organic acid, a salt, and may further include chelating compounds, surfactants, and biocides to enhance performance and stability. A key embodiment of the OAS utilizes citric acid or its salts, such as sodium citrate, in combination with a monovalent salt like sodium chloride or ammonium sulfate. The general formula for the primary active component of the OAS can be represented by a water-soluble organic acid anion capable of forming stable complexes with trivalent metal cations, with citrate being a preferred example.

The process steps involve contacting the REE-bearing material with the OAS under controlled conditions. The OAS is buffered to maintain a pH within a specific range, typically between 3 and 6, which has been found to optimize the selective leaching of REEs while suppressing the dissolution of undesirable gangue elements like aluminum, silicon, and iron. The organic acid component acts through multiple mechanisms: it provides anions that complex with REE cations in solution, stabilizing them and driving the leaching equilibrium forward; it can interact with the clay surface to modify its zeta potential, promoting the dispersion of clay particles and increasing the accessibility of adsorbed REEs; and it may facilitate the partial, selective dissolution of certain REE-bearing mineral phases, such as phosphates. The salt component provides a source of exchangeable cations (e.g., Na⁺, NH₄⁺) that displace the REE cations from their adsorption sites on the clay minerals via an ion-exchange mechanism. This synergistic action of complexation and ion exchange enables the OAS to recover a significantly higher fraction of the total REE content compared to conventional salt-only lixiviants.

Following the leaching step, the pregnant leach solution (PLS), now enriched in REEs, is separated from the solid residue. This PLS can then be subjected to downstream processing, such as sorbent capture using specialized resins, to selectively concentrate and purify the REEs into a final REY (rare earths and yttrium) concentrate. The process is highly versatile and can be adapted to various operational modes, including ex-situ heap leaching of stockpiled coal wastes or in-situ recovery (ISR) within an open-pit or underground mine setting, where the OAS is injected through wells and the PLS is recovered from production wells. The key advantages of this invention include its operation at ambient temperature and pressure, its high selectivity for REEs over gangue and radioactive elements, its use of relatively non-toxic and biodegradable reagents, and its applicability to a wide range of domestic, clay-rich REE resources, thereby offering a sustainable and economically viable pathway for securing a domestic REE supply chain.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present patent application describes a novel and industrially applicable process for the extraction of rare earth elements (REEs) from geological materials. The accompanying drawings, which are incorporated herein by reference, illustrate flow charts for exemplary embodiments of the process, including an Ex-Situ Heap Recovery (ESHR) configuration and an In-Situ Recovery (ISR) configuration, detailing the sequence of unit operations involved. The motivation for this invention stems from the strategic need to establish a secure, domestic source of REEs, essential for national security and economic competitiveness, by leveraging abundant domestic resources such as coal-related wastes that are currently underutilized.

The REE extraction process of the present invention is predicated on the unique geochemistry of certain sedimentary deposits. In many regions, REE-rich host rocks, such as igneous intrusions or volcanic ashes, have undergone extensive chemical weathering over geologic time. This weathering process breaks down primary minerals, releasing REE ions into solution. These mobile REE ions are subsequently transported by groundwater and accumulate in secondary environments, most notably in clay-rich sediments. The formation of these aluminosilicate-rich clay deposits, such as lateritic soils or, in the context of this invention, underclays associated with coal seams, provides an ideal trap for REEs. The clay minerals, including kaolinite, halloysite, illite, and smectite, possess a high cation exchange capacity (CEC) due to their negatively charged surfaces, which readily adsorb the positively charged REE³⁺ ions from percolating fluids. In some cases, REEs can also be deposited within coal-forming peats or become associated with authigenic minerals like apatite, rhabdophane, monazite, xenotime, and crandallite during diagenesis. The mobilization and accumulation of REE ions in these clay-rich sediments create a resource that is distinct from hard-rock REE ores, as a significant portion of the REE inventory is present in a weakly bound, ion-exchangeable form that is amenable to hydrometallurgical recovery.

The present invention capitalizes on this geology by employing a tailored leaching strategy. Extensive research has demonstrated that the optimum leaching conditions for recovering these ion-exchangeable REEs involve a balance between providing sufficient exchangeable cations and maintaining a solution chemistry that promotes REE complexation without causing excessive matrix dissolution. This has led to the motivation for using organic acids, which offer a dual functionality. The benefits of organic acid leaching are manifold: they are generally less corrosive and more environmentally benign than mineral acids; their anions act as effective ligands to form stable, soluble complexes with REEs, preventing their re-adsorption; and their dissociation behavior allows for precise pH buffering, which is crucial for selectivity. This methodology is not limited to coal underclays but is broadly applicable to other geological materials where REEs are hosted in a similar manner, such as certain shales, sandstones, and bauxitic residues.

A representative flow chart of the REE extraction process involves first preparing the REE-bearing material, which may include crushing and sizing for ex-situ operations. The material is then contacted with the OAS. This contacting step can be performed in a variety of reactors or heaps, with the key parameters being a liquid-to-solid ratio, a contact time, and maintenance of the solution at ambient temperature (e.g., 20-25°C) and a controlled pH (e.g., 3-6). The OAS solution composition is critical and typically comprises an organic acid, such as citric, acetic, or oxalic acid, at a concentration of 0.01 M to 1.0 M, combined with a salt, such as NaCl, KCl, or (NH₄)₂SO₄, at a concentration of 0.1 M to 2.0 M. The use of organic acids in conjunction with salts creates a synergistic effect: the salt provides the ionic strength and exchangeable cations necessary for displacing REEs from clay surfaces, while the organic acid anion complexes the liberated REEs, enhancing overall recovery and selectivity. This combined approach offers significant benefits over using either component alone, as it achieves higher REE yields with lower co-dissolution of impurities.

The process is highly adaptable and can be applied in different modes of REE extraction. For instance, it is particularly well-suited for the treatment of clay-rich coal-related wastes, such as refuse piles or preparation plant tailings, which represent a vast, untapped domestic resource. A key feature of the invention is its ability to achieve the selective removal of REEs and other critical metals while leaving behind the bulk of the aluminosilicate matrix and minimizing the leaching of problematic elements like thorium and uranium. This selectivity is a direct result of the carefully controlled chemistry of the OAS, which targets the specific bonding environment of the desired metals.

### Experimental Studies

A series of controlled experiments were conducted to validate the efficacy of the OAS. In a standard benchtop test, a 10:1 liquid-to-solid ratio was employed at 22°C for 24 hours with a solution pH of 5. These tests demonstrated that a treatment using a combination of citrate and sodium chloride yielded REE concentrations in the pregnant leach solution (PLS) that were directly comparable to, and in many cases superior to, those achieved with conventional lixiviants like (NH₄)₂SO₄ and HCl. Further experiments using a 0.1M solution of the organic acid Indole-3-acetic acid confirmed the broader potential of organic acids as selective lixiviants for REE recovery. Notably, throughout these leaching experiments, the final pH of the solution remained remarkably stable and similar to the initial pH, highlighting the inherent buffering capacity of the OAS formulations, which is a significant operational advantage.

To simulate field-scale in-situ conditions, experimental flow-through tests were performed at 25°C under a pore pressure of approximately 2,000 psi. The fluid flow rate was varied between 0.03 and 0.05 ml/min, and the leaching solution consisted of 0.1M sodium citrate and 0.5M NaCl, with tests running for up to 24 hours. These dynamic tests analyzed the effect of particle size fractions on REE extraction efficiency and conclusively showed that isolating the finest clay particles (<0.002 mm) led to a marked increase in extraction efficiency due to their higher surface area.

The feedstock for these studies was a characterized Appalachian Basin underclay. Mineralogical analysis identified the primary clay minerals as halloysite, kaolinite, smectite, and illite, while the non-clay phases included quartz, K-feldspar, carbonates, and ilmenite. Critically, the REE mineral phases were identified as apatite, rhabdophane, monazite, xenotime, and crandallite. A sequential digestion of the underclay powders was performed to understand REE speciation. This analysis indicated that a significant portion of the REEs were bound in residual, refractory phases, which showed high recovery only upon strong acid digestion with HCl. However, it also revealed that a meaningful fraction of REEs were associated with more labile phases, such as carbonates or Fe/Mn oxides, which are prime targets for the mild OAS.

An Ex-Situ Heap Recovery (ESHR) example was developed, with a corresponding flow chart depicting the process. This embodiment uses coal wastes as the primary feedstock. The core of the ESHR process is an ion exchange step that utilizes a dilute organic acid, such as citric acid, in combination with one or more monovalent salts. The process operates under mild conditions, including ambient temperatures, a controlled acid-to-dry-coal ratio, and a pH-buffered system to ensure optimal and selective REE recovery.

In parallel, an In-Situ Recovery (ISR) example was outlined, complete with a detailed flow diagram. This configuration is designed for direct in-situ extraction and recovery within an existing open-pit or underground mine. The ISR process can utilize either a liquid-phase OAS or a hot gas to facilitate ion exchange. It involves standard drilling and well construction techniques to establish an injection and production well network. During operations, the OAS is injected into the ore zone, where it contacts the REE-bearing material, and the resulting PLS is recovered from the production wells. The PLS is then filtered to remove solids and may be subjected to drying or calcining to produce a final REY concentrate that also contains other valuable critical metals. The collective results of these experimental studies and process examples underscore the significant advantages of using organic acids for REE extraction, namely their high selectivity, environmental compatibility, and operational simplicity under ambient conditions.
