## BACKGROUND

The blood-brain barrier (BBB) represents a formidable physiological obstacle to the delivery of therapeutic agents to the central nervous system, as it restricts the passage of most molecules from the bloodstream into brain tissue. This barrier is formed by tightly joined endothelial cells that line cerebral capillaries and express efflux transporters, metabolic enzymes, and low rates of transcytosis, thereby limiting the bioavailability of many drugs. Consequently, there exists a significant unmet need for strategies that can facilitate the transport of therapeutics across the BBB without compromising its protective function. Receptor-mediated transport systems have emerged as promising avenues to overcome this limitation, leveraging endogenous cellular uptake mechanisms to ferry cargo across biological barriers. Among these, the transferrin receptor (TfR) pathway has garnered considerable attention due to its high expression on brain endothelial cells and its natural role in iron homeostasis via clathrin-mediated endocytosis.

A key feature of many intracellular trafficking pathways is their dependence on pH-induced conformational changes in proteins, which are driven by the protonation states of specific amino acid residues. Histidine, with a side-chain pKa typically ranging between 5.5 and 6.5 in protein environments, plays a pivotal role in mediating such pH-sensitive interactions. Proteins have evolved to exploit histidine’s unique ionization properties to regulate binding, release, and recycling events within the acidic milieu of endosomes and lysosomes. This principle has been harnessed in therapeutic protein engineering to modulate pharmacokinetics and intracellular fate. For instance, modifications to the Fc region of monoclonal antibodies to enhance binding to the neonatal Fc receptor (FcRn) at acidic pH have extended serum half-life, while engineered dissociation at neutral pH ensures efficient release into circulation.

Transferrin receptor (TfR) serves as an attractive target for therapeutic intervention not only because of its role in iron metabolism but also due to its elevated expression in many cancer cells, which require increased iron for proliferation. The natural ligand, transferrin, binds TfR with high affinity at physiological pH (7.2–7.4), triggering internalization. Within acidified endosomes (pH 5.0–6.0), iron is released, and the resulting apo-transferrin exhibits increased affinity for TfR, facilitating receptor recycling to the cell surface where it dissociates at neutral pH. Cytotoxic conjugates of transferrin or anti-TfR antibodies have been explored as targeted cancer therapeutics, with efficacy linked to the extent of cellular association and internalization. However, conventional anti-TfR antibodies often remain bound to TfR throughout the endosomal-lysosomal pathway, leading to degradation and limiting therapeutic payload delivery.

To address these limitations, there is a compelling need for antibodies that exhibit pH-dependent binding—maintaining high affinity at physiological pH for efficient targeting and rapid dissociation in acidic endosomes to escape lysosomal degradation and enable enhanced intracellular accumulation or transcytosis. Such pH-sensitive antibodies would decouple internalization from post-endocytic trafficking dynamics, thereby improving the efficiency of drug delivery across biological barriers like the BBB or into tumor cells.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a pH-dependent antibody that specifically binds to the transferrin receptor (TfR) with high affinity at neutral pH (approximately 7.4) and rapidly dissociates from TfR at acidic pH (approximately 5.5), mimicking the natural pH-responsive behavior of transferrin but engineered for superior intracellular release. This antibody exhibits enhanced cellular internalization and accumulation compared to conventional anti-TfR antibodies, making it particularly suitable for targeted delivery of therapeutic agents across the blood-brain barrier or into cancer cells.

In preferred embodiments, the pH-dependent antibody is a monoclonal antibody, humanized antibody, chimeric antibody, or recombinant human antibody, and may be full-length IgG or an antibody fragment such as a single-chain variable fragment (scFv), Fab, or F(ab')₂. The pH-sensitive binding property is conferred by the strategic incorporation of histidine residues within one or more complementarity-determining regions (CDRs), particularly within the heavy chain CDR1 (CDRH1), where multiple histidines in central positions (e.g., VH31–VH34) create a protonation-sensitive interface that destabilizes antigen binding under acidic conditions.

The invention further encompasses a method of delivering a therapeutic agent to a target cell or across a biological barrier, comprising administering a pharmaceutical composition comprising the pH-dependent antibody conjugated to or associated with a therapeutic agent. The method exploits the antibody’s ability to bind TfR at the cell surface, undergo endocytosis, and release its cargo upon acidification of the endosomal compartment, thereby enhancing intracellular bioavailability. Various embodiments include formulations for systemic, intravenous, or localized administration, as well as kits and therapeutic devices incorporating the pH-dependent antibody for diagnostic or therapeutic applications.

## DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

The scope of the present invention encompasses pH-dependent antibodies that bind the transferrin receptor (TfR) in a pH-sensitive manner, along with pharmaceutical compositions, methods of use, and related products. As used herein, “subject” refers to a human or non-human animal, including mammals such as primates, rodents, and companion animals, to whom the invention may be administered. “Administering” or “administration” denotes any route of delivery, including intravenous, intraperitoneal, subcutaneous, oral, intranasal, or intrathecal, and may be performed by a healthcare provider or self-administered. “Contacting” or “exposing” refers to bringing the pH-dependent antibody into proximity with a cell, tissue, or biological barrier such that binding to TfR occurs.

A “pharmaceutical composition” comprises the pH-dependent antibody in combination with a pharmaceutically acceptable carrier, diluent, or excipient. An “effective amount” is that quantity sufficient to achieve a desired therapeutic or diagnostic effect without undue toxicity. Suitable carriers include aqueous solutions such as saline or buffered solutions (e.g., phosphate-buffered saline), as well as non-aqueous vehicles like oils or polyethylene glycols. Controlled or sustained-release formulations may employ biodegradable polymers, liposomes, or nanoparticles to prolong exposure.

An “antibody” includes full-length immunoglobulins and functional fragments thereof. A “monoclonal antibody” is derived from a single clone and exhibits uniform specificity. “Human monoclonal antibodies” are fully human in sequence, while “chimeric antibodies” combine human constant regions with non-human variable regions. “Humanized antibodies” retain only the CDRs from a non-human source grafted onto a human framework. “Recombinant human antibodies” are produced using genetic engineering in host cells.

Antibody fragments include Fab (antigen-binding fragment), Fab' (with hinge-region thiol), F(ab')₂ (dimeric Fab'), scFv (single-chain variable fragment linking VH and VL via a peptide linker), Fv (non-covalent VH-VL pair), dsFv (disulfide-stabilized Fv), Fd (VH-CH1 fragment), dAbs (domain antibodies), and TandAbs (tandem diabodies). These are produced by proteolytic cleavage, recombinant expression, or chemical synthesis.

The variable regions—light chain (VL) and heavy chain (VH)—contain three complementarity-determining regions (CDRs) each, which form the antigen-binding site. CDRH1, CDRH2, and CDRH3 refer to the heavy chain CDRs. A “pH-sensitive antibody” is defined as one that exhibits at least a two-fold reduction in antigen-binding affinity when pH is lowered from 7.4 to 5.5, preferably dissociating rapidly (<10 minutes) under acidic conditions.

The transferrin receptor (TfR) is a transmembrane glycoprotein that mediates cellular uptake of iron-bound transferrin via clathrin-mediated endocytosis. “Endocytosis” refers to the internalization of extracellular material via vesicle formation. The invention utilizes yeast display—a technique wherein antibody fragments are expressed on the yeast surface—for screening and selection of pH-sensitive variants. Saturation mutagenesis, particularly histidine saturation, introduces histidine codons at defined positions to engineer pH responsiveness.

A “therapeutic agent” includes cytotoxic drugs, nucleic acids, proteins, peptides, radionuclides, or imaging agents. The present invention features a pH-dependent antibody comprising histidine residues in CDRH1, preferably at positions VH31–VH34, constituting at least 30% of the CDRH1 residues. This is achieved by mutating a wild-type antibody, such as scFv H7, via histidine saturation mutagenesis. Exemplary sequences are provided as SEQ ID NOs.

Pharmaceutical compositions may be formulated as solutions, lyophilized powders, or particulate systems. The antibody-targeted brain delivery system comprises the pH-dependent antibody conjugated to a therapeutic agent, enabling transport across the BBB. Administration may be via injection, infusion, or implantable devices. The antibody may be expressed as a fusion protein, displayed on liposomes or nanoparticles, or encoded in an expression vector within a host cell (e.g., yeast, CHO, HEK293). Kits include the antibody, instructions, and optionally a therapeutic agent or delivery device.

Production involves transforming host cells with an expression vector encoding the antibody, culturing under inducing conditions, and purifying the product. Conjugation to therapeutic agents may be covalent (e.g., via maleimide-thiol chemistry) or non-covalent (e.g., biotin-streptavidin). Biotinylation of the antibody enables modular assembly with streptavidin-linked payloads. Liposomes or nanoparticles decorated with the antibody serve as targeted carriers.

### EXAMPLES

The following examples illustrate the design, screening, and characterization of pH-dependent anti-TfR antibodies. Biological systems frequently exploit pH-dependent mechanisms for regulated binding and release; histidine residues are central to this due to their pKa near endosomal pH. To engineer such behavior into an anti-TfR scFv, histidine saturation mutagenesis was applied to CDRH1 of scFv H7, a known TfR binder. A yeast surface display library was constructed wherein every position in CDRH1 was randomized to allow histidine incorporation.

Screening employed fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) with a pH-shift protocol: yeast displaying scFv variants were saturated with recombinant human TfR (rhTfR) at pH 7.4, then exposed to pH 5.5 to induce dissociation. Clones exhibiting reduced TfR binding at low pH were enriched over four rounds of sorting. This yielded scFv M16, which showed >90% dissociation from TfR after 10 minutes at pH 5.5, compared to only 44% for wild-type H7.

Sequence analysis revealed that M16 contained multiple histidines in central CDRH1 positions (VH31–VH34) and proline substitutions at flanking sites, a motif absent in pH-insensitive clones like N5. Binding assays confirmed M16 retained sub-nanomolar affinity at pH 7.4 (Kd ≈ 420 pM) while rapidly releasing TfR at pH 5.5. In contrast, N5 maintained strong binding at low pH.

Cellular studies in SK-BR-3 breast cancer cells demonstrated that M16 achieved 1.5-fold higher total cellular association and 2.6-fold greater internalization than H7. Trypsinization confirmed the internalized fraction. Critically, treatment with bafilomycin A1, which inhibits endosomal acidification, abolished M16’s enhanced internalization, proving pH-dependence. Immunocytochemistry showed M16 accumulated in large intracellular vesicles with reduced co-localization with EEA1, LAMP1, and LAMP2, suggesting altered trafficking away from canonical lysosomal degradation.

These results validate that engineering histidine-rich CDRH1 loops creates pH-sensitive antibodies with superior intracellular delivery properties. The methodology—combining histidine saturation mutagenesis, yeast display, and pH-shift FACS—is broadly applicable to other receptor targets. The resulting antibodies hold significant promise for brain delivery, oncology, and any application requiring endosomal escape.
