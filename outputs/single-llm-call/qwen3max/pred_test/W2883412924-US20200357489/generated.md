# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNICAL FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention pertains to the field of biomedicine, particularly in the domain of drug discovery and evaluation, and more specifically to a novel system and method for high-throughput screening of nanoformulated drugs. The invention further integrates advanced artificial intelligence techniques, particularly deep learning architectures based on convolutional neural networks, to enable rapid, accurate, and interference-resistant assessment of drug efficacy at the single-cell level. This interdisciplinary approach bridges the gap between nanomedicine and computational biology, offering a robust platform for evaluating the biological effects of complex drug delivery systems that traditional assays fail to characterize reliably. The invention is especially suited for use in preclinical research settings where the development of nanoparticle-based therapeutics demands innovative analytical tools capable of discerning subtle cellular responses under conditions of short exposure time and in the presence of physicochemical interferences inherent to nanomaterials.

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

Drug development remains a critical yet arduous endeavor in modern biomedicine, requiring extensive resources, time, and precision to identify compounds with therapeutic potential. In recent years, nanomaterials have emerged as promising vehicles for enhancing drug delivery, bioavailability, and targeted action, thereby improving the efficacy of conventional therapeutics. However, the evaluation of these nanoformulated drugs presents unique challenges that existing methodologies are ill-equipped to address. Traditional high-throughput screening technologies, such as the 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT) assay and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) release assays, rely heavily on optical or enzymatic readouts that are frequently distorted by the intrinsic properties of nanoparticles—including high absorbance, optical activity, large surface area, and energetic surface interactions. These interferences compromise the reliability of cytotoxicity and viability measurements, rendering conventional screening methods inadequate for the nuanced assessment of nano-drug systems.

In response to these limitations, computer technology has been increasingly applied to drug discovery, with virtual screening approaches leveraging computational models to predict compound behavior without physical experimentation. Among these, machine learning has gained traction due to its capacity to detect patterns in complex datasets and infer biological outcomes from molecular or cellular features. Nevertheless, existing machine learning-based virtual screening systems are constrained by their dependence on manually curated databases and predefined structural or ligand-based features, which restrict their adaptability to novel nanocarrier formulations lacking prior characterization. Moreover, these methods often fail to capture the dynamic, morphological, and functional changes occurring in live cells upon drug exposure, especially during the early stages of treatment.

This context underscores the urgent need for a new drug screening system that is both generalizable across diverse nanocarrier platforms and sensitive enough to detect transient cellular responses. Deep learning, a subset of machine learning inspired by the hierarchical processing of the human visual cortex, offers a compelling solution. By operating directly on image data, deep learning models—particularly convolutional neural networks (CNNs)—can automatically extract high-order features from raw inputs without reliance on hand-engineered descriptors. This capability enables the detection of subtle phenotypic alterations in single cells that may be invisible to conventional assays or human observers.

Notably, Chinese Patent Application No. 2017101273955 discloses a preliminary framework for using image-based analysis in drug screening but does not address the specific challenges posed by nanoformulated drugs, nor does it incorporate a deep learning architecture optimized for interference resistance, multi-channel image integration, and rapid classification under short incubation conditions. The present invention builds upon this foundation by introducing a comprehensive, end-to-end deep learning-based drug screening system specifically designed to overcome the limitations of both traditional assays and prior computational approaches in the context of nanomedicine.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a deep learning-based drug screening system, herein referred to as DeepScreen, which is specifically engineered for the rapid, accurate, and robust evaluation of nanoformulated drug efficacy. The system comprises several integrated modules: a picture preprocessing module, a channel merging module, a picture standardization module, and a neural network module. These components work in concert to transform raw single-cell image data into reliable efficacy predictions, even under conditions of short drug exposure and in the presence of significant physicochemical interference.

The picture preprocessing module receives single-cell images captured via image flow cytometry, typically consisting of multiple spectral channels such as bright-field and fluorescence-based markers (e.g., Annexin V-APC). The channel merging module concatenates these individual channels into a multi-channel composite image, preserving spatial and contextual information across modalities. Subsequently, the picture standardization module resizes and normalizes the merged images to a uniform dimension and intensity scale, ensuring compatibility with the downstream neural network architecture.

The core of the system is the neural network module, which implements a deep convolutional neural network based on an enhanced “network-in-network” architecture inspired by the Google Inception model. This network contains 46 layers, including convolutional, pooling, and regularization layers, but deliberately omits fully connected dense layers to reduce overfitting and enhance generalization. The network structure incorporates parallel branches at multiple levels, each performing distinct convolutional or pooling operations, with outputs concatenated channel-wise to enrich feature representation. This design enables the model to simultaneously capture low-level textures, mid-level morphological contours, and high-level phenotypic signatures associated with drug response.

The training method employs balanced mini-batch sampling to ensure equal representation of all efficacy classes (ineffective, low, medium, high), combined with regularization techniques including weight decay, dropout, and batch normalization. Optimization is performed using the Adam algorithm with empirically tuned hyperparameters. The resulting model is capable of classifying drug efficacy from single-cell images obtained after only 2–6 hours of treatment, dramatically accelerating the screening timeline compared to conventional 24–72 hour assays.

The method of deep learning-based drug screening involves treating cells with candidate nanoformulated drugs, acquiring single-cell images via image flow cytometry, preprocessing and standardizing the images, and feeding them into the trained neural network for classification. The picture preprocessing module ensures consistent input quality, while the neural network module delivers a probabilistic efficacy score. Key advantages of the invention include: (1) elimination of manual intervention and subjective bias; (2) resistance to interference from nanoparticle autofluorescence, absorbance, and morphology; (3) ability to distinguish subtle early-stage cellular responses; (4) flexibility to incorporate additional fluorescent markers for enhanced accuracy; and (5) compatibility with mixed cell populations and diverse nanocarrier types.

In summary, the invention provides a complete, automated, and scalable platform for nanoformulated drug screening that outperforms existing biochemical and flow cytometric methods in speed, accuracy, and robustness, thereby addressing a critical unmet need in nanomedicine development.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE PREFERRED EMBODIMENT

The construction process of the drug screening system model begins with the acquisition of high-quality single-cell image data from image flow cytometry. Human cancer cell lines, such as A549 (lung adenocarcinoma) and HEpG2 (hepatocellular carcinoma), are treated with nanoformulated drugs—including inorganic layered double hydroxide loaded with etoposide (LDH-VP16) and lipid-based solid lipid nanoparticles loaded with curcumin (SLN-Cur)—at concentrations corresponding to known efficacy levels (ineffective, low, medium, high) as previously established by 24-hour MTT assays. Cells are then stained with Annexin V-APC to mark early apoptosis and, optionally, with anti-EGFR-FITC for cell-type-specific targeting. Image flow cytometry (e.g., Amnis Flowsight) captures millions of single-cell images, each comprising bright-field and one or more fluorescence channels.

### Example 1 A Deep Learning-Based Quick and Precise High-Throughput Drug Screening System

The construction of the DeepScreen model proceeds through a series of well-defined steps. First, the picture preprocessing module processes raw image files by isolating individual channels and removing background noise. Next, the channel merging module combines the bright-field and fluorescence channels into a single multi-channel tensor, preserving spatial alignment. The picture standardization module then resizes all images to 70×70 pixels using bicubic interpolation and applies z-score normalization to standardize pixel intensity distributions across the dataset.

The neural network module is implemented using TensorFlow and trained on dual NVIDIA GTX 1080Ti GPUs. The network structure follows a modified Inception architecture with 46 layers, featuring inception blocks that contain parallel convolutional paths with kernel sizes of 1×1, 3×3, and 5×5, along with max-pooling branches. All layers are convolutional or pooling-based; no fully connected layers are used, reducing parameter count and mitigating overfitting. The final layers consist of a feature convolution layer, a global average pooling layer, and a 1×1 convolution layer that outputs class probabilities.

The training method utilizes a balanced sampling strategy wherein each mini-batch contains an equal number of samples from each of the four efficacy classes. This approach improves model performance by 1–2% over random sampling. Regularization is enforced via L2 weight decay (λ = 1e−4), dropout (rate = 0.5), and batch normalization. The Adam optimizer is configured with a learning rate of 0.001, β1 = 0.9, β2 = 0.999, and ε = 1e−8. Training continues until validation loss plateaus, typically after 50–100 epochs.

The classification model outputs a four-class prediction representing the likelihood of ineffective, low, medium, or high drug efficacy. Training data consists of 103,568 A549 and 115,968 HEpG2 single-cell images from 2- and 6-hour treatments, labeled according to the 24-hour MTT reference. Testing data includes 25,892 A549 and 28,992 HEpG2 images from independent experiments. The model testing process evaluates accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-score across all classes and cell types.

Model accuracy reaches 0.851 for mixed-cell testing, 0.864 for HEpG2, and 0.908 for A549 when using both bright-field and Annexin V-APC channels. Accuracy increases to 0.966 when anti-EGFR-FITC is added for A549, demonstrating the system’s adaptability. Even with only bright-field images, accuracy remains at 0.705, offering a simplified workflow. The model exhibits strong anti-interference capabilities, maintaining performance despite the autofluorescence of curcumin and the morphological complexity of LDH and SLN nanoparticles.

In summary, the DeepScreen model offers unprecedented advantages: it reduces screening time from days to hours, eliminates manual analysis, resists nanomaterial-induced interference, and provides interpretable results via class activation mapping. Its modular design allows seamless integration of new markers or cell types, making it a versatile and future-proof platform for next-generation drug discovery.
