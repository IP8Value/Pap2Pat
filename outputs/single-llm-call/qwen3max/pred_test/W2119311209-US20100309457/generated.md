# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

Phase sensing devices are instrumental in a wide range of scientific and industrial applications where the detection and analysis of optical wavefronts provide critical information beyond what is available through intensity-based imaging alone. Traditional image sensors, such as charge-coupled devices (CCDs) and complementary metal-oxide-semiconductor (CMOS) chips, are inherently limited to detecting only the amplitude or intensity component of incident light, effectively discarding the phase information carried by the optical wavefront. This limitation significantly constrains their utility in scenarios where transparent or weakly scattering specimens—such as live biological cells, microorganisms, or certain engineered materials—are under investigation, as these samples often modulate only the phase of transmitted light without appreciably altering its intensity.

Commercial optical sensors currently dominate imaging markets but suffer from an intrinsic inability to resolve phase variations directly. While post-processing techniques and auxiliary optical components can indirectly infer phase information, these approaches typically involve complex setups, specialized illumination sources, or mechanical scanning, which increase cost, reduce robustness, and limit real-time applicability. The importance of phase in imaging cannot be overstated: it encodes spatial variations in refractive index, thickness, and internal structure of transparent objects, enabling label-free visualization of cellular dynamics, subcellular organelles, and microfluidic processes without the need for staining or fluorescent tagging.

Applications of phase microscopes span biomedical diagnostics, developmental biology, ophthalmology, and environmental monitoring. For instance, in field settings where rapid identification of waterborne pathogens is essential, or during surgical procedures requiring immediate assessment of tumor margins, phase microscopy offers a non-invasive, preparation-free alternative to conventional histopathology. Similarly, in assisted reproductive technologies, the ability to monitor embryo development without chemical fixation or staining preserves sample viability while providing morphological insights.

Conventional differential interference contrast (DIC) microscopes have long served as the workhorse of phase imaging in biological laboratories. These instruments exploit polarized light and birefringent prisms to convert phase gradients into intensity differences, thereby generating pseudo-three-dimensional images with enhanced edge contrast. However, the principle of conventional DIC devices relies fundamentally on the interference of orthogonally polarized light beams that pass through adjacent regions of the specimen. This design necessitates precise alignment of polarizers and Wollaston prisms, introduces directional bias dependent on prism orientation, and critically, entangles amplitude and phase information within the final image. As a result, DIC images are qualitative rather than quantitative, making them unsuitable for precise measurements of optical path length or refractive index distributions.

Moreover, illustrations of conventional DIC devices reveal bulky optical trains with multiple moving parts, limiting portability and increasing susceptibility to misalignment. A significant drawback arises when imaging birefringent samples—such as muscle fibers, collagen matrices, or starch granules—where the sample’s intrinsic birefringence interferes with the polarization state manipulated by the DIC optics, leading to severe artifacts that obscure true structural features. These limitations underscore the inadequacy of conventional phase microscopes for robust, artifact-free, and quantitative phase imaging across diverse sample types.

In response, researchers have developed alternative phase microscopy techniques, including phase-shifting interferometry, digital holography, Hilbert phase microscopy, swept-source phase microscopy, polarization quadrature microscopy, and harmonically matched grating-based methods. While these approaches enable quantitative phase retrieval, they often require coherent laser illumination, high-speed modulation, or computationally intensive reconstruction algorithms, rendering them impractical for routine clinical or field use. Some schemes calculate optical phase by capturing multiple images at different focal planes, but this demands mechanical actuation of the camera or objective lens, inherently limiting temporal resolution and introducing motion artifacts in live-cell imaging.

Despite advances, existing techniques for calculating optical phase remain constrained by trade-offs between complexity, speed, cost, and compatibility with standard microscopy platforms. Many rely on external optical elements that cannot be integrated into compact sensor architectures, and none offer a direct, single-shot, polarization-independent method for simultaneously measuring both amplitude and phase gradient with high spatial density using a simple, manufacturable chip-scale device. Consequently, there exists a compelling need for a new class of phase-sensing technology that overcomes these limitations by embedding wavefront measurement capability directly into the image sensor itself.

## BRIEF SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention introduces a novel wavefront imaging device, referred to herein as a Wavefront Imaging Sensor (WIS), which is capable of simultaneously measuring both amplitude and phase variations of an incident light field without requiring external optical components or polarized illumination. The WIS comprises three primary functional layers: an aperture layer containing one or more light-transmissive apertures, a light detector array configured to receive light transmitted through the apertures, and a transparent layer interposed between the aperture layer and the light detector. This layered architecture enables the WIS to operate based on a self-focusing diffraction regime that enhances sensitivity while maintaining simplicity.

In operation, light passing through each aperture forms a localized projection spot on the underlying light detector. The lateral position of this spot shifts in proportion to the local phase gradient of the incident wavefront across the aperture, while the total integrated signal within the spot corresponds to the local amplitude or intensity. By precisely estimating these lateral shifts—using sub-pixel centroid or Fourier-demodulation algorithms—the WIS quantitatively determines the normalized phase gradient in two orthogonal directions. Concurrently, the amplitude is derived from the summed pixel responses associated with each projection spot.

From these raw measurements, the WIS system numerically generates high-fidelity images representing both intensity and phase gradient distributions across the object plane. Critically, because phase and amplitude are measured independently and without reliance on polarization, the resulting images are free from the ambiguities and artifacts that plague conventional DIC microscopy. Furthermore, the WIS operates effectively with broadband, unpolarized illumination sources such as standard halogen lamps, making it compatible with existing bright-field microscopes without modification. This integration allows any standard microscope to be converted into a quantitative wavefront microscope capable of delivering simultaneous bright-field and phase-gradient imaging in a single acquisition.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

The Wavefront Imaging Sensor (WIS) represents a paradigm shift in optical sensing by integrating phase gradient measurement functionality directly onto a semiconductor image sensor chip. The core components of the WIS include an aperture layer, a light detector array, and a transparent spacer layer separating the two. Unlike conventional wavefront sensors such as Shack-Hartmann devices—which require macroscopic lenslet arrays and large working distances—the WIS leverages a high Fresnel number optical regime to achieve intrinsic self-focusing of diffracted light, thereby enabling dense aperture packing and high spatial resolution on a compact chip footprint.

This self-focusing scheme arises when the distance between the aperture and the detector is chosen such that the Fresnel number \( F = \frac{a^2}{\lambda H / n} \) exceeds approximately 0.5, where \( a \) is the aperture radius, \( \lambda \) is the wavelength of light, \( H \) is the physical separation, and \( n \) is the refractive index of the transparent layer. In this regime, diffraction causes the transmitted light to converge before diverging, forming a tightly confined projection spot whose minimum width occurs near the self-focusing plane. By positioning the light detector at or near this plane, the WIS achieves enhanced sensitivity to lateral shifts induced by phase gradients, while maintaining a small spot size that minimizes crosstalk between adjacent apertures.

A key advantage of the WIS over conventional wavefront sensors is its ability to implement phase gradient measurement on a simple, monolithic sensor chip fabricated using standard semiconductor processes. This eliminates the need for precision alignment of external optics, reduces system bulk, and significantly lowers manufacturing costs. Moreover, because the WIS does not rely on polarized light or interference, it avoids the artifacts associated with birefringent samples and enables straightforward quantitative interpretation of phase data.

The aperture layer may consist of one or more apertures arranged in one-dimensional or two-dimensional arrays, with spacing tailored to the application. Each aperture defines a light-transmissive region, which may be an open hole or filled with a transparent dielectric material, and is typically circular with diameters ranging from 2 to 10 micrometers. Aperture-to-aperture pitch is selected to ensure sufficient sampling of the wavefront while avoiding overlap of adjacent projection spots; in exemplary embodiments, pitches of 8–15 µm yield image resolutions compatible with standard microscope objectives.

The transparent layer, composed of one or more films of glass, polymer (e.g., SU-8), or other optically clear materials, serves both as a mechanical spacer and as a medium influencing the effective optical path. Its thickness \( H \) is carefully engineered—typically between 10 and 50 µm—to place the detector at the self-focusing plane for optimal spot confinement. The light detector, implemented as a CMOS or CCD pixel array, generates electrical signals proportional to incident photon flux. Each aperture is assigned a dedicated cluster of detector pixels (e.g., 5×5), allowing independent measurement of spot position and total intensity.

During operation, an incident wavefront impinging on the aperture layer produces a set of projection spots on the detector. The lateral displacement \( \Delta x \) of each spot relative to a reference (e.g., normal incidence) is related to the normalized phase gradient \( \theta_x = \frac{\lambda}{2\pi n H} \frac{\partial \phi}{\partial x} \) by \( \Delta x = H \theta_x \). Similar relations hold for the y-direction. By applying centroid estimation or cyclic Fourier-demodulation algorithms to the discrete pixel data, sub-pixel shifts as small as 1.8 nm can be resolved, corresponding to phase gradient sensitivities below 0.1 mrad under typical illumination conditions.

The high Fresnel number design not only improves sensitivity but also enhances robustness against noise and manufacturing tolerances. Simulations and experiments confirm that spot widths remain below 5 µm over a broad range of \( H \), ensuring consistent performance despite minor process variations. Calibration involves illuminating the WIS with tilted plane waves to establish a linear relationship between known incident angles and measured shifts, yielding accurate determination of \( H \) and system linearity.

Applications of the WIS extend far beyond microscopy. In photography, it enables depth-from-defocus and focus-assist functionalities. In machine vision, phase gradients reveal surface topology for 3D shape recognition. In ophthalmology, it facilitates high-resolution retinal imaging and LASIK guidance. In optofluidics, it supports real-time monitoring of cell morphology and flow dynamics. Critically, the WIS requires no lenses, polarizers, or moving parts, making it ideal for miniaturized, low-cost, and field-deployable systems.

Fabrication begins with a commercial CMOS sensor, whose native microlenses may be preserved or planarized depending on design goals. An opaque metal layer (e.g., aluminum) is deposited and patterned to define the aperture array via photolithography or focused ion beam milling. The transparent spacer is formed either by spin-coating polymers or leveraging existing dielectric stacks within the sensor package. The entire structure is compatible with foundry-scale production, enabling mass deployment at minimal incremental cost.

Experimental validation demonstrates superior performance over DIC: unstained biological specimens show clear phase contrast without staining; birefringent samples exhibit no polarization artifacts; and stained samples allow unambiguous separation of absorption (intensity) and refractive index (phase) effects. Quantitative phase images are generated by integrating orthogonal gradient fields or displaying magnitude maps, providing clinicians and researchers with interpretable, artifact-free data.

Software implementations on host computers execute centroid or cyclic algorithms, perform calibration corrections, and render composite images. Adaptive optics strategies can further correct for tissue-induced aberrations in deep imaging. Variations include color-capable WIS using Foveon or Bayer architectures, integration into smartphone cameras, and use in multiphoton microscopy for in vivo studies.

In summary, the WIS is a compact, cost-effective, polarization-independent wavefront sensor that measures both amplitude and phase gradient simultaneously via a high-Fresnel-number aperture-detector architecture. It transforms standard optical systems into quantitative phase imagers without modification, offering unprecedented accessibility to phase-sensitive applications across science, medicine, and industry.
