# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention pertains to the field of medical monitoring, particularly to systems and methods for the continuous and accurate measurement of arterial pressure in critically ill patients. More specifically, the invention relates to a device and associated methodology configured to assess, monitor, and correct distortions introduced into the arterial pressure signal by the hydraulic connection between an indwelling arterial catheter and a pressure transducer. Such distortions commonly arise due to mechanical and fluid-dynamic properties of the tubing system used in invasive hemodynamic monitoring, including phenomena such as overdamping or attenuation, which can significantly compromise the fidelity of the measured waveform. The invention further extends to real-time quality assurance of the pressure signal, enabling reliable derivation of clinically relevant hemodynamic indices such as pulse pressure variation (PPV) and maximal rate of pressure rise (dP/dt), which are critical for guiding fluid resuscitation and assessing cardiac contractility in intensive care settings.

## SUMMARY

Arterial pressure measurement is a cornerstone of hemodynamic monitoring in critical care medicine, typically performed via an indwelling catheter connected through fluid-filled tubing to an external pressure transducer. This configuration allows continuous recording of the arterial pressure waveform, from which key parameters—including systolic, diastolic, and mean arterial pressures—are derived. Current clinical practice relies on this setup due to its relative simplicity, real-time capability, and integration into standard monitoring systems. A significant advantage of this approach is its ability to provide beat-to-beat pressure data, facilitating dynamic assessment of cardiovascular status and responsiveness to therapeutic interventions. However, this method suffers from inherent limitations stemming from the physical characteristics of the hydraulic connection—namely, the compliance, length, internal diameter, and air content of the tubing—which collectively define a transfer function that distorts the true incident arterial pressure signal.

These distortions manifest primarily as overdamping (characterized by reduced systolic pressure, elevated diastolic pressure, and preserved mean pressure) or general attenuation (reduction across all pressure components). Such artifacts directly impair the accuracy of derived indices like PPV and dP/dt, potentially leading to inappropriate clinical decisions regarding fluid administration or inotropic support. Although trained clinical staff may occasionally recognize gross waveform abnormalities, subtle or intermittent distortions often go undetected, especially during prolonged monitoring periods. Proposed solutions have included manual fast-flush tests to estimate the natural frequency and damping coefficient of the system, but these are intermittent, operator-dependent, and incapable of continuous correction.

The limitations of existing approaches underscore a clear need for an automated, integrated system capable of continuously evaluating and, where possible, compensating for hydraulic-induced signal degradation. The present invention addresses this need by providing a method and device for improving the quality of the measured arterial pressure signal through real-time characterization of the hydraulic connection’s dynamic behavior. The method involves determining the dynamic parameters of the hydraulic connection, analyzing the frequential content of the incident arterial pressure signal, and detecting specific pathological states of the connection. Three primary situations are identified: (a) normal operation with minimal distortion, (b) overdamping, and (c) generalized attenuation.

To facilitate diagnosis and correction, the invention employs a controlled mechanical action applied to the tubing—such as a brief pulse or sinusoidal excitation—to probe the system’s response. This mechanical action is synchronized with the diastolic phase of the cardiac cycle to minimize interference with hemodynamic measurements. Alternative mechanical actions include vibrational stimuli or stepwise compressions. The system also incorporates a decoupling detection mechanism, particularly relevant in non-invasive modalities like applanation tonometry, where loss of sensor contact with the artery can mimic damping artifacts. In such cases, pulse wave velocity (PWV) propagation is analyzed by comparing proximal and distal pressure waveforms; a significant deviation from a reference PWV value triggers an alarm indicating probable decoupling.

The invention further comprises a dedicated device for continuous monitoring and correction, featuring a monitoring and correcting module, an actuator module, and a remote processing system. This device integrates seamlessly into conventional arterial pressure monitoring chains without requiring changes to existing clinical workflows. Its advantages include ease of installation, operational transparency to hospital staff, and the ability to be neutralized when not in active use. The monitoring module includes a first processor dedicated to real-time signal processing and a second processor responsible for implementing corrective algorithms, ensuring redundant and fail-safe treatment of critical data. The actuator module may be connected via wired or wireless interfaces and powered by a rechargeable battery, housed within a sterilizable casing compatible with clinical environments. Communication between modules and with the remote processing system enables centralized data logging, quality assurance, and calibration. The applanation tonometry variant includes a specialized casing designed to maintain consistent arterial contact while allowing dynamic assessment of coupling integrity.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention introduces a monitoring device specifically engineered for the accurate measurement of arterial pressure in clinical settings, particularly intensive care units where signal fidelity directly impacts patient outcomes. The device comprises three principal components: a monitoring and correcting module, an actuator module, and a remote processing system. The monitoring and correcting module serves as the core analytical unit, tasked with characterizing the dynamic parameters of the hydraulic connection linking the arterial catheter to the pressure transducer. It performs a detailed frequential analysis of the incident arterial pressure signal, estimates the aptitude of the hydraulic system to faithfully reproduce the true arterial waveform, detects measuring artefacts or incidents such as overdamping or attenuation, and provides actionable information regarding the state of the hydraulic connection along with recommended corrective measures.

The monitoring and correcting module operates in three distinct modes: active, passive, and neutral. In active mode, it continuously analyzes and corrects the pressure signal; in passive mode, it monitors without intervention; and in neutral mode, it is effectively disabled, ensuring compatibility with legacy systems. Architecturally, the module incorporates two independent processors: a first processor optimized for high-speed signal acquisition and spectral decomposition, and a second processor dedicated to executing correction algorithms and managing communication protocols. These processors exchange data securely and redundantly, interfacing with both the actuator module and the remote processing system via encrypted channels. A power management module ensures energy efficiency, enabling prolonged operation even during battery-powered scenarios.

The actuator module functions by applying precise mechanical actions to the fluid-filled tubing—such as transient compressions or oscillatory loads—to excite the hydraulic system and elicit a measurable response. These excitations may take the form of pulse-like stimuli mimicking a fast flush or brief sinusoidal loads at known frequencies. Critically, these actions are synchronized with the diastolic phase of the cardiac cycle to avoid superimposing artificial perturbations on systolic pressure peaks. The module consists of a miniature electromechanical actuator powered by a compact, rechargeable battery, designed for low-profile integration into standard monitoring setups.

The remote processing system receives the corrected arterial pressure signal and derives clinically relevant indices such as PPV, dP/dt, and stroke volume variation. It displays real-time arterial pressure plots, archives measurement records, and performs longitudinal quality follow-up and periodic calibration checks. Signal processing methods implemented within the system include both percussion-based and harmonic approaches. The percussion method utilizes data from routine fast-flush maneuvers: the system isolates the transient response embedded within the hemodynamic signal, then calculates the damping factor (ζ) and natural frequency (f₀) using established second-order system models. However, this method is limited by its dependence on manual flush events and susceptibility to noise.

To overcome these constraints, the harmonic method applies a controlled series of sinusoidal mechanical loads via the actuator module and analyzes the amplitude and phase response across frequencies. This yields a more robust estimation of ζ and f₀ but requires additional hardware and careful timing. The invention therefore proposes a hybrid approach that combines both methods: using percussion data when available and supplementing with harmonic probing during quiescent periods. This combination enhances reliability while minimizing patient disturbance.

Alternative signal processing techniques include direct spectral analysis of the arterial waveform using Fast Fourier Transform (FFT). By examining the frequency distribution of energy in the pressure signal—particularly the ratio of high-frequency to low-frequency components—the system can infer the degree of damping without external excitation. A first estimation method compares observed spectral roll-off against theoretical models of underdamped systems. A second, more refined method correlates spectral features with empirically derived correction factors, illustrated through validation datasets showing strong concordance between predicted and actual waveform distortion.

Continuous monitoring of the hydraulic connection enables early detection of artefacts. Abnormal attenuation is flagged when both systolic and diastolic pressures decline disproportionately relative to mean pressure, triggering automatic correction and clinician alerts. All incidents are logged with timestamps and severity scores for audit and quality improvement. Decoupling detection is especially vital in applanation tonometry applications. Here, the system compares radial and aortic (or carotid) pressure waveforms. Using the Moens-Korteweg equation, it estimates pulse wave velocity (PWV) from the time delay between proximal and distal pressure upstrokes. A significant reduction in PWV—indicative of poor sensor coupling rather than true vascular change—activates a warning. Distances between measurement sites are input manually or via anatomical presets, and a data fusion device integrates multi-site signals to enhance diagnostic accuracy.

In conclusion, the invention provides a comprehensive, automated solution for ensuring the integrity of arterial pressure measurements by actively diagnosing and mitigating hydraulic-induced distortions, thereby enhancing the safety and efficacy of hemodynamic-guided therapy in critically ill patients.
