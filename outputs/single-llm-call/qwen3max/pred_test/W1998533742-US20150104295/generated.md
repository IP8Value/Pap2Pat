# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

The present application relates to turbomachine rotors, particularly those employed in high-performance gas turbine engines such as turbofans, compressors, and turbines. These rotors are typically composed of bladed disks or drums—monolithic structures known as blisks—fabricated using advanced materials and precision manufacturing techniques that yield extremely low inherent structural damping. While such designs enhance aerodynamic efficiency and reduce part count, they also render the rotor highly susceptible to resonant vibrations under dynamic excitations encountered during operation. These excitations arise primarily from interactions between rotating blades and stationary stator vanes, generating periodic aerodynamic forces whose frequencies are integer multiples of the rotational speed (commonly referred to as engine orders). When these excitation frequencies coincide with natural frequencies of the rotor’s vibration modes, resonance occurs, leading to large-amplitude oscillations that can induce high-cycle fatigue and catastrophic failure over time.

In turbomachinery, the vibratory response is governed by the modal characteristics of rotationally periodic structures. Such structures exhibit normal modes characterized by harmonic displacement patterns in the circumferential direction, manifesting as nodal diameters—imaginary lines across the rotor where displacement remains zero during vibration. The number of nodal diameters, denoted *n*, defines the spatial frequency of the mode shape and is intrinsically linked to the excitation order and rotational speed through resonance conditions derived from Floquet theory and traveling wave dynamics. For a given operating condition, only specific modes with particular *n* values are critically excited; thus, targeted damping of these modes is both sufficient and efficient for ensuring structural integrity.

Blade resonance and associated vibratory levels are therefore central concerns in turbomachine design. Unmitigated, these vibrations accelerate material degradation through cyclic stress accumulation, ultimately compromising fatigue resistance. To address this, various damping strategies have been explored, including friction-based mechanisms (e.g., blade shrouds, underplatform dampers), viscoelastic coatings, and active or semi-active control systems. Among these, piezoelectric-based approaches offer a promising alternative due to their ability to convert mechanical strain energy into electrical energy, which can then be dissipated externally.

One such approach involves shunted piezoelectric systems, wherein piezoelectric transducers bonded to the rotor structure are connected to passive electrical networks—typically resistive-inductive (RL) circuits—that are tuned to the frequency of a target vibration mode. In an RL shunt configuration, the inductor and the inherent capacitance of the piezoelectric element form a resonant circuit that, when properly tuned, maximizes energy extraction from the mechanical system at the target frequency. However, prior art implementations suffer from significant limitations. Conventional designs employ independent RL loops for each transducer, necessitating a large number of high-inductance components—often on the order of several henries—which are physically bulky, heavy, and impractical for integration into rotating machinery. Moreover, synthetic inductors based on active electronic circuits (e.g., gyrators) have been proposed to emulate large inductances, but these introduce reliability concerns, power requirements, and electromagnetic interference issues that preclude their use in harsh, high-speed rotating environments.

Furthermore, the tuning of RL shunts is highly sensitive to variations in natural frequency caused by operational factors such as temperature changes, centrifugal stiffening, or manufacturing tolerances (mistuning). While switched or adaptive shunts offer broader bandwidth, they rely on real-time sensing and switching logic, again requiring active components incompatible with purely passive, maintenance-free rotor systems. Consequently, there exists a critical need for a passive, lightweight, and robust vibration damping system capable of effectively attenuating targeted resonant modes in turbomachine rotors without the drawbacks of existing piezoelectric shunt technologies.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE PREFERRED EMBODIMENT

The present invention overcomes the aforementioned problems of prior art by introducing a novel architecture for a vibration-damping system specifically tailored for turbomachine rotors exhibiting rotational periodicity. This system significantly improves damping efficacy while simultaneously reducing overall system weight and complexity, thereby enabling practical implementation in high-speed rotating machinery without reliance on active electronics or excessively large passive components.

The core innovation lies in a strategic arrangement and interconnection of piezoelectric transducers that leverages the symmetry and spatial characteristics of targeted vibration modes. Specifically, the invention proposes a system for damping turbomachine rotor vibration that organizes multiple piezoelectric transducers into grouped parallel electrical loops, rather than connecting each transducer to an independent RL circuit. This configuration exploits the modal deformation pattern—particularly the alternating curvature and strain distribution associated with modes possessing *n* nodal diameters—to combine the electrical outputs of multiple transducers constructively, thereby amplifying the effective capacitance seen by the dissipative circuit and drastically reducing the required inductance.

In a preferred embodiment, the rotor comprises a bladed disk or drum fabricated as a monolithic structure (blisk) with *N* blades arranged cyclically around a central axis. Bonded to the inner surface of the blade support rim—or another structurally active region not interfering with aerodynamics—are a plurality of identical piezoelectric transducers, preferably operating in the *d₃₁* mode, which generates charge in response to in-plane bending strains. The number of transducers is selected as a multiple of 4*n*, where *n* is the number of nodal diameters of the critical mode to be damped. For instance, to target a mode with *n = 7* nodal diameters, 28 transducers (i.e., 4×7) are employed.

These transducers are distributed circumferentially with precise angular spacing that aligns with the antinodal regions of the target mode shape. Critically, adjacent transducers within each half-period of the modal wavelength are mounted with inverted electrical polarity. This polarity inversion ensures that, under the symmetric deformation of the *n*-diameter mode, the voltages generated by paired transducers add constructively rather than canceling. The transducers are then connected into two or more parallel electrical loops: in the ideal case, all transducers with one polarity orientation are wired in parallel to form a first loop, and those with the opposite polarity form a second loop. Each loop thus comprises 2*n* transducers connected in parallel, resulting in a total loop capacitance of 2*nC*, where *C* is the capacitance of a single transducer.

Each parallel loop is connected to a dedicated dissipative circuit comprising a resistor and an inductor configured as an RL shunt. Due to the increased capacitance, the resonant frequency ωᵣ of the RLC circuit (where the inductive reactance balances the capacitive reactance) is given by ωᵣ = 1/√(L·2nC). To tune this circuit to the natural frequency ωₙ of the target mode, the required inductance is L = 1/(ωₙ²·2nC). Compared to a system with independent loops—each requiring L₀ = 1/(ωₙ²·C)—the inductance per loop is reduced by a factor of 2*n*. Since only two loops are needed instead of 4*n* individual circuits, the total inductance demand is reduced by a factor of 4*n²*, a substantial improvement that enables the use of compact, passive inductors even for modes with high *n*.

This damper system may comprise either a single set of transducers organized into two parallel loops or multiple sets for enhanced robustness or multi-mode capability. The transducers are preferably identical in material, geometry, and electromechanical properties to ensure uniform response. Their placement exploits the symmetry of the rotor deformation: under vibration in the targeted *n*-diameter mode, the structure deforms such that diametrically opposed sectors experience equal but opposite curvatures, allowing the polarity-inverted transducer pairs to generate coherent electrical signals.

The dissipative means—resistors and inductors—are pooled per loop, simplifying wiring and reducing component count. This pooling enhances overall damping efficacy by concentrating energy dissipation at the precise frequency and spatial pattern of the critical mode. Moreover, the system demonstrates improved handling of reduced frequencies and remains effective even when the rotor operates near critical speeds where resonance is most severe.

In an axial turbomachine such as a turbofan, the rotor forms part of a larger assembly including a fan, compressor stages, and turbine wheels, all mounted on a common shaft. During operation, bladed rotor wheels rotate at high speeds, subjecting blades to dynamic excitations from upstream stator vanes. Vibration is observed primarily in blade-dominated or drum-dominated modes, depending on frequency. The integrated vibration-damping system actively reduces these vibrations by converting mechanical strain into electrical current, which is then dissipated as heat in the resistive elements of the shunt circuits.

The modal shape with *n* nodal diameters exhibits *n* lines of zero displacement spaced evenly around the circumference. Transducers are placed away from these nodal diameters, preferably near antinodes where strain is maximal. A first set of piezoelectric transducers is positioned to capture positive curvature, while a second set—angularly offset by π/*n* radians—is oriented to capture negative curvature, with inverted polarization to ensure additive voltage output. The vibration-damping system architecture thus comprises mechanically coupled transducers electrically grouped into resonant loops.

Each piezoelectric transducer functions as an electromechanical transducer, generating charge proportional to local strain. Their placement on the rotor structure—specifically on the inner rim opposing blade roots—ensures strong coupling to global drum modes without perturbing airflow. The rotor structure, typically made of titanium or nickel-based superalloys, provides a stiff yet flexible substrate for transducer bonding.

Under dynamic excitations, the rotor vibrates predominantly in modes identified via Campbell diagrams or frequency-nodal diameter plots. The damping system selectively attenuates the targeted *n*-diameter mode by extracting energy precisely when and where it is generated. Experimental validation on circular plates and 76-blade drums confirms significant vibration reduction, particularly when mode shapes remain close to ideal harmonic forms.

The resonance frequency of each RLC circuit is deliberately matched to the natural frequency of the target mode, enabling efficient energy transfer. The electromechanical system thus facilitates low-frequency natural mode damping—a regime where traditional passive dampers often underperform—by leveraging the scalable capacitance of parallel-connected transducers to relax inductor requirements. This enables purely passive, reliable, and lightweight integration into next-generation turbomachinery.
