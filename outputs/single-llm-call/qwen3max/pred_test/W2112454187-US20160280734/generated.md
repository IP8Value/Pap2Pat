# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNICAL FIELD

The present invention relates generally to the field of radiochemistry, and more specifically to automated systems for the synthesis of radiolabeled compounds used in positron emission tomography (PET) imaging. Radiochemistry applications encompass the preparation of short-lived radioactive tracers that are essential for non-invasive diagnostic imaging in clinical and preclinical settings. These tracers, typically labeled with positron-emitting isotopes such as fluorine-18 (¹⁸F), must be synthesized rapidly, reliably, and under stringent conditions to ensure high radiochemical purity, specific activity, and compliance with regulatory standards for human or animal administration. The technical challenges inherent in radiochemistry—such as handling volatile and corrosive reagents, performing high-temperature and high-pressure reactions, managing radiation exposure, and achieving reproducibility across diverse chemical pathways—demand specialized instrumentation. The invention disclosed herein addresses these challenges by providing an integrated, modular, and highly flexible automated radiosynthesizer designed to support both tracer development and routine production without requiring hardware modifications between different syntheses. This platform is particularly suited for synthesizing complex ¹⁸F-labeled nucleoside analogs and other PET tracers that have historically been difficult to automate due to their demanding reaction conditions, thereby expanding the scope of accessible molecular imaging probes for oncology, neurology, and cardiology applications.

## BACKGROUND

Positron emission tomography (PET) is a powerful molecular imaging modality that enables the visualization of biochemical processes in living organisms through the detection of gamma rays emitted by positron-emitting radionuclides incorporated into biologically active molecules. The most widely used PET tracer, 2-[¹⁸F]fluoro-2-deoxy-d-glucose ([¹⁸F]FDG), exploits the increased glucose metabolism of cancer cells for tumor detection and staging. However, the growing demand for novel PET tracers targeting specific biological pathways—such as cell proliferation, receptor expression, or enzyme activity—has highlighted the limitations of existing automated synthesis platforms. While [¹⁸F]FDG can be produced reliably on commercial radiosynthesizers, many next-generation tracers, including nucleoside analogs like 2-deoxy-2-[¹⁸F]fluoro-β-d-arabinofuranosylcytosine (d-[¹⁸F]FAC) and 2-deoxy-2-[¹⁸F]fluoro-5-methyl-β-l-arabinofuranosyluracil (l-[¹⁸F]FMAU), require reaction conditions that exceed the operational envelopes of conventional systems. These include elevated temperatures (often above 150°C), use of volatile organic solvents, and the presence of corrosive reagents, which can generate high internal pressures during synthesis.

Automated radiosynthesizers were developed to reduce operator radiation exposure, improve reproducibility, and enable decentralized production of PET tracers. However, most existing systems rely on fixed fluidic pathways with permanent tubing and valve connections directly attached to the reaction vessel. This architecture inherently limits the maximum pressure the system can withstand, as the seals and joints in the fluid path become failure points under stress. Consequently, chemists attempting to automate challenging syntheses often resort to modifying reaction protocols—such as lowering reaction temperatures, substituting solvents, or reducing reagent concentrations—to accommodate the synthesizer’s constraints, frequently at the cost of yield, purity, or reaction kinetics. This compromises the fidelity of the chemistry and hinders the translation of promising tracers from research to clinical use.

The need for a more robust and adaptable platform has led to the development of hybrid systems that combine the reliability of automation with the flexibility of manual synthesis. A key innovation in this direction is the use of movable reactor assemblies that can be dynamically reconfigured relative to a disposable fluidic cassette, thereby eliminating permanent wetted connections to the reaction vessel. Such a design allows the reaction vessel to be sealed only during high-pressure steps, while other operations—such as reagent addition, evaporation, and product transfer—are performed through distinct, dedicated interfaces. This approach not only enhances pressure tolerance but also simplifies cleaning, reduces cross-contamination, and accelerates the transition from method development to routine production. The present invention fulfills this unmet need by providing a fully integrated, three-reactor automated radiosynthesizer that leverages disposable cassettes, a reagent and gas handling robot, and a sophisticated control system to perform a wide array of radiosyntheses—including those previously deemed too complex for automation—without hardware reconfiguration.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides an automated radiosynthesizer device configured for the reliable and flexible synthesis of positron emission tomography (PET) tracers, including those requiring high-temperature, high-pressure, or corrosive reaction conditions. The device comprises a plurality of reactor assemblies, each adapted to receive and thermally regulate a reaction vessel, and capable of vertical and horizontal movement to align the vessel with different functional positions on a disposable cassette. The disposable cassettes serve as self-contained, single-use fluidic modules that house reagents, define fluid and gas pathways, and provide sealing interfaces for the reactor vessels, thereby eliminating the need for system cleaning or hardware modification between different syntheses. Integrated within the synthesizer is a reagent and gas handling robot, which includes a multi-axis motion system equipped with a vial gripper and a gas supply manifold, enabling automated delivery of reagents from sealed storage vials and controlled application of inert gas and vacuum to the cassette during synthesis operations.

The control system of the device orchestrates all mechanical, thermal, and fluidic functions through a software interface that organizes synthesis protocols into modular “unit operations,” such as REACT, EVAPORATE, ADD, and TRANSFER, allowing users to construct complex sequences without low-level programming. In operation, the automated method of performing radiosynthesis begins with the movement of a reactor vial to a designated position beneath the cassette, where it is raised to seal against a gasket on the cassette’s underside. The system then performs operations on radiosynthesis reagents—such as heating, stirring, pressurization, or evacuation—according to the programmed protocol, followed by the transfer of the radiosynthesis product to a subsequent reaction vessel, purification cartridge, or collection vial.

In an alternative embodiment, the reagent and gas handling robot is actuated to perform an evaporation step, wherein the reaction vessel is sealed at an evaporate position, heated, and subjected to a combination of inert gas flow and vacuum to remove volatile solvents efficiently. In another embodiment, the same robot is actuated to perform a reagent addition operation, wherein a sealed vial is retrieved from storage, moved to an addition position, and pressurized to deliver its contents into the reaction vessel. The system is further capable of manufacturing multiple PET tracers by simply replacing the disposable cassette and loading a corresponding synthesis protocol, demonstrating unprecedented versatility in a single platform. This architecture enables the synthesis of tracers such as d-[¹⁸F]FAC, l-[¹⁸F]FMAU, [¹⁸F]FDG, [¹⁸F]FLT, [¹⁸F]fallypride, [¹⁸F]FHBG, and [¹⁸F]SFB without any physical reconfiguration of the instrument, thereby bridging the gap between tracer development and routine production.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF ILLUSTRATED EMBODIMENTS

The automated radiosynthesizer of the present invention is illustrated as a compact, integrated system comprising mechanical, fluidic, and electronic subsystems housed within a shielded enclosure. The synthesizer components include three reactor assemblies, a reagent and gas handling robot, a set of disposable cassettes, and a centralized control system. The control system interface is implemented as a client-server software architecture, allowing users to interact with the synthesizer through remote client devices such as tablets, laptops, or desktop computers. This client device interface represents a paradigm shift from traditional embedded synthesizer controls, offering a graphical, drag-and-drop environment for synthesis protocol creation. Protocols are constructed by arranging predefined “unit operations”—atomic chemistry steps such as ADD, REACT, EVAPORATE, and TRANSFER—into a sequence that defines the entire synthesis workflow.

Examples of unit operations include: moving a reactor to a specific position, raising the reactor to seal against the cassette, heating the reactor to a target temperature, activating stirring, actuating the reagent robot to retrieve a vial, delivering reagent via pressurization, applying vacuum for evaporation, and transferring product through a purification cartridge. On the client device, the user interface displays a real-time system status panel showing reactor temperatures, robot positions, and radiation levels. A sequencer display presents the list of unit operations in the current protocol, with the current unit operation highlighted for clarity. An abort button is prominently displayed to allow immediate termination of the synthesis in case of emergency.

The software architecture employs a Linux-based server that communicates with client devices over a secure network. The server hosts multiple applications: a web server application that delivers the user interface to clients, a core server application that manages protocol execution and system state, a database server application that stores synthesis logs, reagent inventories, and user profiles, and a video server application that streams live camera feeds from the reactor zones. Additionally, a command-line interface is available for advanced diagnostics and maintenance. The synthesizer subsystems are coordinated through this software layer. The reactor assemblies consist of spring-biased heating chucks that hold 5-mL glass V-vials, each equipped with independent cartridge heaters and K-type thermocouples for precise temperature control up to 185°C. Active liquid cooling is achieved via a closed-loop glycol-water system with a radiator and fans.

Mounted behind each reactor is a camera and mount, providing visual feedback during synthesis. Vertically-oriented actuators raise and lower the reactors, while horizontally-oriented actuators translate them between functional positions beneath the cassette. Reactor assembly movement is monitored via Hall-effect sensors and linear actuator feedback to ensure positional accuracy. The synthesizer, designated as synthesizer 12, accommodates disposable cassettes 80, which are molded polyurethane modules containing all fluid paths, reagent vials, and sealing interfaces. The cassette 80 structure includes a bottom surface with a custom PTFE-coated silicone gasket that forms a pressure-tight seal with the reactor vial top. Top-view features include eleven vial storage positions 88 for inverted crimp-sealed reagent vials, and three reagent addition positions 108a, 108b, 108c, each with dual needles for fluid delivery and gas pressurization.

Inlet gas ports 114a–114e on the cassette top interface with the gas handling robot, while a cartridge waste vial location 116 and recovery vial location 118 house collection vessels. A vacuum port 120 and inlet ports 122 facilitate gas flow, and outlet ports 124 connect to external lines or downstream cassettes. The side profile of the cassette reveals internal gas flow path 128 and vacuum flow path 130, which direct gases to the appropriate functional zones. During an EVAPORATE unit process, the reactor assembly 50 is positioned beneath the evaporate zone and sealed, with vacuum applied to remove solvent vapor. In a REACT unit process, the reactor is sealed in a dedicated reaction zone with no fluid connections, enabling high-pressure chemistry. In an ADD unit operation, the reactor aligns with a reagent addition position, and the robot delivers reagent from a storage vial. In a TRANSFER unit operation, the reactor seals at the transfer position, and product is pushed through a purification cartridge 132 mounted on the cassette.

The radioisotope handling configuration supports trapping of [¹⁸F]fluoride on anion exchange cartridges and elution into the reaction vessel. The reagent and gas handling robot 140 includes a head portion 142 with a vial gripper 158 and a gas manifold 152. Motion is provided by x-axis motion actuator 144, y-axis motion actuator 146, and z-axis actuators 148, 150. Hall-effect sensors confirm vial presence and actuator positions. An in-line check valve prevents backflow, and a cold-trap protects the vacuum pump. Inert gas is supplied from a regulated source, with analog pressure regulators and solenoid valve banks controlling flow. The control system 14 integrates a PLC or embedded computer 164, motor controllers, and a RoboNET network controller 182. A microcontroller 180 interfaces with stir motor drivers 184, heater controllers 186, a radiation amplifier 188, an HPLC controller 190, valve drivers, position sensors 194, and a vacuum pump 196.

The automated synthesizer 10 performs radiosynthesis by executing a sequence of unit operations. Radioisotope handling involves trapping [¹⁸F]fluoride on a QMA cartridge and eluting with a basic solution. Reagent handling uses the robot to move and pressurize vials. Reactions occur under sealed, heated conditions with optional stirring. Evaporations combine heat, vacuum, and gas flow. Transfer and purification utilize Sep-Pak cartridges for crude product cleanup. Radiosynthesis materials include anhydrous solvents, silylated precursors, and preconditioned cartridges. The synthesis protocol is defined in software, with semi-preparative and analytical HPLC used for purification and quality control. Results demonstrate high yields, purity, and reproducibility for d-[¹⁸F]FAC and l-[¹⁸F]FMAU, with in vivo imaging confirming biological equivalence to manually produced tracers.

The advantages of the automated synthesizer 10 include elimination of permanent wetted connections, support for high-pressure chemistry, rapid cassette-based changeover, and intuitive software control. Its flexibility enables synthesis of diverse tracers without hardware changes. The scope of the invention extends to any radiosynthesis requiring dynamic reconfiguration of fluid paths, including multi-step, multi-reactor sequences. Modifications may include additional reactors, alternative cassette geometries, or integration with formulation and dispensing modules. The dimensions of the drawings are illustrative, and the invention is not limited by scale. Limitations include reliance on cassette availability and the need for initial protocol development. The claims of the invention encompass the apparatus, methods, and software described herein, and equivalents include functionally similar systems employing movable reactors, disposable fluidics, and robotic reagent handling for automated radiosynthesis.
