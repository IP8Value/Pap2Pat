# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates to magnetic graphene-like nanoparticles, particularly those comprising intercalated paramagnetic metal ions such as manganese within graphitic or graphene-based nanostructures. These magnetic compositions are designed for use as contrast agents in magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), offering enhanced relaxivity and improved diagnostic performance compared to conventional small-molecule chelate-based contrast agents. The invention further encompasses methods of synthesizing these magnetic nanoparticles, formulations for their administration, and their application in clinical and preclinical imaging settings.

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is a widely used non-invasive diagnostic modality that provides detailed anatomical and functional information about soft tissues and organs. To enhance image contrast and improve diagnostic accuracy, MRI contrast agents (CAs) are routinely employed. These agents function by altering the relaxation times of water protons in their vicinity, thereby increasing the signal intensity differences between normal and pathological tissues. The two primary types of MRI contrast agents are T1 agents, which shorten longitudinal relaxation time and produce bright contrast, and T2 agents, which shorten transverse relaxation time and produce dark contrast. The efficacy of a contrast agent is quantified by its relaxivity, defined as the change in relaxation rate per unit concentration of the agent.

Gadolinium (Gd³⁺)-based chelates have long dominated the clinical market for T1 MRI contrast agents due to their high paramagnetism and favorable relaxation properties. However, concerns over nephrogenic systemic fibrosis (NSF) in patients with renal impairment have led to regulatory restrictions and renewed interest in alternative paramagnetic metals. Manganese (Mn²⁺) has emerged as a promising substitute because it is an essential biological trace element with a similar ionic radius and coordination chemistry to calcium, and it exhibits strong paramagnetic properties suitable for T1 contrast enhancement.

In parallel with the search for safer metal ions, researchers have explored carbon nanostructures as platforms for contrast agent development. Gadofullerenes and gadonanotubes—carbon nanostructures encapsulating Gd³⁺ ions—have demonstrated significantly higher relaxivities than conventional chelates, attributed to the confinement of paramagnetic ions within nanoporous carbon architectures. These findings suggest that geometric confinement can profoundly influence the interaction between paramagnetic centers and surrounding water protons, thereby enhancing relaxivity. Despite these advances, the potential of Mn²⁺-based carbon nanostructures has remained largely unexplored.

Graphene, a two-dimensional allotrope of carbon, has attracted considerable attention for its unique electronic, mechanical, and thermal properties. Theoretical studies have predicted various magnetic phenomena in graphene, including defect-induced magnetism and spin polarization. Experimentally, graphene oxide and graphene nanoribbons have been synthesized at scale using oxidative chemical methods involving potassium permanganate (KMnO₄). During these syntheses, trace amounts of manganese ions become unintentionally intercalated between graphene layers. Recent studies have begun to investigate whether this intercalation imparts useful magnetic properties to the resulting materials.

Large-scale production methods now enable the synthesis of graphene nanoplatelets and graphene nanoribbons with controlled dimensions and surface functionalities. Graphene oxide, in particular, possesses oxygen-containing functional groups that facilitate water solubilization and biocompatibility. The physicochemical properties of these graphene-like nanostructures—including thickness, lateral dimensions, surface chemistry, and defect density—can be tuned to optimize performance for biomedical applications. Experimental characterization of manganese-intercalated graphene materials has revealed unexpected magnetic behaviors, including room-temperature superparamagnetism and ferromagnetism, suggesting their potential utility as MRI contrast agents.

The nuclear magnetic resonance dispersion (NMRD) profiles of these manganese-intercalated graphene nanostructures differ markedly from those of conventional Mn²⁺ chelates, exhibiting substantially higher relaxivities across a broad range of magnetic fields. This behavior parallels that observed in gadonanotubes, implying that confinement within graphitic structures may represent a general strategy for enhancing relaxivity. However, the precise mechanisms underlying this enhancement—particularly the roles of water exchange dynamics, rotational correlation times, and inner-sphere coordination—are not fully understood. The present invention addresses these gaps by providing well-defined magnetic graphene-like nanoparticles with optimized relaxometric properties for use in MRI.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a magnetic composition comprising a graphene-like nanostructure intercalated with a magnetic metal, wherein the composition exhibits enhanced relaxivity suitable for use as an MRI contrast agent. The graphene-like nanostructure is a two-dimensional carbon material derived from graphite, having a thickness of less than 100 nanometers and preferably between 1 and 20 nanometers. In specific embodiments, the nanostructure is selected from the group consisting of graphene nanoplatelets, graphene nanoribbons, and oxidized derivatives thereof. The composition further includes a water solubilizing moiety covalently or non-covalently attached to the surface of the graphene-like nanostructure to ensure colloidal stability in aqueous media.

The magnetic metal is selected from paramagnetic, ferromagnetic, or superparamagnetic elements, with manganese being a preferred embodiment due to its biocompatibility and strong T1 contrast properties. The magnetic composition demonstrates a longitudinal relaxivity (r1) of at least 50 mM⁻¹s⁻¹ at 0.47 Tesla and 40°C, and in some embodiments exceeds 200 mM⁻¹s⁻¹ at low magnetic fields (0.01 MHz). The invention also encompasses a method of performing MRI, comprising administering to a subject an effective amount of the magnetic composition and acquiring MR images using standard imaging protocols. Additionally, the invention provides a pharmaceutical composition formulated for parenteral or oral administration, containing the magnetic composition in a pharmaceutically acceptable carrier.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

The magnetic composition of the present invention comprises a graphene-like nanostructure intercalated with a magnetic metal. As used herein, “graphene-like nanostructure” refers to a two-dimensional carbon material composed of one or more stacked layers of sp²-hybridized carbon atoms arranged in a hexagonal lattice, wherein the structure may include defects, functional groups, or edge modifications. The term encompasses graphene, graphene oxide, reduced graphene oxide, graphene nanoplatelets, and graphene nanoribbons. The thickness of the graphene-like nanostructure is typically less than 100 nanometers, and in preferred embodiments ranges from 1 to 20 nanometers, corresponding to approximately 3 to 60 atomic layers. Thinner structures generally exhibit higher surface area and improved interaction with water protons, enhancing relaxivity.

The graphitic nano- or microstructure may alternatively refer to three-dimensional aggregates of graphene layers, such as graphite nanoplatelets or exfoliated graphite, with thicknesses up to several micrometers. However, for optimal MRI performance, nanostructured forms with high aspect ratios and large surface-to-volume ratios are preferred. Carbon nanoplatelets are disc-shaped particles with diameters ranging from 50 to 5000 nanometers and thicknesses of 1–100 nanometers. Carbon nanoribbons are elongated structures with widths of 10–500 nanometers, lengths of 100–10,000 nanometers, and thicknesses comparable to nanoplatelets.

A water solubilizing moiety is attached to the surface of the graphene-like nanostructure to confer aqueous dispersibility and prevent aggregation. Suitable moieties include polyethylene glycol (PEG), Pluronic surfactants, carboxylate groups, sulfonate groups, and polysaccharides. Attachment may occur via covalent bonding to oxygen-containing functional groups on oxidized graphene surfaces or through non-covalent adsorption mediated by hydrophobic or π–π interactions.

The magnetic metal is selected from elements that exhibit paramagnetic, ferromagnetic, or superparamagnetic behavior at room temperature. Paramagnetic metals include Mn²⁺, Gd³⁺, Fe³⁺, and other lanthanides. Ferromagnetic metals include Fe, Co, and Ni. Rare earth metals such as Gd, Dy, and Ho may also be employed. In a preferred embodiment, the magnetic metal is manganese, present predominantly in the +2 oxidation state. The composition may contain a single magnetic metal or a combination of multiple metals to tune magnetic properties. The magnetic metal is intercalated between the graphene layers during synthesis, typically at concentrations ranging from 0.01 to 5 weight percent, as determined by inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectroscopy (ICP-OES).

Preferred embodiments include manganese-intercalated graphene oxide nanoplatelets and graphene nanoribbons, which exhibit r1 relaxivities of 80–260 mM⁻¹s⁻¹ at 0.47 T and enhanced performance at low magnetic fields. The method of performing MRI involves intravenous or oral administration of the magnetic composition at a dose of 0.01–0.5 mmol Mn per kg body weight, followed by image acquisition using T1-weighted sequences. A kit for MRI imaging includes the magnetic composition in lyophilized or liquid form, along with instructions for reconstitution and use.

Toxicity studies indicate low cytotoxicity at clinically relevant concentrations. The composition may be formulated for parenteral administration as a sterile aqueous suspension or for oral delivery using enteric coatings. The magnetic composition can also serve as a platform for targeted drug delivery by conjugating targeting ligands such as antibodies or peptides. Synthesis involves oxidative exfoliation of graphite or multi-walled carbon nanotubes in the presence of KMnO₄, followed by reduction with hydrazine or alternative reducing agents such as sodium borohydride or ascorbic acid. An alternative method employs direct intercalation of pre-formed graphene with manganese salts under hydrothermal conditions.

### EXAMPLES

The present invention is further illustrated by the following examples. Graphene nanoplatelets (GNPs) were synthesized by modified Hummer’s method: analytical grade micro-graphite (200 mg) was oxidized, then exfoliated by sonication in water (200 mL, 1 h), yielding oxidized GNPs. Reduction was achieved by adding hydrazine hydrate (1.5 mL) and heating at 100°C for 12 h to produce reduced GNPs. Graphene nanoribbons (GNRs) were prepared by longitudinal unzipping of multi-walled carbon nanotubes (MWCNTs, 150 mg) using concentrated H₂SO₄ and KMnO₄ (750 mg) at 55–70°C for 1 h, followed by washing and centrifugation.

Samples were analyzed by transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and Raman spectroscopy. TEM images confirmed the platelet morphology of GNPs (lateral size ~1 µm, thickness ~5 nm) and ribbon-like structure of GNRs (width ~50 nm, length >1 µm). Atomic force microscopy (AFM) of graphene oxide nanoplatelets showed average thicknesses of 1.2–1.8 nm, consistent with few-layer graphene. Raman spectra exhibited characteristic D and G bands at ~1350 cm⁻¹ and ~1580 cm⁻¹, respectively; the intensity ratio (I_D/I_G) indicated defect density. Additional peaks at 650 cm⁻¹ in reduced GNPs corresponded to hausmannite (Mn₃O₄), confirming manganese presence.

Elemental analysis by ICP-OES detected manganese at concentrations of 0.1–0.5 wt% in all samples, with trace iron (<50 µg/g) in GNRs. SQUID magnetometry revealed diamagnetic behavior in pristine graphite, paramagnetism in oxidized GNPs, and superparamagnetism in reduced GNPs at 300 K with a blocking temperature of 40 K. GNRs exhibited room-temperature weak ferromagnetism, with saturation magnetization of 0.1 emu/g and coercivity of 250 Oe at 10 K. Field-cooled/zero-field-cooled (FC/ZFC) curves confirmed irreversible magnetic behavior above 300 K.

Relaxivity measurements were performed at 0.47 T (20 MHz) and 40°C using a Minispec NMR spectrometer. Samples were dispersed in 1% Pluronic F127, sonicated, and centrifuged to remove aggregates. No free Mn²⁺ was detected in supernatants (detection limit: 1 ppm). Longitudinal (T1) and transverse (T2) relaxation times were measured via inversion recovery and CPMG sequences, respectively. Relaxivity values (r1) were calculated from the slope of relaxation rate vs. manganese concentration. GNPs and GNRs exhibited r1 values of 80–100 mM⁻¹s⁻¹, approximately 10-fold higher than clinical Mn-DTPA (r1 ≈ 8 mM⁻¹s⁻¹).

NMRD profiles (0.01–80 MHz) showed distinct field-dependent relaxivity: GNRs displayed a steep increase below 10 MHz, reaching 724 mM⁻¹s⁻¹ at 0.01 MHz, surpassing gadonanotubes. Fitting to Solomon-Bloembergen-Morgan equations yielded inner-sphere parameters: q = 8 water molecules, τ_R = 1–10 ns, τ_M = 100–500 ns, and r_MH ≈ 2.9 Å. These values indicate slow rotation and prolonged water residence, consistent with nanoconfinement effects.

In vitro phantom MRI using 2D spin-echo imaging at 1.5 T demonstrated strong T1 contrast enhancement. Signal intensity ratios (sample/control) exceeded 3.0 at 0.1 mg/mL Mn, confirming efficacy. Collectively, these results verify that manganese intercalation within graphene nanostructures yields high-relaxivity, biocompatible MRI contrast agents. Example 2 (not detailed here) would further demonstrate in vivo imaging, biodistribution, and toxicity profiling.
