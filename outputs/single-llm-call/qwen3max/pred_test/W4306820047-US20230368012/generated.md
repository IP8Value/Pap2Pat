# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

Object recognition constitutes a foundational capability in modern computer vision systems, enabling applications ranging from autonomous navigation and surveillance to biometric identification and industrial automation. Conventional object recognition systems rely on digital artificial neural networks (ANNs) that process digitized images captured by optical sensors. These systems typically involve a multi-stage pipeline: an optical imaging system forms a real-space image of a target object; an optoelectronic sensor converts the analog optical signal into a digital representation; and a digital processor executes computationally intensive neural network algorithms to classify or identify the object. While effective, this architecture suffers from inherent limitations including high power consumption, latency due to sequential data conversion and processing steps, physical bulkiness arising from discrete optical and electronic components, and vulnerability to cybersecurity threats stemming from the digital nature of intermediate data representations.

Digital ANNs, despite their remarkable success in pattern recognition tasks, are fundamentally constrained by the von Neumann bottleneck—the separation between memory and processing units—which limits computational throughput and energy efficiency. Moreover, the requirement for analog-to-digital conversion introduces quantization errors and further delays, while the reliance on active electronic components necessitates continuous power supply, rendering such systems unsuitable for deployment in resource-constrained or remote environments. As demand grows for real-time, low-power, and secure edge computing solutions, these limitations become increasingly prohibitive.

Optical neural networks (ONNs) have emerged as a promising alternative paradigm that leverages the intrinsic properties of light—such as ultrafast propagation, parallelism, and interference—to perform computations directly in the optical domain. Unlike digital ANNs, ONNs encode information in the amplitude, phase, polarization, and spatial profile of light waves, and manipulate these optical signals through engineered photonic structures to achieve inference without intermediate digitization. This approach offers the potential for orders-of-magnitude improvements in speed and energy efficiency, as well as enhanced data security due to the absence of digital intermediates that could be intercepted or tampered with.

However, existing ONN implementations face significant challenges. Integrated photonic circuits based on Mach-Zehnder interferometers (MZIs) offer programmability but suffer from large footprints relative to the wavelength of light, limiting the number of trainable parameters and thus the expressive power of the network. Diffractive ONNs realized with 3D-printed masks or spatial light modulators (SLMs) operate in the terahertz or visible spectrum but lack subwavelength resolution and cannot simultaneously control all degrees of freedom of light (phase, amplitude, and polarization). Hybrid optoelectronic architectures partially address these issues but reintroduce power consumption and latency by relying on electronic nonlinearities. Furthermore, many ONN platforms require complex alignment, active tuning, or non-standard fabrication processes, hindering scalability and practical deployment. These challenges underscore the need for a compact, passive, manufacturable, and high-performance ONN platform capable of executing complex recognition tasks with robustness and efficiency.

## SUMMARY

The present invention introduces a target recognition system based on metasurface-enabled optical neural networks that directly processes incident light waves scattered from objects to perform high-accuracy classification and verification tasks without digital conversion or external power. The system comprises a metasurface—either as a singlet or doublet—composed of subwavelength nanostructures termed meta-units, arranged on a transparent substrate to form a diffractive optical neural network (ONN). Incident coherent light illuminates a target object, generating a complex optical wavefront that propagates to the metasurface, which applies a spatially varying phase (and optionally polarization-dependent) modulation to the wavefront. The modulated light then propagates to an output plane containing predefined detection zones, where the intensity distribution encodes the identity or class of the input object.

The system operates in the near-infrared wavelength range, specifically at approximately 1,550 nanometers, leveraging mature telecom-compatible light sources and detectors. It functions entirely passively, requiring no electrical power beyond the initial illumination source, and computes at the speed of light propagation through the device. The target types recognizable by the system include handwritten digits, alphabetical characters, typographic styles, and grayscale facial images, demonstrating versatility across binary and grayscale, simple and complex visual patterns.

The metasurface is fabricated using CMOS-compatible nanofabrication techniques on a transparent dielectric substrate such as silicon dioxide, ensuring scalability and integration potential. Meta-units are constructed from passive dielectric materials like amorphous silicon, chosen for low optical loss in the operational wavelength band. These meta-units exhibit either isotropic or birefringent optical responses, enabling polarization-multiplexed operation wherein orthogonal polarization states can be used to perform independent recognition tasks simultaneously on the same physical device.

The output plane features spatially separated detection zones—square or circular regions—each corresponding to a specific target class or identity. Recognition is achieved by concentrating the majority of the optical intensity into the zone associated with the correct classification, with decision logic based on identifying the zone with peak intensity. The system supports both single-task recognition (e.g., digit classification) and multi-task recognition (e.g., letter identity and font style) through polarization multiplexing. Additionally, double-layered metasurfaces (metasurface doublets) enhance expressive power, enabling advanced tasks such as facial verification by mapping high-dimensional facial images into low-dimensional optical barcodes represented as intensity arrays on the detection plane. The similarity between two such barcodes is evaluated via Euclidean distance, allowing binary match/non-match decisions with accuracy comparable to shallow digital convolutional neural networks.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

For the purposes of this patent application, technical terms are defined as follows. The term “about” or “approximately” refers to a variation of up to ±10% of the stated numerical value, unless otherwise specified by context or technical convention. The system for processing light comprises an illumination source, an input object plane, a metasurface layer, and an output detection plane, all aligned along an optical axis. Light from the source illuminates the input object, which may be a binary or grayscale transparency representing a digit, letter, or facial image. The scattered or transmitted light forms a complex wavefront that propagates to the metasurface.

A metasurface is a two-dimensional array of subwavelength scattering elements, herein referred to as meta-units, engineered to impart spatially varying modifications to the phase, amplitude, and/or polarization state of an incident optical wavefront. A metasurface is considered “coupled” to another optical element when light propagating from one element directly impinges upon and interacts with the other without intermediate detection or conversion; coupling may be achieved through free-space propagation over a defined distance, referred to as the object distance (between input and metasurface) or imaging distance (between metasurface and output plane).

The system for recognizing targets implements a diffractive optical neural network (ONN), wherein the input object, metasurface, and detection zones correspond to input, hidden, and output layers of a neural network, respectively. Each pixel in these layers functions as an artificial neuron, with optical interference providing implicit nonlinear activation through cross-term generation during wave propagation. The ONN operates passively and requires no external power supply beyond the illumination source, functioning entirely through linear optical scattering and diffraction.

The transparent substrate may be composed of silicon dioxide, fused silica, or other dielectric materials with refractive indices between 1.4 and 1.8 and low absorption at the operational wavelength. Passive dielectric materials such as amorphous silicon, titanium dioxide, or silicon nitride are used to construct meta-units due to their high refractive index and minimal optical loss in the near-infrared. Actively tunable materials, such as phase-change materials or liquid crystals, may be incorporated in alternative embodiments to enable reconfigurable operation, though the primary embodiment relies on static, passive structures.

Meta-units are patterned on the substrate using electron-beam lithography or deep ultraviolet photolithography, followed by etching or deposition processes, yielding nanostructures with heights of approximately 1 micrometer and lateral dimensions below the operational wavelength (e.g., 750 nm periodicity at 1,550 nm wavelength). Two libraries of meta-units are employed: an isotropic library comprising nanostructures with four-fold rotational symmetry (e.g., cylinders or squares), which impart polarization-independent phase shifts; and a birefringent library comprising structures with two-fold symmetry (e.g., rectangles or crosses), which provide distinct phase responses for orthogonal linear polarization states.

Optical amplitude and phase are controlled by tailoring the geometry, orientation, and material composition of each meta-unit. Engineering optical dispersion—i.e., the wavelength dependence of phase and amplitude response—is achieved by varying cross-sectional dimensions to tailor resonant behavior across the spectrum. The output plane contains detection zones whose locations may be modified during training to optimize classification performance; light intensity is concentrated into the correct zone via constructive interference engineered through the metasurface’s phase profile.

Object recognition is accomplished by training the metasurface to map input wavefronts to target intensity distributions on the output plane. A diffractive ONN is formed by numerically simulating light propagation using Rayleigh-Sommerfeld diffraction theory and iteratively optimizing the metasurface phase profile via gradient-based methods (e.g., Adam optimizer) to minimize a loss function based on cross-entropy between predicted and target intensity distributions. The system recognizes handwritten digits (e.g., MNIST dataset), achieving >99% accuracy for 4-class tasks and ~80% for 10-class tasks, and extends to alphabetical letters and typographic styles via polarization multiplexing.

Double-layered metasurfaces (metasurface doublets) enhance expressive power by introducing an additional diffractive layer, enabling translation of grayscale facial images into 3×3 optical barcodes. Similarity between barcodes is evaluated via Euclidean distance, facilitating facial verification with ~80% accuracy. Additional metasurfaces may be cascaded or arranged in arrays to handle incoherent illumination or increase classification capacity. Critically, the system bypasses digitization entirely, preserving data privacy and eliminating cyber vulnerabilities associated with digital intermediates.

The method for processing light involves illuminating an object with coherent light, allowing the resulting wavefront to propagate to the metasurface, applying a trained phase modulation, and detecting the resulting intensity pattern at the output plane. Training involves feeding thousands of labeled input images into a numerical model of the ONN, computing forward propagation, calculating loss, and backpropagating gradients to update the metasurface design. Robustness against experimental errors—such as misalignment, distance variations, and illumination non-uniformity—is improved by incorporating these perturbations into the training data and augmenting the loss function with an inter-zone contrast term that maximizes intensity in the correct zone relative to background and competing zones.

The final optical output constitutes an “optical barcode”—a low-dimensional intensity vector—that uniquely represents the input object. The configuration of meta-units is chosen during training to maximize target recognition accuracy under realistic experimental constraints, balancing phase coverage, fabrication tolerance, and optical efficiency.
