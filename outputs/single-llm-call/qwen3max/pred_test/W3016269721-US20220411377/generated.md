# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

Nitric oxide synthases (NOSs) constitute a family of homodimeric enzymes that are fundamentally responsible for the biosynthesis of nitric oxide (NO), a critical signaling molecule in mammalian physiology. These enzymes catalyze the oxidation of L-arginine to produce L-citrulline and NO, a reaction that is essential for numerous biological processes ranging from neurotransmission to immune response and vascular regulation. The functional architecture of NOS enzymes is highly conserved, featuring each monomer composed of a reductase domain and an oxygenase domain separated by a calmodulin-binding region. The reductase domain harbors binding sites for flavin adenine dinucleotide (FAD), flavin mononucleotide (FMN), and reduced nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate (NADPH), while the oxygenase domain contains binding sites for heme, (6R)-5,6,7,8-tetrahydrobiopterin (H4B), and the substrate L-arginine. Electron transfer occurs from the reductase domain of one monomer through FAD and FMN to the oxygenase domain of the opposite monomer, where it facilitates the conversion of L-arginine to NO and L-citrulline.

In mammalian systems, NO serves as a vital secondary messenger with diverse physiological roles that are tightly regulated by the spatial and temporal expression of three distinct NOS isoforms: neuronal NOS (nNOS), endothelial NOS (eNOS), and inducible NOS (iNOS). Each isoform exhibits unique patterns of tissue distribution, subcellular localization, and regulatory mechanisms that correspond to specific physiological functions. nNOS is predominantly expressed in the central and peripheral nervous systems, where it plays a crucial role in normal neuronal communication, synaptic plasticity, and memory formation. eNOS is primarily localized in vascular endothelial cells and is responsible for maintaining vascular tone and regulating blood pressure through the production of NO that induces smooth muscle relaxation. iNOS, in contrast, is expressed in various cell types in response to inflammatory stimuli and produces large quantities of NO as part of the immune defense mechanism against pathogens.

The selective inhibition of nNOS has emerged as a therapeutically desirable strategy for the treatment of various neurodegenerative disorders and neurological conditions characterized by neuronal damage or death. Overproduction of NO by nNOS in the central and peripheral nervous systems has been directly implicated in the pathogenesis of Alzheimer's disease, Huntington's disease, Parkinson's disease, amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), stroke, ischemic events, cerebral palsy, and both acute and chronic brain injuries. In these conditions, excessive NO production leads to oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and ultimately neuronal apoptosis, contributing to the progressive loss of neuronal integrity and associated neurological deficiencies. Therefore, the rational control of NO levels in neuronal tissues through selective nNOS inhibition represents a promising therapeutic approach for mitigating neuronal damage and preserving cognitive and motor functions.

Despite the clear therapeutic rationale for selective nNOS inhibition, existing nNOS inhibitors suffer from significant limitations that have hindered their clinical development. Most compounds initially investigated for nNOS inhibition were designed as competitive mimics of L-arginine, the natural substrate of NOS enzymes. However, these arginine-mimetic inhibitors typically possess high basicity and polarity, large total polar surface areas (tPSA), and an overabundance of hydrogen bond donors, which collectively result in poor pharmacokinetic properties. Specifically, these molecular characteristics severely compromise blood-brain barrier (BBB) permeability, limiting their ability to reach therapeutic concentrations in the central nervous system where nNOS-mediated neurotoxicity occurs. Additionally, many existing inhibitors lack sufficient selectivity between nNOS and the other NOS isoforms, particularly eNOS, which can lead to unwanted cardiovascular side effects due to interference with normal vascular function. The development of nNOS inhibitors that simultaneously achieve high potency, excellent isoform selectivity, and favorable BBB penetration remains a significant unmet need in the field of neuroprotective therapeutics.

## SUMMARY

The present invention discloses novel 7-phenyl-2-aminoquinoline compounds and pharmaceutical compositions containing these compounds, which exhibit potent and selective inhibition of neuronal nitric oxide synthase (nNOS). These disclosed compounds are specifically designed to address the limitations of existing nNOS inhibitors by achieving an optimal balance of high inhibitory potency against nNOS, exceptional selectivity over endothelial NOS (eNOS) and inducible NOS (iNOS), and favorable blood-brain barrier permeability characteristics. The method of using the disclosed compounds involves administering a therapeutically effective amount of at least one compound of the present invention to a subject in need thereof for the treatment, prevention, or management of diseases or disorders associated with nNOS overactivity. This includes neurodegenerative diseases such as Alzheimer's disease, Huntington's disease, Parkinson's disease, and amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), as well as conditions involving neuronal damage or death resulting from stroke, ischemic events, cerebral palsy, and acute or chronic brain injuries. The selective inhibition of nNOS by the disclosed compounds provides a rational approach to controlling excessive nitric oxide production in neuronal tissues, thereby mitigating oxidative stress, preserving neuronal integrity, and preventing the progression of neurological impairments.

The motivation for selective nNOS inhibition stems from the critical role that excessive NO production plays in the pathogenesis of various neurological disorders. Unlike non-selective NOS inhibitors that may interfere with essential physiological functions mediated by eNOS (vascular regulation) or iNOS (immune defense), the compounds of the present invention specifically target nNOS while sparing the other isoforms. This selectivity is achieved through strategic molecular design that exploits key structural differences between the nNOS and eNOS active sites, particularly the presence of aspartate residues (Asp597/Asp602 in rat/human nNOS) that are replaced by asparagine in eNOS isoforms. The disclosed compounds are engineered to form favorable interactions with these nNOS-specific aspartate residues, thereby achieving unprecedented levels of n/eNOS selectivity.

The disclosed compounds offer several significant advantages over prior art nNOS inhibitors. First, they demonstrate superior isoform selectivity, with some compounds exhibiting selectivity ratios exceeding 800-fold for nNOS over eNOS. Second, they possess favorable physicochemical properties that enable good blood-brain barrier penetration, as evidenced by effective permeability values exceeding 4.0 × 10^-6 cm/s in PAMPA-BBB assays. Third, they exhibit clean off-target profiles when screened against a panel of 45 pharmacologically relevant CNS targets and receptors, indicating a low potential for adverse side effects. Fourth, they demonstrate high metabolic stability in human liver microsome assays, suggesting favorable pharmacokinetic properties in vivo. Finally, crystallographic studies confirm that these compounds bind to the nNOS active site in a manner that maximizes interactions with isoform-specific residues while avoiding deleterious steric clashes.

The general formula for the disclosed compounds encompasses a central 7-phenyl-2-aminoquinoline scaffold with various substituents at specific positions designed to optimize nNOS binding and selectivity. The compounds may contain meta-substituted benzylamine moieties that position the terminal amino group to interact favorably with the nNOS-specific aspartate residue, 4-position alkoxy or heterocyclic substituents that form van der Waals interactions with isoform-specific methionine residues, and constrained amine functionalities that reduce conformational flexibility and enhance binding affinity. Specific embodiments include compounds with cyclopropylmethoxy, pyridinylmethyl ether, and thiazole substituents that demonstrate exceptional potency and selectivity profiles.

The potential applications of the disclosed compounds extend beyond neurodegenerative disorders to include any condition where selective nNOS inhibition would be therapeutically beneficial. This includes pain management, where nNOS-derived NO contributes to central sensitization and chronic pain states; psychiatric disorders such as depression and anxiety, where dysregulated NO signaling may play a role; and neuroinflammatory conditions where excessive nNOS activity contributes to neuronal damage. The compounds may be formulated as standalone therapeutics or in combination with other neuroprotective agents to provide synergistic benefits in the treatment of complex neurological disorders.

### ILLUSTRATIVE EMBODIMENTS

The illustrative embodiments of the present invention are defined by specific structural features that optimize nNOS inhibition and selectivity. A compound of formula I is defined wherein X represents various substituents designed to maximize interactions with the nNOS active site. In embodiment 1, X is specifically defined as a meta-substituted benzylamine group that positions the terminal amino functionality to interact with the nNOS-specific aspartate residue (Asp597/Asp602 in rat/human nNOS). The substituents of X in this embodiment include aminomethyl, aminoethyl, and other alkylamino groups that maintain optimal distance and orientation for hydrogen bonding or electrostatic interactions with the aspartate carboxylate.

Embodiment 2 defines X as a 4-substituted phenyl group wherein the substituent is selected from alkoxy, haloalkoxy, or heterocyclic ether groups. In this embodiment, X is specifically designed to form van der Waals interactions with Met336/Met341 in rat/human nNOS, residues that are replaced by smaller valine residues in eNOS. The substituents of X in embodiment 2 include methoxy, ethoxy, cyclopropylmethoxy, and pyridinylmethyl ether groups that have been optimized through structure-activity relationship studies to balance potency and selectivity.

Embodiment 3 introduces a variable Z that represents the heterocyclic portion of the molecule, specifically defining Z as a quinoline ring system substituted at the 4-position with methyl or other small alkyl groups. Embodiment 4 defines R as the terminal amino group substituent, which may be hydrogen, alkyl, or part of a constrained ring system. The substituents of R in this embodiment include methyl, ethyl, and cyclic amine groups that have been evaluated for their impact on nNOS binding affinity and isoform selectivity.

Embodiment 5 specifies Z as a quinoline ring system with specific substitution patterns that optimize interactions with the conserved glutamate residue (Glu592/Glu602 in rat/human nNOS) in the active site. Embodiment 6 defines compounds wherein the central phenyl ring is substituted with both meta-aminoalkyl and para-alkoxy groups to simultaneously engage both the aspartate-specific pocket and the methionine-containing hydrophobic region. Embodiment 7 specifies X as a heteroaromatic ether group, particularly pyridinylmethyl ether, which can function as either a hydrogen bond acceptor or hydrophobic moiety depending on the nNOS isoform.

The synthesis of derivatives followed systematic approaches to generate structural diversity and optimize activity. Derivative 49 was synthesized using the Ellman auxiliary method to install the (S)-alpha-methyl-phenethylamine group with high diastereoselectivity. Derivatives 10/11 and 12/13 were prepared from commercially available ortho- and meta-substituted benzylamines and phenethylamines through Boc-protection, Suzuki coupling, and deprotection sequences. The Suzuki coupling reactions between the 7-BPin quinoline intermediate and various aryl halides proceeded efficiently under microwave irradiation with sodium bicarbonate as base, providing protected phenylquinolines in good yields.

Deprotection of derivatives 58-66 was accomplished through a stepwise process involving potassium carbonate-mediated cleavage of the acetyl group followed by methanolic HCl treatment to remove the Boc protecting group. Derivative 14, containing a 5-cyano substituent, was synthesized through an unexpected mono-Boc protection pathway when treating the bromobenzene precursor with Boc2NH. Derivative 15, featuring a pyridine ring instead of phenyl, required careful optimization of deprotection conditions to remove both Boc groups without decomposition.

Derivatives 16-18 were prepared through different synthetic strategies to incorporate constrained amine functionalities. Derivative 16 utilized commercially available 4-bromoisoindoline, while derivatives 17 and 18 were synthesized from bromoindanones using the Ellman method to install the amino group. Derivatives 19-24 were accessed from commercially available benzaldehydes or toluene derivatives through conversion to benzyl halides, Delépine reactions, and subsequent functionalization. The preparation of 4-ether halide derivatives 25-37 employed a more efficient route involving reduction of a phenol precursor followed by ether formation with various alkyl or benzyl halides.

Inhibitory constants were determined using the hemoglobin capture assay against purified rat nNOS as a prescreen, with selected compounds further evaluated against human nNOS, murine iNOS, and human eNOS to determine isoform selectivity. Crystallography studies provided atomic-level insights into binding modes and selectivity mechanisms. Initial inhibitory and structural data revealed that the lead compound 5 binds in the nNOS active site with the quinoline portion mimicking arginine and forming bifurcated hydrogen bonds with conserved active site residues.

The X-ray crystal structure of compound 5 bound to rnNOS, hnNOS, and heNOS demonstrated that the central phenyl ring resides between heme propionates A and D, with the tail phenethylamine moiety making different interactions in each isoform. In heNOS, two molecules of compound 5 were observed bound, with one in the active site and another displacing H4B in the pterin binding pocket. Optimization of the conformational positioning of the tail amine led to the discovery that meta-substitution (compound 12) provided superior activity and selectivity compared to para-substitution (compound 5).

X-ray crystal structures of compound 7 bound to rnNOS and hnNOS revealed well-defined density for the aminoquinoline and central ring regions, but poor density for the tail aminomethyl group, suggesting free rotation. Structure-activity relationship (SAR) analysis showed that phenylaminoquinoline compounds shared some similarities with previously reported phenyl ether compounds, such as preference for benzyl over phenethyl linkers, but also exhibited distinct differences, particularly regarding N-methylation effects.

Ortho-substituted isomers (compounds 10 and 11) demonstrated reduced inhibitory potency compared to their para-substituted counterparts, with compound 10 showing a sharp decrease in rn/miNOS selectivity despite improved hn/heNOS selectivity. Meta-substituted isomers 12 and 13 exhibited comparable or superior potencies to para-substituted derivatives, with compound 12 showing exceptional nNOS selectivity (rn/miNOS = 440, hn/heNOS = 877). Kinetic data for compound 12 confirmed its high potency and selectivity profile.

X-ray crystal structures of rnNOS-12 and hnNOS-12 revealed that the meta-positioned tail amino group participates in maximal binding interactions with a water-filled polar pocket containing Asp597/Asp602, Gln478/Gln483, and Arg481/Arg486. In contrast, the heNOS-12 structure showed the tail aminomethyl group oriented toward heme propionate A, with Asn366 replacing the aspartate residue found in nNOS isoforms. The homologated analogue 13 was proposed to potentially displace structural waters near the aspartate residue, but demonstrated weaker nNOS inhibitory activity, with crystal structures confirming that the aminoethyl group could only reach heme propionate A rather than the aspartate site.

Installation of a nitrile at the 5-position (compound 14) proved deleterious to activity, with Ki values increasing dramatically compared to compound 12. Crystal structure analysis indicated that the rigid phenylquinoline scaffold prevented the nitrile from reaching the auxiliary pocket observed in more flexible phenyl ether molecules, instead causing steric clashes with nearby residues. The pyridine analogue 15 was expected to be potent based on crystallographic observations showing hydrogen bonding with heme propionate D, but demonstrated approximately 5-fold lower potency than compound 12, possibly due to electronic effects or altered water interactions.

Constrained amine analogues 16-18 were designed to solidify contacts with the aspartate residue by reducing conformational flexibility. The 4-alkoxy-3-aminomethyl analogues 19-29 were prepared to investigate interactions with Met336/Met341 and His342 residues. Compound 24, featuring an ethoxy substituent, restored potency in rnNOS and showed interesting binding mode differences between rat and human isoforms, with the central phenyl ring flipping 180° in hnNOS relative to rnNOS.

Further improvements in rnNOS inhibition were achieved with small cycloalkyl groups, with compound 29 (cyclopropylmethoxy) emerging as the most potent rnNOS inhibitor in the series (Ki = 39 nM). The crystal structure of compound 29 bound to rnNOS revealed optimal fitting of the cyclopropane ring into the hydrophobic pocket surrounded by Met336, Leu337, and Tyr706. Pyridinylmethyl ether groups in compounds 32 and 33 were introduced to provide contact with Leu337 in rnNOS or His342 in hnNOS, with both compounds showing slight improvements in rnNOS potency.

Crystal structure analysis of rnNOS-32 revealed nearly identical binding positions for the aminoquinoline and tail pyridine compared to compound 33, but different orientations of the central phenyl ring affected water displacement capabilities. The hnNOS-32 structure showed decreased binding affinity due to the bulkier His342 side chain pushing the tail pyridine away, preventing close contacts with heme propionate D. Despite this, compound 32 maintained high selectivity ratios due to substantially diminished binding to miNOS and heNOS.

Compound 33 demonstrated equal potency against rat and human nNOS enzymes (Ki = 44-45 nM), with the 3-pyridyl moiety enabling favorable interactions in both isoforms. The hnNOS-33 structure suggested potential hydrogen bonding between the pyridine and His342, while the rnNOS-33 structure showed hydrophobic interactions with Leu337 and Met336. The heNOS-33 structure revealed better interactions with heme propionate A compared to compound 32, explaining the 3-fold improvement in heNOS binding affinity but resulting in poorer n/eNOS selectivity.

Heterocyclic modifications led to compound 35 (4-(methyloxy)-1,3-thiazole), which emerged as the most potent hnNOS inhibitor in the series (Ki = 31 nM). However, its selectivity was compromised by micromolar activity against miNOS and heNOS. Compound 36 (oxazole) showed slightly reduced hnNOS potency but excellent selectivities (rn/miNOS = 382, hn/heNOS = 433). Human iNOS inhibition studies confirmed that general inhibition trends were consistent between murine and human iNOS, with minor exceptions attributed to sequence differences at key positions.

Directed mutagenesis studies supported the importance of water-mediated interactions with Asp597 in rnNOS for achieving selectivity. Testing against rnNOS mutants (D597N and M336V/D597N) confirmed that the aspartate residue accounts for approximately half of the binding energy difference between nNOS and eNOS, with the remaining selectivity arising from more complex factors including active site dynamics and anchored plasticity. Off-target profiling through the National Institute of Mental Health's Psychoactive Drug Screening Program demonstrated clean profiles for representative compounds, with minimal binding to 45 pharmacologically relevant CNS targets.

Membrane permeability studies using the PAMPA-BBB assay classified all tested compounds as "CNS (+)" with effective permeability values exceeding 4.0 × 10^-6 cm/s. The SwissADME predictive model provided complementary insights, with compounds lacking polar heterocycles (like compound 29) showing better agreement between experimental and predicted BBB penetration. Microsome stability studies demonstrated high stability for compound 12 relative to positive controls, with half-lives exceeding 60 minutes in human liver microsomes.

In summary, the novel 7-phenyl-2-aminoquinolines represent a significant advancement in nNOS inhibitor design, combining high potency, exceptional selectivity, favorable BBB penetration, and clean off-target profiles. Modifications to the lead compound 12, guided by extensive crystallographic analysis and mutagenesis studies, have yielded compounds with optimized interactions with isoform-specific residues. The most promising candidates, compounds 12 and 29, are being advanced into preclinical studies based on their superior overall profiles.
