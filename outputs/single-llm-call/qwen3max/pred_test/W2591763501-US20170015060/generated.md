## BACKGROUND

Additive manufacturing, also known as three-dimensional (3D) printing, represents a transformative approach to fabricating complex structures by building them layer-by-layer from digital models. Unlike conventional subtractive or formative manufacturing methods, additive manufacturing enables the creation of intricate geometries, internal features, and customized architectures that are otherwise difficult or impossible to achieve. Among the various additive manufacturing techniques, direct ink writing (DIW) has emerged as a particularly versatile method for depositing functional materials with high spatial control, especially when applied to composite systems containing reinforcing fibers.

The state of technology in additive manufacturing of fiber-reinforced composites has historically been constrained by limitations in material compatibility, fiber alignment control, and process scalability. Traditional methods for producing carbon fiber composites—such as hand layup, resin transfer molding, and filament winding—rely on continuous fibers embedded in thermoset or thermoplastic matrices, which provide high strength-to-weight ratios but require significant tooling and labor. In contrast, additive manufacturing offers the potential for rapid prototyping and on-demand production of complex composite parts without molds or extensive post-processing. However, early attempts to integrate short or discontinuous carbon fibers into 3D-printable inks often resulted in poor dispersion, random orientation, nozzle clogging, and inadequate mechanical performance due to insufficient fiber alignment and matrix-fiber interaction.

U.S. Pat. No. 6,299,810 discloses a method and apparatus for manufacturing fiber-reinforced composite articles using a lathe assembly wherein a mandrel is rotated while a carriage moves axially along its length, dispensing resin-impregnated fiber tow onto the mandrel surface. This process involves applying a release agent to the mandrel, winding the fiber under tension, and subsequently curing the resin within a heated vacuum chamber to consolidate the laminate. While effective for axisymmetric parts such as pipes or pressure vessels, this technique lacks the geometric flexibility required for complex, non-axisymmetric components and does not enable localized control over fiber orientation or volume fraction within a single part.

Carbon fiber composite manufacturing via such traditional winding processes typically employs epoxy or polyester resins that are applied either as pre-impregnated tapes (prepregs) or as liquid resins during winding. The lathe assembly includes precision-guided carriages that control the placement of fiber tows, while heating elements may be used to partially cure or tackify the resin during deposition. Release agents ensure easy demolding after full cure, and the entire assembly is often placed in a vacuum chamber to remove entrapped air and apply uniform pressure during the final compression and curing stages. Finishing processes such as machining or surface coating may follow to meet dimensional or aesthetic requirements.

More recently, U.S. Published Patent Application No. 20140361460 describes a fiber-reinforced additive manufacturing system that supplies an unmelted composite filament containing continuous fibers to a print head, where it is heated and melted before being deposited onto a build platen. The system applies an “ironing force” via a roller or blade to flatten and bond successive layers, improving interlayer adhesion. The print head is designed to accommodate both polymer and fiber feed, and the build platen may be heated to maintain material tackiness. A computer controller coordinates the motion of the print head and platen in three dimensions to fabricate 3D objects. Despite these advances, this system is limited to thermoplastic matrices and cannot readily process high-performance thermoset resins, which are essential for aerospace and structural applications due to their superior thermal stability and mechanical properties.

A critical limitation of existing fiber-reinforced additive manufacturing systems is their inability to effectively align high-aspect-ratio short carbon fibers within a thermoset matrix during extrusion. Conventional fused deposition modeling (FDM) approaches struggle with thermosets because they require in-situ mixing of reactive components or suffer from premature curing. Moreover, even when fibers are incorporated, they often remain randomly oriented, negating the primary advantage of fiber reinforcement—directional strength and stiffness. The introduction of “chaos elements” or complex flow geometries in some systems aims to improve mixing but often disrupts alignment and increases viscosity unpredictably. Vacuum chamber processing, while useful for degassing, does not address the fundamental rheological challenges of extruding fiber-laden thermoset inks.

Thus, there remains a significant need for an additive manufacturing apparatus and method capable of precisely controlling the orientation, distribution, and volume fraction of high-aspect-ratio carbon fibers within a high-performance thermoset resin matrix during 3D printing, thereby enabling the fabrication of composites with unprecedented orthotropic mechanical, thermal, and electrical properties.

## SUMMARY

The present invention introduces novel apparatus, systems, and methods for the additive manufacturing of meso-structurally ordered carbon fiber/epoxy composites with controlled fiber alignment and tunable orthotropic properties. Prior art systems are limited by their reliance on thermoplastics, inability to process aromatic thermoset resins, poor control over fiber orientation, and frequent nozzle clogging at practical fiber loadings. These limitations prevent the realization of high-performance composites suitable for aerospace, defense, and advanced engineering applications.

The inventor’s apparatus and systems overcome these deficiencies by integrating a specially formulated shear-thinning, thixotropic epoxy ink containing high-aspect-ratio short carbon fibers with a precision direct ink writing (DIW) platform. The ink is based on a bisphenol-F epoxy oligomer modified with colloidal silica nanoparticles to impart non-Newtonian rheology, enabling extrusion through fine nozzles while maintaining rapid physical solidification upon deposition. Critically, the resin system employs latent thermal cure catalysis—using a Lewis acid generator such as a boron trifluoride salt—that remains stable at ambient temperatures but rapidly initiates homopolymerization of epoxide groups upon mild heating, eliminating the need for multi-component mixing during printing.

The print head and resin system are engineered to facilitate shear-induced alignment of carbon fibers during microextrusion. As the ink flows through a conical or tapered nozzle under pressure, high shear rates align the fibers predominantly in the direction of extrusion. This alignment is preserved upon deposition due to the ink’s rapid recovery of high static viscosity, allowing the formation of self-supporting, freestanding filaments with diameters as small as 250 μm. The computer-controlled DIW stage provides precise 3-, 4-, 5-, or 6-axis motion, enabling arbitrary placement of aligned fiber bundles in three-dimensional space to create virtual layups analogous to woven composites but with far greater design freedom.

The additive manufacturing process begins with the preparation of a stable ink containing up to 8 volume% carbon fibers, though higher loadings are achievable with optimized nozzle geometry. The ink is loaded into a syringe-based print head and extruded onto a temperature-controlled build platen. After printing, the green part is subjected to a two-stage thermal cure: first at 80°C for 12 hours to complete network formation, followed by a post-cure at 120°C for 24 hours to maximize crosslink density. The resulting additively manufactured carbon fiber-reinforced composite (AMCFRC) exhibits mechanical, electrical, and thermal properties that are highly anisotropic and superior to randomly filled counterparts at equivalent fiber loadings.

Industrial applications of this invention include lightweight structural components for aerospace vehicles, tailored electromagnetic shielding, directional thermal management systems, and custom orthopedic or prosthetic devices requiring site-specific mechanical response. The scope of the invention encompasses not only the apparatus and methods described but also the resulting AMCFRC materials, which possess engineered orthotropy in multiple physical domains due to controlled microstructural architecture.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF SPECIFIC EMBODIMENTS

High-performance carbon fiber/epoxy composites are indispensable in industries demanding exceptional strength-to-weight ratios, thermal stability, and chemical resistance. However, current manufacturing processes—such as autoclave curing, resin transfer molding, or filament winding—are capital-intensive, time-consuming, and geometrically restrictive. Additive manufacturing and 3D printing technologies offer a promising alternative by enabling rapid, tool-free fabrication of complex parts. Yet, existing systems for additively manufacturing continuous carbon fiber epoxy composites face significant hurdles, including the incompatibility of thermoset resins with melt-based extrusion, difficulty in achieving fiber alignment with short fibers, and inadequate interlayer bonding.

Commercial epoxy resin systems are typically two-part formulations requiring immediate mixing and use, which is impractical for DIW. Systems like the MarkForged 3D printer address this by using thermoplastic matrices with embedded continuous fibers, but these lack the thermal and mechanical performance of aromatic thermosets. The inventor’s apparatus, systems, and methods resolve these issues through a novel integration of chemistry, rheology, and precision engineering.

The 3D printing process begins with the formulation of an ink comprising an oligomerized bisphenol-F diglycidyl ether (BPFE) resin, fumed silica nanoparticles (e.g., Cabosil TS530), and chopped high-aspect-ratio carbon fibers (e.g., HTS40 grade, 600 μm length, aspect ratio >50). The silica imparts thixotropic behavior, transforming the Newtonian resin into a shear-thinning fluid that flows under extrusion pressure but rapidly solidifies upon deposition. Carbon fibers are dispersed at 3–8 volume% to balance printability and performance.

Illustrated in embodiment 100, the system includes a print head 106 mounted on a multi-axis DIW stage, a computer controller 110, and a fiber feed mechanism. The extruded material 102 emerges as a continuous filament with aligned fibers. The product creation process involves layer-by-layer deposition of these filaments according to a digital model, forming a green body that is subsequently cured.

Referring to FIG. 2, the print head 106 features a nozzle 108 with a tapered or conical internal geometry to promote shear alignment. Upstream, a resin chamber 120 holds the formulated ink, and a fiber core shaper 122 may be employed to pre-align fibers before entry into the nozzle. FIG. 3 details the operation of system 100, which operates through seven coordinated stages.

The first stage involves preimpregnation of the carbon fiber core within the silica-modified resin under vacuum to ensure complete wetting and eliminate air pockets. The second stage maintains the print head body open to atmospheric pressure to prevent premature curing or pressure buildup. The third stage utilizes a computer-controlled precision 3–6 axis DIW stage (e.g., Aerotech ABL 9000) to position the nozzle with micron-level accuracy in x, y, and z. The fourth stage is the nozzle 108 itself, where shear forces align fibers during extrusion. The fifth stage includes a fiber feed reel 123 that supplies dry or pre-impregnated fibers directly into the ink stream, enabling dynamic adjustment of fiber loading. The sixth stage defines the drawn fiber 102 path from nozzle to substrate, optimized for minimal disturbance to alignment. The seventh stage incorporates a satellite head 124 that may deliver additional resin, catalyst, or secondary materials to specific regions for property grading.

System 100 functionality is further enhanced by a resin bath and vacuum process during ink preparation, ensuring homogeneity and degassing. Computational fluid dynamics (CFD) and discrete particle simulations guide nozzle design to avoid flow expansions that cause fiber clumping. The resulting AMCFRC exhibits Young’s modulus up to 37% higher than random pressed parts at equal fiber loading, and electrical conductivity anisotropy exceeding four orders of magnitude between axial and transverse directions.

In conclusion, the scope of this application encompasses all apparatus, methods, and materials that utilize shear-aligned short fiber composites in thermoset matrices via direct ink writing, particularly those employing latent-cure epoxy chemistries and multi-axis deposition for engineered orthotropy.
