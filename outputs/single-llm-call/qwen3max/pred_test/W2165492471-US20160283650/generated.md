# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

Synthetic lethality (SL) represents a genetic interaction in which the simultaneous loss of function of two nonessential genes results in cellular inviability, while the loss of either gene alone remains viable. Originally characterized in model organisms such as yeast, synthetic lethality has emerged as a powerful paradigm for targeted cancer therapeutics, particularly in the context of precision oncology. In cancer cells harboring specific mutations or deficiencies, targeting a second gene that forms a synthetic lethal pair with the mutated gene can selectively induce cell death without affecting healthy cells that retain the functional wild-type allele. This principle underpins the clinical success of PARP inhibitors in BRCA-deficient cancers and motivates extensive research into identifying novel SL interactions for therapeutic exploitation. Despite its promise, systematic experimental mapping of SL interactions in humans is hindered by formidable challenges, including ethical constraints, the vast combinatorial space of potential gene pairs—exceeding 200 million—and the context-dependent nature of SL, which may vary across tissue types, disease states, and cellular environments. Consequently, high-throughput experimental screening across all human gene pairs in multiple biological contexts is infeasible, necessitating the development of robust computational approaches to prioritize candidate SL pairs for validation. Prior computational strategies have largely relied on interspecies extrapolation based on genetic homology, assuming that SL relationships are conserved between orthologous gene pairs across evolutionarily distant species. However, this approach suffers from significant limitations: only a small fraction of human genes possess clear orthologs in well-studied model organisms like *Saccharomyces cerevisiae*, and evolutionary divergence—including gene duplication events, functional rewiring, and the emergence of species-specific redundancies—can disrupt the conservation of SL interactions even among homologous genes. Alternative methods based on protein structural similarity, shared functional annotations from Gene Ontology, or univariate network metrics such as information centrality have demonstrated limited predictive power. These shortcomings underscore the need for a more comprehensive, systems-level framework that captures the topological and organizational principles of biological networks to infer SL relationships across species in a manner independent of sequence or functional homology.

## SUMMARY

The present invention introduces a novel method for identifying synthetic lethal gene pairs through the concept of connectivity homology and a species-independent translation framework. The core innovation lies in the recognition that genes exhibiting similar patterns of interaction within their respective biological networks—regardless of sequence or functional similarity—are more likely to participate in analogous genetic interactions, including synthetic lethality. This insight is operationalized through a computational pipeline that leverages experimentally validated SL data from a well-characterized source species, such as *S. cerevisiae*, to train a predictive model based on multivariate network connectivity profiles. The method begins by constructing a protein-protein interaction (PPI) network for the source species and computing a comprehensive set of graph-theoretic parameters for each gene and gene pair, thereby generating a high-dimensional connectivity profile. These profiles serve as input features for a machine learning algorithm, such as a random forest classifier, which is trained to distinguish between known synthetic lethal (SL) and non-synthetic lethal (NSL) pairs. A critical step in the method involves the normalization of network parameters to enable cross-species comparison; specifically, rank normalization is employed to rescale parameter values within each species to a uniform [0,1] range, thereby mitigating differences in network scale, density, and topology between species. This normalized representation allows the model trained on the source species to be directly applied to the target species’ PPI network, generating a probabilistic score—the SINaTRA score—for every possible gene pair in the target species, indicating the likelihood of a synthetic lethal relationship. The method further incorporates a post-processing filtering step to reduce false positives by leveraging human population genomic data; gene pairs for which deleterious homozygous co-mutations are observed in healthy individuals are excluded from the final predictions, as such co-occurrence implies non-lethality. The invention also provides a framework for selecting cancer drug treatments by identifying high-scoring SL pairs where both genes are known or putative targets of existing or investigational therapeutics, thereby suggesting novel combination therapy strategies. Finally, the method includes a filtering mechanism to refine predicted SL pairs based on biological context, such as tissue-specific gene expression, ensuring that only pairs relevant to a particular cellular environment are considered for downstream applications.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The method for identifying synthetic lethal (SL) interactions disclosed herein is predicated on the analysis of biological network connectivity profiles. The process initiates with the acquisition of a protein-protein interaction (PPI) network for a source species, typically one with extensive experimental SL data, such as *Saccharomyces cerevisiae*. This network is pruned to ensure it comprises a single connected component, removing isolated nodes that cannot contribute to meaningful connectivity analysis. For each node (gene) and each pair of nodes within this network, a vector of network parameters is computed. These parameters include, but are not limited to, degree centrality, betweenness centrality, closeness centrality, eigenvector centrality, clustering coefficient, and shortest path length, along with pairwise metrics such as shared neighbors and communicability. This collection of metrics constitutes a comprehensive connectivity profile for each gene and gene pair. To facilitate comparison across species, these raw parameter values, which are inherently non-comparable due to differences in network size and density, are translated into a common scale. The preferred translation method is rank normalization, wherein each parameter value is replaced by its rank within the distribution of that parameter across all nodes or node pairs in the species-specific network, and then divided by the total number of nodes or pairs, yielding a value between 0 and 1. This normalized, translated dataset is then used to train a machine learning model, specifically a random forest classifier, using known SL pairs as positive examples and a representative sample of NSL pairs as negative examples. The trained model, now encoding the connectivity signatures of SL, is applied to the similarly processed and normalized PPI network of a target species, such as *Homo sapiens*. The model outputs a SINaTRA score for every possible gene pair in the target network, representing the probability of a synthetic lethal interaction. To enhance prediction accuracy, the method incorporates a filtering step that removes gene pairs for which population genomic data (e.g., from the 1000 Genomes Project) indicate the presence of individuals homozygous for deleterious mutations in both genes, as this co-occurrence is strong evidence against lethality. The selection of a source species is guided by the availability of high-quality, comprehensive SL data; *S. cerevisiae* is the preferred source due to its extensive experimental characterization. The concept of connectivity homology is formalized by representing the connectivity profile of each gene as a multidimensional vector, where the Euclidean distance between vectors of two genes serves as a measure of their connectivity similarity. The network parameters are determined using standard graph theory libraries, such as NetworkX, ensuring reproducibility and computational efficiency. The pruning of networks to a single connected component is a crucial preprocessing step to maintain the integrity of path-based metrics. The normalization of network parameters, as described, is essential for the species-independent applicability of the model. An entropy analysis can be performed on the parameter distributions to assess the informativeness of each feature, though it is not a required step in the core pipeline. The training of the synthetic lethality model involves a supervised learning process where the algorithm learns to discriminate between the multivariate connectivity patterns of SL and NSL pairs. The selection of SL and NSL pairs for training is based on curated databases like BioGRID, with NSL pairs being randomly sampled from the complement of the known SL set. The performance of the trained model is rigorously evaluated using cross-validation and metrics such as the Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve (AUC).

### EXAMPLE 1

The efficacy of the disclosed method was first demonstrated by predicting SL interactions in *Schizosaccharomyces pombe* (*S. pombe*), using a model trained on *S. cerevisiae* data. The PPI networks for both species were constructed from BioGRID and processed to contain a single connected component. Network parameters were computed for all nodes and node pairs. When the model was applied using the raw, untranslated parameters from the *S. cerevisiae* network to predict SL in *S. pombe*, the performance was modest, achieving an AUC of 0.67. This poor performance underscores the incompatibility of raw network metrics across species. In stark contrast, when the same model was applied to the rank-normalized, translated parameters of the *S. pombe* network, the predictive performance improved dramatically, yielding an AUC of 0.86. This significant gain in performance, validated by DeLong’s test for comparing ROC curves, conclusively demonstrates that the translation of network parameters is a critical and enabling step in the cross-species prediction of synthetic lethality. The model was able to identify numerous high-confidence SL pairs in *S. pombe* that had not yet been experimentally validated, suggesting a rich landscape of undiscovered genetic interactions. The robustness of the method was further confirmed by its ability to perform well even when the target network was artificially degraded to simulate the lower completeness typical of mammalian PPI networks.

### EXAMPLE 2

The method was subsequently applied to predict SL interactions in the house mouse, *Mus musculus* (*M. musculus*). Given the scarcity of experimentally validated SL pairs in mice (only nine recorded in BioGRID at the time of analysis), direct validation of the model’s performance was challenging. Nevertheless, the model, trained on the *S. cerevisiae* source network and applied to the normalized *M. musculus* PPI network, generated SINaTRA scores for all mouse gene pairs. Of the nine known mouse SL pairs, eight were assigned a score of 0.5 or higher, and five received a score of 0.70 or higher, indicating a strong alignment with existing knowledge. To provide a comparative benchmark, the model’s performance was assessed against a functional similarity model based on Gene Ontology (GO) annotations. The SINaTRA-based prediction achieved an AUC of 0.937, significantly outperforming the GO-based model, which attained an AUC of only 0.687. This result confirms that the connectivity homology framework is not only applicable to distantly related unicellular organisms but also effectively translates to complex mammalian systems, providing a valuable resource for guiding future experimental work in murine models of human disease.

### EXAMPLE 3

The primary application of the disclosed method is the genome-wide prediction of synthetic lethal interactions in humans. The model, trained on *S. cerevisiae* and applied to the normalized human PPI network, generated a SINaTRA score for over 109 million human gene pairs. To refine these predictions and remove likely false positives, a stringent filter was applied using data from large-scale human population genomics projects, specifically the 1000 Genomes Project and the Sweden-Schizophrenia exome sequencing study. Any gene pair for which at least one individual was found to be homozygous for a deleterious mutation in both genes was classified as a confirmed non-synthetic lethal (NSL) pair and removed from the candidate list. This filtering process identified 450,010 such pairs, which were found to have significantly lower median SINaTRA scores than the general population of gene pairs, validating the filter’s logic. After filtering, the false discovery rate for predictions with a SINaTRA score of 0.85 or higher was estimated to be a remarkably low 0.36%. The high-confidence predictions (score > 0.95) were found to be significantly enriched for gene pairs that reside within the same KEGG biological pathway and, even more strongly, for pairs whose protein products are subunits of the same protein complex, as defined by the CORUM database. This enrichment provides strong biological validation for the method’s predictions. To explore the context-specific nature of SL, the global predictions were filtered using tissue-specific gene expression data from the Human Protein Atlas. This yielded customized lists of SL pairs for various tissues and cell lines, revealing that some tissues, like the small intestine and respiratory epithelium, retained a significantly higher proportion of SL pairs than expected by chance, suggesting unique vulnerabilities. The predictions were compared against two existing resources: the Syn-Lethality database of experimentally verified human SL pairs and the DAISY computational predictions. The gene pairs from both datasets exhibited significantly higher SINaTRA scores than random pairs, demonstrating concordance with prior knowledge. The method successfully identified SL pairs involving genes with known cancer-associated deficiencies, such as those in the Syn-Lethality database. An analysis of the landscape of human SL revealed that the majority of high-confidence pairs are within-pathway, with the immune system, apoptosis, and gene expression pathways showing dense interconnectivity. A mechanistic analysis of the predicted pairs, categorized by their functional annotations, revealed distinct enrichments: for instance, DNA repair and chromatin organization pairs were enriched for a "complex" mechanism, while immune system and signal transduction pairs were enriched for a "parallel pathway" mechanism. Finally, the method was applied to the field of cancer therapy by mapping high-scoring SL pairs to known drug targets. This analysis revealed "hotspots" of high SINaTRA scores corresponding to pairs of genes that are already the focus of combination therapy development in preclinical and clinical pipelines, thereby retrospectively validating the method’s utility. More importantly, it identified numerous novel, high-scoring gene pairs that are druggable but not yet explored in combination, offering a rich pipeline of candidates for the development of new, rationally designed cancer polypharmacological treatments.
