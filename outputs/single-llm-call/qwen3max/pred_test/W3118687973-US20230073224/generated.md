# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

Superconducting circuits represent a leading platform for the realization of scalable quantum processors, leveraging well-established microfabrication techniques and exhibiting rapid progress in coherence times, gate fidelities, and system complexity over the past two decades. These circuits encode quantum information in the collective degrees of freedom of superconducting electrical elements—typically Josephson junctions, capacitors, and inductors—forming artificial atoms whose energy levels can be precisely engineered. Among the various circuit architectures developed, transmon qubits have become the dominant choice in large-scale quantum computing efforts due to their relative insensitivity to charge noise, achieved by shunting the Josephson junction with a large capacitor. However, this design comes at the cost of reduced anharmonicity, which limits the speed and fidelity of single-qubit gates when driven with microwave fields. Alternative designs such as flux qubits offer high anharmonicity but suffer from extreme sensitivity to magnetic flux noise, leading to rapid dephasing unless mitigated through additional circuit engineering. The fluxonium qubit emerged as a compromise, combining a Josephson junction with a large superinductor to suppress flux noise sensitivity while retaining sufficient anharmonicity. Further refinements led to the "heavy-fluxonium" architecture, which incorporates both a large shunting capacitor and a high-inductance element to simultaneously suppress charge dispersion and enhance coherence. Despite these advances, coherent control of heavy-fluxonium qubits has remained challenging due to suppressed dipole matrix elements that render conventional microwave-driven rotations inefficient. This background sets the stage for the present invention, which introduces novel methods for initialization, readout, and ultrafast control of heavy-fluxonium qubits operating at megahertz-scale transition frequencies—orders of magnitude lower than conventional superconducting qubits—thereby opening a new regime of quantum operation with enhanced coherence and compatibility with simplified control electronics.

## SUMMARY

The present invention is motivated by the need to overcome the limitations of standard control techniques for flux qubits, particularly those arising from weak coupling to external electromagnetic drives and susceptibility to environmental noise. Flux qubits, while offering high anharmonicity beneficial for fast gate operations, are notoriously sensitive to magnetic flux fluctuations, which cause rapid dephasing. The fluxonium variant addresses this by incorporating a large superinductor that suppresses low-frequency flux noise, yet its computational states remain difficult to manipulate efficiently using conventional microwave pulses due to vanishingly small charge matrix elements. The invention leverages the unique properties of the heavy-fluxonium circuit—a further refinement featuring a large shunting capacitor—to enable operation at extremely low transition frequencies (e.g., 14 MHz), where thermal and quantum noise spectral densities are dramatically reduced, thereby extending coherence times beyond those of state-of-the-art transmons. To address the challenge of slow or inefficient control, the invention introduces fast control methods based on nonadiabatic magnetic flux pulses that exploit Landau-Zener transitions to perform single-qubit rotations within a fraction of the qubit’s Larmor period. For initialization, the invention provides a reset protocol that uses higher-energy plasmon-like states coupled to a readout resonator to actively cool the qubit to an effective temperature far below the physical bath temperature, achieving ground-state preparation fidelities exceeding 97%. Readout is enhanced through a plasmon-assisted scheme that transfers population from the computational excited state to a higher-lying state with stronger dispersive coupling to the resonator, thereby amplifying the measurement signal despite the large detuning between the qubit and resonator frequencies. Critically, the invention achieves universal single-qubit control by combining fast flux-driven rotations about axes in the equatorial plane of the Bloch sphere with idling periods that generate precise phase shifts about the z-axis, enabling arbitrary unitary operations without reliance on microwave tones. These methods collectively transform the heavy-fluxonium from a passive, long-lived memory element into an actively controllable quantum processor node with high-fidelity operations, positioning it as a viable alternative to transmon-based architectures.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The invention centers on a quantum system comprising a fluxonium qubit 120 capacitively coupled to a readout resonator 110, with the entire device threaded by an external magnetic flux 130 generated by an on-chip flux bias line. The fluxonium qubit 120 is defined by a Hamiltonian Hf that includes Josephson, charging, and inductive energy terms, resulting in a multi-level spectrum where the lowest two states |g⟩ and |e⟩ form the computational basis separated by a transition frequency Δ ≈ 14 MHz at the flux-frustration point (Φext = Φ0/2). The readout resonator 110 is a microwave cavity with a resonant frequency of approximately 5.7 GHz, chosen to be far detuned from the qubit frequency to minimize Purcell decay while still enabling dispersive coupling. Thermal background photons in the resonator are minimized through cryogenic filtering and attenuation, ensuring that spontaneous emission dominates relaxation processes during measurement. The state of the fluxonium qubit 120 is measured by first applying a π pulse to transfer population from |e⟩ to a higher-lying plasmon state |f⟩, which exhibits a significantly larger dispersive shift χf relative to the ground state, thereby enhancing the readout contrast. FIG. 2 illustrates the double-well potential energy landscape of the fluxonium at the flux-frustration point, where the wavefunctions of |g⟩ and |e⟩ are symmetric and antisymmetric superpositions localized across both wells, with tunneling splitting Δ1 defining the energy spacing. Higher excited states |f⟩ and |h⟩ correspond to intra-well plasmon oscillations and exhibit gigahertz-scale energies, enabling their use in initialization and readout without leakage errors. FIG. 3 depicts the composite energy-level diagram of the coupled qubit-resonator system, showing how spontaneous photon emission from the |e1⟩ state (qubit in |e⟩, resonator in first excited state) drives the reset protocol toward |e0⟩. Alternative energy-dissipating devices, such as engineered loss channels or auxiliary qubits, may also be employed to extract entropy. The reset method initializes the quantum system 100 by simultaneously applying a first microwave field 302 resonant with the |g0⟩ → |h0⟩ transition and a second microwave field 304 driving |h0⟩ → |e1⟩; the latter decays rapidly via spontaneous emission, steering the system into |e0⟩. A subsequent π pulse on the |g⟩–|e⟩ transition prepares |g0⟩. Rabi frequencies are determined by transition dipole moments, which are large for plasmon transitions due to significant charge displacement. Experimental demonstration confirms 97% reset fidelity in 15 μs. The readout method distinguishes |g⟩ and |e⟩ by exploiting dispersive frequency shifts: after a π pulse 308 transfers |e⟩ to |f⟩, the resonator frequency shift becomes proportional to χf − χg, five times larger than χe − χg. This enhanced interaction enables single-shot readout, with fidelity improvable via parametric amplification. The method assumes continuous external magnetic flux 130 but may accommodate dynamic flux changes. It is applicable to other qubits with metastable states and structured spectra. Microwave fields may be replaced by other electromagnetic excitations. For manipulation, a fast magnetic pulse applied via the flux line induces a rotation of the Bloch vector; the pulse duration is shorter than the Larmor period (≈70 ns), enabling nonadiabatic control. The energy splitting depends on flux offset from Φ0/2, with optimal coherence at the frustration point. Pulse shapes—triangular, Gaussian, or numerically optimized—are generated via digital-to-analog converters. A magnetic-pulse sequence comprising a first pulse, idling period, and second antisymmetric pulse implements universal rotations by controlling X and Z components independently. Zero-area sequences minimize harmonic distortions. Complex pulses crossing nominal flux enable advanced control. The invention further includes methods for z-basis and x-basis measurement via quantum non-demolition dispersive coupling, active pulses for state manipulation, and initialization via plasmon dissipation and radiative cooling, leveraging the large gap to excited states to achieve sub-thermal populations. Combinations of initialization, manipulation, and readout methods yield a complete operational toolkit for heavy-fluxonium quantum processors.

### Experimental Demonstration

The experimental demonstration employs superconducting circuits fabricated on sapphire substrates using niobium metallization and aluminum-based Josephson junctions. The heavy-fluxonium circuit is motivated by its superior coherence over transmons, which suffer from limited anharmonicity constraining gate speeds, and standard flux qubits, which exhibit poor dephasing times. The heavy-fluxonium integrates a small Josephson junction shunted by a large capacitor (Cq) and a superinductor formed from 300 large-area junctions, yielding an effective Hamiltonian with a 14 MHz qubit frequency and gigahertz-scale excited states. Device coherence is characterized by measuring T1 ≈ 315 μs at the flux-frustration point, increasing to 4.3 ms away from degeneracy, limited primarily by dielectric loss in the capacitor and, at certain flux biases, Purcell loss via higher states resonant with the readout resonator. Dephasing times T2e ≈ 300 μs are extracted from spin-echo experiments, with Ramsey T2* ≈ 70–100 μs indicating 1/f flux noise dominance away from the sweet spot. Fast single-cycle flux gates are implemented using triangular flux pulses of 20–60 ns duration, performing Y/2, Z/2, and X/2 rotations within one Larmor period. Randomized benchmarking yields average gate fidelity Favg = 0.9980, with individual fidelities FZ/2 = 0.9999, FY/2 = 0.9992, and FX/2 = 0.9976. Gate errors stem largely from calibration inaccuracies rather than decoherence, suggesting room for improvement. The reset protocol utilizes |g0⟩ → |h0⟩ and |h0⟩ → |e1⟩ drives followed by spontaneous emission, achieving 97% |e⟩ population; a final π pulse initializes |g⟩. Plasmon-assisted readout enhances dispersive shifts by transferring |e⟩ to |f⟩ before measurement. Relaxation modeling via Fermi’s golden rule attributes T1 limits to dielectric loss (∝1/ωq), inductive loss (∝1/ωq³), radiative loss to charge lines, and Purcell effects, with 1/f flux noise suppressed by E_L² scaling. The experimental setup features extensive filtering, attenuation, and shielding to minimize thermal photons and noise. Fabrication follows standard lithographic processes, ensuring scalability. The demonstration confirms that heavy-fluxonium qubits combine long coherence, high-fidelity control, and efficient initialization/readout, establishing them as competitive candidates for scalable quantum computation.

## CONCLUSION

The heavy-fluxonium qubit described herein represents a significant advancement in superconducting quantum computing, uniquely combining megahertz-scale transition frequencies with coherence times exceeding 300 microseconds and single-qubit gate fidelities above 99.7%. By operating deep in the low-frequency regime, the invention drastically reduces the impact of environmental noise spectral density, thereby extending T1 and T2e beyond those of conventional transmon qubits. The integration of plasmon-assisted reset and readout protocols overcomes the challenges posed by the qubit’s low frequency and weak dispersive coupling, enabling high-fidelity state preparation and measurement. Furthermore, the introduction of ultrafast flux-driven gates—executed within a single Larmor period via nonadiabatic Landau-Zener transitions—eliminates the need for complex microwave pulse shaping and provides a path toward simplified, high-speed control electronics. These attributes collectively position the heavy-fluxonium as a robust, scalable, and high-performance qubit architecture suitable for fault-tolerant quantum computation, with immediate applicability in near-term quantum processors and long-term potential for operation at elevated temperatures due to its sub-thermal frequency scale.

## COMBINATION OF FEATURES

The invention includes a method for initializing a quantum system comprising a fluxonium qubit coupled to an energy dissipater in the form of a readout resonator. The method comprises exciting the quantum system by simultaneously applying electromagnetic fields resonant with the |g0⟩ → |h0⟩ and |h0⟩ → |e1⟩ transitions, wherein the qubit transition frequency between |g⟩ and |e⟩ is approximately 14 MHz. The fluxonium qubit is threaded with an external magnetic flux set to Φ0/2, the flux-frustration point. The threading, exciting, and coupling to the resonator occur simultaneously for a duration of 15 microseconds, after which a π pulse at the |g⟩–|e⟩ transition frequency transfers the system to |g0⟩. The quantum system is prepared in a thermal mixed state prior to initialization and cryogenically cooled to a base temperature of 15 mK, resulting in metastable state occupation probabilities near 50% before reset. The method is repeated to ensure high-fidelity initialization. Additionally, the invention provides a method for measuring the qubit state of a fluxonium qubit by coupling the quantum system dispersively to a readout resonator and performing a π pulse to transfer population from |e⟩ to |f⟩ before dispersive readout. A method for rotating the flux qubit comprises applying a first fast magnetic pulse to rotate the Bloch vector about an axis in the equatorial plane, idling for a controlled duration to accumulate a z-phase, and applying a second antisymmetric pulse to complete the desired rotation, thereby enabling universal single-qubit control.
