# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

Solar cells represent a cornerstone technology in the global transition toward sustainable energy solutions, converting sunlight directly into electricity through the photovoltaic effect. As demand for renewable energy sources intensifies, driven by environmental concerns and the finite nature of fossil fuels, solar photovoltaics have emerged as a leading alternative energy source due to their scalability, modularity, and decreasing cost per watt. Among various photovoltaic technologies, crystalline silicon solar cells—particularly those fabricated from multi-crystalline silicon (mc-Si)—dominate the commercial market owing to their balance of efficiency, reliability, and manufacturability using established industrial processes.

The fundamental structure of a conventional silicon solar cell includes a p-n junction formed by doping opposite sides of a silicon wafer with complementary impurities, typically boron for the p-type base and phosphorus for the n-type emitter. Incident photons generate electron-hole pairs within the silicon; the built-in electric field at the p-n junction separates these carriers, enabling current flow when an external circuit is connected. However, significant energy losses occur due to optical reflection at the air-silicon interface and electrical recombination at surfaces and interfaces. To mitigate optical losses, surface texturing is employed to reduce reflectance by promoting multiple light bounces, thereby increasing the probability of photon absorption within the silicon substrate.

One advanced architecture designed to enhance both optical and electrical performance is the selective emitter structure. In a selective emitter solar cell, the emitter region is non-uniformly doped: heavily doped beneath the front metal contacts to minimize contact resistance, and lightly doped in the inter-finger regions to reduce surface recombination and improve blue response. This dual-doping profile optimizes the trade-off between series resistance and carrier collection efficiency, particularly in the short-wavelength spectrum where carriers are generated near the surface.

Conventionally, selective emitters are fabricated using photolithographic masking processes. In this method, a photoresist is applied, patterned via UV exposure through a mask, and developed to define regions for heavy doping. While effective in laboratory settings, photolithography is costly, time-consuming, and involves multiple wet chemical steps—including resist stripping and etching—that consume large volumes of hazardous chemicals and generate significant waste. The stripping process typically requires strong organic solvents or plasma ashing, followed by an etching step to remove native oxide or diffusion barriers, all of which add complexity and expense to manufacturing.

Moreover, the conventional method suffers from several limitations in high-throughput industrial environments. Photolithography demands precise alignment, cleanroom conditions, and extensive process control, making it incompatible with low-cost, high-volume screen printing lines commonly used for mc-Si solar cell production. Additionally, the repeated use of aggressive etchants and strippers leads to material loss from the silicon wafer, particularly during over-etching, which can compromise mechanical integrity and reduce yield. Furthermore, residual contaminants or incomplete removal of masking layers can introduce defects that increase surface recombination velocity, thereby degrading open-circuit voltage (V<sub>oc</sub>) and fill factor (FF).

These challenges underscore a critical need for a simplified, cost-effective, and environmentally benign method to form selective emitter structures without relying on photolithography. Such a method must preserve the optical benefits of advanced texturing—such as that achieved by reactive ion etching (RIE)—while mitigating plasma-induced damage that otherwise degrades electrical performance. The present invention addresses this unmet need by integrating a novel damage removal etch (DRE) process with a non-photolithographic patterning technique, enabling high-efficiency mc-Si solar cells to be manufactured using standard industrial screen printing equipment.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a solar cell and a method for its manufacture that significantly improves photoelectric conversion efficiency while reducing manufacturing complexity and cost. Central to the invention is the integration of reactive ion etching (RIE) texturing with an optimized damage removal etch (DRE) and a non-photolithographic selective emitter formation process. This approach eliminates the need for conventional photolithographic masking, thereby reducing etchant consumption, minimizing silicon material loss, and simplifying the overall fabrication sequence.

By employing RIE texturing, the front surface of the multi-crystalline silicon wafer is structured with nanoscale features that dramatically reduce reflectance across the solar spectrum, particularly in the short-wavelength region below 600 nm. This enhances light trapping and increases short-circuit current (I<sub>sc</sub>). However, RIE inherently introduces plasma-induced damage that elevates surface recombination, leading to reduced V<sub>oc</sub> and FF. The invention overcomes this limitation through a precisely controlled DRE using a dilute HNO<sub>3</sub>/HF mixture, which removes the damaged layer without excessively smoothing the beneficial texture, thus preserving low reflectance while restoring high V<sub>oc</sub> and FF.

The selective emitter is formed using a screen-printable etch paste that locally removes a deposited SiO<sub>2</sub> diffusion barrier mask, enabling localized heavy doping during a second POCl<sub>3</sub> diffusion step. This patterning method is compatible with existing screen printing infrastructure, eliminating the need for photolithography, resist stripping, and associated chemical baths. Consequently, the invention minimizes etchant usage, avoids unnecessary silicon etching, and reduces surface recombination by maintaining a clean, well-controlled interface between the emitter and the anti-reflective coating.

Furthermore, the invention enables easy patterning of the front surface through direct screen printing of the etch paste, which aligns naturally with the subsequent metallization grid. This self-aligned process reduces misalignment errors and improves contact quality. The resulting solar cell exhibits reduced series resistance due to the heavily doped contact regions, while the lightly doped inter-finger regions suppress Auger and surface recombination, collectively enhancing both I<sub>sc</sub> and V<sub>oc</sub>.

In summary, the invention offers a streamlined, industrially scalable method for producing high-efficiency mc-Si solar cells with conversion efficiencies exceeding 17.6%, absolute I<sub>sc</sub> gains of over 200 mA in 6-inch cells, and improved external quantum efficiency in the blue spectrum—all achieved without the cost and complexity of photolithography. The method reduces manufacturing costs, conserves materials, and enhances environmental sustainability, making it ideally suited for mass production in modern photovoltaic fabs.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF EXEMPLARY EMBODIMENTS

The invention is implemented through a series of process steps that begin with a p-type multi-crystalline silicon wafer of dimensions 156 × 156 mm², resistivity of approximately 1.9 ohm·cm, and thickness of 200 μm. Initially, the wafer undergoes isotropic acidic texturing to create a base microstructure of bowl-like features, which serves as a foundation for subsequent nanostructuring. Following this, the wafer is processed in a reactive ion etching (RIE) reactor, where plasma species and energetic ions generate a dense array of nanoscale protrusions and deep cavities, resulting in a highly textured, low-reflectance surface with a dark appearance.

To address the plasma-induced damage inherent in RIE, a damage removal etch (DRE) is performed using a dilute mixture of HNO<sub>3</sub> and HF. The etching duration is critically optimized—typically between 20 and 30 seconds—to selectively remove the damaged surface layer without excessively planarizing the nanostructures. Scanning electron microscopy confirms that this treatment smooths the sharpest features while retaining sufficient roughness for low reflectance, particularly below 600 nm wavelength.

Next, a thin SiO<sub>2</sub> layer is deposited uniformly across the front surface, serving as a diffusion barrier mask. A screen-printable etch paste is then applied in a pattern corresponding to the desired front electrode grid. During a thermal treatment, the paste locally etches the SiO<sub>2</sub> layer, exposing silicon only beneath the future contact fingers. The wafer is then subjected to a first POCl<sub>3</sub> diffusion in a conventional tube furnace, creating a shallow, lightly doped emitter with a sheet resistance of approximately 80 Ω/□. A second diffusion step, enabled by the patterned openings, forms heavily doped regions (20 Ω/□) precisely under the contact areas, establishing the selective emitter profile.

After diffusion, the phosphosilicate glass (PSG) and remaining SiO<sub>2</sub> are simultaneously removed using a 10% HF solution, simplifying the cleaning process. An anti-reflective coating of silicon nitride (SiN<sub>x</sub>) is then deposited via plasma-enhanced chemical vapor deposition (PECVD), with a refractive index of 2.06 and thickness of 85 nm, further reducing reflectance and providing surface passivation.

Front and rear electrodes are formed by screen printing silver and aluminum pastes, respectively, followed by co-firing in a lamp-heated belt furnace. The rear electrode benefits from the formation of a back-surface field (BSF) during firing, which repels minority carriers from the rear contact, reducing recombination. Alternatively, both electrodes may be formed using advanced techniques such as plating or laser-fired contacts, though screen printing remains preferred for cost and compatibility.

This process sequence eliminates photolithography, resist stripping, and multiple wet etch steps, thereby simplifying manufacturing, reducing chemical consumption, and minimizing silicon loss. The result is a high-efficiency mc-Si solar cell with enhanced I<sub>sc</sub> from RIE texturing, preserved V<sub>oc</sub> and FF from optimized DRE, and low series resistance from the selective emitter—all achieved using standard industrial equipment. The method reduces production costs while improving photoelectric conversion efficiency, demonstrating clear advantages over conventional approaches.
