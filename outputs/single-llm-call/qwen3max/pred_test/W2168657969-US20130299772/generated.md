# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates to doped semiconductor nanoparticles, particularly colloidal nanocrystals of semiconductor materials that have been intentionally and controllably doped with heterovalent impurity atoms to modulate their electronic, optical, and transport properties. These doped nanoparticles represent a new class of nanoscale materials wherein the density of states, Fermi energy, and charge carrier concentration are engineered through precise incorporation of dopant species during or after synthesis. The invention encompasses both n-type and p-type doped semiconductor nanocrystals, hybrid structures incorporating metallic domains, and methods for their preparation and integration into functional devices such as diodes, transistors, and large-area display arrays. The field bridges the disciplines of nanomaterials chemistry, solid-state physics, and electronic device engineering, offering a platform for tunable quantum-confined systems with tailored doping profiles unattainable in bulk semiconductors.

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

Doping of bulk semiconductors is a foundational technique in modern electronics, enabling control over electrical conductivity, carrier type, and optical response by introducing trace amounts of impurity atoms that donate or accept electrons. In silicon, for example, phosphorus acts as an n-type dopant by contributing extra electrons, while boron serves as a p-type dopant by creating holes. This paradigm has enabled the development of diodes, transistors, and integrated circuits. However, extending this concept to colloidal semiconductor nanocrystals—quantum dots (QDs)—has proven exceptionally challenging due to fundamental differences in scale, surface-to-volume ratio, and thermodynamic constraints. In bulk crystals, dopants can diffuse over long distances and occupy substitutional sites under equilibrium conditions, but in nanocrystals, the high surface energy, limited diffusion pathways, and strong self-purification tendencies often expel impurities or trap them at surfaces rather than within the core lattice.

Early attempts to dope colloidal nanocrystals yielded inconsistent results, with many reports later attributed to surface adsorption or secondary phase formation rather than true lattice incorporation. The small size of nanocrystals (typically 2–10 nm in diameter) means that even a single dopant atom can significantly alter the electronic structure, yet achieving reproducible, controlled doping at levels sufficient to shift the Fermi level without degrading optical quality remained elusive. Several strategies have been explored, including co-precipitation during growth, post-synthetic diffusion, and cation exchange. Co-precipitation often leads to inhomogeneous doping or phase segregation, while cation exchange may distort the crystal lattice or introduce defects. Post-synthetic diffusion, though promising, requires overcoming kinetic barriers that prevent dopant atoms from penetrating the nanocrystal core.

Recent progress in producing doped quantum dots has demonstrated that certain metal impurities—such as copper, silver, and gold—can be incorporated into III-V semiconductors like indium arsenide (InAs) under carefully controlled conditions. These studies have shown shifts in absorption and emission spectra, changes in photoluminescence quenching, and evidence of altered carrier concentrations. However, a comprehensive understanding of how dopant identity, concentration, and location influence the emergence of impurity bands, band-tailing, and Fermi level positioning in the quantum-confined regime has been lacking. Moreover, prior art has not established a general method for achieving heavy doping—defined herein as the incorporation of at least two dopant atoms per nanocrystal—with predictable electronic outcomes. The present invention addresses these limitations by providing a robust, scalable process for preparing heavily doped colloidal semiconductor nanocrystals with controlled dopant dispersion and demonstrable modulation of fundamental electronic properties.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The invention provides heavily doped colloidal semiconductor nanocrystals and methods for their preparation, wherein the electronic properties—including band gap, Fermi energy, and charge carrier density—are precisely controlled through the intentional incorporation of heterovalent dopant atoms. Semiconductor nanocrystals, particularly those composed of III-V materials such as InAs, are doped with at least one dopant material selected from metals such as Cu, Ag, or Au, resulting in n-type or p-type behavior depending on the valence and site occupancy of the dopant. The dopant material is dispersed within the semiconductor matrix, altering the density of states and enabling the emergence of a confined impurity band and quantum-confined Urbach tail under strong doping conditions. This successful control of doping is verified through optical measurements, scanning tunneling spectroscopy (STS), and theoretical modeling, which collectively demonstrate shifts in the Fermi level consistent with donor or acceptor behavior.

In one aspect, the invention provides a nanoparticle material comprising a semiconductor material doped with at least two atoms of a dopant material, wherein the dopant induces charge carriers in the nanoparticle material. In another aspect, the nanoparticle comprises a semiconductor material and at least two atoms of a dopant material that is heterovalent relative to the host atoms, thereby altering the density of states. The nanoparticle may consist essentially of the doped semiconductor material, or it may include regions of different compositions, such as a doped semiconducting core and a metallic or metal alloy domain on the surface, forming a hybrid nanoparticle. The dimensions of the nanoparticles typically range from 2 to 10 nanometers in diameter, ensuring strong quantum confinement effects.

The semiconductor material may be selected from binary, ternary, or quaternary compounds, including but not limited to InAs, CdSe, PbS, ZnO, and their hydrates or derivatives. Dopant selection is guided by ionic radius, diffusion coefficient, and valence compatibility; suitable dopants include Cu, Ag, Au, Mn, and Co, introduced via metal salts such as CuCl₂, AgNO₃, or AuCl₃. The dopant is dispersed homogeneously or in a gradient profile within the nanoparticle, with concentrations ranging from 0.1% to 10% atomic percent, corresponding to at least two dopant atoms per nanocrystal. This doping alters the electronic structure by introducing shallow or deep impurity levels, shifting the Fermi level, and modifying optical absorption and emission characteristics.

The invention further provides a method for manufacturing doped nanoparticles by contacting undoped colloidal nanocrystals with a solution of dopant or dopant precursor in the presence of surface ligands such as dodecylamine (DDA) and didodecyldimethylammonium bromide (DDAB). These ligands facilitate dopant association while passivating the surface to prevent aggregation. The resulting doped nanoparticles find application in electronic and optoelectronic devices, including diodes, bipolar transistors, and large-area display arrays, where layers of n-doped and p-doped nanoparticles form active junctions. Specific embodiments include Ag-doped InAs nanoparticles exhibiting p-type behavior and Cu-doped InAs nanoparticles showing n-type characteristics, as confirmed by STS-measured Fermi level shifts.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF EMBODIMENTS

The invention enables the preparation of doped semiconductor nanoparticles with controlled electronic properties through a post-synthetic doping process that overcomes the limitations of traditional nanocrystal doping approaches. Central to this achievement is the recognition that strong quantum confinement in nanocrystals fundamentally alters the behavior of dopants compared to bulk systems. In bulk semiconductors, high doping levels lead to phenomena such as the Moss-Burstein effect—where the Fermi level moves into the conduction band, causing a blue shift in absorption—and the formation of an Urbach tail due to disorder-induced band-tailing. In nanocrystals, however, the discrete nature of energy levels and limited number of available states mean that even a few dopant atoms can dramatically reshape the density of states.

The successful controlled doping described herein involves the addition of impurity atoms to pre-formed InAs nanocrystals under inert conditions. When a single impurity atom is introduced, it creates localized S- or P-like impurity states within the band gap, leading to red shifts in both absorption and photoluminescence due to reduced transition energies. However, at higher doping levels—specifically, when multiple impurities are present in a single nanocrystal—their wavefunctions begin to overlap, leading to delocalization and interaction of impurity charge carriers. This results in the formation of a confined impurity band and, critically, a shift in the Fermi level that can manifest as either a red or blue shift in optical spectra, depending on the dopant type and concentration.

Disorder effects, arising from lattice distortions induced by dopant incorporation, contribute to band-tailing via a quantum-confined Urbach tail mechanism. For example, Ag doping in InAs introduces significant lattice strain due to the larger ionic radius of Ag⁺ compared to In³⁺, leading to p-type behavior through the creation of acceptor states. In contrast, Cu, with a smaller ionic radius, tends to occupy interstitial sites and acts as a donor, yielding n-type characteristics. X-ray diffraction (XRD) and X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) confirm that the InAs crystal structure is preserved upon doping, with no evidence of secondary metal phases, while ICP-AES quantifies dopant loading at 2–10 atoms per nanocrystal depending on the metal-to-nanocrystal ratio.

Scanning tunneling spectroscopy (STS) provides direct evidence of Fermi level shifts: Cu-doped InAs QDs exhibit a negative shift in the gap center, consistent with n-doping, whereas Ag-doped QDs show a positive shift, indicative of p-doping. Absorption and emission spectra corroborate these findings—Cu treatment increases the first exciton energy (blue shift in absorption) while leaving emission unchanged, whereas Ag treatment reduces both exciton and emission energies (red shifts). Theoretical modeling using pseudo-crystal and Z-model frameworks explains these observations through the interplay of impurity localization, Coulomb interactions, and quantum confinement. Crucially, the invention demonstrates that controlled heavy doping is achievable without phase separation, enabling the design of nanoscale building blocks for next-generation electronic devices.

### EXPERIMENTAL

The materials used in the preparation of doped nanoparticles include high-purity precursors: In(III)Cl₃ (99.999+%), tris(trimethylsilyl) arsenide (TMS₃As), trioctylphosphine (TOP, purified by vacuum distillation), AuCl₃ (99%), AgCl (99+%), AgNO₃ (99+%), CuCl₂ (99.999%), dodecylamine (DDA, 98%), didodecyldimethylammonium bromide (DDAB, 98%), toluene (anhydrous), and methanol (anhydrous). All manipulations were conducted under inert atmosphere using Schlenk techniques.

InAs nanocrystals were synthesized by injecting a mixture of InCl₃ in TOP and TMS₃As into hot TOP (300°C), followed by growth at 260°C. Size was monitored by UV-Vis absorption, and size-selective precipitation with toluene/methanol yielded monodisperse samples (10% size distribution). For metal-atom doping, a solution containing 10 mg metal salt (CuCl₂, AgNO₃, AgCl, or AuCl₃), 80 mg DDAB, and 120 mg DDA in 10 mL toluene was prepared. This solution was added dropwise to 2 mL of InAs nanocrystal toluene solution under stirring. After 15 minutes, optical properties were measured, and nanoparticles were precipitated with methanol (Cu, Au) or acetone (Ag). The entire procedure was performed under nitrogen or argon to prevent oxidation. The metal-to-nanocrystal ratio was calibrated using literature absorption cross-sections and verified by ICP-AES, enabling precise control over dopant loading from sub-single-atom to multi-atom regimes per nanocrystal.
