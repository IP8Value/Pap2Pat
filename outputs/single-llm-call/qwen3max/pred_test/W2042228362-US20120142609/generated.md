# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates to a non-human animal model exhibiting increased retinal vascular permeability. More specifically, the invention provides genetically modified non-human animals, particularly mice, in which the expression of Dp71—a dystrophin isoform encoded by the Duchenne muscular dystrophy (DMD) gene—is reduced or absent. These animals serve as valuable models for studying diseases characterized by breakdown of the blood-retinal barrier (BRB), such as diabetic retinopathy, retinal vein occlusion, and retinal edema. The invention further encompasses methods of using such animal models for drug screening, elucidating disease pathophysiology, and identifying diagnostic or prognostic biomarkers associated with glial dysfunction and BRB compromise.

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

Diabetic retinopathy is a leading cause of vision loss among working-age adults worldwide. It arises from chronic hyperglycemia-induced microvascular damage in the retina, ultimately resulting in macular edema, neovascularization, and irreversible photoreceptor degeneration. A critical early event in diabetic retinopathy is the breakdown of the blood-retinal barrier (BRB), which normally restricts the passage of plasma proteins and fluid from retinal capillaries into the neural retina. This barrier dysfunction leads to retinal thickening, accumulation of extracellular fluid, and impaired neuronal function.

Glial cells, particularly Müller cells, play a central role in maintaining retinal homeostasis and BRB integrity. Müller cells are radial glia that span the entire thickness of the retina and form intimate associations with neurons, blood vessels, and the vitreous. They regulate extracellular ion concentrations, recycle neurotransmitters, buffer potassium ions, and facilitate water clearance—processes essential for normal retinal function. Among their many roles, Müller cells contribute directly to the formation and maintenance of the BRB through structural and signaling interactions with endothelial cells.

A key aspect of Müller cell physiology is potassium buffering and water drainage, mediated primarily by the inwardly rectifying potassium channel Kir4.1 and the water channel aquaporin-4 (AQP4). In healthy retina, Kir4.1 and AQP4 are highly polarized, concentrated in Müller cell endfeet at the inner limiting membrane and perivascular processes. This precise subcellular localization is essential for efficient spatial buffering of potassium and directional water movement out of the retina.

This polarized distribution depends critically on Dp71, a C-terminal isoform of dystrophin. Dp71 forms part of the dystrophin-associated protein complex (DAPC), which anchors Kir4.1 and AQP4 to specific membrane microdomains. Genetic inactivation of Dp71 disrupts the DAPC, leading to mislocalization of Kir4.1 and downregulation of AQP4. Consequently, Müller cells lose their ability to properly regulate potassium and water homeostasis, resulting in cellular swelling, impaired glutamate uptake, and neuronal dysfunction.

Studies in Dp71-null mice have demonstrated that loss of this protein causes profound alterations in Müller cell morphology and physiology, even in the absence of overt injury. These include depolarization of the glial membrane potential, reduced potassium conductance, and increased susceptibility to osmotic stress. Importantly, Dp71 deficiency also leads to a significant increase in retinal vascular permeability, indicating a direct link between Müller cell cytoskeletal integrity and BRB function.

Glial dysfunction, particularly involving Kir4.1 and AQP4 mislocalization, has been documented in numerous retinal pathologies, including diabetic retinopathy, retinal detachment, and ischemia-reperfusion injury. In these conditions, reactive gliosis—an adaptive response marked by upregulation of glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP)—is accompanied by early downregulation of Dp71, suggesting that Dp71 loss may be a common upstream event in the cascade leading to BRB breakdown and edema formation.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a non-human animal model exhibiting increased retinal vascular permeability due to reduced or absent expression of Dp71. This model recapitulates key features of human retinal diseases characterized by blood-retinal barrier dysfunction, including impaired potassium and water homeostasis, Müller cell swelling, and elevated vascular leakage. The model is particularly useful for investigating the molecular mechanisms underlying BRB failure, screening therapeutic agents aimed at restoring barrier integrity, and identifying novel biomarkers of glial dysfunction.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

### Experimental Retinal Detachment Model

To investigate the role of Dp71 in retinal injury responses, an experimental retinal detachment model was employed in both wild-type (C57BL/6) and Dp71-null mice. Retinal detachment was induced by subretinal injection of sodium hyaluronate under anesthesia, creating a localized separation of the neurosensory retina from the retinal pigment epithelium. This procedure reliably triggers reactive gliosis in Müller cells, as evidenced by robust upregulation of GFAP within 24 hours. In wild-type mice, retinal detachment led to a significant reduction—approximately 50%—in both Dp71 mRNA and protein levels, indicating rapid transcriptional and post-transcriptional downregulation of Dp71 in response to injury. Concurrently, Kir4.1 channels lost their polarized localization, becoming diffusely distributed along Müller cell processes, while AQP4 expression decreased by about 30%. These changes were accompanied by functional deficits, including reduced potassium current densities and increased osmotic swelling of Müller cell somata under hypotonic stress.

### Results of Experimental Retinal Detachment Model

In Dp71-null mice, which lack Dp71 expression due to targeted disruption of the Dp71-specific exon, baseline levels of Kir4.1 and AQP4 were already mislocalized or downregulated, mirroring the phenotype observed in injured wild-type retinas. Notably, experimental retinal detachment did not induce further significant changes in these channels in Dp71-null mice, suggesting that Dp71 loss is sufficient to drive the pathological cascade independently of additional injury. Electrophysiological recordings revealed that while absolute potassium current amplitudes were only modestly reduced after detachment, membrane capacitance increased significantly—indicating cellular hypertrophy—and thus current density (current per unit membrane area) was markedly diminished. This reduction impairs the capacity of Müller cells to buffer extracellular potassium, potentially contributing to neuronal hyperexcitability and excitotoxicity. Furthermore, Dp71-null mice exhibited a four-fold increase in BRB permeability compared to controls, as measured by Evans blue extravasation, confirming that Dp71 is essential for vascular barrier integrity.

### Non Human Model According to the Invention

The non-human animal model of the invention is defined as any non-human mammal, preferably a rodent such as a mouse or rat, in which the expression of Dp71 is genetically reduced or abolished, resulting in increased retinal vascular permeability. “Increased retinal vascular permeability” refers to a measurable elevation in the leakage of plasma proteins or tracer molecules (e.g., Evans blue-albumin complexes) from retinal blood vessels into the neural retina, indicative of compromised BRB function.

Dp71 is a 71-kDa isoform of dystrophin, transcribed from an internal promoter in intron 62 of the DMD gene. It lacks the N-terminal actin-binding domain but retains the cysteine-rich and C-terminal domains necessary for interaction with the dystrophin-associated protein complex (DAPC). Multiple Dp71 transcripts exist due to alternative splicing, generating isoforms such as Dp71d and Dp71f, which differ in their C-terminal sequences and subcellular targeting.

The DAPC is a multi-protein assembly that includes dystroglycans (α- and β-DG), sarcoglycans, syntrophins, and dystrobrevins. In skeletal muscle, the DAPC links the intracellular cytoskeleton to the extracellular matrix, providing mechanical stability during contraction. In the retina, the DAPC serves a distinct role: it organizes membrane microdomains in Müller cell endfeet, ensuring proper clustering and retention of Kir4.1 and AQP4 channels.

“Inhibitors of expression” encompass any agent that reduces Dp71 or DAP gene expression at the transcriptional, post-transcriptional, or translational level. These include small organic molecules that interfere with promoter activity or mRNA stability; antisense oligonucleotides designed to hybridize with Dp71 pre-mRNA or mRNA and induce RNase H-mediated degradation; siRNA and shRNA constructs that trigger RNA interference; and catalytic ribozymes that cleave target mRNA. To enhance stability and cellular uptake, oligonucleotide-based inhibitors may be chemically modified with phosphorothioate backbones, 2′-O-methyl or 2′-fluoro ribose substitutions, or cholesterol conjugates.

Vectors for delivering these inhibitors include viral and non-viral systems. Viral vectors—such as retroviruses (for stable integration in dividing cells), adenoviruses (for high-level transient expression), and adeno-associated viruses (AAVs, for long-term expression in post-mitotic cells like Müller glia)—are engineered to carry expression cassettes under the control of heterologous regulatory regions. Plasmid vectors, delivered via electroporation, lipofection, or hydrodynamic injection, offer a non-integrating alternative. For Müller cell-specific targeting, promoters such as those from the glutamine synthetase (GS) or cellular retinaldehyde-binding protein (CRALBP) genes may be used.

Deficient animals for Dp71 or DAP genes can be generated by homologous recombination, CRISPR/Cas9-mediated gene editing, or transposon-based mutagenesis. Conditional knockouts, using Cre-loxP or similar systems, allow spatial and temporal control of gene deletion—for example, restricting Dp71 ablation to Müller cells via a GFAP-Cre or Rlbp1-Cre driver line. The preferred model is the Dp71-null mouse, in which the first exon of Dp71 is replaced by a β-geo reporter cassette, abolishing Dp71 without affecting other dystrophin isoforms.

This model is used to screen drugs that restore BRB integrity, study disease mechanisms involving glial dysfunction, and identify biomarkers (e.g., altered Kir4.1 localization or elevated cyclooxygenase-2) predictive of edema risk. Diseases modeled include diabetic retinopathy, retinal vein occlusion, uveitis, and inherited retinal dystrophies with secondary edema.

Drug screening involves administering candidate compounds to the animal model and measuring BRB integrity via Evans blue assay, optical coherence tomography (OCT) for retinal thickness, or fluorescein angiography. A reduction in vascular leakage or Müller cell swelling indicates therapeutic efficacy.

### Uses of the Non Human Model According to the Invention

The Dp71-deficient animal model is employed to screen pharmaceutical agents for their ability to reduce retinal vascular permeability or restore Müller cell function. It is also used to dissect the molecular pathways linking glial cytoskeletal integrity to BRB maintenance, including roles for inflammatory mediators like prostaglandins. Additionally, the model facilitates discovery of diagnostic markers—such as soluble DAP components in vitreous humor or imaging signatures of Müller cell swelling—that correlate with early BRB breakdown before clinical symptoms manifest.

### Diagnostics Methods According to the Invention

Diagnostic methods involve analyzing biological samples (e.g., blood, vitreous, or retinal biopsies) from subjects for mutations in DMD or DAP genes, or altered expression of Dp71, Kir4.1, or AQP4. Mutation detection uses sequencing, allele-specific PCR, or microarray hybridization. Expression analysis includes RT-PCR or qPCR for mRNA levels, with RNA extracted via Trizol and reverse-transcribed using random hexamers. Protein levels are assessed by Western blot, ELISA, or immunohistochemistry using validated antibodies. Normal expression levels are established from age-matched healthy controls; deviations beyond two standard deviations indicate pathology.

### Therapeutic Methods and Pharmaceutical Compositions According to the Invention

“Treating” means alleviating symptoms, slowing progression, or preventing onset of BRB-related retinopathies. Therapeutics include Dp71-expressing vectors, activators of Dp71 transcription (e.g., histone deacetylase inhibitors), or agents that stabilize Kir4.1/AQP4 localization. Polypeptides are produced recombinantly in E. coli, yeast, or mammalian cells. Nucleic acid constructs are formulated with pharmaceutically acceptable carriers—saline, phosphate buffers, polysorbate 80—and administered via intravitreal injection, topical eye drops, or systemic routes. Ophthalmic formulations include sterile isotonic solutions containing antibacterial agents (e.g., benzalkonium chloride) and viscosity enhancers (e.g., hyaluronic acid) for prolonged residence time.

## EXAMPLE

Dp71-null mice, generated by replacing the Dp71-specific exon with a β-geo cassette, and age-matched C57BL/6 controls were used under institutional animal care guidelines. Antibodies against GFAP, Kir4.1, AQP4, utrophin, β-dystroglycan, and Dp71 were sourced commercially or previously characterized. Retinal detachment was induced by subretinal sodium hyaluronate injection under ketamine/xylazine anesthesia with tropicamide-induced mydriasis; eyes with surgical complications were excluded.

Immunohistochemistry involved paraformaldehyde fixation, sucrose cryoprotection, cryosectioning, Triton X-100 permeabilization, blocking with serum/BSA, primary antibody incubation (2 h, RT), Alexa Fluor-conjugated secondary antibodies (1 h), and fluorescence microscopy. Western blotting used Tris-acetate gels, PVDF transfer, milk/BSA blocking, HRP-conjugated secondaries, and ECL detection. qRT-PCR employed SYBR Green on a LightCycler with β-actin normalization.

Müller cells were isolated via papain/DNase digestion for patch-clamp recording. Retinal detachment in wild-types induced GFAP upregulation, 50% Dp71 loss, Kir4.1 mislocalization (without protein loss), and 30% AQP4 reduction. Utrophin increased at the ILM, possibly compensatory. In Dp71-null mice, Kir4.1 and AQP4 were constitutively mislocalized/downregulated; detachment caused no further change.

Electrophysiology showed reduced K+ current densities post-detachment due to increased capacitance (hypertrophy), not channel loss. Dp71-null Müller cells exhibited baseline osmotic swelling (118% vs. 102% in controls), unresponsive to Ba2+, implicating chronic inflammation. Adenosine or inhibitors of phospholipase A2 (4-bromophenacyl bromide) and cyclooxygenase (indomethacin) blocked swelling, confirming inflammatory mediator involvement.

Evans blue assay revealed 4-fold higher BRB permeability in Dp71-null mice. Collectively, Dp71 is essential for Müller cell polarization, volume regulation, and BRB integrity. Its loss mimics injury-induced gliosis, establishing Dp71-null mice as a robust model for retinal edema and a platform for therapeutic development.
