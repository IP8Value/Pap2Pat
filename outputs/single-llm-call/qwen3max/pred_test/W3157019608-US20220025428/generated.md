# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates to the field of digital data storage, and more specifically to a novel form of non-volatile memory based on nucleic acid architectures. The invention provides a system and method for encoding, storing, and retrieving digital information using engineered nucleic acid structures, particularly DNA origami nanostructures, in which binary data is represented by the presence or absence of specific single-stranded DNA domains at addressable locations within the architecture. This approach, termed digital Nucleic Acid Memory (dNAM), leverages the programmability of DNA hybridization and the spatial precision of DNA nanotechnology to create a high-density, durable, and optically readable memory medium. Unlike conventional DNA-based storage systems that rely on sequencing to recover data encoded in nucleotide sequences, dNAM decouples data representation from sequence content and instead encodes information in the physical arrangement of molecular components, enabling optical readout via super-resolution microscopy. The invention further encompasses integrated error-correcting algorithms, encoding schemes, and decoding methodologies tailored to the unique error profiles associated with optical detection of molecular binding events, thereby ensuring robust data recovery even in the presence of significant noise or structural defects.

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

The exponential growth of global data generation has placed unprecedented demands on existing archival storage technologies, many of which are approaching fundamental physical and economic limits. Magnetic tape, currently the dominant medium for long-term archival storage, suffers from finite durability, limited areal density, and environmental concerns related to its production and disposal. While recent advances have pushed tape densities to approximately 31 Gbit/cm², these gains are incremental and constrained by the physics of magnetic domain stability and read/write head miniaturization. Moreover, magnetic media typically degrade over decades, requiring periodic data migration to maintain integrity. In response to these challenges, researchers have explored alternative materials for archival storage, with deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) emerging as a particularly promising candidate due to its extraordinary information density—estimated at exabytes per gram—and potential for million-year stability under appropriate conditions. DNA also offers low energy consumption during storage and is biodegradable, aligning with sustainability goals. However, conventional DNA data storage approaches encode digital information directly into the nucleotide sequence of synthetic oligonucleotides, necessitating next-generation sequencing for data retrieval—a process that remains costly, slow, and incompatible with random access or in situ editing. These limitations have spurred interest in divergent strategies that exploit other properties of nucleic acids beyond their linear sequence. Advances in DNA nanotechnology, particularly the development of DNA origami and related self-assembling architectures, have demonstrated exquisite control over the spatial organization of molecules at the nanoscale. These techniques enable the precise positioning of functional elements, including fluorescent probes, at defined locations within two- or three-dimensional scaffolds. Concurrently, breakthroughs in super-resolution microscopy, such as DNA-Points Accumulation for Imaging in Nanoscale Topography (DNA-PAINT), have made it possible to visualize individual molecular binding events with sub-10-nanometer resolution, far below the diffraction limit of light. The convergence of these fields presents an opportunity to reimagine nucleic acid memory not as a linear code to be sequenced, but as a spatially addressable matrix to be imaged, thereby enabling direct optical readout of digital information stored in synthetic DNA nanostructures.

## BRIEF SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a digital nucleic acid memory (dNAM) system comprising engineered nucleic acid architectures that serve as stable, high-density substrates for encoding and storing digital information. In this system, data is represented not by the sequence of nucleotides per se, but by the presence or absence of specific single-stranded DNA domains—referred to herein as data strands—at predetermined, addressable locations within a larger nucleic acid scaffold. These architectures are designed such that each addressable location corresponds to a bit in a binary string, with a "1" state indicated by the inclusion of a data strand and a "0" state by its omission. The invention specifically employs DNA origami nanostructures, formed by the self-assembly of a long scaffold strand and a set of shorter staple strands, wherein selected staple strands are extended to include docking sequences for data-imager hybridization. Digital information is encoded by selecting which staple strands include these extensions, thereby creating a physical pattern of potential binding sites that constitutes the stored data. Reading of the stored information is achieved through super-resolution microscopy, particularly DNA-PAINT, which detects the transient binding of fluorescently labeled imager strands to the data domains, allowing reconstruction of the binary pattern with nanometer precision. The invention further includes a comprehensive encoding scheme that translates arbitrary digital data into a series of such origami structures, each carrying a portion of the data along with robust error-correcting information. This encoding integrates fountain codes—which allow message recovery from any sufficient subset of data packets—with a custom bi-level parity and checksum system embedded within each origami’s design to correct errors arising from incomplete assembly, imaging noise, or fluorophore failure. The decoding algorithm processes the raw imaging data, identifies and corrects bit errors using the embedded redundancy, recovers the original data droplets, and reconstructs the complete message. Collectively, these components form an integrated memory platform that enables writing, stable archival storage, and optical reading of digital information in a nucleic acid format, offering advantages in density, durability, and potential for parallel readout compared to both conventional electronic and sequencing-based DNA storage systems.

A “nucleotide duplex” is a double-stranded structure formed by the hybridization of two complementary nucleotide strands. “Nucleotide origami” or “origami” refers to a nanostructure formed by folding a long single-stranded nucleic acid scaffold with the aid of multiple shorter “staple” strands that bind to specific regions of the scaffold, thereby dictating its final conformation. A “decoding algorithm” is a computational procedure that converts observed signals into recovered digital data, incorporating error correction.

### Nucleic Acid Architecture

The nucleic acid architectures of the present invention are engineered macromolecular assemblies designed to provide a stable, addressable matrix for digital data storage. These architectures exploit the specificity of Watson-Crick base pairing, wherein adenine (A) pairs with thymine (T) (or uracil (U) in RNA) and guanine (G) pairs with cytosine (C), to achieve precise spatial control over component placement. Architectures may be designed using computational tools such as caDNAno, Tiamat, or custom software that models hybridization energetics and steric constraints. The nucleobase composition may include natural bases (A, T, G, C, U) or synthetic analogs such as isoG, isoC, P, Z, or others that expand the genetic alphabet and enable orthogonal hybridization. Nucleotide analogs, including locked nucleic acids (LNA), peptide nucleic acids (PNA), and 2'-O-methyl RNA, may be incorporated to enhance stability, alter hybridization kinetics, or confer resistance to nucleases. Polymerization of nucleotides into oligomers is typically achieved via solid-phase synthesis, yielding strands of defined length and sequence. Design of oligomers considers melting temperature, secondary structure propensity, and cross-hybridization risks. Nucleotide brick molecular canvases consist of hundreds to thousands of short, unique strands that assemble into complex shapes via cooperative binding. Single-pot synthesis allows simultaneous folding of all components, while serial fluidic flow assembly enables stepwise construction for enhanced yield. The origami approach utilizes a long scaffold strand—commonly the 7,249-nucleotide M13mp18 bacteriophage genome—folded by hundreds of staple strands, each 20–60 nucleotides long, that bind to discontinuous regions of the scaffold, forcing it into a desired conformation. Single-stranded tiles (SSTs) and nucleic acid bricks offer alternative modular assembly strategies. Addressability is achieved by assigning unique coordinates to each potential binding site within the architecture. Single-stranded oligomers, typically 5–100 nucleotides in length, serve as data or imager strands. Two-dimensional architectures, such as flat rectangles or honeycomb lattices, and three-dimensional structures, like boxes or polyhedra, are both contemplated. Attachment to solid substrates (e.g., glass, silicon) is facilitated by chemical functionalization or electrostatic interactions. In the dNAM origami embodiment, data strands are extensions of staple strands that protrude from the origami surface, providing docking sites for imager strands. Data density is determined by the spacing of these sites, which can be as close as 3.5 nm (one helical turn of DNA), enabling theoretical areal densities exceeding 1,000 Gbit/cm².

### Dyes

Dyes play a critical role in the optical readout of dNAM by providing detectable signals upon binding to data domains. Chromophores are light-absorbing moieties, while fluorophores additionally emit light upon excitation. In the present invention, dyes are covalently conjugated to imager strands, which are short, single-stranded DNA sequences complementary to the docking sequences of data strands. Typically, a single dye is bound per imager strand to ensure a one-to-one correspondence between signal and bit state. However, multiple dyes may be attached at multiple turns of the DNA helix to amplify signal intensity. Within a single dNAM origami, all dyes may be identical to simplify detection, or multiplexed using orthogonal binding sequences paired with spectrally distinct dyes to increase bit depth per site. Multiplexing significantly enhances data density by enabling more than one bit per physical location. Exemplary dye classes include xanthene derivatives (e.g., fluorescein, rhodamine), cyanine derivatives (e.g., Cy3, Cy5), squaraine, naphthalene, coumarin, oxadiazole, anthracene, pyrene, oxazine, acridine, arylmethine, tetrapyrrole, and dipyrromethene derivatives. Commercially available dyes such as Alexa Fluor, LI-COR IRDyes, ATTO, WellRED, and Dyomics are suitable. Dye modifications may adjust solubility, hydrophobicity, symmetry, or placement to optimize performance. The use of dyes enables high-sensitivity, super-resolution imaging of data domains, with DNA-PAINT leveraging transient binding to achieve sub-diffraction resolution. Environmental benefits arise from the biodegradability of nucleic acid components, though protection via encapsulation in silica nanoparticles may be employed to enhance stability at ambient temperatures. Unlike sequencing-based DNA storage, dNAM uses dyes solely for readout, not for encoding, decoupling data representation from sequence. Data encryption can be implemented by obfuscating the mapping between physical locations and logical bits. Miniaturization enables applications in product tracking and regulatory labeling. Temporary storage is feasible using heat-sensitive or enzymatically degradable constructs. Sugar modifications (e.g., 2'-fluoro) tune stability. The dNAM system integrates with computer processors for encoding/decoding, and data reading employs microscopes coupled to image-processing software that captures localizations, identifies data strands, generates binary symbols, and compiles messages. Computer systems implementing NAM/dNAM include CPUs, memory, communication interfaces, and machine-readable media executing algorithms for data management.

## EXAMPLES

### Example 1

The dNAM concept was demonstrated by encoding the message “Data is in our DNA!\n” into fifteen distinct DNA origami nanostructures. Each origami was designed as a 6×8 matrix of potential data domains spaced ~10 nm apart, corresponding to 48 addressable bits. Binary states were defined by the presence (1) or absence (0) of a data strand extension on staple strands. The message was converted to ASCII binary, segmented into 15 overlapping 16-bit droplets using a fountain code, and augmented with error-correcting bits: 4 index bits, 4 orientation markers, 4 checksum bits, and 20 parity bits per origami. Origami were synthesized by folding M13 scaffold with custom staple mixes, purified, and deposited on glass coverslips. Data was read via DNA-PAINT using a Cy3B-labeled imager strand, with 40,000-frame recordings capturing blinking events. Localization data was processed to assign binary values to each site, and a decoding algorithm applied bi-level parity checks and fountain code reconstruction to recover the message. Quality control via AFM confirmed structural integrity and data domain presence. Error analysis revealed mean false-negative rates of 7.3±1.2 per origami, with false positives rarer (1.7±0.5). Template matching validated design fidelity. Sampling analysis showed ~750 correctly decoded origami sufficed for 100% message recovery. Simulations indicated robustness up to 7.4 errors per origami, with message recovery dropping sharply beyond. Compared to magnetic tape, dNAM offers higher theoretical density (330 Gbit/cm² after overhead) and vastly superior durability (millions vs. decades of years). Current limitations include write speed (constrained by liquid handling) and read throughput, though innovations in DNA-PAINT and deep learning promise improvements. The fountain code’s robustness to lost packets ensures message recovery if sufficient droplets are observed. Defect analysis suggested inactive data domains—not missing staples—were the primary error source, pointing to sequence optimization as a key improvement avenue. Larger origami and multiplexed dyes could further boost capacity. In conclusion, dNAM establishes a viable, optically readable DNA memory platform suitable for high-density, durable archival storage of small-to-medium data sets.

## Materials and Methods

### Buffers

Deposition buffer comprised 0.5× Tris-borate-EDTA (TBE) and 18 mM MgCl₂. Imaging buffer added 60 nM protocatechuate 3,4-dioxygenase (PCD), 1 mM Trolox, 3 nM imager strands, and 10 mM protocatechuic acid (PCA) to deposition buffer, with PCA added immediately before imaging.

### Encoding Algorithm

A fountain code divided the message into k segments, then generated droplets by XOR-ing d randomly selected segments, with d drawn from a Soliton distribution to ensure optimal single-segment droplet frequency. Each droplet was mapped to a 6×8 origami matrix, with data, index, orientation, checksum, and parity bits arranged in a rotation-invariant layout to facilitate error correction prior to orientation determination.

### DNA Origami Folding

Origami were designed as 90×70 nm rectangles with 48 docking sites. Folding mixed 22 nM M13 scaffold, 10× unmodified staples, 50× extended staples, 1× Tris-acetate-EDTA (TAE), and 18 mM MgCl₂ in 100 µL nuclease-free water. Thermal cycling: 90°C for 1 min, 80°C for 2 min, then ramp to 25°C over 12 h. Purification used 0.8% agarose gel electrophoresis in 0.5× TBE + 8 mM MgCl₂, with band excision and elution.

### Glass Coverslip Preparation

Coverslips were sonicated in 0.1% Liquinox and nanopure water, dried at 40°C, then coated with 0.2 pM 150-nm gold nanoparticles as fiducial markers for 10 min, rinsed with methanol and water, and stored at 40°C.

### Fluorescence Microscopy

Imaging used an inverted Nikon Ti2 microscope in TIRF mode with a 561-nm laser, 100×/1.49 NA objective, and EMCCD camera (300-ms exposure, 3 fps). 40,000 frames were recorded per field of view.

### DNA-PAINT Fluorophore Localization

Localization centers were identified using ThunderSTORM plugin in ImageJ, then drift-corrected and rendered with Picasso software.

### Localization Data Processing

Clusters were selected based on inner/outer ring intensity thresholds. Grid fitting used maximum likelihood estimation with a Poisson noise model. Binary assignment applied an empirical intensity threshold. Template matching compared observed structures to known designs for error analysis.

### Decoding Algorithm

Step 1: For each origami, parity and checksum mismatches were used to compute bit weights. A greedy search flipped high-weight bits up to nine times to find a valid matrix. Step 2: Recovered droplets were queued by degree (number of XOR-ed segments). Degree-one droplets were stored as segments; others were reduced via XOR until all segments were recovered.

## Data Simulation Test

Simulated origami encoded random messages of 160–12,800 bits, with 0–9 random bit flips per origami. Recovery rates were averaged over 40 trials per condition.
