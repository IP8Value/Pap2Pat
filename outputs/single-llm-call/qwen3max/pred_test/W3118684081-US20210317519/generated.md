# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention resides in the field of molecular biology and epigenetics, specifically relating to methods and compositions for the detection, identification, and quantitative analysis of modified cytosine nucleobases in nucleic acids. More particularly, the invention provides novel bisulfite-free chemical and enzymatic methodologies for base-resolution sequencing of 5-methylcytosine (5mC), 5-hydroxymethylcytosine (5hmC), 5-formylcytosine (5fC), and 5-carboxylcytosine (5caC) in both DNA and RNA. These methods enable direct, accurate, and high-fidelity mapping of epigenetic modifications across entire genomes or transcriptomes without the DNA degradation, sequence complexity loss, or indirect inference limitations inherent in traditional bisulfite-based approaches. The invention further encompasses kits, reagents, and integrated workflows designed to facilitate comprehensive epigenomic profiling in research, diagnostic, and therapeutic applications.

## BACKGROUND

DNA methylation at the 5-position of cytosine, yielding 5-methylcytosine (5mC), is a fundamental epigenetic mark that regulates gene expression, genomic imprinting, X-chromosome inactivation, and genome stability. 5mC is often referred to as the “fifth base” due to its prevalence and functional significance in mammalian genomes. This modification is dynamically regulated through oxidation by the ten-eleven translocation (TET) family of Fe(II)- and α-ketoglutarate-dependent dioxygenases, which successively convert 5mC to 5-hydroxymethylcytosine (5hmC), then to 5-formylcytosine (5fC), and finally to 5-carboxylcytosine (5caC). These oxidized derivatives—particularly 5hmC, regarded as the “sixth base”—are not merely transient intermediates but also stable epigenetic marks with distinct regulatory roles, especially in neuronal tissues and embryonic stem cells. The conversion of 5fC and 5caC back to unmodified cytosine occurs via thymine DNA glycosylase (TDG)-mediated base excision repair (BER), completing an active DNA demethylation pathway.

While 5mC and its oxidized forms are well-characterized in DNA, emerging evidence indicates that analogous modifications exist in RNA, including N4-methylcytidine (m4C), 5-methylcytidine (m5C), and potentially 5-hydroxymethylcytidine (hm5C). Although less abundant and less studied than their DNA counterparts, RNA modifications influence RNA stability, splicing, translation, and subcellular localization, thereby contributing to post-transcriptional gene regulation.

Historically, bisulfite sequencing (BS-seq) has served as the gold standard for detecting 5mC and 5hmC at single-base resolution. In this method, unmodified cytosines are deaminated to uracil under harsh acidic conditions, while 5mC and 5hmC resist conversion and are read as cytosine after PCR amplification. However, bisulfite treatment causes severe DNA fragmentation—degrading up to 99% of input material—and reduces sequence complexity by converting most cytosines to thymines, leading to poor mapping efficiency, biased coverage, and compromised data quality. Modified versions such as oxidative bisulfite sequencing (oxBS-seq) and TET-assisted bisulfite sequencing (TAB-seq) enable discrimination between 5mC and 5hmC but require paired experiments and subtraction-based inference, which amplifies technical noise and necessitates high sequencing depth. RNA bisulfite sequencing (RNA-BS-seq) suffers from similar drawbacks and is further complicated by RNA’s inherent instability.

Recent advances have introduced bisulfite-free alternatives, such as APOBEC-coupled epigenetic sequencing (ACE-seq) and enzymatic methyl-seq (EM-seq), which replace chemical deamination with enzymatic conversion. While these reduce DNA damage, they still rely on indirect detection by converting unmodified C to T, preserving the core limitation of reduced sequence complexity. Consequently, there remains a critical need for a unified, direct, non-destructive, and quantitative platform capable of independently and accurately resolving all four cytosine modifications—5mC, 5hmC, 5fC, and 5caC—in both DNA and RNA without requiring subtraction or suffering from sequence bias. The present invention fulfills this unmet need through a suite of chemically precise, borane reduction-based sequencing strategies.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The invention provides a novel method termed TET-Assisted Pyridine Borane Sequencing (TAPS) for the direct, quantitative, and base-resolution detection of 5-methylcytosine (5mC) and 5-hydroxymethylcytosine (5hmC) in nucleic acids. The TAPS reaction involves the enzymatic oxidation of 5mC and 5hmC to 5-carboxylcytosine (5caC) using a TET dioxygenase, followed by reductive decarboxylation and deamination of 5caC to dihydrouracil (DHU) using pyridine borane. DHU is subsequently amplified and sequenced as thymine (T), enabling direct readout of modified sites as C-to-T transitions. This approach avoids bisulfite-induced DNA damage, preserves sequence complexity, and yields superior mapping rates and coverage uniformity.

A key advantage of TAPS is its compatibility with selective blocking strategies to distinguish between 5mC and 5hmC without subtraction. In one embodiment, 5hmC is selectively protected by β-glucosyltransferase (βGT)-mediated addition of a glucose moiety, rendering it resistant to TET oxidation. Subsequent TET oxidation and borane reduction convert only 5mC to DHU, enabling 5mC-specific sequencing—a method designated TAPSβ. This represents the first bisulfite-free, base-resolution, and quantitative method for direct 5mC detection.

Conversely, for 5hmC-specific identification, the invention provides Chemical-Assisted Pyridine Borane Sequencing (CAPS). In CAPS, 5hmC is chemically oxidized to 5fC using potassium ruthenate (K₂RuO₄), while 5mC remains unreactive. The resulting 5fC is then converted to DHU by pic-borane (2-methylpyridine borane) and read as T. Unmodified cytosines and 5mC remain as C, allowing direct assignment of 5hmC positions. This subtraction-free strategy eliminates error propagation and enables accurate profiling even in low-abundance contexts.

For detection of demethylation intermediates, the invention includes Pyridine Borane Sequencing (PS), wherein 5fC and 5caC are directly converted to DHU by pyridine borane and sequenced as T. To resolve 5caC specifically, PS-c employs O-ethylhydroxylamine to block 5fC prior to borane treatment, ensuring only 5caC is converted. These methods support quantitative analysis across diverse nucleic acid types, including genomic DNA, cell-free DNA, and RNA.

The invention further extends to RNA applications, enabling detection of 5mC and 5hmC in RNA molecules using analogous chemistry. Target nucleic acids may be derived from any biological source—including cultured cells, tissues, blood, or biofluids—and processed in quantities as low as nanograms. The methods are compatible with standard library preparation, PCR amplification, and next-generation sequencing platforms, facilitating integration into existing genomic workflows. Collectively, these innovations provide a comprehensive, unified platform for epigenetic and epitranscriptomic analysis with unprecedented accuracy, sensitivity, and robustness.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

The TAPS method constitutes a paradigm shift in epigenetic sequencing by replacing destructive bisulfite chemistry with a mild, two-step enzymatic-chemical conversion process. Initially, a nucleic acid sample—typically double-stranded DNA—is treated with a TET enzyme (e.g., recombinant mTET1 catalytic domain) in the presence of cofactors (Fe²⁺, α-ketoglutarate, ascorbate) to oxidize 5mC and 5hmC to 5caC. This oxidation is highly specific and efficient, achieving near-complete conversion under optimized conditions. Subsequently, the oxidized DNA is subjected to pyridine borane in acidic buffer (e.g., sodium acetate, pH 4.3), which reductively decarboxylates 5caC to DHU. DHU pairs with adenine during PCR, resulting in C-to-T transitions at originally modified sites during sequencing.

To identify 5mC exclusively, the method incorporates a blocking step prior to TET oxidation. Specifically, 5hmC is glucosylated using β-glucosyltransferase (βGT) and UDP-glucose, forming β-glucosyl-5-hydroxymethylcytosine, which is inert to TET-mediated oxidation. Only 5mC is then oxidized to 5caC and converted to DHU, enabling direct 5mC mapping. This TAPSβ workflow achieves >97% conversion of 5mC and <2% conversion of 5hmC, with false-positive rates below 0.3%.

For 5hmC-specific detection, CAPS utilizes chemical oxidation instead of enzymatic oxidation. After library ligation with uracil-containing adaptors (to protect ends during oxidation), DNA is denatured and treated with K₂RuO₄, which selectively oxidizes 5hmC to 5fC. A second oxidation round ensures completeness. Pic-borane then converts 5fC to DHU. This single-stranded protocol achieves ~83% 5hmC-to-T conversion with minimal background.

To profile 5fC and 5caC, PS applies pyridine borane directly to native DNA, converting both modifications to DHU. For 5caC-specific detection (PS-c), 5fC is first blocked by reaction with O-ethylhydroxylamine, forming a stable oxime that resists borane reduction. The remaining 5caC is then converted to DHU.

These methods apply to both DNA and RNA. Target nucleic acids may originate from mammalian cells (e.g., mouse embryonic stem cells), human tissues, circulating cell-free DNA, or synthetic constructs. Input amounts can range from 100 ng down to sub-nanogram levels using specialized library protocols. Following modification, nucleic acids are amplified via PCR using polymerases tolerant of DHU (e.g., KAPA HiFi Uracil+), and sequenced using Illumina or other platforms. Data analysis involves alignment to reference genomes and calling of C-to-T conversions as indicators of original modifications.

Kits for practicing the invention include TET enzymes, βGT, UDP-glucose, pyridine borane, pic-borane, K₂RuO₄, O-ethylhydroxylamine, buffers, and control DNAs. Optional components include USER enzyme for adaptor processing, purification columns, and indexed primers. The methods support absolute quantification through spike-in controls with known modification densities, enabling precise measurement of modification frequencies across genomic regions.

## EXAMPLES

Model DNA substrates were synthesized to validate conversion efficiency. Oligonucleotides containing C, 5mC, 5hmC, 5fC, or 5caC were prepared by solid-phase synthesis or enzymatic incorporation. A 222 bp fragment was generated and methylated using M.SssI to produce CpG-methylated DNA. Spike-in controls included 2 kb unmodified DNA, 120-mer oligos with site-specific modifications, and synthetic duplexes bearing N5mCNN or N5hmCNN motifs. These were mixed into genomic DNA from mouse embryonic stem cells (mESCs) at defined ratios.

For HPLC-MS/MS validation, DNA samples were digested to nucleosides using nuclease P1, alkaline phosphatase, and phosphodiesterase I in the presence of a deaminase inhibitor. Samples were filtered and analyzed by LC-MS/MS to quantify modification levels before and after chemical treatment.

Recombinant NgTET1 and mTET1CD proteins were expressed in mammalian cells, purified via anti-FLAG affinity chromatography, and validated for oxidation activity. Oxidation reactions were performed on model and genomic DNA, followed by reduction with pic-BH3 or pyridine borane. Blocking reactions used βGT for 5hmC, O-ethylhydroxylamine for 5fC, and no blocker for 5caC.

Conversion efficiency was assessed by TaqαI restriction assay (sensitive to 5mC status), qPCR, and Sanger sequencing. Whole-genome applications used 100 ng mESC gDNA spiked with controls. Libraries were prepared using KAPA Hyper kits with NEBNext adaptors. For CAPS, USER enzyme opened adaptor loops to protect DNA ends during oxidation.

Low-input protocols employed double-stranded or single-stranded library construction for inputs as low as 1 ng. Cell-free DNA TAPS used similar workflows with size selection. Sequencing was performed on NovaSeq 6000 (150 bp PE).

Data processing involved trimming with Trim Galore!, alignment to mm9 + spike-ins using BWA-MEM, duplicate removal, and methylation calling with asTair. Coverage, mapping rates, and base quality were compared to WGBS, oxBS-seq, TAB-seq, and ACE-seq. Correlation analyses used Pearson’s r on CpG sites with ≥10× coverage. Genomic distributions were visualized in IGV; enrichment at regulatory elements was tested against random CG sites.

TAPSβ showed 97.6% 5mC conversion, 1.9% 5hmC conversion, and 0.24% false positives. CAPS achieved 83.1% 5hmC conversion and 0.72% background. PS and PS-c demonstrated >93% 5caC conversion and 76–95% 5fC conversion with <0.3% false positives. All methods outperformed bisulfite sequencing in DNA recovery, fragment length preservation, PCR bias, mapping rate (>90% vs <70%), and coverage uniformity. TAPSβ correlated strongly with oxBS-seq (r=0.72–0.77); CAPS correlated with TAB-seq (r=0.79) and ACE-seq (r=0.67), while subtraction-based 5hmC calling showed poor correlation (r<0.55). PS detected genuine 5fC/5caC signals at enhancers and promoters marked by H3K4me1/me3, including at the Nanog locus.

These examples confirm that the invention enables accurate, direct, and comprehensive mapping of all major cytosine modifications in DNA, with potential extension to RNA, using mild, scalable, and sequencing-compatible chemistry.
