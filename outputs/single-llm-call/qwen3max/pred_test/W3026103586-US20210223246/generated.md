# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNICAL FIELD

The present invention relates generally to the field of cancer diagnosis, particularly to methods and devices for the intraoperative detection of lymph node metastasis in patients with epithelial cancers, such as breast carcinoma. More specifically, the invention provides a real-time, noninvasive, and rapid diagnostic technique based on the measurement of pH values in lymphatic fluid extracted from or present within sentinel lymph nodes (SLNs) or other lymph nodes suspected of harboring metastatic cancer cells. The method leverages metabolic alterations in cancer cells that have metastasized to lymph nodes, which result in acidification of the local microenvironment due to increased fatty acid oxidation (FAO) and consequent production of carbon dioxide and protons. This acidification serves as a biochemical marker that can be readily detected using simple, biocompatible, and clinically adaptable tools, thereby enabling accurate discrimination between cancer-involved and healthy lymph nodes during surgical procedures without the need for extensive tissue processing or time-consuming pathological analysis.

## BACKGROUND

The lymphatic system is a critical component of the human immune and circulatory systems, comprising a network of lymphatic vessels, ducts, and lymph nodes that transport lymphatic fluid—also known as lymph—throughout the body. Lymph, a clear fluid derived from interstitial fluid, contains lymphocytes, proteins, lipids, and cellular debris, and plays a vital role in immune surveillance and waste removal. Lymph nodes, small bean-shaped organs distributed along the lymphatic pathways, act as filters that trap pathogens, foreign particles, and abnormal cells, including cancer cells. In the context of malignancy, particularly in carcinomas such as breast cancer, the lymphatic system often serves as the primary route for metastatic spread. Cancer cells detach from the primary tumor, enter lymphatic capillaries, and are carried to regional lymph nodes, where they may establish secondary tumors.

The sentinel lymph node (SLN), defined as the first lymph node or group of nodes to receive lymphatic drainage from a primary tumor site, is of particular clinical significance. The presence or absence of metastatic disease in the SLN is a key prognostic indicator and directly influences treatment decisions, including the extent of lymph node dissection and the necessity of adjuvant therapies. Current standard practice for evaluating SLN status during surgery involves intraoperative frozen section analysis, a histopathological technique wherein a portion of the excised lymph node is rapidly frozen, sectioned, stained, and examined under a microscope by a pathologist. However, this method is inherently limited by several factors: it is time-consuming, typically requiring 20–30 minutes per sample; it depends heavily on the skill and experience of the pathologist; and it often samples only a small, visually selected portion of the node, potentially missing micrometastases or isolated tumor cells. Consequently, false-negative results are not uncommon, leading to under-staging of disease and suboptimal treatment planning.

Alternative approaches, such as fluorescence imaging using tumor-specific antibodies, microwave ablation-based detection, and tissue impedance mapping, have been explored to improve intraoperative lymph node assessment. While these technologies offer certain advantages, they remain complex, require specialized equipment, lack real-time simplicity, or have not yet demonstrated sufficient reliability for widespread clinical adoption. There remains a compelling unmet need for a real-time, noninvasive, cost-effective, and highly accurate method to detect cancer involvement in lymph nodes during surgery—one that can be performed at the bedside or in the operating room without disrupting workflow, preserving healthy lymphatic tissue, and minimizing patient morbidity such as lymphedema, nerve damage, or chronic pain associated with unnecessary lymph node resection.

## SUMMARY

The present invention introduces a novel method for detecting cancer-involved lymph nodes by measuring the pH value of lymphatic fluid within or extracted from a lymph node. It has been discovered that lymph nodes infiltrated by metastatic cancer cells exhibit a significantly acidic internal environment, with pH values consistently below 7.0—and often below 6.0—due to metabolic reprogramming of cancer cells toward fatty acid oxidation (FAO), which generates carbon dioxide and protons that acidify the surrounding fluid. In contrast, healthy lymph nodes maintain a neutral or slightly alkaline pH, typically ranging from 7.0 to 10.0. The method comprises interacting lymphatic fluid with a biocompatible buffer solution, such as phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), and measuring the resulting pH using a pH-sensing paper, such as litmus paper or commercially available pH test strips. A color change in the pH-sensing paper indicating an acidic pH (e.g., red, orange, or yellow hues corresponding to pH < 7.0) is interpreted as evidence of cancer involvement in the lymph node.

In one embodiment, the buffer solution is injected directly into the lymph node in situ, and a pH-sensing paper is inserted into the node via a hypodermic needle to detect the color change. In another embodiment, a diagnostic instrument—referred to herein as the Metabolism-based Metastatic Lymph Diagnoser (MMLD)—is employed, which integrates a syringe, a gauge 18 needle, a pre-loaded volume of sterile PBS, and a segment of pH-sensing paper housed within the needle lumen. The procedure involves placing the pH-sensing paper inside the needle, filling the syringe with buffer solution, inserting the needle into the lymph node, allowing the lymphatic fluid to interact with the buffer, and observing the color change on the paper through the transparent or translucent barrel of the syringe or upon withdrawal. This entire process can be completed in less than 30 seconds, providing immediate feedback to the surgeon. The invention thus enables real-time, intraoperative discrimination between metastatic and non-metastatic lymph nodes, facilitating precise surgical decision-making and preservation of healthy lymphatic tissue.

The present invention is motivated by the urgent clinical need for early and accurate detection of cancer-involved lymph nodes, particularly in patients undergoing surgery for breast cancer. Recent research has revealed that cancer cells metastasized to lymph nodes undergo a metabolic shift from glycolysis to fatty acid oxidation (FAO), a process selectively driven by the expression and activation of Yes-Associated Protein (YAP). This transcriptional co-activator upregulates FAO-related genes, enhancing the breakdown of fatty acids to generate energy. During FAO, carbon dioxide is produced as a byproduct, which dissolves in the aqueous lymphatic fluid to form carbonic acid, dissociating into bicarbonate and hydrogen ions according to the reversible reaction: CO₂ + H₂O ⇌ H⁺ + HCO₃⁻. This accumulation of protons lowers the pH of the lymph node microenvironment, creating a distinct acidic signature that can serve as a reliable biomarker for metastasis.

To exploit this discovery, the invention provides a diagnostic instrument and method for cancer diagnosis based on real-time pH sensing. The method was calibrated by correlating live pH measurements of freshly resected sentinel lymph nodes with gold-standard pathological assays, specifically hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining of permanent sections. This calibration established that lymph nodes with pH < 7.0—particularly < 6.0—are highly likely to harbor metastatic cancer cells, whereas those with pH ≥ 7.0 are typically benign. Critically, this approach allows for the potential preservation of free (non-metastatic) SLNs, avoiding their unnecessary removal and the associated complications such as lymphedema.

In an exemplary embodiment for diagnosing breast cancer, the method involves measuring the pH value of lymphatic fluid from a lymph node (step 102) and detecting the node as cancer-involved if the pH is acidic (step 104). This can be performed either in vitro—on a resected lymph node—or in vivo—on a node still in situ. In the in vitro procedure, the lymph node is resected, and a needle containing pH-sensing paper is inserted after buffer injection. In the in vivo procedure, the same steps are performed without prior resection, enabling true real-time assessment.

Referring to FIG. 1, the basic diagnostic workflow is illustrated: lymphatic fluid is put into interaction with a buffer solution (step 202), the pH of the mixture is measured (step 204), and if the pH is acidic, the node is flagged as cancer-involved (step 206). Upon insertion into the lymph node (step 306), the lymphatic fluid contacts the paper and mixes with the buffer (step 308). A color change indicating acidity triggers a diagnosis of metastasis (step 310).

### Example 1: Real-Time Diagnosis of Cancer Involved Lymph Nodes

An exemplary instrument and method—the MMLD—utilizes injectable PBS and ColorpHast® pH test strips embedded in a gauge 18 syringe needle. The diagnostic procedure begins with in-vitro preparation of fresh lymph node samples obtained from patients undergoing breast cancer surgery. The MMLD is inserted into the node after PBS injection, and real-time pH sensing occurs within seconds. Calibration against pathological results from H&E staining establishes a clear threshold: pH < 6.0 indicates metastasis.

H&E staining, the gold standard, involves deparaffinization of tissue sections in xylene, hydration through graded alcohols, staining with hematoxylin (nuclear stain), differentiation in acid alcohol, bluing in alkaline solution, counterstaining with eosin (cytoplasmic stain), and dehydration through alcohols followed by clearing in xylene. This process confirms the presence or absence of tumor cells.

Testing of the pH-sensing method on 65 lymph nodes from 25 patients demonstrated superior performance compared to frozen section diagnostics. The MMLD achieved approximately 92% sensitivity and 90% selectivity, versus 83% for both metrics in frozen sections. FIG. 6 illustrates the MMLD instrument in use, measuring pH of secreted fluid from SLNs. FIG. 5 shows H&E images confirming that pH values correlate strongly with cancer cell percentage—nodes with >50% tumor infiltration consistently exhibited pH < 6.0.
