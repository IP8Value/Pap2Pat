# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

Malaria remains one of the most devastating infectious diseases worldwide, caused by protozoan parasites of the genus *Plasmodium*, with *Plasmodium falciparum* being the most lethal species in humans. The disease is transmitted through the bite of infected female *Anopheles* mosquitoes and is characterized by recurrent episodes of fever, chills, anemia, and, in severe cases, cerebral complications, organ failure, and death. According to the World Health Organization, malaria caused an estimated 216 million clinical cases and 655,000 deaths globally in 2011, with the majority of fatalities occurring in sub-Saharan Africa among children under five years of age. These statistics underscore the persistent and urgent need for an effective malaria vaccine capable of inducing durable, high-grade protection against infection and disease.

The impact of malaria extends beyond civilian populations, significantly affecting military personnel deployed to endemic regions. Historically, malaria has incapacitated more troops than combat injuries during numerous military campaigns, thereby compromising operational readiness and mission success. Despite advances in vector control, chemoprophylaxis, and treatment regimens, the emergence of drug-resistant parasite strains and insecticide-resistant mosquito vectors continues to challenge conventional malaria control strategies. Current prevention methods, including antimalarial drugs and bed nets, are often insufficient due to issues of compliance, logistical constraints, and incomplete efficacy. Moreover, these approaches do not confer long-term immunity and require continuous implementation, highlighting the critical need for a prophylactic vaccine that can provide sustained protection without repeated intervention.

Vaccine development for malaria has been pursued for decades, motivated by evidence that natural exposure or controlled immunization with whole parasites can induce sterile immunity. For instance, studies have demonstrated that immunization with irradiated *P. falciparum* sporozoites—delivered via bites of infected mosquitoes—can elicit complete protection in human volunteers, primarily mediated by cell-mediated immunity (CMI), particularly cytotoxic CD8+ T cells. These effector cells recognize parasite antigens presented on the surface of infected hepatocytes via MHC class I molecules and eliminate the intracellular liver-stage parasites before they mature and enter the bloodstream. This proof-of-concept supports the feasibility of a subunit vaccine designed to recapitulate such protective immune responses without the risks associated with live-parasite administration.

Current malaria vaccine efforts have largely focused on subunit approaches, with the most advanced candidate being RTS,S/AS01, a recombinant protein vaccine based on the circumsporozoite protein (CSP) of *P. falciparum*. While RTS,S has shown modest efficacy (~50%) in controlled human malaria infection trials, its protection is primarily antibody-mediated and does not effectively target the intracellular hepatic stages of the parasite, limiting its durability and breadth. Furthermore, RTS,S fails to induce robust CD8+ T cell responses, which are considered essential for eliminating liver-stage infection. Consequently, there is a compelling need for next-generation vaccines that can harness the power of CMI to achieve higher levels of sterile protection.

In this context, gene-based vaccine platforms—including DNA plasmids and viral vectors—have emerged as promising alternatives. These technologies facilitate endogenous expression of malaria antigens within host cells, leading to processing and presentation via the MHC class I pathway, thereby stimulating potent CD8+ T cell responses. Preclinical studies in mice and non-human primates have demonstrated that heterologous prime-boost regimens, wherein a DNA vaccine is used for priming and a viral vector (such as adenovirus) for boosting, can significantly enhance the magnitude and quality of cellular immune responses compared to homologous regimens. Early-phase human trials have corroborated these findings, showing that DNA/adenovirus prime-boost strategies can induce measurable T cell responses against malaria antigens.

However, current vaccine approaches face several limitations. First, many adenoviral vectors, particularly those based on human serotype 5 (Ad5), are subject to pre-existing neutralizing antibodies in the population due to prior natural exposure, which can blunt vaccine immunogenicity. Second, while adenoviral vectors are highly immunogenic, repeated administration is often ineffective due to anti-vector immunity. Third, achieving balanced immune responses against multiple parasite life stages—pre-erythrocytic (sporozoite and liver) and blood stages—remains a challenge. Finally, existing constructs often lack optimization for mammalian expression, such as codon usage, signal sequences, and promoter selection, which can limit antigen yield and immunogenicity.

To address these shortcomings, the field has turned to the development of immune-stimulatory conjugates and advanced vector engineering. DNA and viral-based vaccines offer modular platforms that can be tailored to express multiple antigens, incorporate immune-enhancing elements, and evade host immune surveillance. In particular, replication-deficient adenoviral vectors have been engineered with deletions in essential early genes (e.g., E1, E4) to enhance safety and accommodate larger transgenes, while modifications to capsid proteins enable targeted delivery to antigen-presenting cells and reduced immunogenicity against the vector itself. Despite these advances, there remains a clear and unmet need for improved methods to induce robust, durable, and broadly protective immune responses against malaria, particularly through the induction of CD8+ T cell-mediated immunity targeting both pre-erythrocytic and blood-stage antigens. The present invention fulfills this need by providing a novel multivalent adenoviral vector-based vaccine platform optimized for enhanced immunogenicity, safety, and efficacy against *Plasmodium falciparum* malaria.

## BRIEF SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a method for inducing a protective immune response against malaria in a mammalian subject, comprising administering a composition comprising a replication-deficient adenoviral vector encoding at least one *Plasmodium falciparum* antigen. The composition is formulated for parenteral administration and is designed to elicit robust cell-mediated immunity, particularly CD8+ T cell responses, against pre-erythrocytic and/or blood-stage malaria parasites. In a preferred embodiment, the method employs a heterologous prime-boost regimen, wherein the subject is first primed with a DNA plasmid encoding the same malaria antigen(s) and subsequently boosted with the adenoviral vector composition. The adenoviral vector is engineered to be replication-deficient through deletion of essential early regions such as E1 and E4, and may further include modifications to coat proteins to enhance targeting to dendritic cells or macrophages, reduce neutralization by pre-existing antibodies, and improve transduction efficiency. The encoded malaria antigens include, but are not limited to, circumsporozoite protein (CSP) and apical membrane antigen-1 (AMA1), which are codon-optimized for human expression and operably linked to strong promoters such as the human cytomegalovirus immediate-early (CMV IE) promoter. The invention further encompasses pharmaceutical compositions comprising the adenoviral vector, methods of manufacturing such vectors, and their use in vaccination protocols to prevent or mitigate *P. falciparum* infection.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

The present invention is directed to a multivalent vaccine platform designed to overcome the limitations of current malaria vaccine strategies by inducing potent and durable cell-mediated immune responses against multiple stages of the *Plasmodium falciparum* life cycle. Unlike traditional subunit vaccines that rely primarily on humoral immunity, the inventive approach leverages the capacity of gene-based vectors to drive endogenous antigen expression, thereby engaging the MHC class I presentation pathway and activating cytotoxic CD8+ T lymphocytes—key effectors in the elimination of intracellular liver-stage parasites. This strategy is particularly advantageous because it mimics the immune mechanisms observed in individuals protected by irradiated sporozoite immunization, offering a path toward high-grade sterile immunity.

A central component of the invention is a replication-deficient adenoviral vector, preferably derived from human adenovirus serotype 5 (Ad5), although alternative serotypes or chimeric vectors may be employed to circumvent pre-existing immunity. The adenoviral vector is rendered replication-deficient through precise genetic deletions in essential early gene regions, most commonly the E1 region, which encodes transactivators necessary for viral replication. Additional deletions in the E3 and/or E4 regions may be incorporated to increase cloning capacity, enhance safety, and modulate host immune responses. An E1/E3-deficient vector is standard, but multiply deficient constructs—such as E1/E4-deficient or E1/E3/E4-deficient vectors—are also encompassed, wherein the deletion endpoints are carefully defined to preserve vector integrity while eliminating functions required for productive infection. Such vectors are incapable of replicating in normal human cells but can be efficiently propagated in complementing cell lines that supply the missing E1 and/or E4 functions in trans.

The adenoviral genome is modified to include a heterologous nucleic acid sequence encoding at least one *Plasmodium falciparum* antigen, such as CSP or AMA1, both of which are expressed during the pre-erythrocytic stage and are critical for hepatocyte invasion. AMA1 is additionally expressed during the blood stage, offering potential dual-stage protection. The nucleic acid sequences are codon-optimized for mammalian expression to enhance translational efficiency and antigen yield. In some embodiments, the native signal sequences of these antigens are replaced with heterologous leader sequences, such as the human tissue plasminogen activator (TPA) signal peptide, to improve secretion and processing. The expression cassette is positioned within the E1-deleted region and is operably linked to a strong promoter, such as the human CMV immediate-early promoter, though alternative promoters—including ubiquitin, SV40, or inducible or tissue-specific promoters—may be used to fine-tune expression kinetics. A polyadenylation signal, such as the bovine growth hormone or SV40 polyA sequence, is included downstream of the transgene to ensure proper mRNA termination and stability.

To further enhance immunogenicity and targeting, the adenoviral coat proteins—particularly the fiber and penton base—are engineered to display non-native amino acid sequences that bind receptors on professional antigen-presenting cells (APCs). For example, an RGD motif may be inserted into the fiber knob domain to facilitate binding to integrins on dendritic cells and macrophages. Alternatively, sequences recognizing CD40, DC-SIGN, or other APC-specific surface proteins may be incorporated via spacer sequences of defined length (e.g., 5–20 amino acids) to ensure proper folding and accessibility. These chimeric coat proteins enable targeted delivery of the vector to immune cells, thereby amplifying antigen presentation and T cell priming. Moreover, to mitigate the problem of anti-vector immunity, the coat proteins may be diversified using directed evolution techniques: libraries of mutated fiber genes are generated, incorporated into adenoviral vectors, and subjected to competitive propagation under immune pressure, followed by screening for variants with reduced neutralization and enhanced transduction in the presence of pre-existing antibodies.

The inventive method is typically administered as part of a heterologous prime-boost regimen. The priming dose comprises a DNA plasmid encoding the same malaria antigen(s), delivered via intramuscular injection, optionally using needle-free jet injection or electroporation to enhance uptake. After an interval of 4–24 weeks, the subject receives a booster dose of the adenoviral vector composition, administered intramuscularly at a dose ranging from 1×10⁹ to 1×10¹¹ particle units. The composition may be formulated in a pharmaceutically acceptable carrier, with stabilizing agents such as sucrose, glycerol, or polysorbate 80 to protect vector integrity during storage and handling. Multiple boosts or alternating vector platforms (e.g., Ad5 followed by Ad26 or MVA) may be employed to sustain immunity.

Clinical evaluation of the NMRC-M3V-D/Ad-PfCA vaccine—a prototype of the invention—demonstrated 27% sterile protection in malaria-naïve volunteers following controlled human malaria infection. Protection was strongly correlated with IFN-γ-producing CD8+ T cells specific for AMA1, as measured by ELISpot and intracellular cytokine staining, with no significant contribution from antibodies. The regimen was well-tolerated, with predominantly mild adverse events. These results validate the inventive approach and support its advancement as a viable strategy for malaria vaccination, particularly in populations where pre-existing Ad5 immunity is low, such as infants in endemic areas. The platform is readily adaptable to include additional antigens, alternative serotypes, or adjuvanting cytokines, ensuring its continued relevance in the global fight against malaria.
