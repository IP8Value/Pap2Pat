## BACKGROUND

### Field

The present invention relates to the field of in vivo ultrasound imaging. More specifically, the invention provides improved compositions and methods for noninvasive, dynamic, and high-resolution imaging of biological processes in living subjects using genetically encoded acoustic reporter genes that produce gas vesicles capable of generating nonlinear ultrasound contrast.

### Description of the Related Art

Noninvasive imaging of biological processes in living organisms is essential for understanding disease mechanisms, monitoring therapeutic interventions, and advancing biomedical research. While various imaging modalities exist, each has significant limitations that restrict their utility in specific contexts. Optical imaging techniques, including fluorescence and bioluminescence imaging, offer high sensitivity and molecular specificity but suffer from poor tissue penetration due to light scattering and absorption, limiting their application to superficial tissues or requiring invasive procedures such as cranial windows for deep tissue imaging. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and positron emission tomography (PET) provide excellent depth penetration but lack the spatial resolution, temporal resolution, or cost-effectiveness needed for many research and clinical applications.

Ultrasound imaging represents a promising alternative that combines deep tissue penetration, high spatial and temporal resolution, real-time imaging capability, and widespread clinical availability at relatively low cost. However, conventional ultrasound imaging relies on endogenous tissue interfaces for contrast generation, which provides limited molecular or cellular specificity. To overcome this limitation, researchers have developed acoustic reporter genes (ARGs) based on gas vesicles (GVs)—hollow, gas-filled protein nanostructures naturally produced by certain aquatic microorganisms for buoyancy control. When expressed heterologously in target cells, these GVs can serve as genetically encodable ultrasound contrast agents.

First-generation ARGs demonstrated proof-of-concept for ultrasound-based gene expression reporting but exhibited significant limitations that hindered their practical utility. Bacterial ARGs derived from Anabaena flos-aquae and Bacillus megaterium showed poor expression at mammalian physiological temperatures (37°C), high metabolic burden on host cells, and crucially, produced only linear ultrasound contrast that was difficult to distinguish from background tissue signals. This necessitated the use of destructive imaging pulse sequences that irreversibly collapsed the GVs, preventing longitudinal monitoring of the same cell population over time. Similarly, first-generation mammalian ARGs suffered from low expression levels, required clonal selection of transduced cells, and depended on treatment with histone deacetylase inhibitors to maintain expression, severely limiting their applicability to diverse experimental systems.

These limitations of first-generation ARGs—poor expression characteristics, linear contrast generation, high metabolic burden, genetic instability, and requirement for destructive imaging—have significantly restricted their adoption in biological research and potential clinical applications. There remains a critical need for improved ARG systems that can produce strong nonlinear ultrasound contrast, enable nondestructive longitudinal imaging, exhibit robust expression in diverse host systems including both bacteria and mammalian cells, and demonstrate sufficient genetic stability for practical implementation in complex biological environments.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides compositions and methods that overcome the limitations of first-generation acoustic reporter genes by introducing second-generation ARG systems with dramatically improved properties for noninvasive in vivo imaging. The invention encompasses nucleic acid compositions encoding optimized gas vesicle polynucleotides derived from phylogenetically diverse sources, delivery compositions for introducing these nucleic acids into target cells, and engineered mammalian cells and probiotic bacterial cells expressing these optimized ARG systems.

The nucleic acid compositions of the invention include gas vesicle assembly genes and gas vesicle structural genes operably connected to promoters suitable for expression in the intended host system. In preferred embodiments, these compositions encode gas vesicle polypeptides that self-assemble into functional gas vesicles capable of producing strong nonlinear ultrasound contrast when expressed in mammalian cells or probiotic bacterial cells. The gas vesicle structural genes typically include multiple copies of gvpA encoding the primary structural protein, while the gas vesicle assembly genes include various combinations of gvpN, gvpJ, gvpK, gvpF, gvpG, gvpW, and gvpV that facilitate proper assembly of the gas vesicle structure.

The promoters operably connected to these gas vesicle genes are carefully selected based on the intended application and host system. For mammalian cell expression, the invention provides compositions utilizing inducible promoters such as tetracycline response elements, context-dependent promoters that respond to specific cellular states or signaling pathways, constitutive promoters for continuous expression, minimal promoters for reduced size, tissue-specific promoters for targeted expression, lineage-specific promoters for developmental studies, and ubiquitous promoters for broad expression patterns. Similarly, for bacterial expression, the compositions may employ inducible promoters such as the L-arabinose-inducible pBAD promoter, constitutive promoters, or other regulatory elements appropriate for the bacterial host.

Expression of these optimized ARG systems in mammalian cells enables nondestructive, longitudinal monitoring of gene expression patterns in deep tissues with high spatial resolution. The mammalian cells suitable for engineering with these ARG systems include various cancer cell lines such as human breast cancer cells (MDA-MB-231), immortalized cell lines (HEK293T), stem cells including embryonic and induced pluripotent stem cells, and therapeutic cells designed for cell-based therapies. The ability to engineer reporting therapeutic cells with these ARG systems allows for real-time monitoring of their location, viability, and function following administration to a subject.

Similarly, expression of these optimized ARG systems in probiotic bacterial cells, particularly tumor-homing bacteria such as Escherichia coli Nissle 1917 (EcN) and attenuated Salmonella enterica serovar Typhimurium, enables noninvasive tracking of bacterial colonization and distribution within tumors and other target sites. The specific bacterial strains engineered with these ARG systems can be used for monitoring bacterial-based cancer therapies, studying microbiome dynamics, or developing diagnostic probiotics for gastrointestinal conditions.

The nonlinear ultrasound contrast generated by these second-generation ARG systems represents a significant improvement over first-generation systems. Unlike linear contrast agents that produce signals proportional to the incident acoustic pressure, nonlinear contrast agents generate harmonic frequencies and other nonlinear signal components that can be specifically detected and separated from background tissue signals using specialized pulse sequences such as cross-propagating amplitude modulation (xAM). This nonlinear behavior enables high-contrast-to-noise ratio imaging without destroying the contrast agent, allowing for repeated imaging sessions over extended periods to monitor dynamic biological processes.

The invention further provides methods for treating or preventing various diseases and disorders by administering compositions containing these engineered cells or nucleic acid delivery systems. These methods are particularly applicable to cancer treatment, where tumor-homing bacteria expressing ARG systems can deliver therapeutic payloads while simultaneously enabling monitoring of their tumor colonization. The methods also extend to metabolic diseases, where engineered therapeutic cells can be tracked following administration to ensure proper engraftment and function.

Dynamic non-destructive imaging using these ARG systems enables unprecedented capabilities for monitoring biological processes in living subjects. The nonlinear ultrasound imaging methods involve applying ultrasound pulses at specific acoustic pressures that generate detectable nonlinear signals from the gas vesicles while minimizing their collapse. By carefully controlling parameters such as peak positive pressure, pulse repetition frequency, and imaging duration, researchers can obtain high-quality images with excellent contrast-to-noise ratios while preserving the gas vesicles for subsequent imaging sessions.

The enhanced performance characteristics of these second-generation ARG systems include significantly improved contrast-to-noise ratios compared to first-generation systems, reduced metabolic burden on host cells leading to better growth rates and viability, robust GV expression even under challenging in vivo conditions, and exceptional genetic stability over multiple generations. The nucleic acid compositions have been optimized through codon optimization, removal of non-essential genes, and strategic arrangement of gene cassettes to maximize expression efficiency and minimize construct size.

Particularly notable improvements include the development of tandem gene expression elements that enable coordinated expression of multiple gas vesicle genes from a single promoter, careful optimization of the stoichiometry between structural (GVS) and assembly (GVA) genes to maximize gas vesicle production, and engineering of GvpC variants or complete omission of GvpC to enhance nonlinear ultrasound contrast properties. The absence of GvpC or use of modified GvpC variants results in gas vesicles with distinctive mechanical and acoustic properties, including lower collapse pressure thresholds and enhanced nonlinear scattering characteristics.

The invention also encompasses various delivery strategies for introducing these ARG systems into target cells, including lipid-based carriers, viral vectors (such as lentiviral and adeno-associated viral vectors), transposable elements (such as PiggyBac transposon systems), and plasmid-based delivery systems incorporating stability elements such as toxin-antitoxin cassettes. These delivery compositions are designed to ensure efficient transfer and stable maintenance of the ARG constructs in the target cells, whether administered in vitro or in vivo.

The methods of the invention enable detection of unique cell states and types based on their specific gene expression profiles. By linking ARG expression to context-dependent promoters that respond to specific cellular conditions—such as aberrant signaling pathways, cell proliferation states, cell stress responses, metabolic activity, or cell-cell interactions—researchers can visualize and monitor specific biological processes in real time. Endogenous context-dependent promoters can be exploited to report on physiological states, pathological states, or specific disease conditions, providing valuable insights into disease mechanisms and treatment responses.

The comprehensive nature of this invention, encompassing optimized nucleic acid compositions, engineered cellular systems, advanced delivery methods, and sophisticated imaging protocols, establishes a powerful platform for noninvasive in vivo imaging that addresses the critical limitations of previous approaches and opens new possibilities for biological research and clinical applications.

### Acoustic Reporter Genes for Nondestructive In Vivo Imaging

Noninvasive in vivo imaging of biological processes represents one of the most significant challenges in modern biomedical research. Traditional optical imaging techniques are fundamentally limited by light scattering and absorption in biological tissues, restricting their utility to superficial structures or requiring invasive procedures for deep tissue access. Ultrasound imaging offers a compelling alternative that combines deep tissue penetration, high spatial and temporal resolution, real-time imaging capability, and widespread clinical availability at relatively low cost. However, conventional ultrasound lacks molecular specificity, relying solely on endogenous tissue interfaces for contrast generation.

First-generation acoustic reporter genes (ARGs) based on gas vesicles (GVs) demonstrated the feasibility of genetically encodable ultrasound contrast but suffered from severe limitations that restricted their practical utility. Bacterial ARGs exhibited poor expression at mammalian physiological temperatures (37°C), high metabolic burden on host cells, and critically, produced only linear ultrasound contrast that was difficult to distinguish from background tissue signals. This necessitated destructive imaging approaches that irreversibly collapsed the GVs, preventing longitudinal monitoring of the same cell population over time. Similarly, first-generation mammalian ARGs required clonal selection and epigenetic modulators to maintain expression, severely limiting their applicability.

The present invention introduces second-generation ARG systems that overcome these limitations through systematic optimization of gas vesicle gene clusters identified through phylogenetic mining of diverse GV-producing organisms. Second-generation mammalian ARGs (mARGs) demonstrate dramatically improved acoustic properties, including strong nonlinear ultrasound contrast generation that enables nondestructive imaging, and enhanced expression characteristics that eliminate the need for clonal selection or epigenetic modulators. These improvements enable in situ gene expression imaging with unprecedented sensitivity and specificity in deep tissues.

Similarly, second-generation bacterial ARGs (bARGs) exhibit superior acoustic properties, robust expression at physiological temperatures, and minimal metabolic burden, enabling long-term monitoring of bacterial populations in complex in vivo environments. The bARG systems have been successfully demonstrated in probiotic bacterial cells, particularly tumor-homing strains such as Escherichia coli Nissle 1917 (EcN) and attenuated Salmonella enterica serovar Typhimurium, enabling noninvasive tracking of bacterial colonization and distribution within tumors.

The compositions of the invention include nucleic acid compositions encoding optimized gas vesicle polynucleotides, delivery compositions for introducing these nucleic acids into target cells, engineered mammalian cells expressing mARG systems, and probiotic bacterial cells expressing bARG systems. The gas vesicle assembly genes include various combinations of gvpN, gvpJ, gvpK, gvpF, gvpG, gvpW, and gvpV that facilitate proper assembly of functional gas vesicles. The gas vesicle structural genes primarily consist of multiple copies of gvpA encoding the primary structural protein that forms the gas vesicle shell.

Nonlinear ultrasound contrast production is achieved through careful engineering of the gas vesicle composition and structure. Particularly important is the strategic omission or modification of gvpC, which encodes a reinforcing protein that stabilizes the gas vesicle shell but reduces nonlinear scattering properties. Gas vesicles lacking GvpC or containing modified GvpC variants exhibit enhanced deformation in response to acoustic pressure, resulting in stronger nonlinear signal generation that can be specifically detected using specialized pulse sequences such as cross-propagating amplitude modulation (xAM).

The probiotic bacterial cells of the invention include tumor-homing bacteria such as EcN and attenuated Salmonella strains that naturally accumulate in the hypoxic and necrotic regions of solid tumors. By engineering these bacteria to express optimized bARG systems, researchers can noninvasively monitor their tumor colonization dynamics, distribution patterns, and persistence over time. This capability is particularly valuable for developing and optimizing bacterial-based cancer therapies.

The mammalian cells suitable for engineering with mARG systems include various cancer cell lines such as human breast cancer cells (MDA-MB-231), immortalized cell lines (HEK293T), stem cells including embryonic and induced pluripotent stem cells, and therapeutic cells designed for cell-based therapies. Reporting therapeutic cells engineered with mARG systems enable real-time monitoring of their location, viability, and function following administration to a subject, providing critical information for optimizing cell therapy protocols.

The diseases and disorders that can be studied or treated using these ARG systems include various cancers, metabolic diseases, inflammatory conditions, and other pathological states where monitoring cellular dynamics is beneficial. Engineered gas-filled protein structures (GVPS) serve as the foundation for these imaging capabilities, with gas vesicle structural proteins (GVS) forming the primary shell components. The GVS shell composition has been optimized through phylogenetic screening and genetic engineering to maximize nonlinear contrast generation while minimizing metabolic burden.

Gas vesicles are defined as hollow, gas-filled protein nanostructures that range from 45-250 nm in diameter and 100-700 nm in length, with cylindrical bodies and conical end caps. The primary structural protein, GvpA, forms a rigid β-sheet structure that provides the gas-impermeable barrier essential for buoyancy function. The GvpC protein, when present, wraps around the exterior of the gas vesicle cylinder, providing mechanical reinforcement that increases the collapse pressure threshold. However, for ultrasound imaging applications, the absence or modification of GvpC enhances nonlinear scattering properties by allowing greater deformation of the gas vesicle shell in response to acoustic pressure.

The acoustic properties of gas vesicles are characterized by their collapse pressure profile, which defines the relationship between applied acoustic pressure and the fraction of gas vesicles that collapse. The midpoint collapse pressure represents the pressure at which 50% of the gas vesicles have collapsed, while the initial collapse pressure indicates the threshold at which collapse begins. These parameters can be tuned through genetic engineering to optimize imaging performance for specific applications.

Hybrid gas vesicle gene clusters have been developed by combining elements from different phylogenetic sources to achieve optimal expression and acoustic properties. The gas vesicle gene clusters have been systematically engineered to remove non-essential genes, optimize gene stoichiometry, and incorporate regulatory elements appropriate for the target host system. This engineering approach has resulted in ARG systems with dramatically improved performance compared to first-generation constructs.

Multiplexed imaging capabilities are enabled by engineering gas vesicles with different collapse pressure profiles, allowing discrimination between multiple cell populations based on their differential response to acoustic pressure. Dynamic non-destructive imaging is achieved through the use of nonlinear ultrasound imaging techniques that specifically detect harmonic frequencies and other nonlinear signal components generated by the gas vesicles, enabling high-contrast-to-noise ratio imaging without destroying the contrast agent.

The nucleic acid compositions have been optimized through codon optimization for the target host system, strategic arrangement of gene cassettes using tandem expression elements, and incorporation of transcript stabilization elements such as WPRE and hGH polyA signals. Promoter selection is critical for achieving appropriate expression patterns, with options including inducible promoters (such as tetracycline response elements), context-dependent promoters that respond to specific cellular states, constitutive promoters for continuous expression, tissue-specific promoters for targeted expression, and ubiquitous promoters for broad expression patterns.

Context-dependent promoter activity enables detection of unique cell states and types based on their specific gene expression profiles or signaling pathway activation. Endogenous context-dependent promoters can report on physiological states, pathological states, or specific disease conditions by linking ARG expression to cellular processes such as aberrant signaling, cell proliferation, cell stress, or metabolic activity. Signal transduction pathways that can be monitored include those involved in cancer progression, inflammatory responses, metabolic regulation, and developmental processes.

The delivery compositions include various vector systems such as plasmids incorporating toxin-antitoxin stability cassettes (such as Axe-Txe) for antibiotic-free maintenance, viral vectors (including lentiviral and adeno-associated viral vectors) for efficient transduction, and transposable elements (such as PiggyBac transposon systems) for genomic integration. Lipid-based carriers and other non-viral delivery methods are also encompassed within the invention for introducing ARG constructs into target cells.

The engineered bacterial cells demonstrate exceptional genetic stability, with mutation rates affecting ARG function occurring at frequencies less than 0.3% over approximately 35 cell generations. This stability is achieved through careful construct design, inclusion of stability elements, and optimization of expression levels to minimize metabolic burden. Secondary gas vesicle polynucleotides can be incorporated to create gas vesicles with selectable buckling and collapse pressure profiles, enabling multiplexed imaging applications.

Contrast enhanced imaging using these ARG systems involves the application of specialized ultrasound pulse sequences that specifically detect nonlinear signal components generated by the gas vesicles. Nonlinear signals arise from the nonlinear relationship between applied acoustic pressure and the resulting scattering response of the gas vesicles, producing harmonic frequencies and other nonlinear components that can be separated from linear background signals using signal processing algorithms.

Ultrasound imaging involves the application of sound waves to a target site and detection of the resulting echoes to create images of internal structures. The physics of sound wave propagation and interaction with acoustic biomolecules such as gas vesicles underlies the imaging capabilities of the invention. Applying ultrasound involves transmitting acoustic pulses at specific frequencies, amplitudes, and durations to generate detectable signals from the gas vesicles while minimizing their destruction.

The imaging process involves sound energy production by an ultrasound transducer, transmission of this energy through tissue, reflection and scattering at interfaces and contrast agents, and detection of the returning echoes to create images. Conventional ultrasound imaging systems have been adapted to implement specialized pulse sequences such as xAM and BURST that optimize detection of nonlinear signals from gas vesicles. The imaging step involves acquiring multiple image frames at different acoustic pressures to extract time-series data for each pixel location.

Ultrasound signal frequencies typically range from 1-20 MHz for small animal imaging and 2-15 MHz for human applications, with higher frequencies providing better resolution but reduced penetration depth. Collapsing and imaging ultrasound pressures are carefully controlled to balance signal strength against gas vesicle preservation, with typical imaging pressures ranging from 0.3-1.3 MPa depending on the specific application and gas vesicle type.

Nonlinear ultrasound imaging involves applying ultrasound pulses with specific characteristics designed to maximize nonlinear signal generation while minimizing gas vesicle collapse. Detecting scattering of ultrasound pulses involves measuring the amplitude and phase of returning echoes at multiple frequencies to separate linear and nonlinear components. Applying ultrasound to the target site involves positioning the transducer appropriately and adjusting imaging parameters based on the depth and characteristics of the target tissue.

Increasing peak positive pressure in successive imaging frames enables characterization of the collapse pressure profile of the gas vesicles, providing additional information about their mechanical properties and concentration. Imaging the target site in successive frames allows extraction of time-series vectors for each pixel, which can be processed using signal separation algorithms to enhance contrast and reduce noise. The hiBURST and loBURST regimes refer to different acoustic pressure ranges that provide complementary information about gas vesicle presence and concentration.

Single-cell imaging capabilities are achieved through high-frequency ultrasound transducers and optimized imaging parameters, while large volume imaging in deep tissue is enabled by lower frequency transducers and specialized beamforming techniques. US imaging with spatiotemporal resolution provides dynamic information about biological processes as they unfold in living subjects, enabling longitudinal studies of disease progression and treatment response.

The target sites for imaging include various diseases and disorders associated with specific tumor antigens or pathological processes. Cancer types that can be studied include breast cancer, colorectal cancer, lung cancer, prostate cancer, and many others where monitoring tumor dynamics is beneficial. Performing biopsies guided by US images enables precise sampling of specific regions within tumors based on their gene expression profiles, facilitating molecular characterization of heterogeneous tumor populations.

Diseases of the gastrointestinal (GI) tract that can be studied include inflammatory bowel diseases such as Crohn's disease and ulcerative colitis, as well as various cancers affecting different sections of the GI tract including the esophagus, stomach, small intestine, colon, and rectum. Target sites within the GI tract can be imaged using specialized transducers and delivery methods appropriate for the specific anatomical location.

Administering transactivator-binding compounds such as doxycycline enables inducible control of ARG expression in systems utilizing tetracycline response elements. Various modes of administration are possible including oral, intravenous, intraperitoneal, subcutaneous, and local delivery, with the choice depending on the specific application and target tissue. The period of time between administration of the inducing compound and application of ultrasound imaging is optimized based on the kinetics of ARG expression, typically ranging from several hours to several days.

Administering additional agents alongside the ARG-expressing cells or nucleic acid compositions can enhance therapeutic efficacy or provide complementary information. ## EXAMPLES

### Example 1

The development of next-generation acoustic reporter genes (ARGs) represents a critical advancement in noninvasive in vivo imaging technology. Recognizing the limitations of first-generation ARG systems—including poor expression at physiological temperatures, high metabolic burden, linear contrast generation requiring destructive imaging, and genetic instability—we hypothesized that a systematic genomic mining approach could identify gas vesicle (GV) gene clusters with superior properties for heterologous expression in both bacterial and mammalian hosts.

To test this hypothesis, we conducted a comprehensive phylogenetic screen of GV-producing organisms, cloning and expressing 15 distinct polycistronic operons from diverse taxonomic groups in Escherichia coli. This approach, previously successful in improving fluorescent proteins, opsins, Cas proteins, and other biotechnology tools, leveraged the natural diversity of GV gene clusters to identify candidates with enhanced expression characteristics and acoustic properties. Of the 15 gene clusters tested, only three showed substantial nonlinear ultrasound contrast when expressed at 37°C, with five showing substantial signal at 30°C, highlighting the stringent requirements for functional heterologous expression.

From this screen, we identified two particularly promising candidates: a GV gene cluster from Serratia sp. 39006 and another from Anabaena flos-aquae. The Serratia cluster produced the strongest ultrasound signals, demonstrating a 9-fold gain in signal intensity (19.2 dB) compared to the next brightest cluster under xAM imaging conditions. We adapted this bacterial ARG from Serratia sp. 39006 (designated bARGSer) for expression in the probiotic bacterium E. coli Nissle 1917 (EcN), a strain known for its ability to colonize tumors and persist in the gastrointestinal tract. This engineered system enabled noninvasive ultrasound imaging of probiotic agents colonizing tumors at depths greater than 1 cm, providing direct visualization of the microscale in vivo distribution of these potential anti-cancer therapeutic agents.

Simultaneously, we adapted the mammalian ARG from A. flos-aquae (designated mARGAna) for expression in human breast cancer cells. This system enabled both noninvasive, in situ microscale imaging and long-term monitoring of heterologous gene expression in developing orthotopic tumors. Critically, the mARGAna system facilitated ultrasound-guided biopsy of genetically defined subpopulations of tumor cells, demonstrating a clinically relevant application that was previously impossible with destructive imaging approaches.

The properties and performance of these second-generation ARGs represent dramatic improvements over first-generation systems. Through our genomic mining approach, we selected 11 representative species from a phylogenetic tree of 288 GV-producing organisms, providing broad sampling of phylogenetic space, cluster architecture, and organismal characteristics. We cloned and expressed these GV operons in E. coli, comparing their acoustic performance using both xAM (nonlinear, nondestructive) and BURST (linear, destructive) imaging modalities, as well as optical methods such as phase-contrast microscopy (PCM) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM).

The Serratia cluster emerged as the clear leader in acoustic performance, prompting us to optimize this construct through systematic deletion of non-essential genes. Testing deletions of gvpC, gvpH, gvpW, gvpX, gvpY, gvpZ, and an unannotated hypothetical protein (Ser39006_001280), we found that deletion of Ser39006_001280 increased both xAM signal and optical opacity, leading us to select the Serratia ΔSer39006_001280 operon (bARGSer) for subsequent experiments.

We transferred bARGSer into E. coli Nissle 1917 (EcN) and tested various inducible promoter architectures, ultimately selecting the L-arabinose-inducible pBAD promoter for its robust control over GV expression without obvious metabolic burden at 37°C. To enable plasmid maintenance without antibiotic selection—a requirement for certain in vivo applications—we incorporated the toxin-antitoxin stability cassette Axe-Txe, which allowed the plasmid to be maintained in EcN for up to 5 days of daily subculturing in liquid media without antibiotics.

Characterization of bARGSer-expressing EcN revealed that the metabolic burden was comparable to that of widely used fluorescent proteins, with similar growth rates during log phase and only modest reductions in colony-forming unit counts upon induction. Genetic stability testing demonstrated exceptional retention of functional ARG expression, with less than 0.3% of colonies exhibiting mutations that disrupted GV production after approximately 35 cell generations.

Acoustic characterization showed that bARGSer-expressing EcN produced detectable xAM signal at acoustic pressures above 0.72 MPa, with optimal imaging achieved at 1.29 MPa for xAM and 1.02 MPa for parabolic AM (pAM) to balance signal strength against GV collapse. Sensitivity testing revealed detection limits of 10^7 cells per milliliter using nondestructive xAM imaging and 10^5 cells per milliliter using destructive BURST imaging, representing significant improvements over first-generation systems.

In vivo validation demonstrated successful imaging of tumor-colonizing bacteria in a mouse model of subcutaneous MC26 tumors. Following intravenous injection of bARGSer-expressing EcN, we observed specific ultrasound contrast localized to the tumor core and interface between live and necrotic tissue—one day after L-arabinose induction. Critically, we demonstrated the ability to collapse GVs with high-pressure ultrasound, re-induce expression, and re-image the same tumors, confirming the nondestructive nature of our imaging approach and enabling longitudinal monitoring of bacterial colonization dynamics.

For the mammalian ARG system, we addressed the limitations of first-generation mARGMega (based on Bacillus megaterium) by testing the A. flos-aquae gene cluster in HEK293T cells. Recognizing that the native A. flos-aquae operon contains multiple gvpA copies, we hypothesized that expressing GvpA at higher stoichiometry relative to assembly factors could improve GV expression. Titration experiments confirmed this hypothesis, showing monotonic increases in BURST signal with increasing gvpA up to eight-fold excess.

Crucially, we discovered that omitting gvpC from our mammalian co-transfection mixture markedly enhanced nonlinear ultrasound contrast in xAM imaging, peaking at a transmit pressure of approximately 0.6 MPa. At this pressure, the A. flos-aquae gene cluster without GvpC produced a 38.8-fold stronger xAM signal compared to the B. megaterium construct. This finding highlighted the importance of GvpC modification or omission for achieving strong nonlinear contrast in mammalian systems.

We constructed a convenient polycistronic plasmid system (mARGAna) linking the assembly factor genes gvpNJKFGWV through P2A co-translational cleavage elements, with gvpA supplied on a separate plasmid to enable stoichiometric tuning. Both transcripts were driven by CMV promoters and labeled with fluorescent reporters (IRES-BFP for gvpA and P2A-GFP for assembly factors) to facilitate analysis and sorting.

Stable cancer cell lines expressing mARGAna were engineered in human breast cancer cells (MDA-MB-231) using PiggyBac transposon-mediated genomic integration under a doxycycline-inducible TRE promoter. These cells showed xAM contrast after a single day of doxycycline induction, with signals increasing through day 6. Similar results were obtained with mouse 3T3 fibroblasts and human HuH7 hepatocytes, demonstrating the broad applicability of the mARGAna system across different mammalian cell types.

In vivo validation in an orthotopic breast cancer model demonstrated bright and specific xAM contrast in doxycycline-induced tumors starting from day 4, with signals absent in uninduced controls. Three-dimensional visualization revealed spatial patterns of gene expression that correlated precisely with viable tumor regions, being absent outside tumor boundaries and within necrotic cores. This microscale resolution surpassed the capabilities of whole-body fluorescence imaging, which lacked spatial information about gene expression distribution within tumors.

The clinical relevance of our mARGAna system was demonstrated through real-time ultrasound-guided biopsy of chimeric tumors containing adjacent regions of mARGAna-expressing and control cells. Fine needle aspiration biopsies targeted to xAM-positive or xAM-negative regions successfully obtained cells with the expected mARGAna positivity or negativity, as confirmed by flow cytometry. This capability represents a significant advance in precision medicine, enabling molecularly guided tissue sampling based on real-time gene expression imaging.

The advantages of these second-generation ARGs are manifold. For bacterial applications (bARGs), the systems enable noninvasive tracking of therapeutic bacteria as they home to and proliferate in tumors or other target organs, providing critical feedback on therapy design and execution. For mammalian applications (mARGs), the systems facilitate visualization of tissue growth and viability, precise sampling under ultrasound guidance, and tracking of therapeutic cells such as T cells or stem cells.

While current ARG systems exhibit expression kinetics on the order of one day—which is acceptable for many applications such as tumor growth monitoring and stem cell expansion—future improvements could focus on accelerating expression through pre-expression of assembly factors and conditional expression of structural proteins. Additional enhancements might include multiplexing different "colors" of ARGs through engineering of GvpC variants with distinct pressure responses, reducing construct size for easier cloning and viral packaging, and consolidating multi-plasmid systems into single vectors to improve transfection efficiency.

Despite these opportunities for further optimization, the second-generation ARG systems described herein represent a transformative advance in noninvasive in vivo imaging technology. By enabling sensitive, high-resolution visualization of cellular function inside intact mammals without the depth limitations of optical methods or the cost and complexity of nuclear imaging or MRI, these ARG systems establish ultrasound as a premier modality for longitudinal biological studies and clinical applications.
