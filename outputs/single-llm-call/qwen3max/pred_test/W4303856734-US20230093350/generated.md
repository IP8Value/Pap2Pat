# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates generally to the field of non-invasive optical spectroscopy for monitoring tissue and organ function. More specifically, the invention pertains to a novel apparatus and method for simultaneously measuring hemodynamic and metabolic parameters in biological tissues using super-continuum laser-based infrared spectroscopy. The invention enables the concurrent assessment of oxygenated and deoxygenated hemoglobin concentrations alongside the redox state of cytochrome c oxidase (CCO), thereby providing a comprehensive evaluation of both oxygen delivery and cellular metabolic activity. This technology is particularly applicable in clinical settings where real-time monitoring of cerebral or organ metabolism and oxygenation is critical, such as in the management of traumatic brain injury, stroke, hemorrhagic shock, and other conditions involving compromised tissue perfusion or metabolic dysfunction. The invention further encompasses portable, cart-based implementations suitable for use in emergency departments, operating rooms, intensive care units, and pre-hospital environments, offering a practical, point-of-care solution for continuous metabolic and hemodynamic monitoring.

## BACKGROUND

Tissue oxygenation is a fundamental physiological parameter that reflects the balance between oxygen delivery and consumption at the cellular level. Adequate oxygenation is essential for aerobic metabolism, which generates adenosine triphosphate (ATP), the primary energy currency of cells. In the brain, disruptions in oxygen supply can lead to rapid neuronal dysfunction and irreversible damage, making the monitoring of cerebral oxygenation a critical aspect of neurocritical care. Functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) has emerged as a powerful tool for assessing brain activity by detecting changes in blood oxygenation, known as the blood oxygen level-dependent (BOLD) signal. While fMRI provides high spatial resolution and whole-brain coverage, it is limited by high cost, immobility, and the inability to provide continuous monitoring, rendering it unsuitable for bedside or emergency use.

Functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS) offers a more accessible alternative by measuring changes in the oxidation state of hemoglobin through the detection of subtle variations in the optical absorption properties of oxygenated hemoglobin (HbO) and deoxygenated hemoglobin (HbR). fNIRS systems are compact, silent, portable, and compatible with other medical devices, making them ideal for bedside monitoring. However, a core limitation of all hemodynamic neuroimaging techniques, including fNIRS and fMRI, is that they measure changes in blood flow and oxygenation as indirect correlates of neural activity, rather than directly assessing the metabolic state of the neural tissue itself. This limitation becomes particularly significant in pathological conditions such as traumatic brain injury or ischemia, where neuronal dysfunction may occur without corresponding alterations in regional hemodynamics, leading to potential misinterpretation of the patient's true metabolic status.

To address this gap, there has been growing interest in measuring optically active markers of cellular metabolism, with cytochrome c oxidase (CCO) being a prime candidate. CCO is the terminal enzyme in the mitochondrial electron transport chain and is responsible for more than 95% of oxygen metabolism in the body. It catalyzes the reduction of molecular oxygen to water, a process that is tightly coupled to ATP production. The enzyme exists in two interconvertible states—oxidized and reduced—and the ratio of these states, known as the redox state, serves as a direct indicator of mitochondrial metabolic activity. Changes in the CCO redox state can therefore reflect whether mitochondria are able to meet cellular energy demands under varying conditions of oxygen availability.

Accurate measurement of the CCO redox state is of immense clinical importance, as it could enable early detection of metabolic dysfunction before structural damage becomes evident on conventional imaging. For instance, in hemorrhagic shock or traumatic brain injury, derangements in cellular metabolism may precede systemic hemodynamic changes or radiographic abnormalities, offering a time-sensitive window for intervention. Simultaneous measurement of CCO alongside HbO and HbR would provide complementary information on both oxygen delivery (hemodynamics) and oxygen utilization (metabolism), significantly enhancing diagnostic and prognostic capabilities.

Despite its potential, the measurement of CCO in vivo presents significant technical challenges. The in vivo concentration of CCO is only 5–10% that of hemoglobin, and its absorption spectrum overlaps with those of HbO and HbR, leading to substantial cross-talk and signal contamination. Moreover, the redox-sensitive absorption peak of CCO—primarily associated with the copper A center—is broad and featureless in the near-infrared (NIR) range (700–900 nm), making it difficult to distinguish from background signals. These factors result in a very low signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) for CCO measurements, often burying the CCO signal within noise from hemodynamic fluctuations, skin interference, and motion artifacts.

Prior art in CCO measurement has largely relied on conventional light sources such as tungsten-halogen lamps or light-emitting diodes (LEDs). These sources suffer from low brightness and poor spatial coherence, limiting the amount of light that can be efficiently coupled into delivery fibers and subsequently into tissue. As a result, the detected signal is weak, and the SNR is insufficient for reliable, real-time CCO monitoring in clinical settings. Some research systems have employed mode-locked laser-based super-continuum sources, but these are complex, expensive, and not well-suited for portable or ruggedized clinical deployment.

Consequently, there remains a critical unmet need for an accurate, non-invasive method to measure the CCO redox state with high fidelity. Such a method must overcome the limitations of prior art by providing sufficient brightness, spectral coverage, and SNR to resolve the subtle CCO signal amidst strong hemoglobin absorption and environmental noise. Furthermore, the system should be capable of simultaneous, real-time measurement of both hemodynamic and metabolic parameters, be portable and robust for use in diverse clinical environments, and operate within established safety limits for human exposure to optical radiation. The present invention addresses these needs through the integration of a novel, all-fiber super-continuum laser source with advanced optical and signal processing techniques, enabling unprecedented sensitivity and specificity in the non-invasive monitoring of tissue metabolism and oxygenation.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides an apparatus and method for non-invasively measuring hemodynamic and metabolic function in biological tissues, with particular emphasis on the simultaneous assessment of oxygenated hemoglobin (HbO), deoxygenated hemoglobin (HbR), and the redox state of cytochrome c oxidase (CCO). The core of the invention is a high-brightness, all-fiber integrated super-continuum laser (SCL) that serves as the light source for broadband infrared spectroscopy. This SCL is engineered to generate a stable, high-power output spanning the near-infrared (NIR) and short-wave infrared (SWIR) spectrum, specifically covering the 670 nm to 2500 nm range, which encompasses the key absorption features of HbO, HbR, and CCO.

The SCL is configured in a master oscillator power amplifier (MOPA) architecture, wherein a seed laser emitting nanosecond pulses is amplified through a multi-stage fiber amplifier system. The first stage is optimized for low noise, while the second stage employs cladding-pumped fibers to minimize nonlinear distortions at high power levels. The amplified pulses are then launched into a cascade of nonlinear fibers, where modulational instability initiates the generation of a super-continuum spectrum through mechanisms such as self-phase modulation, four-wave mixing, and the Raman effect. This design yields a spectrally flat, high-brightness output with excellent power stability and spatial coherence, enabling efficient coupling into optical delivery systems.

The apparatus further includes a specialized probe configuration comprising a sample arm and a reference arm arranged in a dual-arm differential measurement setup. The probe is designed for contact with the tissue of interest, with adjustable spacing between the light delivery and collection fibers to optimize penetration depth and signal quality. The reference arm is used to monitor and compensate for fluctuations in the SCL output and environmental disturbances, thereby enhancing measurement stability.

Light reflected from the tissue and the reference path is collected by matched photodetectors, and the resulting signals are processed using a lock-in amplification technique synchronized to an optical chopper. This approach dramatically improves the signal-to-noise ratio by rejecting ambient light and other out-of-phase noise sources. Additionally, a polarizer is incorporated into the optical path to ensure a single polarization state, mitigating noise arising from polarization-dependent components and birefringence in the tissue or fibers.

The detected signals are digitized and processed by a computing system executing machine-readable instructions that implement a modified Beer-Lambert law algorithm. This algorithm deconvolves the wavelength-dependent attenuation data to determine the absolute or relative concentration changes of HbO, HbR, and CCO. By analyzing the temporal dynamics and correlations among these three parameters, the system can determine the metabolic state of the tissue (e.g., whether ATP demand is being met), the oxygenation state (e.g., presence of hypoxia or hyperoxia), and overall organ or tissue functionality. The dual-arm differential configuration, combined with the high brightness of the SCL and the lock-in detection scheme, collectively serve to improve the signal-to-noise ratio by orders of magnitude compared to prior art, enabling reliable, real-time monitoring of CCO—a feat previously unattainable in clinical practice.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention introduces advanced techniques for the non-invasive measurement of metabolic tissue state and tissue oxygenation, leveraging the unique biochemical and optical properties of key endogenous chromophores. Central to this approach is the measurement of cytochrome c oxidase (CCO), the terminal enzyme in the mitochondrial electron transport chain, which plays a pivotal role in cellular respiration and ATP synthesis. CCO catalyzes the four-electron reduction of molecular oxygen to water, a process that is directly coupled to the generation of the proton gradient driving ATP production. The enzyme cycles between oxidized and reduced states during this catalytic cycle, and the ratio of these states—the redox state—serves as a sensitive indicator of the balance between cellular energy demand and the availability of oxygen and reducing equivalents. An increase in ATP utilization, such as during neuronal activation, leads to a higher rate of electron flow through the chain, resulting in a greater proportion of CCO in the reduced state and thus a decrease in the measured oxidized CCO signal.

Concurrently, the invention measures tissue oxygenation by quantifying the concentrations of oxygenated hemoglobin (HbO) and deoxygenated hemoglobin (HbR). Hemoglobin, contained within red blood cells, is the primary oxygen carrier in the blood, and its oxidation state reflects the adequacy of oxygen delivery to the tissue. By monitoring both HbO and HbR, the system can assess total hemoglobin concentration (a surrogate for blood volume) and tissue oxygen saturation. The correlation or dis-correlation between CCO and hemoglobin signals provides profound physiological insights: for example, a rise in HbO accompanied by a decrease in oxidized CCO during cognitive tasks indicates increased oxygen delivery meeting heightened metabolic demand, whereas a drop in HbO with a concomitant decrease in CCO suggests ischemia where oxygen supply fails to meet even baseline metabolic needs.

A key advantage of the present invention over conventional fNIRS techniques is its ability to distinguish the CCO signal from confounding factors such as hemodynamic responses, skin interference, and motion artifacts. This is achieved through a combination of high spectral brightness, differential measurement geometry, and advanced signal processing. The invention employs a super-continuum laser infrared spectroscopy system specifically designed for the non-invasive interrogation of the redox state of the CCO enzyme. The light source is an all-fiber integrated super-continuum laser (SCL) that generates a broadband output from 670 nm to 2500 nm with a time-averaged power of up to 11 W. This high brightness—nearly an order of magnitude greater than conventional lamp sources in a fiber-coupled configuration—ensures sufficient photon flux to penetrate tissue and return a detectable signal even from the weakly absorbing CCO.

The all-fiber design inherently reduces motion artifacts, as there are no free-space optical alignments to disturb during subject movement. Moreover, the system enables the simultaneous measurement of HbO and HbR across their distinct absorption spectra, providing a comprehensive hemodynamic picture alongside metabolic data. The signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) is further enhanced by deploying a pulsed SCL source in conjunction with a synchronized detection system. Although the current embodiment uses a mechanical spectrometer for wavelength selection, future implementations may employ acousto-optic tunable filters to achieve sub-second acquisition times, minimizing motion-induced corruption. The high brightness and diffraction-limited beam quality of the SCL also facilitate efficient coupling into delivery fibers, allowing for deeper tissue penetration—particularly valuable for cerebral monitoring through the scalp and skull—and improved discrimination of the CCO signal from background clutter.

The SCL system is configured with a two-arm differential set-up, where a small fraction of the light is split off into a reference arm. This reference signal is used to normalize the sample arm signal in real time, effectively canceling out intensity noise from the laser and environmental fluctuations. Quantitative analysis demonstrates that this configuration achieves high sensitivity and selectivity for CCO measurements, resolving concentration changes on the order of micromolar levels. While the primary application demonstrated is cerebral monitoring, the technology is equally applicable to other organs and tissues, such as muscle, kidney, or liver, for assessing viability during surgery, transplantation, or critical illness.

In clinical scenarios involving risk of brain ischemia or injury—such as cardiac arrest, stroke, or traumatic brain injury—the ability to monitor neuronal metabolism and cerebral oxygenation simultaneously offers transformative potential. Early detection of metabolic dysfunction, indicated by a decline in oxidized CCO despite stable or even elevated HbO, could signal mitochondrial failure before irreversible damage occurs, guiding timely therapeutic interventions. Similarly, in hemorrhagic shock, monitoring CCO in both the brain and peripheral tissues could inform decisions regarding resuscitation strategies like partial resuscitative endovascular balloon occlusion of the aorta (pREBOA).

Referring to FIG. 1, a block diagram illustrates the optical set-up for in vitro laboratory measurements. The all-fiber integrated super-continuum laser (SCL) emits broadband light that is passed through an optical chopper operating at 271 Hz to enable lock-in detection. The chopped beam enters a grating spectrometer, which selects a narrow wavelength band for interrogation. A polarizer ensures a consistent polarization state, and a 50:50 beam splitter divides the light into sample and reference arms. The sample arm illuminates a cuvette containing a CCO solution, while the reference arm passes through a cuvette of purified water. Matched silicon photodetectors capture the transmitted light in both arms, and their outputs are fed into lock-in amplifiers synchronized to the chopper frequency. The resulting DC signals are digitized and processed by a computer to extract absorption spectra.

FIG. 2A presents a block-diagram of the SCL system, detailing the MOPA configuration. A distributed feedback semiconductor seed laser emits ~1 ns pulses at a tunable repetition rate (100 kHz to 4 MHz). These pulses are amplified in two stages: a low-noise preamplifier and a high-power cladding-pumped amplifier. Between stages, a band-pass filter and isolator suppress amplified spontaneous emission and back-reflections. The amplified pulses are coupled into a few meters of standard single-mode fiber (SMF), where modulational instability breaks the nanosecond pulses into picosecond solitons. These solitons then enter a nonlinear super-continuum generation fiber, where spectral broadening occurs via self-phase modulation, four-wave mixing, and stimulated Raman scattering, producing the final broadband output.

FIG. 2B plots the optical output spectrum of the SCL, showing a smooth, flat profile from 670 nm to 2500 nm with a time-averaged power of 11 W at 4 MHz repetition rate. FIG. 3 compares the spectral density of the SCL (black curve) to that of a 3000 K black body radiator (red curve) over the 750–900 nm range relevant for CCO measurements. The SCL exhibits nearly an order of magnitude higher spectral density, directly translating to improved SNR in tissue measurements. This brightness advantage is even more pronounced at longer wavelengths in the SWIR region, opening possibilities for monitoring additional chromophores.

### Empirical Examples

The empirical validation of the invention begins with the measurement of cytochrome c oxidase (CCO) in controlled laboratory settings. Bovine heart CCO samples were prepared in a Tris-HCl buffer at a concentration of approximately 16 µM. The samples were measured in both oxidized and reduced states, with the latter achieved by adding sodium dithionite as a reductant. Using the SCL system configured as in FIG. 1, the optical absorption spectrum of CCO was recorded across the 750–900 nm range. The resulting spectrum exhibited a broad, featureless peak centered near 825 nm, in excellent agreement with literature reports, confirming the system’s accuracy in capturing CCO’s characteristic absorption profile. Minor shifts in the peak wavelength were observed over time, likely due to gradual changes in the oxidation level or interactions with buffer components, underscoring the dynamic nature of the enzyme’s redox state.

The SCL system design for human testing, illustrated in FIG. 5A, incorporates several refinements over the laboratory setup. A 99:1 beam splitter directs most of the light to the sample arm (coupled to a fiber probe placed on the subject’s skin) while a small fraction goes to the reference arm for real-time normalization. The probe, detailed in FIG. 5B, consists of 2-mm diameter borosilicate fiber bundles for light delivery and collection, mounted on a solid base with an adjustable 2 cm spacing to optimize the "banana-shaped" photon path for ~2 cm tissue penetration. For absolute concentration change measurements, the system employs a modified Beer-Lambert law algorithm that accounts for wavelength-dependent attenuation, differential pathlength factors, and known extinction coefficients of HbO, HbR, and CCO. Reflected light is collected, and its attenuation is used to calculate optical pathlength and, subsequently, absolute concentration changes of the chromophores.

Three empirical examples demonstrate the system’s capabilities in human subjects. In the blood pressure test, a cuff was inflated on the upper arm while the probe monitored the forearm. Results showed the expected hemodynamic response: HbR increased and HbO decreased during occlusion, while CCO remained unchanged—consistent with literature, as forearm muscle metabolism is relatively unaffected by brief ischemia. In the breath-holding test at the forehead, both the SCL system and a commercial fNIRS device recorded an increase in HbO during apnea, validating the hemodynamic measurements. Critically, the SCL system additionally revealed a decrease in oxidized CCO, indicating heightened metabolic activity in the brain during breath-holding, likely due to respiratory drive activation.

The cognitive attention test, conducted on 25 healthy participants, represented the most significant validation. A modified probe (FIG. 12) interleaved SCL and fNIRS fibers to ensure co-localized measurements on the forehead. During attention tasks, both systems showed increased HbO, confirming frontal lobe activation. However, only the SCL system detected a concurrent decrease in oxidized CCO, demonstrating its unique ability to capture metabolic demand. Due to the system’s ~17 s scan time, data were susceptible to motion artifacts, necessitating rigorous screening. Two methods were employed: "best of 6," selecting the six highest-quality blocks per participant, and "HbO increasing," retaining only blocks where HbO rose during the task. Both methods consistently showed CCO decreasing when HbO increased, supporting the hypothesis that attention tasks elevate ATP demand, reducing CCO.

These results highlight the physiological principle that CCO redox state reflects cellular ATP demand: increased neuronal activity consumes ATP, increasing electron flow through the respiratory chain and reducing CCO. The SCL system’s high SNR—achieved through SCL brightness, differential referencing, polarization control, and lock-in detection—enabled this definitive observation, whereas prior studies with lower-SNR systems reported inconclusive CCO changes. The system’s non-invasive nature, portability (as evidenced by the cart-based prototype transported between institutions), and ability to provide simultaneous metabolic and hemodynamic data position it as a transformative tool for diagnosing and managing conditions like concussion, stroke, and hemorrhagic shock. Future enhancements, such as faster wavelength tuning, will further improve temporal resolution and robustness, expanding its clinical utility across diverse fields including neurology, critical care, and sports medicine.
