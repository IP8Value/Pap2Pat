# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates to computer graphics, and more specifically to methods and systems for rendering deformable and animated surface reflectance fields under arbitrary illumination conditions. The invention enables the photorealistic animation of real-world objects captured using image-based modeling techniques, preserving complex material appearance—including spatially varying reflectance properties such as anisotropy, specularity, and translucency—during geometric deformation. The invention is particularly applicable to hybrid rendering frameworks that combine approximate 3D geometry with high-dimensional image-based appearance data, and provides a novel approach to maintaining visual fidelity when the underlying geometry is warped, bent, or otherwise nonrigidly transformed.

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

Visualization and animation of realistic three-dimensional computer graphics models are essential for numerous applications, including cinematic special effects, interactive gaming, virtual reality, architectural visualization, and e-commerce product previews. Achieving photorealism in such contexts requires accurate representation of both object geometry and surface reflectance behavior under varying lighting and viewing conditions. Historically, three broad strategies have been employed: model-based rendering using parametric representations, pure image-based rendering, and hybrid approaches that combine elements of both.

Model-based rendering relies on explicit geometric models paired with parametric Bidirectional Reflectance Distribution Functions (BRDFs) to describe how light interacts with surfaces. These BRDFs are often fit to measured data and can be efficiently evaluated on modern graphics hardware. Furthermore, the underlying geometry can be animated using well-established techniques such as skeletal skinning, vertex blending, or physics-based simulation. However, parametric BRDFs are inherently limited in their ability to capture complex real-world phenomena such as interreflections, subsurface scattering, self-shadowing, and translucency, which are critical for the appearance of many natural and manufactured materials.

Image-based rendering circumvents these limitations by directly capturing and replaying appearance from photographs, without assuming any particular reflectance model. Techniques such as light fields or view-dependent texture mapping store images of an object from multiple viewpoints and interpolate between them during rendering. While highly effective for static scenes, these methods typically lack an explicit 3D structure, making geometric deformation extremely difficult or impossible. Moreover, they often restrict allowable camera positions and cannot support relighting with arbitrary illumination.

Hybrid rendering approaches attempt to reconcile the strengths of both paradigms by associating image-based appearance data with a simplified 3D proxy geometry, commonly referred to as an impostor. One such representation is the surface light field, introduced by Miller et al., which maps captured images onto the object’s surface. Wood et al. later demonstrated rudimentary animation of surface light fields by deforming the impostor geometry. However, surface light fields are fundamentally limited to fixed illumination environments, as they encode only the object’s appearance under a specific lighting configuration.

To overcome this limitation, the surface reflectance field was developed as a more general hybrid representation. A surface reflectance field captures an object’s appearance under a wide range of lighting directions, enabling relighting with arbitrary illumination. It consists of a dense set of reflectance images—each showing the object from a fixed viewpoint under varying directional lighting—mapped onto an impostor geometry. This allows rendering from any viewpoint under new lighting conditions, while preserving complex reflectance behaviors that defy parametric modeling. Despite its advantages, no prior art has successfully addressed the animation of surface reflectance fields under arbitrary deformations while preserving material appearance.

Prior art in 3D rendering includes the lumigraph, which combines a visual hull geometry with a dense light field to improve interpolation quality; view-dependent texture mapping, which uses sparse textures on coarse geometry; and opacity light fields, which extend surface light fields with view-dependent opacity to handle complex silhouettes. Surface reflectance fields build upon these concepts by incorporating relightability, but interpolation across both view and lighting dimensions remains challenging. Unstructured lumigraph rendering provides a flexible framework for such interpolation, but it does not address deformation.

Animation of image-based data has received scant attention. Feature-based light field morphing requires extensive user input and is unsuitable for general animation. Furukawa et al. presented a system for capturing and deforming Bidirectional Texture Functions (BTFs), but it relies on tightly fitted geometry from range scanning and does not explicitly address appearance preservation under nonuniform deformations. Consequently, there remains a critical gap in the art: a method for animating surface reflectance fields mapped onto approximate geometry that preserves the perceptual qualities of spatially varying BRDFs during arbitrary warps.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a method for rendering deformed and animated surface reflectance fields that preserves the visual appearance of real-world materials under arbitrary geometric deformations and dynamic lighting. The method operates by augmenting the impostor geometry of a surface reflectance field with a local parameterization that enables correct evaluation of acquired reflectance data during deformation. A key innovation is a look-up function that maps shading queries in deformed object space back to the original acquisition space in a manner that approximately preserves the local BRDF characteristics. This mapping is constructed as a rigid transformation—comprising translation and rotation—that aligns the deformed surface frame with the original acquisition frame, satisfying three appearance-preserving conditions: correspondence of surface points, preservation of lighting and viewing angles relative to the surface normal, and maintenance of azimuthal orientations for anisotropic materials. The invention further includes a deferred shading scheme optimized for cache coherence, enabling efficient rendering of large-scale deformable surface reflectance fields. The method is applicable to any hybrid image-based representation that uses an impostor geometry and supports relighting with point light sources or environment maps without requiring per-pixel refiltering.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE PREFERRED EMBODIMENT

A surface reflectance field (SRF) is a hybrid representation comprising a large collection of reflectance images associated with an impostor geometry. Each reflectance image is a stack of high dynamic range photographs of an object captured from a fixed camera viewpoint under varying directional illumination. The impostor geometry, typically a visual hull derived from object silhouettes, serves as a proxy for the true object surface and enables rendering via interpolation across views and lighting conditions. Rendering an SRF involves first computing, for each viewpoint, a synthesized image under the desired illumination by interpolating the reflectance images, and then rendering these images using unstructured lumigraph rendering or similar techniques.

To animate an SRF, the impostor geometry is deformed by applying a differentiable warp function Ψ: ℝ³ → ℝ³. However, naive deformation disrupts the relationship between geometry and appearance data, leading to visual artifacts. The present invention introduces a local parameterization of the impostor geometry that enables appearance-preserving evaluation of the SRF during deformation. At each point p* on the deformed impostor, the parameterization stores the original position p₀ = Ψ⁻¹(p*), an original orthogonal tangential frame R₀ = (u₀, v₀, n₀), and the current deformed tangential vectors u and v, obtained by applying the warp’s Jacobian to the original tangents. This parameterization allows reconstruction of a look-up function L without requiring an explicit inverse warp.

The animation process proceeds as follows: during rendering, for each shading query defined by a deformed surface point p*, viewing direction v*, and lighting direction l*, the look-up function L maps this query to the acquisition space as (p, l, v) = L(p*, l*, v*). The point p is set to p₀, ensuring correspondence with the original acquisition geometry. The directions l and v are rotated to align with the original tangential frame, preserving the angular relationships critical to BRDF appearance.

### Approximate BRDF Preservation

Exact preservation of appearance under arbitrary deformation is impossible without knowledge of the true object geometry and complete light transport. The present invention therefore adopts a local BRDF approximation, aiming to preserve the perceptual characteristics of spatially varying reflectance. This is achieved through a look-up function L that enforces three conditions. First, the surface point in acquisition space must correspond to the deformed point, approximated by setting p = p₀. Second, the angles between lighting/viewing directions and the surface normal must be preserved to maintain the shape of reflectance lobes. Third, for anisotropic materials, the azimuthal orientations of l and v relative to the surface frame must be conserved.

These conditions imply that the directional mapping component Lₚ* must be an isometry—specifically, a rotation (or reflection, though rotation is assumed here). Thus, L acts as a rigid transformation: translating p* to p₀ and rotating the local frame. The rotation is derived from the deformed tangential vectors u and v, which are orthogonalized to form a basis ("u", "v") that minimizes angular deviation from the original frame. The rotation Lₚ* is then the transformation that maps ("u", "v", n*) to (u₀, v₀, n₀), where n* is the deformed normal.

This parameterization avoids reliance on the inverse warp Ψ⁻¹ by storing p₀ and R₀ directly at each point. During rendering, the deformed tangents u and v are updated by applying the warp’s differential (e.g., via central differences), and Lₚ* is reconstructed on the fly. The method assumes that the impostor normal approximates the true surface normal sufficiently for appearance preservation, an assumption validated empirically.

For shading, the invention proposes simulating point light sources rather than filtering environment maps per pixel—a costly operation. A point light source at position pₗ defines a lighting direction l* from p* to pₗ. The look-up function maps (p*, l*, v*) to (p, l, v), and the SRF yields a reflectance coefficient. The shaded color is then Iₚ = SRF(p, l, v) · Iₗ · A(d), where Iₗ is the light color and A(d) is distance attenuation. Novel lighting directions are interpolated by treating light sources as cameras at infinity and applying unstructured lumigraph interpolation, ensuring smooth transitions. Environment maps can be approximated by subsampling into a dense set of point lights, trading explicit filtering for interpolation-based reconstruction.

## EFFECT OF THE INVENTION

The present invention enables the first practical method for animating surface reflectance fields under arbitrary deformations while preserving complex material appearance. By introducing a local impostor parameterization and a rigid look-up transformation, the invention approximately preserves spatially varying BRDFs—including anisotropic effects—without requiring knowledge of true surface normals or exact object geometry. The deferred shading scheme with cache-optimized evaluation dramatically improves performance for large datasets, making real-time or near-real-time animation feasible. The method is generalizable to other hybrid image-based representations and supports dynamic relighting with point sources or environment maps. Experimental results demonstrate high-fidelity preservation of diverse materials—diffuse, specular, and textured—under significant nonrigid deformations, overcoming a longstanding limitation in image-based rendering.
