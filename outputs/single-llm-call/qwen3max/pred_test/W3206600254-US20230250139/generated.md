# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates to the field of molecular recording of protein expression within living cells. More specifically, the invention provides compositions and methods for encoding, storing, and subsequently reading out temporal histories of cellular physiological states through the use of genetically encoded, self-assembling protein filaments that incorporate detectable epitope tags in a time-resolved manner. This technology enables continuous, long-term recording of dynamic biological processes such as gene expression, signal transduction events, or responses to external stimuli, with the recorded information preserved in a linear protein architecture that can be interrogated post hoc using standard immunostaining and imaging techniques after cell fixation. The invention thus bridges the gap between live-cell dynamic monitoring and fixed-tissue high-throughput analysis by converting transient intracellular signals into stable, spatially ordered molecular records.

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

Biological research has long sought methods to read out signals and processes that unfold over time within living cells, tissues, and whole organisms. Genetically encoded fluorescent reporters have enabled real-time monitoring of specific biological activities in live cells; however, such live imaging approaches are inherently limited by practical constraints. Long-term live imaging requires dedicated microscopy equipment for extended durations, is labor-intensive, and typically restricts observation to a small number of cells due to field-of-view and phototoxicity limitations. In contrast, fixed-tissue imaging benefits from scalable methodologies—including tissue sectioning, optical clearing, and expansion microscopy—that permit high-resolution, multiplexed analysis across vast cellular populations, even entire mammalian brains. Yet conventional fixed-cell methods like RNA fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) or protein immunostaining generally capture only one or two discrete time points, failing to provide continuous temporal records of physiological dynamics. While nucleic acid-based cellular recorders have been developed to store historical information in DNA for sequencing-based readout, these approaches necessitate cell lysis or dissociation, thereby sacrificing spatial context and precluding correlation with morphological or structural features of intact cells and tissues. There remains an unmet need for a system that combines the temporal continuity of live recording with the scalability, spatial preservation, and multimodal compatibility of fixed-sample analysis.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The invention provides an expression-recording island (XRI) composition comprising a self-assembling filament-forming monomer fused to a detectable tag and a protein spacer. The self-assembling filament-forming monomer is engineered to polymerize into a linear protein assembly in a unidirectional or bidirectional manner within the cytosol of a cell, with new monomers added sequentially to the growing ends over time. The detectable tag, which may be an epitope such as HA, FLAG, or V5, enables post-fixation visualization via immunostaining. The protein spacer, exemplified by maltose-binding protein (MBP), functions as an insulator to prevent lateral aggregation and ensure longitudinal growth fidelity, thereby preserving the temporal order of monomer incorporation. The linear protein assembly grows continuously as monomers are expressed, with the local density of each tag reflecting the relative abundance of its corresponding monomer at the time of incorporation, effectively encoding a time-resolved record of cellular states. A vector encoding the XRI composition is provided, enabling stable or transient expression in target cells. The invention further encompasses a cell comprising such an XRI vector, as well as an adeno-associated virus (AAV) particle packaging the XRI composition for efficient delivery. A cell transduced with the AAV-XRI construct is also disclosed. The invention introduces a method for identifying an expression history record by expressing the XRI in a cell under conditions where monomer expression is modulated by physiological or experimental variables. Detection of the expressed XRI is performed after cell fixation using antibodies against the detectable tags. The expression record is identified by analyzing the spatial distribution and intensity profiles of the tags along the filament. Comparing expression records between test and control cells reveals differences in temporal dynamics. Fixing cells prior to detection preserves spatial integrity for high-resolution imaging. The amount of expressed XRI is determined by quantifying total tag signal, while the pattern and identity of epitope tags are resolved through multiplexed immunostaining. The temporal history of expression is reconstructed by mapping tag density profiles onto a calibrated time axis derived from constitutive tag incorporation. The invention is applicable to diverse cell types, particularly post-mitotic cells such as neurons, and supports multiplexing through the use of multiple detectable labels separated by distinct protein spacers.

## BRIEF DESCRIPTION OF CERTAIN OF THE SEQUENCES

The protein motifs used in XRI compositions include a filament-forming domain derived from 1POK(E239Y), a human-engineered variant of Escherichia coli isoaspartyl dipeptidase, which mediates self-assembly into linear filaments with a fixed longitudinal monomer spacing of approximately 4 nanometers. This domain is fused at its C-terminus to a protein spacer, specifically maltose-binding protein (MBP), which acts as a steric insulator to suppress lateral aggregation and promote unidirectional elongation. N-terminal to the filament-forming domain is a short epitope tag sequence, such as the hemagglutinin (HA) tag (YPYDVPDYA), FLAG tag (DYKDDDDK), or V5 tag (GKPIPNPLLGLDST), which serves as a detectable label for immunostaining. These motifs are codon-optimized for mammalian expression and arranged in-frame within expression vectors to yield a single polypeptide chain that retains both self-assembly competence and epitope accessibility.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

### Compositions and Components

The XRI composition comprises a recombinant polypeptide engineered to function as a molecular tape recorder within living cells. This polypeptide includes three key functional domains: a self-assembling filament-forming monomer, a detectable tag, and a protein spacer. The filament-forming monomer is based on 1POK(E239Y), a computationally designed protein that spontaneously polymerizes into linear filaments in the cytosol. The detectable tag is a short peptide epitope—such as HA, FLAG, or V5—that is recognized by commercially available high-affinity antibodies, enabling robust detection via immunofluorescence after cell fixation. The protein spacer, exemplified by MBP, is fused to the lateral surface of the filament-forming domain to sterically hinder unwanted lateral interactions that would otherwise lead to amorphous aggregates, thereby ensuring that polymerization proceeds predominantly in the longitudinal direction. A nucleic acid sequence encoding this XRI polypeptide is provided, wherein the coding sequence is operably linked to a promoter for controlled expression. Non-limiting examples of self-assembling proteins include DHF40 and other computationally designed filaments, though 1POK(E239Y) demonstrated superior performance in neuronal contexts. Detectable tags may also include fluorescent proteins like mEGFP, though epitope tags are preferred for multiplexing. Engineered sequences incorporate mammalian codon optimization and may include cleavage sites such as P2A for bicistronic expression. In summary, the XRI composition is a modular, genetically encodable unit that self-assembles into a linear, time-ordered polymer suitable for retrospective decoding of cellular history.

### Expression and Use of XRI Components

The XRI system functions as a reporter platform for recording cellular physiological histories over extended periods. By placing the XRI coding sequence under the control of a constitutive promoter (e.g., UBC), a baseline filament grows continuously, serving as a temporal scaffold. When a second XRI variant, bearing a different epitope tag, is placed under an inducible promoter (e.g., c-fos), its expression—and thus incorporation into the growing filament—is triggered by specific stimuli, creating a permanent spatial record of the event’s timing and duration. Baseline expression characteristics are first established in control cells lacking induction. Test cells are then exposed to candidate stimuli—such as potassium chloride depolarization, pharmacological agents, or optogenetic activation—and their XRI filaments are compared to controls to reveal stimulus-induced changes in tag incorporation patterns. Communication between cells can also be studied by co-culturing XRI-expressing reporter cells with stimulated neighbors. The system assesses both internal states (e.g., transcription factor activity) and external environments (e.g., drug exposure). Therapeutic screening is enabled by treating XRI-expressing cells with compound libraries and quantifying resulting expression dynamics. Normal developmental processes can be recorded by expressing XRI during differentiation. In sum, the XRI recorder system provides a versatile, single-cell resolution tool for capturing and comparing temporal trajectories of gene expression and signaling activity.

### Molecules and Compounds

XRI-based recorder systems encompass not only the core polypeptide but also functional variants, polynucleotides, and formulated compositions. Functional variants include conservative amino acid substitutions that preserve self-assembly and epitope recognition, as well as fusion proteins incorporating additional domains (e.g., localization signals). Polynucleotides encoding XRI components are isolated and may be codon-optimized for specific host species (e.g., human, mouse). Compounds comprising XRI components include pharmaceutical formulations for in vivo delivery. Additional components may include adjuvants, stabilizers, or targeting moieties. Isolated nucleic acid sequences are free of genomic context and suitable for cloning or synthesis. Host cells—ranging from HEK293 to primary neurons—can be engineered to express XRI. Delivery to cells is achieved via transfection, electroporation, or viral transduction. Variant components are validated for effectiveness by assessing filament morphology and recording fidelity. Codon-optimized sequences enhance expression levels and reduce immune recognition in mammalian systems.

### Delivery of XRI-Based Recorder Components

Delivery of XRI components to target cells employs art-known means including lipid-mediated transfection, electroporation, and viral vectors. Fusion proteins may incorporate cell-penetrating peptides or receptor ligands to enhance uptake. Targeting sequences—such as nuclear localization signals or mitochondrial transit peptides—can direct XRI assembly to specific subcellular compartments. In vivo delivery leverages adeno-associated viruses (AAVs) with tropism for neural tissue (e.g., AAV9). Genetic targeting is achieved by placing XRI under cell-type-specific promoters (e.g., synapsin for neurons). Summary: XRI components are delivered efficiently and selectively to desired cell populations using established biochemical and genetic tools.

### Vectors, Plasmids, and Molecules

A vector is defined as a nucleic acid molecule capable of carrying foreign DNA into a host cell. Episomes are extrachromosomal vectors that replicate autonomously. Expression vectors contain regulatory elements—promoters, enhancers, polyadenylation signals—to drive transgene expression. Examples include pAAV backbones, lentiviral constructs, and plasmids. Lentivirus enables stable integration and long-term expression. Promoters may be constitutive (UBC, CMV), inducible (Tet-On), or cell-specific (synapsin). Inducible promoters allow temporal control of XRI subunit expression. Fusion proteins link XRI to reporters (e.g., mEGFP-P2A-XRI). Trafficking sequences—such as KDEL for ER retention or myristoylation motifs for membrane anchoring—localize XRI. Detectable label polypeptides include HA, FLAG, V5, and fluorescent proteins. A table of constructs details promoter-tag combinations used in validation experiments.

### Cells and Subjects

XRI-based recorder systems are compatible with prokaryotic and eukaryotic cells, though optimal performance is observed in post-mitotic mammalian cells. Mammalian cells include neurons, cardiomyocytes, and hepatocytes. Non-mammalian cells such as Drosophila S2 or C. elegans neurons are also viable. Excitable cells—neurons, muscle cells—are particularly relevant due to their dynamic signaling. Abnormal cells like cancer lines (U2OS) support XRI expression but may exhibit altered kinetics. Control cells lack inducible components or receive vehicle treatment. The system is included in cells via transduction or transfection, enabling studies in vitro and in vivo.

### Controls

XRI systems assess changes by comparing test cells, tissues, or organisms to appropriate controls. Control values establish baseline tag incorporation in the absence of stimuli. Comparative groups include untreated, vehicle-treated, or genetically matched cells. Statistical analysis determines significance of observed differences in temporal recording patterns.

### Administration Means

Administration of XRI components occurs via in vitro transduction or in vivo injection. Pharmaceutical compositions include XRI-encoding AAVs in saline with stabilizers. Pharmaceutically acceptable carriers ensure biocompatibility. Delivery into cells uses stereotaxic surgery for brain targets. Modes include intraperitoneal, intravenous, or local infusion. Protocols specify dose, timing, and route for research or veterinary use.

### Assessment Methods

XRI systems assess disorders by recording pathological signaling dynamics in disease models. Diseases include neurodegeneration, epilepsy, and cancer. Monitoring tracks progression or treatment response. Uses span basic research, drug discovery, and diagnostics. Nucleic acid sequences are prepared by cloning and expressed via standard methods.

## EXAMPLES

### Example 1

Animal procedures followed institutional guidelines. Neuron cultures were prepared from postnatal day 0–1 Swiss Webster mice. Cells were plated on Matrigel-coated coverslips in MEM-based medium supplemented with B27, glucose, and serum. Molecular cloning used codon-optimized XRI sequences inserted into pAAV-UBC or pAAV-cFos backbones. DNA transfection employed calcium phosphate at DIV7, using 5–50 ng XRI plasmid per well. AAV transduction used AAV9 serotype at specified GC/ml concentrations. Chemical treatments included 1 µM 4-OHT for 15 min or 55 mM KCl for varying durations. 4-OHT induction activated Cre-dependent XRI-FLAG expression. Potassium chloride depolarized neurons to induce c-fos. U2OS cells were transfected with TransIT-X2. Electrophysiology used whole-cell patch clamp on DIV14–16 neurons. Animal surgery involved stereotaxic AAV injection into mouse CA1 at coordinates AP −2.0, ML ±1.5, DV −1.5. AAV mixtures were prepared fresh and injected at 0.6–1.0 µl. Post-surgery care included buprenorphine analgesia. 4-Hydroxytamoxifen was injected i.p. at 40 mg/kg. Histology used 4% PFA perfusion and 50-µm vibratome sections. Immunofluorescence stained cultured cells with anti-HA/FLAG/V5 and Nissl. Brain slices underwent extended blocking and staining. Expansion microscopy involved AcX anchoring, gelation, and denaturation. Fluorescence microscopy used spinning-disk confocal with 40x objective. Antibodies included anti-HA (sc-7392), anti-FLAG (740001), and NeuroTrace Blue. RNA-Seq extracted RNA with Trizol, generated cDNA via SMART-Seq, and sequenced on MiSeq. Image analysis measured intensity profiles along XRI centerlines. Line profiles were split at the geometric center, integrated, and normalized to HA cumulative signal. FLAG signal was derived as the derivative of its integral with respect to HA fraction. Optimal split points minimized inter-half differences. Fraction of HA integral at FLAG rise onset was calculated by extrapolating the initial rising phase. Statistical analysis used Kruskal-Wallis with Dunn’s post hoc. Human-designed filaments were screened in hippocampal neurons; 1POK(E239Y) showed clear fibers. MBP fusion reduced aggregates. Chemically inducible Cre activated FLAG-XRI at defined times. XRIs in live neurons elongated over 7 days. Electrophysiology confirmed normal neuronal function. In vivo, AAV-XRI formed filaments in mouse CA1. c-fos-driven V5-XRI recorded KCl-induced depolarization. Sequential stimulations produced dual peaks. Time calibration mapped HA integral fraction to absolute time.
