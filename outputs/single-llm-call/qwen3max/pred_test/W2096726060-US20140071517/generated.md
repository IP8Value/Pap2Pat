## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates generally to optical materials and, more specifically, to nanomaterials capable of optical upconversion. The invention further pertains to composite structures comprising transition metal-doped or rare earth-doped nanocrystals coupled with organic dye molecules functioning as optical antennas. These nanomaterials are designed to absorb low-energy photons—particularly in the infrared spectral region—and emit higher-energy photons in the visible or ultraviolet range through a sequential multiphoton absorption and energy transfer process. The invention finds utility in diverse technological domains including photovoltaics, infrared detection, laser threat warning systems, anti-counterfeiting markers, and bioimaging.

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

Optical upconversion is a nonlinear optical phenomenon wherein two or more low-energy photons are absorbed sequentially, resulting in the emission of a single photon of higher energy. Unlike harmonic generation processes that require extremely high peak power densities, upconversion can occur at relatively modest continuous-wave optical intensities due to the long-lived excited states of certain luminescent ions. Historically, lanthanide-doped inorganic materials have served as the primary platform for upconversion, leveraging the unique electronic structure of trivalent lanthanide ions (Ln³⁺) which exhibit sharp, atomic-like absorption and emission lines arising from shielded 4f–4f transitions. However, these transitions are Laporte-forbidden, leading to inherently low absorption cross-sections—typically on the order of 0.5 to 5 M⁻¹ cm⁻¹—and narrow spectral bandwidths, often less than 10 nm full width at half maximum (FWHM). Consequently, conventional lanthanide-based upconverters suffer from poor light-harvesting efficiency, especially under broadband or non-resonant illumination.

Efforts to overcome these limitations have focused on embedding Ln³⁺ ions within low-phonon-energy host matrices such as sodium yttrium tetrafluoride (NaYF₄), which minimizes nonradiative relaxation via multiphonon processes. While this improves quantum yield, it does not address the fundamental issue of weak and spectrally narrow absorption. To enhance photon capture, researchers have explored coupling organic dyes to the surface of upconverting nanoparticles, where the dye acts as an “optical antenna” due to its large molar extinction coefficients (often exceeding 200,000 M⁻¹ cm⁻¹) and broad absorption profiles spanning tens to hundreds of nanometers. Energy absorbed by the dye can then be transferred nonradiatively to the lanthanide sensitizer ion—commonly Yb³⁺—via Förster resonance energy transfer (FRET), provided the donor (dye) emission overlaps spectrally with the acceptor (sensitizer) absorption and the separation distance is within the Förster radius (typically 2–6 nm).

Despite promising demonstrations, prior art suffers from several drawbacks. First, most systems rely on a single excitation wavelength and produce a fixed emission color, limiting their ability to discriminate between different infrared inputs. Second, the chemical instability of many near-infrared (NIR) dyes under the basic conditions required for silica shell formation has hindered robust covalent integration. Third, inefficient energy transfer due to suboptimal dye-nanoparticle spacing or mismatched spectral overlap reduces overall upconversion efficiency. There remains a critical need for engineered upconverting nanomaterials that combine broad and tunable infrared absorption with distinct, wavelength-dependent visible emissions, enabling advanced functionalities such as spectral encoding, multi-channel sensing, and enhanced solar energy harvesting.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides an optical upconverting nanomaterial comprising a nanocrystal doped with one or more transition metal or rare earth elements, and an organic dye molecule covalently attached to the nanocrystal surface, wherein the dye functions as an optical antenna to absorb incident radiation and transfer energy to the dopant ions via Förster resonance energy transfer (FRET), thereby enabling efficient upconversion of infrared photons to visible or ultraviolet emission. In a preferred embodiment, the nanocrystal is composed of a low-phonon-energy host material such as NaYF₄, doped with a sensitizer ion (e.g., Yb³⁺, Er³⁺, or Nd³⁺) and an activator ion (e.g., Er³⁺, Tm³⁺, or Ho³⁺), though in some configurations a single ion species serves both roles.

The invention offers significant advantages over existing technologies. By decoupling light absorption from the intrinsic atomic transitions of the dopant ions, the effective spectral response of the upconverter is dramatically broadened—by over tenfold in bandwidth—and its peak absorption cross-section is increased by up to six orders of magnitude. This enables efficient operation under non-laser, broadband, or off-resonance illumination. Moreover, by pairing specific dyes with specific dopant combinations, the system can be engineered to produce distinct visible emission signatures in response to different infrared input wavelengths, thereby enabling discrimination of otherwise indistinguishable IR signals.

Applications of the invention include enhancing the spectral response of silicon-based photodetectors and solar cells beyond their native cutoff (~1100 nm), providing visible warnings in response to invisible laser threats (e.g., 800–1600 nm military designators), and serving as covert, wavelength-specific security markers for anti-counterfeiting. The optical upconverting nanomaterial is defined herein as a composite nanostructure wherein incident photons are first captured by an organic dye antenna, transferred nonradiatively to a sensitizer dopant via FRET, and subsequently upconverted through sequential energy transfers to an emitter dopant, which radiates at a shorter wavelength.

In certain embodiments, the dopants are transition metals such as Cr³⁺, Mn²⁺, Fe³⁺, Co²⁺, Ni²⁺, or Cu²⁺, which offer broader absorption bands and potentially lower cost than rare earths. Energy transfer facilitating transition metal dopants absorb energy from the dye and relay it to emitter transition metal dopants, which generate the upconverted luminescence. Alternatively, a single transition metal element may serve both functions. The system may also support multiple parallel upconversion pathways within the same nanocrystal, enabling simultaneous response to multiple distinct input wavelengths (e.g., 785 nm and 830 nm) and/or generation of multiple output wavelengths (e.g., green and red emission).

The optical antenna comprises an organic dye selected from heptamethine cyanines, dithiones, squaryliums, tris azo compounds, or members of the Dyomics series, functionalized with reactive groups such as NHS esters, isothiocyanates, or carboxylic acids. The dye is covalently bonded to the nanocrystal via an amide linkage formed between the dye’s functional group and an amine moiety on a surface-bound siloxane layer. An optional encapsulating silica shell may surround the dye-nanocrystal assembly to improve chemical stability and facilitate dispersion in polymeric matrices. Additional ligands may be introduced to tailor solubility or compatibility with host materials.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

As used herein, “optical means” refers to any device or system capable of generating, transmitting, modulating, detecting, or utilizing electromagnetic radiation in the ultraviolet, visible, or infrared regions of the spectrum. “Infrared (IR)” denotes wavelengths from approximately 700 nm to 1 mm, subdivided into near-infrared (NIR: 700–1400 nm), short-wave infrared (SWIR: 1400–3000 nm), and longer bands. “Visible” light spans 400–700 nm, while “ultraviolet (UV)” encompasses 10–400 nm. “Near infrared (NIR)” specifically refers to 700–1400 nm, a region of particular interest due to its transmission through biological tissue and compatibility with telecommunications and laser diodes.

An “optical antenna” is an organic chromophore engineered to absorb incident radiation efficiently and transfer the excitation energy nonradiatively to a proximal acceptor. “Upconversion” is a photophysical process involving the sequential absorption of two or more low-energy photons followed by emission of one higher-energy photon. “Förster resonance energy transfer (FRET)” is a dipole-dipole coupling mechanism enabling nonradiative energy transfer over distances of 1–10 nm, dependent on spectral overlap and relative orientation. “Transition elements/metals” include d-block elements such as Ti, V, Cr, Mn, Fe, Co, Ni, Cu, and Zn, whose ions can exhibit rich photoluminescent behavior. “Rare earth elements/metals” refer to lanthanides (La–Lu) and sometimes Sc and Y, commonly used as luminescent dopants.

An “organic dye” is a synthetic or natural compound with strong, broad absorption in the UV-VIS-NIR range, exemplified by cyanines, xanthenes, and phthalocyanines. A “sensitizer” is a dopant ion that absorbs energy and transfers it to an activator/emitter. “Energy transfer facilitating material” denotes dopants that mediate energy flow without being the final emitter.

The optical upconverting nanomaterial comprises a crystalline nanocrystal core, typically 10–100 nm in size, composed of a host lattice such as NaYF₄, LaF₃, or Y₂O₃, doped with sensitizer and activator ions at optimized concentrations (e.g., 20% Yb³⁺, 2% Er³⁺). The nanocrystal exhibits hexagonal phase crystallinity, confirmed by XRD and HR-TEM, which maximizes upconversion efficiency.

### X=—NH₂, —CO₂M′, —SH, —OH,

The dye structure includes functional groups X enabling covalent attachment. Common variants include amine (—NH₂) for isothiocyanate coupling, carboxylate (—CO₂M′) for carbodiimide-mediated amide bond formation, thiol (—SH) for gold or maleimide chemistry, and hydroxyl (—OH) for silane or ester linkages.

### M=H₂, Cu, Co, CoR₃, Ni, SiR₃R₄, AlR₃, Zn, Fe, FeR₃, Mn

Metal ions M in dye complexes may include diamagnetic or paramagnetic centers that tune photophysics. For example, Zn²⁺ in phthalocyanines enhances fluorescence, while Cu²⁺ may quench it.

### R₄=—R₃, —Cl, —CH₃, —H, -Ph, —OCH₃, —OCH₂CH₃

Substituents R₄ modulate solubility, absorption maxima, and photostability. Phenyl (–Ph) groups extend conjugation; methoxy (–OCH₃) enhances electron donation.

### M′=K, Li, Na, H

Counterions M′ balance charge in carboxylate-functionalized dyes, influencing solubility in polar media.

Heptamethines (e.g., DY-831, DyLight 800) feature polymethine chains with NIR absorption. Dithiones contain thiocarbonyl groups; squaryliums are based on squaric acid cores; tris azos incorporate three azo linkages. The Dyomics series offers NHS-ester-functionalized NIR dyes with high extinction coefficients (>200,000 M⁻¹cm⁻¹). Specific examples include DY-831, DyLight 800, DLS-776A, NIR-797, and fluorescein-free acid.

Energy transfer facilitators include Yb³⁺ (for 980 nm excitation), Nd³⁺ (808 nm), or Er³⁺ (1550 nm). Emitters include Er³⁺ (green/red), Tm³⁺ (blue), Ho³⁺ (green). In transition metal systems, Cr³⁺ may act as both absorber and emitter.

Operation involves: (1) dye absorbs IR photon; (2) excited dye transfers energy via FRET to sensitizer; (3) sensitizer undergoes second excitation (directly or via another FRET event); (4) energy migrates to activator; (5) activator emits upconverted photon. Dexter mechanism (electron exchange) may supplement FRET at very short distances (<1 nm).

Composite materials integrate nanomaterials into PMMA, sol-gel glasses, or textiles. Parallel pathways allow one nanocrystal to respond to 785 nm (via DyLight 800 → Er³⁺) and 830 nm (via DY-831 → Yb³⁺/Ho³⁺), emitting green and red, respectively.

Distinct elements enable modular design: e.g., Nd³⁺ as sensitizer for 808 nm, Tm³⁺ as blue emitter. Multiple dyes with overlapping emission but distinct absorption can funnel energy to a common emitter, producing a unified output spectrum despite varied inputs.

Fabrication begins with synthesis of oleate-capped NaYF₄:Ln³⁺ nanocrystals via thermal decomposition. Silica coating introduces amine groups via (3-aminopropyl)trimethoxysilane in reverse microemulsion. Dye attachment proceeds in ethanol at 35°C for carboxylates or RT for NHS esters. Films are cast by dispersing nanoparticles in MMA monomer, followed by thermal curing to form transparent PMMA composites.

## Synthesis of Lanthanide-Doped NaYF₄ Nanocrystals

Upconversion efficiency in nanocrystalline materials is highly sensitive to crystal phase, with the hexagonal phase of NaYF₄ exhibiting superior performance over cubic or amorphous forms. The synthetic method employs a modified hot-injection approach under inert atmosphere. Stoichiometric lanthanide acetates (e.g., Y(CH₃COO)₃, Yb(CH₃COO)₃, Er(CH₃COO)₃) and sodium acetate are combined in octadecene with oleic acid, heated to 120°C under vacuum to form a clear solution of metal oleates, then cooled. Ammonium fluoride in methanol is added, precipitating amorphous NaYF₄. The mixture is degassed, then heated to 324°C for 20 minutes to induce crystallization into the hexagonal phase. Nanocrystals are isolated by ethanol precipitation and centrifugation, yielding rods ~25×15 nm. Reaction time and temperature control size; ligand ratio tunes aspect ratio. XRD confirms phase purity; TEM reveals morphology.

### Encapsulation of Attached Dye

NaYF₄:Ln³⁺ nanoparticles dispersed in cyclohexane are mixed with Igepal CO-520 to form a reverse microemulsion. Amine-functionalized dye is pre-reacted with (3-aminopropyl)trimethoxysilane, then added to the microemulsion. Hydrolysis and condensation form an inner silica shell embedding the dye, minimizing exposure to degradation.

### Attachment of Dye to Encapsulated Nanoparticle

Alternatively, nanoparticles are first coated with a thin amine-functionalized silica shell via APS condensation. Purified particles are then reacted with NHS-ester dyes in ethanol, forming stable amide bonds. This two-step process preserves dye integrity under milder conditions.

### Fabrication of Films

Transparent films are fabricated by dispersing dye-nanoparticle composites in methyl methacrylate (MMA) monomer containing initiator. The mixture is cast between glass slides and cured at 60–80°C to form clear PMMA films, following modifications of Boyer et al.’s protocol to prevent aggregation and ensure homogeneity.
