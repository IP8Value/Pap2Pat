# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNICAL FIELD

The present invention relates to methods for the purification of human papillomavirus (HPV) virus-like particles (VLPs), specifically those composed of the L1 major capsid protein. More particularly, the invention provides an improved downstream processing method for purifying recombinant HPV L1 proteins expressed in yeast host cells, such as *Saccharomyces cerevisiae*, wherein the purification process enhances both the structural integrity and immunogenicity of the resulting VLPs. The method is especially suited for large-scale production of high-purity, conformationally intact HPV VLPs suitable for use in prophylactic vaccines against HPV-associated diseases, including cervical cancer.

## BACKGROUND ART

Human papillomavirus (HPV) is a well-established etiological agent of cervical cancer, which remains one of the leading causes of cancer-related mortality among women worldwide. Over 200 HPV genotypes have been identified, with approximately 14 classified as high-risk types due to their strong association with oncogenesis; among these, HPV types 16 and 18 are responsible for nearly 70% of all cervical cancer cases. The viral capsid of HPV is composed primarily of the L1 major structural protein, which self-assembles into icosahedral virus-like particles (VLPs) when expressed in heterologous systems. A minor capsid protein, L2, is also present but is not required for VLP formation. Current prophylactic HPV vaccines, such as Gardasil and Cervarix, are based on L1 VLPs produced in yeast or insect cell expression systems and have demonstrated high efficacy in preventing persistent infection by targeted HPV types.

Despite the success of existing vaccines, significant limitations remain. First, the high cost of goods associated with current manufacturing processes restricts global accessibility, particularly in low-resource settings where cervical cancer burden is highest. Second, downstream purification of HPV VLPs from host cell lysates is complex and inefficient, often involving multiple chromatographic steps, ultracentrifugation, or precipitation techniques that can compromise VLP conformation, reduce yield, or fail to remove host cell contaminants effectively. Third, VLPs produced in *S. cerevisiae*—a preferred low-cost expression platform—are frequently reported to exhibit suboptimal structural stability, which correlates with diminished antigenicity and reduced ability to elicit neutralizing antibodies. This instability is attributed to improper folding or incomplete assembly during intracellular biogenesis, exacerbated by standard fermentation conditions that prioritize rapid growth over protein quality.

Conventional purification strategies for HPV L1 VLPs include ammonium sulfate precipitation, size-exclusion chromatography, ion-exchange chromatography (e.g., heparin or phosphocellulose resins), and cesium chloride density gradient ultracentrifugation. While these methods can isolate L1 protein, they often fail to preserve the native quaternary structure essential for presenting conformational neutralizing epitopes. Moreover, residual host proteins, nucleic acids, or endotoxins may persist, necessitating additional polishing steps that further reduce yield and increase production costs. Previous attempts to improve VLP quality through in vitro refolding or redox treatments have shown limited success and are difficult to scale. Critically, insufficient attention has been paid to how upstream culture conditions—such as carbon source concentration and duration—affect the intrinsic quality of L1 protein prior to purification. Given that VLP assembly begins intracellularly, the cellular environment during expression fundamentally influences the capacity of L1 monomers to form immunogenic, structurally authentic particles. Thus, there exists a pressing need for an integrated purification method that not only efficiently isolates HPV L1 VLPs but also preserves or enhances their native conformation and immunological potency, thereby enabling the production of more effective and affordable HPV vaccines.

## DISCLOSURE

### Technical Problem

The technical problem addressed by the present invention is the lack of an efficient, scalable, and conformation-preserving method for purifying recombinant HPV L1 proteins expressed in yeast host cells. Existing purification protocols often result in VLPs with compromised structural integrity, reduced antigenicity, and suboptimal immunogenicity, while also suffering from low yield, high cost, and operational complexity. Furthermore, conventional fermentation strategies using standard carbon source concentrations (2–4%) and short culture durations fail to support the intracellular conditions necessary for proper L1 folding and assembly, thereby limiting the quality of the starting material for purification.

### Technical Solution

The present invention provides a novel method for purifying HPV L1 proteins that significantly enhances both purity and immunogenicity. The method comprises culturing transformed host cells—preferably *Saccharomyces cerevisiae* engineered to express codon-optimized HPV L1 genes—in a medium containing an elevated concentration of carbon source (6% to 8% total, typically as a 1:1 mixture of glucose and galactose) for an extended duration (approximately 6 days at 30°C). Following culture, the host cells are disrupted using mechanical means such as glass bead vortexing to release intracellular contents. The resulting homogenate is then treated with a reducing agent, such as β-mercaptoethanol (β-ME) or dithiothreitol (DTT), at a concentration ranging from 1 mM to 50 mM, preferably 10 mM to 20 mM, and allowed to react for 15 to 120 minutes at room temperature. This reducing step facilitates disulfide bond rearrangement, promoting correct L1 folding and VLP assembly.

In a key embodiment, the method further includes a thermal treatment step comprising heating the homogenate to a temperature between 45°C and 65°C, preferably 56°C, for a duration of 10 to 60 minutes, followed by rapid chilling on ice or at 0–4°C for 10 to 30 minutes. This heating-chilling cycle selectively denatures and precipitates host cell contaminants while stabilizing properly assembled VLPs. The treated lysate is then subjected to chromatography-based purification. In a preferred implementation, two sequential chromatography steps are employed using heparin affinity resin. The first chromatography involves loading the sample—either dialyzed or non-dialyzed—onto a heparin column equilibrated in binding buffer (e.g., phosphate-buffered saline with 0.5 M NaCl and 0.01% Tween 80), washing with five column volumes, and eluting bound proteins with increasing salt gradients (e.g., 0.6–1.0 M NaCl). The L1-containing fractions are pooled, concentrated, and optionally subjected to a second heparin chromatography under identical or optimized conditions to achieve higher purity. Alternative chromatographic methods, including cation-exchange (e.g., phosphocellulose P-11) or size-exclusion chromatography, may also be used, though heparin chromatography is particularly advantageous due to the high affinity of HPV L1 for heparin sulfate proteoglycans, mimicking natural receptor interactions and thereby enriching for conformationally intact VLPs.

### Advantageous Effects

The disclosed purification method yields HPV L1 VLPs of markedly enhanced purity, structural integrity, and immunogenicity compared to conventional approaches. By combining optimized upstream culture conditions (high carbon source, extended duration) with a tailored downstream process featuring reducing agent treatment and controlled thermal cycling, the method promotes intracellular and extracellular maturation of L1 into authentic VLPs while efficiently removing host contaminants. The resulting VLPs display superior reactivity with conformation-dependent neutralizing monoclonal antibodies (e.g., H16.V5 and H16.E70), exhibit smaller hydrodynamic diameters consistent with compact, well-assembled particles, and induce significantly higher titers of neutralizing antibodies in vivo. These improvements translate into a more potent vaccine antigen, enabling dose-sparing and broader protection. Additionally, the method is scalable, avoids ultracentrifugation, reduces processing time, and lowers manufacturing costs, thereby enhancing industrial applicability and global accessibility of HPV vaccines.

## MODES OF THE INVENTION

The invention may be practiced in various modes, all falling within the scope of the claims. In one mode, *S. cerevisiae* strain Y2805 is transformed with a plasmid encoding codon-optimized HPV16 L1 and cultured in YPDG medium containing 6% or 8% total carbon source (3% glucose + 3% galactose or 4% each) for six days. Cells are harvested, disrupted with glass beads, and the lysate treated with 10–20 mM β-ME for 30–60 minutes. The mixture is then heated to 56°C for 30 minutes and immediately chilled on ice for 20 minutes. After centrifugation to remove precipitated debris, the supernatant is loaded onto a heparin column without prior dialysis. Elution with 0.8–0.9 M NaCl yields highly pure L1 VLPs. In another mode, the same process is applied to HPV18 or HPV58 L1 proteins, demonstrating broad applicability across high-risk HPV types. The method may also incorporate ammonium sulfate precipitation as an initial capture step, though it is less effective than the thermal-reduction approach in preserving conformational epitopes.

## EXAMPLES

In a representative example, *S. cerevisiae* expressing HPV16 L1 was cultured in media containing 2%, 4%, 6%, or 8% carbon source. Cell disruption was performed using glass beads, followed by treatment with 20 mM β-ME for 1 hour. One set of samples underwent heating at 56°C for 30 minutes and chilling on ice for 20 minutes (T-5 method), while another set was processed without thermal treatment (T-1 method). Purification involved two rounds of heparin chromatography. SDS-PAGE and Western blotting confirmed that T-5 yielded L1 protein of >95% purity, whereas T-1 and non-treatment methods showed significant contaminant bands. Dynamic light scattering revealed hydrodynamic diameters of ~55 nm for T-5 VLPs versus >65 nm for others, indicating tighter assembly. Transmission electron microscopy visualized uniform, spherical VLPs only in T-5 preparations. Antigenicity was assessed via ELISA using H16.V5 and H16.E70 monoclonal antibodies; T-5 VLPs exhibited 2–3-fold higher reactivity. BALB/c mice immunized with 10 ng of T-5 VLPs generated neutralizing antibody titers 9-fold higher than those receiving VLPs from 2% carbon cultures. Similar results were obtained for HPV18 and HPV58 L1 VLPs purified via T-5, confirming cross-type efficacy. Statistical analysis (ANOVA, p<0.01) validated the superiority of the combined high-carbon culture and T-5 purification protocol. Comparative experiments demonstrated that omission of either β-ME treatment or the heating-chilling step resulted in significant loss of purity and immunogenicity, underscoring the necessity of both components. Ammonium sulfate precipitation alone failed to match the performance of heparin chromatography in preserving conformational integrity. These examples collectively demonstrate that the invention enables reproducible, high-yield production of immunologically superior HPV VLPs suitable for next-generation vaccines.

## INDUSTRIAL APPLICABILITY

The HPV L1 protein purification method disclosed herein is directly applicable to the industrial-scale manufacture of prophylactic HPV vaccines. By integrating optimized fermentation with a streamlined, chromatography-based downstream process that enhances VLP quality, the invention reduces production costs while improving vaccine potency. This method is compatible with Good Manufacturing Practice (GMP) standards and can be implemented in existing biopharmaceutical facilities using standard equipment, thereby facilitating rapid technology transfer and global deployment. The resulting high-purity, high-immunogenicity VLPs are ideal for formulating multivalent vaccines targeting multiple high-risk HPV types, contributing to the eradication of cervical and other HPV-related cancers worldwide.
