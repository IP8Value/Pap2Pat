# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

Hematologic diseases encompass a broad spectrum of disorders affecting the components of blood, including red blood cells, white blood cells, platelets, and plasma proteins. Among these, abnormalities in hemoglobin—the oxygen-carrying protein within red blood cells—are particularly prevalent and clinically significant. Hemoglobin plays a central role in maintaining physiologic homeostasis by facilitating the transport of oxygen from the lungs to peripheral tissues and returning carbon dioxide for exhalation. When hemoglobin concentrations fall below established thresholds—typically less than 13 g/dL in adult males and less than 12 g/dL in adult females—the resulting condition, known as anemia, can lead to systemic hypoxia, fatigue, cognitive impairment, and increased risk of cardiovascular complications such as heart attack and stroke. Anemia may arise from diverse etiologies, including acute or chronic blood loss (e.g., gastrointestinal bleeding or heavy menstruation), impaired erythropoiesis due to nutritional deficiencies (notably iron, vitamin B12, or folate), bone marrow disorders, or accelerated red blood cell destruction as seen in hemolytic conditions.

A particularly severe inherited form of hemoglobinopathy is sickle cell disease (SCD), which results from a point mutation in the β-globin gene leading to the production of abnormal hemoglobin S. This variant polymerizes under low-oxygen conditions, causing red blood cells to adopt a rigid, sickle-like morphology that obstructs microvascular flow, induces hemolysis, and triggers recurrent vaso-occlusive crises. SCD imposes a substantial global health burden, with over 250,000 affected newborns annually in sub-Saharan Africa alone and estimated annual healthcare costs exceeding $1.5 billion in the United States. Given the high prevalence and clinical urgency of hemoglobin-related disorders—impacting over 1.6 billion people worldwide—accurate and timely assessment of hemoglobin concentration is a cornerstone of diagnostic and therapeutic decision-making across primary care, emergency medicine, obstetrics, and chronic disease management.

Current standard methods for hemoglobin quantification rely predominantly on invasive laboratory techniques, most notably the cyanmethemoglobin assay, which involves drawing venous blood, lysing red blood cells, converting all hemoglobin species to cyanmethemoglobin, and measuring absorbance spectrophotometrically at 540 nm. While this method is considered the gold standard for accuracy, it suffers from several critical limitations: it requires trained phlebotomists, specialized laboratory infrastructure, reagents with limited shelf life, and turnaround times that delay clinical intervention. Moreover, repeated venipuncture causes patient discomfort, anxiety, and risk of infection or hematoma, particularly problematic in pediatric populations, individuals with needle phobia (affecting approximately 10% of the global population), and those requiring frequent monitoring such as patients with chronic kidney disease or undergoing chemotherapy. Even minimally invasive alternatives—such as finger-prick tests using portable hemoglobinometers—still involve skin puncture and are susceptible to errors from inadequate sample volume, improper technique, or environmental factors.

In response to these challenges, there has been growing interest in non-invasive point-of-care (POC) tools capable of estimating hemoglobin levels without blood extraction. Such technologies promise enhanced patient compliance, reduced healthcare costs, and expanded access in resource-limited settings where laboratory infrastructure is scarce. However, existing commercial non-invasive devices often suffer from poor portability, high cost, complex operation requiring external modules, or insufficient accuracy across diverse populations. The advent of smartphones—equipped with high-resolution cameras, powerful processors, and programmable lighting—offers a transformative platform for developing affordable, user-friendly, and widely deployable POC hemoglobin assessment systems. Nevertheless, realizing this potential requires overcoming significant technical hurdles related to signal acquisition fidelity, motion artifact suppression, ambient light interference, and robust algorithmic modeling that accounts for physiological and demographic variability. Thus, there remains an unmet need for a reliable, non-invasive hemoglobin measurement method that leverages ubiquitous mobile technology while ensuring clinical-grade accuracy through rigorous signal processing and machine learning frameworks.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides a novel method and system for non-invasively determining hemoglobin concentration in human subjects using a smartphone or similar mobile computing device equipped with a camera and controllable light sources. The method begins by acquiring a time-based series of digital images—typically in video format—of a selected anatomical site, preferably the fingertip, while illuminating the tissue with specific wavelengths of near-infrared (NIR) light. Each acquired image frame is subdivided into spatial blocks, and for each block, a time-series signal is generated by tracking pixel intensity variations across consecutive frames. These signals are then processed to isolate photoplethysmographic (PPG) waveforms, which reflect pulsatile blood volume changes synchronized with the cardiac cycle. Individual PPG cycles are identified through peak detection algorithms applied to the time-series data.

From these extracted PPG cycles, key features are computed to serve as inputs for hemoglobin estimation. Critically, the method employs dual-wavelength illumination—specifically at 850 nm and 1070 nm—to exploit differential absorption characteristics of hemoglobin and plasma in the NIR spectrum. For each wavelength, the PPG signal is normalized by computing the ratio of its alternating current (AC) component (representing pulsatile arterial blood) to its direct current (DC) component (representing baseline tissue and venous blood). The ratio of these normalized signals—denoted R850/R1070—is then calculated, forming a primary predictor strongly correlated with hemoglobin concentration. Additional morphological features of the PPG waveform, such as rise time, peak amplitude, diastolic notch depth, and pulse area, are also extracted to enrich the feature set.

These features are subsequently input into a predictive model, specifically a Support Vector Machine Regression (SVR) algorithm, which has been trained on a dataset pairing non-invasive PPG-derived features with corresponding laboratory-measured hemoglobin values. The SVR model outputs an estimated hemoglobin concentration in grams per deciliter (g/dL). The entire process is implemented within a mobile application that coordinates image capture, real-time signal processing, feature extraction, and model inference, delivering results directly to the user interface within seconds.

The accompanying system comprises a mobile computing device with an integrated camera sensor, controllable NIR light-emitting diodes (LEDs) emitting at 850 nm and 1070 nm, and a light-restrictive enclosure to minimize ambient light interference. The LEDs are configured to illuminate the ventral pad of a subject’s finger placed over the camera lens, enabling transmittance-mode PPG signal acquisition. Processed data may be stored locally or transmitted via wireless networks to remote servers for model updating, quality assurance, or integration with electronic health records. The system is designed for flexibility, supporting variations in hardware configuration, body site selection (e.g., palpebral conjunctiva as an alternative), and adaptive recalibration based on user-specific or population-level data. This approach enables accurate, rapid, painless, and cost-effective hemoglobin screening suitable for home use, community health programs, and clinical triage.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The invention is grounded in the principles of photoplethysmography (PPG), an optical technique that detects volumetric changes in blood circulation by measuring light absorption or reflection from perfused tissue. A typical PPG system consists of a light source, a photodetector (here, a smartphone CMOS camera sensor), and signal processing circuitry. When NIR light is transmitted through or reflected from tissue such as the fingertip, the intensity of detected light fluctuates with each heartbeat due to cyclic expansion and contraction of arterioles. These fluctuations constitute the PPG signal, which comprises a static DC component (baseline absorption from non-pulsatile tissue, venous blood, and bone) and a dynamic AC component (superimposed pulsations from arterial blood inflow).

Near-infrared light in the range of 700–1100 nm is employed because it falls within the “tissue optical window,” where absorption by water, lipids, and melanin is minimized, allowing deeper penetration (up to 1–2 cm) and stronger interaction with hemoglobin. Hemoglobin exhibits distinct absorption spectra depending on its oxygenation state, but both oxy- and deoxy-hemoglobin absorb more strongly at 850 nm than at 1070 nm, whereas blood plasma shows relatively flat absorption across this range. By capturing PPG signals at both wavelengths simultaneously or sequentially, the method isolates hemoglobin-specific contributions from confounding factors like tissue scattering and plasma content.

In operation, the subject places their fingertip over the smartphone camera lens, which is surrounded by 850 nm and 1070 nm NIR LEDs. The LEDs illuminate the dorsal side of the finger, and transmitted light exiting the ventral pad is captured by the camera as a video sequence (e.g., 30 frames per second for 15 seconds). Each video frame is preprocessed to identify a region of interest (ROI)—typically the central portion of the fingertip—and subdivided into uniform blocks (e.g., 10×10 grid). For each block, the average pixel intensity over time is extracted, yielding multiple time-series signals. These are bandpass-filtered (e.g., 0.5–5 Hz using a Butterworth filter) to remove respiratory artifacts, motion noise, and high-frequency camera sensor noise, adhering to the Nyquist sampling criterion.

The filtered signal is further refined by cropping to exclude periods of excessive motion or signal dropout, resulting in a clean PPG waveform. The AC component is derived as the peak-to-trough amplitude of individual cardiac cycles, while the DC component is the mean signal level over the same interval. Normalized PPG signals are computed as AC/DC for both 850 nm (R850) and 1070 nm (R1070). The ratio R = R850 / R1070 is then calculated, serving as a primary hemoglobin-sensitive metric. Additional features—including systolic rise time, dicrotic notch prominence, pulse width at half-maximum, and harmonic content via Fourier analysis—are extracted to capture waveform morphology.

These features undergo normalization (e.g., z-score scaling) and are fed into a pre-trained SVR model with a radial basis function kernel. The model was developed using a training dataset of paired non-invasive features and laboratory hemoglobin values from diverse subjects, with hyperparameters optimized via cross-validation. Performance is evaluated using mean absolute percentage error (MAPE = (1/n) Σ |(Aₜ − Eₜ)/Aₜ| × 100%), Pearson correlation coefficient (r), and Bland-Altman analysis to assess bias and limits of agreement against reference measurements.

The system architecture includes a mobile application that controls LED activation, video capture, real-time signal processing, and result display. Data may be encrypted and transmitted to a cloud server for longitudinal tracking or model refinement. The hardware may incorporate a detachable light-tight enclosure to ensure consistent illumination and exclude ambient light. Alternative configurations support reflectance-mode operation (camera and LEDs on same side) or use of other body sites like the lower eyelid. The invention thus provides a scalable, accurate, and accessible solution for non-invasive hemoglobin monitoring, addressing critical gaps in global anemia screening and management.
