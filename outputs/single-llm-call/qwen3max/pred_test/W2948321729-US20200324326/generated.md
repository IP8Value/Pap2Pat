# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates to remediation methods of heavy metal contaminated soil. More specifically, the invention provides an integrated system and method for the efficient, sustainable, and scalable removal of toxic heavy metal cations from contaminated soils through a novel electrochemical filtration process combined with a recirculating chelating agent washing system. The invention is particularly suited for application in agricultural lands, urban redevelopment zones, and industrial sites where heavy metal contamination poses significant environmental and public health risks. By leveraging asymmetrical alternating current electrochemistry (AACE) in conjunction with functionalized porous carbon electrodes, the invention enables not only the extraction and immobilization of heavy metals in their zero-valent metallic states but also the recovery and reuse of chelating agents, thereby minimizing chemical consumption, preventing secondary pollution, and preserving essential soil nutrients.

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

Soil contamination represents a pressing global environmental crisis, exacerbated by rapid industrialization, mining activities, and intensive agricultural practices. Among the most hazardous contaminants are heavy metals—such as cadmium (Cd), lead (Pb), and copper (Cu)—which accumulate in the upper crust of the Earth due to anthropogenic emissions and waste disposal. These metals persist indefinitely in the environment, bioaccumulate in the food chain, and pose severe risks to human health, including neurotoxicity, carcinogenicity, and organ damage. Consequently, the remediation of heavy metal–contaminated soils is critical for sustainable land use, especially as demand for arable land increases worldwide.

Conventional approaches to soil remediation include soil washing with strong chelating agents such as ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA). While effective at mobilizing heavy metal cations from soil particles by forming soluble complexes, this method suffers from several critical limitations. First, it requires large quantities of chelating agents, which are costly and environmentally burdensome to produce. Second, the resulting washing effluent—laden with toxic metal-chelate complexes—presents a secondary waste stream that is difficult to treat without generating additional pollution. Third, non-selective chelation often strips essential nutrient cations like calcium (Ca²⁺) and magnesium (Mg²⁺) from the soil, leading to long-term degradation of soil fertility and structure.

Alternative strategies, such as the application of high-surface-area sorbents, aim to immobilize heavy metals by adsorption. However, these materials rely on physicochemical interactions that are inherently slow, capacity-limited, and reversible under changing environmental conditions. Moreover, immobilized metals remain in the soil matrix, requiring perpetual monitoring to ensure stability and prevent remobilization.

Phytoremediation—using plants to uptake and sequester heavy metals—offers an energy-efficient and aesthetically benign solution. Yet its practical utility is constrained by extremely slow kinetics, often requiring years to achieve meaningful decontamination. Additionally, the harvested biomass becomes a hazardous waste product that must be safely disposed of or processed, introducing risks of secondary contamination.

Electrochemical methods have emerged as promising alternatives because they can directly reduce heavy metal cations to their zero-valent, elemental forms—a thermodynamically stable and potentially recoverable state. Traditional direct current (DC) electrokinetic remediation applies a constant electric field across soil to drive ion migration via electroosmosis, followed by electrodeposition at the cathode. Despite its conceptual appeal, this approach is hindered by high energy demands, inefficient ion transport in low-permeability soils, and parasitic reactions such as water electrolysis, which generate gas bubbles, alter local pH, and consume substantial electrical power without contributing to metal removal.

Furthermore, when applied to chelated metal complexes—such as MEDTA²⁻ (where M = Cd, Pb, Cu)—conventional DC systems face a fundamental electrostatic barrier: the negatively charged complexes are repelled by the cathode, drastically reducing deposition efficiency. This limitation renders standard electrochemical filtration ineffective for real-world soil washing effluents, where metals exist predominantly as anionic chelates.

Thus, there remains a critical unmet need for a remediation technology that combines high efficiency, chemical sustainability, minimal nutrient loss, and applicability to diverse contamination scenarios—particularly one capable of transforming mobile, toxic metal ions into inert, recoverable metallic solids while enabling closed-loop recycling of reagents.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention introduces a novel method and system for the remediation of heavy metal–contaminated soil, designated herein as the Asymmetrical Alternating Current Electrochemistry (AACE) method. This integrated approach couples a recirculating soil washing system with an AACE filtration device to achieve rapid, selective, and sustainable decontamination. In operation, a chelating agent—such as EDTA—is circulated through excavated contaminated soil to solubilize heavy metal cations into anionic complexes (e.g., MEDTA²⁻). The resulting effluent is then passed through an electrochemical filter comprising amidoxime-functionalized porous carbon (Ami-PC) electrodes, where an asymmetrical alternating voltage waveform is applied to first capture and then reduce the metal cations to their zero-valent metallic states.

The AACE method overcomes the limitations of prior art by exploiting a two-phase voltage cycle: a brief positive bias (e.g., +5 V) establishes an electrical double layer that attracts anionic metal-chelate complexes to the electrode surface, where amidoxime functional groups competitively bind the metal cations; this is followed by a longer negative bias (e.g., −10 V) that electrochemically reduces the bound cations to elemental metals, while simultaneously repelling the now-unbound EDTA anions back into solution. Critically, essential nutrient cations—such as Ca²⁺ and Mg²⁺—also form chelates but remain in solution during the reduction phase due to their more negative reduction potentials, thereby preserving soil fertility upon recirculation.

Key advantages of the AACE method include dramatically reduced chelating agent consumption through continuous recycling, elimination of secondary liquid waste streams, high remediation speed (achieving regulatory compliance within hours), and the production of recoverable metallic deposits rather than unstable precipitates. The method demonstrates exceptional sustainability, making it feasible for agricultural applications where soil quality must be maintained post-treatment. Exemplary chelating agents include EDTA, NTA, DTPA, and citric acid, while target heavy metal cations encompass Cd²⁺, Pb²⁺, Cu²⁺, Zn²⁺, Ni²⁺, and Hg²⁺, among others prevalent in industrial and mining waste.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The AACE remediation method is implemented through an integrated system comprising a recirculating chelating agent washing subsystem and an AACE filtration device. Contaminated soil is excavated and placed in a treatment column, through which a chelating solution—typically 30 mM EDTA disodium salt—is continuously pumped. The chelating agent mobilizes heavy metal cations by forming water-soluble anionic complexes, which are carried in the effluent to the AACE filter. This filter consists of two parallel Ami-PC electrodes separated by a porous insulator, connected to an AC power supply capable of generating asymmetrical waveforms.

The Ami-PC electrodes are fabricated by dip-coating carbon felt substrates with a slurry of polyacrylonitrile (PAN) and activated carbon nanoparticles in N,N-dimethylformamide, followed by hydrothermal treatment with hydroxylamine hydrochloride and sodium carbonate to convert nitrile groups to amidoxime functionalities. This modification renders the electrode surface hydrophilic and introduces high-affinity chelation sites that outcompete EDTA for metal binding under applied bias. The resulting electrode morphology features a three-dimensional porous network with fiber diameters of approximately 20 μm and pore sizes ranging from tens to hundreds of micrometers, providing high surface area and efficient fluid permeability.

The AACE method operates in three sequential steps per cycle. In Step I, the washing effluent containing randomly dispersed ions enters the filtration chamber. In Step II, a +5 V bias is applied for 0.5 milliseconds, inducing migration of anions—including MEDTA²⁻ complexes—toward the working electrode. Amidoxime groups on the electrode surface selectively bind the metal cations, effectively stripping them from EDTA and anchoring the complexes near the electrode interface. In Step III, the bias reverses to −10 V for 2 milliseconds, driving the electrochemical reduction of the bound metal cations to zero-valent atoms that nucleate into micrometer-scale metallic particles on the electrode surface. Simultaneously, the now-decomplexed EDTA²⁻ anions are electrostatically repelled back into the bulk solution due to the negative electrode potential and lack of affinity for neutral metal particles.

Remediation performance was evaluated using synthetically contaminated soils spiked with Cu (10,000 ppm), Pb (1,000 ppm), or Cd (100 ppm), aged to simulate field conditions. After treatment, all metals were reduced below California Human Health Screening Levels for residential use. Filtration efficiency—the fraction of metals removed from effluent—exceeded 90% at optimized flow rates (e.g., 0.1 mL/min), with higher flow rates diminishing contact time and lowering efficiency. Nutrient metals like Mg²⁺ were initially extracted but reached equilibrium after the first cycle, preventing further depletion; in contrast, fresh EDTA washing caused progressive nutrient loss.

In mixed-contamination tests (Cu, Pb, Cd co-spiked), AACE achieved >97% removal of all metals within 6 hours, whereas DC electrochemical filtration under identical conditions achieved only 30–70% removal due to inefficient complex handling and competitive precipitation of Ca(OH)₂. Characterization via XPS confirmed that AACE produced predominantly zero-valent Cu, Pb, and Cd, while DC yielded metal hydroxides and oxides. TEM analysis verified crystalline metallic structures with lattice spacings matching Cu(110), Pb(110), and Cd(002) planes.

Long-term testing over 25 cycles showed only 20% decline in EDTA extraction capacity, with no significant accumulation in treated soil. Electrode fouling after 14 cycles reduced filtration efficiency to ~90%, but regeneration via 0.1 M HCl elution with +1 V DC bias fully restored performance. Plant assays using pea sprouts demonstrated that AACE-treated soil supported normal growth with Cd levels in tissues below 0.1 ppm—well under international food safety standards—whereas fresh EDTA washing caused stunted growth due to nutrient depletion. These results confirm the AACE method’s feasibility for agricultural remediation without compromising soil health.
