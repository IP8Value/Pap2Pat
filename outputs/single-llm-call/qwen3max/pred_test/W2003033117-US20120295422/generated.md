# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

Group III–V nitride semiconductors, particularly indium gallium nitride (InGaN), have emerged as critical materials in the development of high-efficiency light-emitting diodes (LEDs) for solid-state lighting and display technologies. Among these, InGaN-based LEDs have enabled commercialization of blue and green emitters, which are foundational components in full-color displays, backlighting systems, automotive lighting, and general illumination. The widespread adoption of such devices has been driven by their superior luminous efficacy, long operational lifetimes, and environmental benefits compared to traditional incandescent and fluorescent light sources. However, despite significant advances in epitaxial growth and device engineering, challenges remain in achieving high internal quantum efficiency—particularly for green and longer-wavelength emitters—due to intrinsic material limitations associated with the incorporation of indium into the GaN lattice.

A key structural element in modern InGaN-based LEDs is the multi-quantum well (MQW) active region, which typically consists of alternating layers of InGaN potential wells and GaN potential barriers. This MQW architecture confines charge carriers within the lower-bandgap InGaN wells, enhancing radiative recombination and light emission. The performance of such devices is highly sensitive to the crystalline quality, compositional uniformity, and interfacial abruptness of the InGaN/GaN heterostructure. In particular, achieving high indium content is essential for extending emission wavelengths into the green and amber spectral ranges; however, this requirement conflicts with the thermodynamic instability of indium during high-temperature growth processes commonly used for GaN barrier layers.

Conventional fabrication methods for InGaN/GaN MQWs involve sequential deposition of an InGaN well layer at a relatively low temperature (typically 700–800°C), followed immediately by ramping the substrate temperature to a higher value (often exceeding 900°C) for subsequent GaN barrier growth. During this thermal transition, the previously deposited InGaN layer is exposed to elevated temperatures in a nitrogen-rich ambient, leading to partial decomposition of the InGaN film. This decomposition manifests as indium desorption, phase segregation, and roughening of the well/barrier interface, all of which degrade optical performance through increased non-radiative recombination centers and inhomogeneous carrier confinement. These effects are especially pronounced in green-emitting structures requiring high indium concentrations, where even minor thermal degradation can significantly reduce photoluminescence intensity and broaden emission linewidths.

The use of alternative substrates such as silicon offers a promising route to cost reduction in GaN-based LED manufacturing, given silicon’s low cost, large wafer availability, and compatibility with established microfabrication techniques. However, growing high-quality InGaN MQWs on silicon introduces additional complexities related to lattice mismatch, thermal expansion differences, and defect propagation, further exacerbating the sensitivity of the active region to thermal processing conditions. Consequently, there exists a pressing need for novel fabrication methodologies that mitigate thermal degradation of InGaN quantum wells during MQW formation, thereby enabling high-efficiency, long-wavelength GaN-based LEDs on scalable and economical substrates.

This need is further underscored by the persistent “green gap”—a well-documented phenomenon wherein the external quantum efficiency of InGaN LEDs drops markedly as emission shifts from blue toward green wavelengths. Addressing this gap requires not only improved indium incorporation but also precise control over quantum well integrity throughout the entire epitaxial process. Thus, innovations that stabilize the InGaN well layer against thermal decomposition during subsequent high-temperature steps represent a critical advancement toward closing the green gap and realizing high-performance, cost-effective solid-state lighting across the visible spectrum.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides a method for fabricating an active region of a gallium nitride (GaN)-based light-emitting diode (LED) that substantially mitigates thermal degradation of indium gallium nitride (InGaN) quantum wells during epitaxial growth. The method comprises growing an InGaN potential well layer at a first, relatively low growth temperature, followed by the immediate deposition of a thin GaN cap layer at the same temperature as the well layer. This GaN cap layer acts as a protective barrier that shields the underlying InGaN from exposure to elevated temperatures during subsequent processing steps. After capping, the structure undergoes a controlled annealing step at an intermediate temperature to stabilize the InGaN/GaN interface without inducing indium desorption or phase separation. Finally, a GaN potential barrier layer is grown at a second, higher temperature suitable for high-quality GaN crystallization, completing one period of the multi-quantum well (MQW) structure.

By maintaining the InGaN well under a protective GaN cap during the temperature ramp to barrier growth conditions, the invention prevents decomposition, indium segregation, and interfacial roughening that typically occur in conventional processes. The thickness of the GaN cap layer is a critical parameter, with optimal performance achieved when the cap is between approximately 15 Å and 30 Å. Thinner caps (e.g., 8 Å) provide insufficient protection, while excessively thick caps may introduce strain or impede carrier injection. The annealing step further enhances crystalline quality by promoting atomic rearrangement at the well/cap interface, resulting in improved compositional homogeneity and sharper heterojunctions. This approach enables the fabrication of high-indium-content InGaN/GaN MQWs with abrupt interfaces, uniform indium distribution, and enhanced photoluminescence intensity—key attributes for efficient green and longer-wavelength LEDs.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

### Overview

The invention pertains to an improved method for fabricating the active region of GaN-based LEDs, specifically designed to preserve the integrity of InGaN quantum wells during epitaxial growth on silicon substrates. In a representative embodiment, a single InGaN/GaN quantum well structure is grown using metalorganic chemical vapor deposition (MOCVD). The process begins with the deposition of an n-type GaN layer on a silicon (111) substrate, followed by the growth of an InGaN potential well at a temperature of approximately 750°C. Immediately thereafter, without changing the substrate temperature, a thin GaN cap layer is deposited directly atop the InGaN well. A temperature versus time diagram illustrates this sequence: after well growth, the system holds at the well temperature during cap deposition, then ramps gradually to a higher temperature (e.g., 870°C) for barrier growth, with an optional intermediate anneal plateau.

For multi-quantum well (MQW) structures, this sequence is repeated multiple times to form periodic stacks of InGaN wells separated by GaN barriers. An exemplary LED cross-section includes, from bottom to top: a silicon (111) substrate, an AlN/AlGaN stress-relief layer, an n-GaN cladding layer, an InGaN strain-prelayer, six periods of InGaN/GaN MQWs fabricated according to the inventive method, and a p-type GaN:Mg contact layer. In contrast, the conventional process involves growing the InGaN well and then immediately increasing the temperature for GaN barrier deposition without any intervening cap layer. A flow chart of the conventional method shows direct transition from well growth to high-temperature barrier growth, exposing the InGaN to thermal stress that causes decomposition.

This conventional approach suffers from significant limitations: indium desorption leads to compositional inhomogeneity, interfacial diffusion blurs the well/barrier boundary, and localized bright spots appear in fluorescence microscopy due to indium-rich clusters. These defects reduce radiative efficiency and broaden emission spectra. The need for a new method arises from the inability of existing techniques to simultaneously achieve high indium content, interface abruptness, and thermal stability—particularly for green-emitting devices.

The inventive process addresses these shortcomings through a three-step sequence per quantum well period: (1) fabricating the InGaN potential well at low temperature; (2) annealing and stabilizing the well via deposition of a same-temperature GaN cap followed by a brief thermal stabilization step; and (3) fabricating the GaN potential barrier at elevated temperature. The GaN cap, typically 15–30 Å thick, physically isolates the InGaN from the high-temperature environment during subsequent steps. Annealing at an intermediate temperature (e.g., 800–830°C) for 30–120 seconds promotes interface smoothing and defect annihilation without triggering indium loss. Finally, the GaN barrier is grown at standard high temperatures (≥870°C) to ensure high crystalline quality.

This method yields MQWs with superior structural and optical properties: X-ray diffraction reveals sharp satellite peaks indicating periodicity and abrupt interfaces; fluorescence microscopy shows uniform emission without bright clusters; and photoluminescence exhibits higher intensity, narrower full-width-at-half-maximum (FWHM), and red-shifted peak wavelength—evidence of increased and homogeneous indium incorporation. Collectively, these advantages enable high-efficiency green LEDs on low-cost silicon substrates, overcoming longstanding barriers in solid-state lighting technology.

## EXAMPLE

An exemplary embodiment of the invention involves the fabrication of an InGaN/GaN multi-quantum well active region for a green LED grown on a silicon (111) substrate using a Thomas Swan 6×2″ MOCVD system with a close-coupled showerhead reactor. Prior to growth, the silicon substrate is cleaned by immersion in a H₂SO₄:H₂O₂ (3:1) solution followed by a 5% HF etch to remove organic contaminants and native oxide. The substrate is then loaded into the reactor and subjected to in situ annealing in hydrogen at 1100°C for 30 minutes. A thin aluminum film is deposited at 800°C using trimethylaluminum (TMAl) in hydrogen to prevent silicon melt-back and nitridation during subsequent nitride growth. Thereafter, a 0.3 μm AlN/AlₓGa₁₋ₓN stress-relief layer and a 3 μm Si-doped n-GaN layer are grown.

Next, a 200 nm InGaN strain-prelayer is deposited, followed by the MQW stack. Each MQW period is formed as follows: first, a 2.5 nm InGaN potential well is grown at 750°C using trimethylindium (TMIn), trimethylgallium (TMGa), and ammonia (NH₃). Immediately after well completion, without altering the substrate temperature, a GaN cap layer is deposited using TMGa and NH₃. In this example, the cap thickness is precisely controlled to 30 Å (sample C). Following cap deposition, the substrate is held at 810°C for 60 seconds to anneal and stabilize the InGaN/GaN interface, allowing surface atoms to rearrange and reducing point defects. Subsequently, the temperature is ramped to 870°C, and a 12 nm GaN potential barrier is grown using TMGa and NH₃. This sequence is repeated six times to form the complete MQW structure. Finally, a 130 nm Mg-doped p-GaN layer is deposited at 950°C to complete the LED epitaxial structure.

Characterization confirms that this process yields a high-quality active region: double-crystal X-ray diffraction shows clear satellite peaks up to the fourth order, indicating excellent periodicity and abrupt interfaces; fluorescence microscopy reveals a uniform emission profile without bright spots; and photoluminescence measurements exhibit a peak emission at 530 nm with high intensity and narrow FWHM—demonstrating successful preservation of indium content and homogeneity. This embodiment exemplifies how the inventive method enables robust, high-performance green LEDs on silicon, combining cost efficiency with superior optoelectronic properties.
