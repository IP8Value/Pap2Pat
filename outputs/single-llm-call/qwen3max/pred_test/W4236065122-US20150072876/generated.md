## BACKGROUND

Gene-expression profiling has emerged as a powerful tool for understanding biological processes, diagnosing diseases, and guiding therapeutic interventions. However, the utility of such profiling is significantly constrained when applied to heterogeneous tissue samples, which inherently consist of multiple cell types in varying proportions. Changes observed in bulk gene-expression data may reflect shifts in cellular composition rather than genuine alterations in the transcriptional activity of specific cell populations. For instance, in breast cancer biopsies, variations in tumor cell content have been shown to profoundly influence expression profiles, thereby confounding differential expression analyses unless cellular proportions are explicitly accounted for. Consequently, accurate interpretation of gene-expression data from whole tissues necessitates methods capable of deconvoluting the mixed signal into its constituent cell-type-specific components.

Current experimental approaches to address this challenge include laser-capture microdissection and fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS), both of which enable physical isolation of specific cell populations prior to RNA extraction and profiling. While effective, these techniques are labor-intensive, often yield insufficient RNA for robust analysis without amplification—which itself can introduce technical artifacts—and are generally impractical for large-scale studies or retrospective analyses of archived samples. Single-cell RNA sequencing offers a promising alternative by directly profiling individual cells; however, its high cost and computational complexity limit its widespread adoption, particularly in studies involving large patient cohorts. As a result, the vast majority of publicly available gene-expression datasets remain derived from heterogeneous tissue samples, representing an underutilized resource for cell-type-resolved biological insights.

In response to these limitations, several computational deconvolution methods have been developed to infer cell-type-specific expression profiles and their relative abundances from bulk tissue data. These methods typically rely on a linear mixing model, wherein the observed expression in a sample is modeled as a weighted sum of the expression signatures of its constituent cell types, with weights corresponding to cellular proportions. Despite their theoretical appeal, existing algorithms impose stringent requirements regarding prior knowledge: some demand exact knowledge of the number of cell types present, others require predefined purified reference signatures matched to the specific experimental context, and still others necessitate known cellular proportions for at least a subset of samples. Such prerequisites are rarely met in real-world scenarios, especially when re-analyzing legacy datasets where no cell-sorting or histopathological quantification was performed. Consequently, the applicability of current deconvolution tools is largely restricted to prospectively designed studies, leaving the immense repository of historical microarray data largely inaccessible for cell-type-level interrogation.

Existing solutions thus fall short in providing a general-purpose, “plug-and-play” framework that can be applied to arbitrary heterogeneous gene-expression datasets without requiring detailed a priori information about tissue composition. This gap severely limits the ability of researchers to extract maximal biological value from the wealth of publicly available transcriptomic data, which encompasses thousands of samples across diverse diseases, tissues, and treatment conditions. There remains a critical need for a robust, blind deconvolution method that can autonomously determine the number of cell types present, identify their biological identities, estimate their relative proportions per sample, and reconstruct their individual gene-expression signatures—using only minimal and readily obtainable prior knowledge, such as a broad list of candidate cell types potentially present in the tissue of interest.

## SUMMARY

The present invention introduces a novel computational algorithm for the blind separation of heterogeneous gene-expression data into its constituent cell-type-specific components, without requiring precise prior knowledge of the number of cell types, their identities, or their proportions in the analyzed samples. The algorithm uniquely integrates non-negative matrix factorization (NMF) with a hypothesis-testing framework grounded in information-theoretic distance measures to simultaneously estimate the true number of cell types, assign biological identities to each inferred component, reconstruct cell-type-specific gene-expression signatures, and compute relative cellular proportions across all samples. Unlike existing methods, the invention operates effectively using only a user-provided set of candidate cell-type labels and corresponding purified reference gene-expression signatures—data that are abundantly available in public repositories such as the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO)—without demanding that these references be disease-, tissue-, or experiment-matched to the target dataset.

The core innovation lies in the algorithm’s ability to perform cell-type determination as an intrinsic step within the deconvolution process. By leveraging symmetric Kullback-Leibler divergence (SKLD) as a robust distance metric between estimated and reference signatures, the method identifies which candidate cell types are genuinely present in the mixture, discarding irrelevant or spurious inputs. This capability enables accurate reconstruction even when the initial candidate list includes extraneous cell types or omits fine-grained subtypes, provided that the true constituents are represented among the candidates. Furthermore, the algorithm incorporates enhancements such as majority voting across multiple random initializations and class-based grouping of similar reference signatures to improve robustness against noise, signature variability, and convergence to local minima—particularly critical when resolving closely related cell populations like immune subsets.

The algorithm’s performance has been rigorously validated on multiple controlled datasets with known ground-truth compositions, including mixtures of liver, brain, and lung tissues; heart and brain cells; and distinct immune cell lines (T cells, B cells, monocytes). In each case, it accurately identified the correct number of cell types, assigned proper biological identities, reconstructed gene-expression signatures highly correlated with true purified profiles, and estimated cellular proportions with low average absolute error—often matching or exceeding the accuracy of methods that require full prior knowledge. Additionally, successful application to a semi-controlled prostate cancer dataset, where proportions were pathologist-estimated, demonstrates practical utility in real-world clinical contexts. The invention thus provides a transformative tool for re-analyzing the vast corpus of existing heterogeneous gene-expression data, unlocking cell-type-resolved insights without the need for new experimental data or extensive metadata curation.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention provides a comprehensive computational framework for the blind deconvolution of heterogeneous gene-expression data, enabling the identification of constituent cell types, their biological identities, their relative proportions per sample, and their individual gene-expression signatures. The algorithm is designed to operate without explicit prior knowledge of the exact cellular composition of the tissue under analysis, relying instead on a user-supplied list of candidate cell types and associated purified reference gene-expression signatures. These reference signatures may be sourced from public databases such as the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) and need not originate from the same disease state, tissue source, or experimental platform as the target dataset, greatly enhancing the method’s flexibility and broad applicability.

### Linear Model for Separation of Gene-Expression

The foundation of the invention rests upon a well-established linear mixing model commonly used in gene-expression deconvolution. In this model, the observed mixed gene-expression matrix **M** (of dimensions *m* genes × *n* samples) is expressed as the product of two non-negative matrices: **G**, representing cell-type-specific gene-expression signatures (*m* × *k*), and **C**, representing the relative proportions of each cell type across samples (*k* × *n*), such that **M** ≈ **G****C**. This formulation assumes that the total expression of each gene in a sample is a linear combination of the expressions contributed by each constituent cell type, weighted by their respective abundances. While this linearity assumption has been empirically validated in numerous studies and yields biologically plausible results, it does impose certain constraints: notably, it requires that the number of cell types *k* be less than or equal to the number of samples *n* to ensure identifiability, and it presumes that interactions between cell types do not induce non-additive transcriptional effects—a reasonable approximation for many biological contexts.

A key limitation of conventional implementations of this model is their dependence on a priori knowledge: either the exact value of *k*, the identities of the *k* cell types, their purified expression profiles, or their proportions in each sample. The present invention overcomes this constraint by reframing the problem as a hypothesis-testing task. Instead of assuming a fixed *k* and known identities, the algorithm begins with a superset of *k̃* ≥ *k* candidate cell types, each associated with a reference signature. The objective is then to select the correct subset of *k* cell types that best explain the observed data **M**, thereby estimating both the true number of components and their biological labels. Critically, this approach requires that the true cell types be included within the candidate set; if a genuine constituent is omitted, it cannot be recovered, and the resulting decomposition may exhibit ambiguities or inaccuracies. However, given the extensive catalog of purified cell-type profiles available in public repositories, constructing a sufficiently inclusive candidate list is feasible for most tissues of interest.

### The Relation to Hyper Spectral Imaging

The mathematical structure of the gene-expression deconvolution problem bears a striking resemblance to non-negative matrix factorization (NMF) problems encountered in hyper-spectral imaging, where the goal is to identify the constituent materials (end-members) of a remote object based on its spectral signature. In hyper-spectral analysis, a data matrix **M** represents spectral measurements across wavelengths for multiple pixels or objects, and NMF seeks to decompose **M** into an end-member matrix **G** (spectral signatures of pure materials) and a proportion matrix **C** (abundance of each material per pixel). This equivalence allows the adaptation of advanced NMF algorithms from the imaging domain to the genomics context.

Specifically, the present invention builds upon an NMF algorithm originally developed by Piper et al. for classifying space objects from ground-based telescope data. That method leverages a library of laboratory-measured spectral signatures of known materials to guide the identification of end-members in unknown objects, even when the reference signatures are not identical to the true underlying materials but merely representative. This paradigm aligns perfectly with gene-expression analysis, where purified reference profiles from disparate studies can serve as proxies for the true cell-type signatures in a heterogeneous sample. The adaptation involves replacing spectral wavelengths with gene identifiers and incorporating biological constraints such as non-negativity and column-wise normalization. Moreover, the use of prior knowledge in the form of reference signatures enhances identifiability and biological interpretability—addressing a major weakness of generic NMF, which often yields mathematically valid but biologically meaningless factors. Extensions to the original Piper algorithm include the integration of SKLD as a distance metric tailored to probability-like distributions, the implementation of majority voting to mitigate non-convexity issues, and the introduction of class-based signature grouping to handle biological variability.

### Algorithm

The algorithm comprises three integrated stages: initialization, estimation of the true number of cell types and their identities, and computation of cell-type proportions. First, the mixed expression matrix **M** and a reference signature matrix **L** (containing *k̃* candidate cell-type profiles) are normalized so that each column sums to its mean. Matrices **H** (proportion estimates) and **W** (signature estimates) are initialized with **W** seeded from **L** and **H** populated with random non-negative values. Non-negative matrix factorization is then applied to minimize the Frobenius norm ||**M** – **W****H**||² under non-negativity and column-sum constraints, yielding preliminary estimates.

In the second stage, the true number of cell types *k*<sub>CT</sub> is determined by computing the symmetric Kullback-Leibler divergence (SKLD) between each column of **W** and all columns of **L**. Each reference signature in **L** is associated with the **W** column exhibiting the smallest SKLD, and only those **W** columns selected by at least one reference are retained. The number of retained columns defines *k*<sub>CT</sub>, and their identities are inherited from the closest-matching references in **L**. To enhance robustness, the algorithm employs majority voting: it is executed multiple times with randomized **H** initializations, and a cell type is included in the final output only if it is selected in more than a user-defined threshold (e.g., 70%) of runs. The final signature matrix **Ĝ** is formed by averaging the selected **W** columns across qualifying runs.

In the third stage, the proportion matrix **Ĉ** is computed via non-negative least squares (NNLS), solving **M** = **Ĝ****Ĉ** subject to **Ĉ** ≥ 0, followed by row-wise normalization to ensure proportions sum to one per sample. The algorithm also supports class-based grouping: multiple reference signatures representing the same biological entity (e.g., different B-cell lines or heart tissue samples from separate studies) can be assigned to a single class label. During the selection phase, all **W** columns associated with a class are averaged and treated as a unified candidate, improving resolution of closely related types and mitigating noise from individual reference profiles. The pseudo-code implementation systematically executes these steps to output **Ĝ** and **Ĉ**, providing a complete deconvolution of the input data.

### Mining Purified Signatures

The success of the algorithm hinges on the availability of high-quality purified reference signatures for the candidate cell types. Fortunately, such data are extensively cataloged in public repositories like GEO, where thousands of cell-type-specific expression profiles—spanning diverse species, tissues, diseases, and experimental conditions—are freely accessible. Users need only perform a targeted search for relevant cell types (e.g., “human T cells,” “rat hepatocytes”) and select one or more representative datasets. Crucially, the references do not need to match the target study’s exact biological context; general signatures suffice, as the algorithm refines them during deconvolution. When multiple references exist for a single cell type, they can be grouped into a class to increase robustness. The primary constraint is that the total number of candidate signatures (*k̃*) must not exceed the number of samples (*n*) to maintain model identifiability. Over-guessing—i.e., including extra, potentially irrelevant cell types—is permissible and often beneficial, as the algorithm will automatically discard non-contributing candidates. Under-guessing, however, risks missing true constituents and should be avoided by consulting literature on typical tissue composition.

### Example 1

The algorithm was first validated on three controlled datasets with known ground-truth compositions. In the liver-brain-lung dataset, mixtures of rat liver, brain, and lung cells were profiled, and the algorithm was provided with reference signatures for these three plus intestine, heart, and granulosa cells. Despite the inclusion of three irrelevant candidates, the method correctly identified liver, brain, and lung as the true constituents. Estimated signatures showed high Pearson correlations (r > 0.95) with true purified profiles and minimal SKLD distances. Proportion estimates exhibited strong agreement with known values (r > 0.98), with an average absolute error of 3.4% ± 2.3%. Notably, the reconstructed signatures were closer to the true profiles than the input references, demonstrating algorithmic refinement. Similarly, in the heart-brain dataset, human heart and brain mixtures were accurately resolved using references that included oocytes and hepatocytes; class grouping of two heart and two brain references improved consistency, yielding proportion errors of just 1.7% ± 1.85%. Finally, in the T-B-Monocytes dataset—featuring closely related immune cell lines—the algorithm not only distinguished T cells, B cells, and monocytes but also resolved two distinct B-cell subtypes (Raji and IM-9), achieving proportion errors of 5.7% ± 3.3%. When B-cell subtypes were unified into a single class, error decreased to 3.67% ± 3.04, confirming the utility of class-based aggregation for biologically appropriate grouping.

### Example 2

Application to a semi-controlled prostate cancer dataset, where tumor cell proportions were estimated by pathologists, further demonstrated real-world utility. References included six prostate tumor cell lines (grouped as “tumor”) and benign epithelial/stromal cells (“other”). The algorithm’s proportion estimates correlated strongly with pathologist assessments (r = 0.82), with an average error of 12.44% ± 12.41. While higher than in controlled settings, this error is comparable to the inherent uncertainty in manual pathological estimation (reported as ~9.5–12.5% in the original study), suggesting the algorithm performs robustly even with imperfect ground truth and non-ideal reference signatures. The use of general stromal/epithelial references—rather than disease-specific BPHE/DCAE profiles—likely contributed to residual error, yet the results remain clinically informative.

### Example 3

To underscore the necessity of the cell-type determination step, the algorithm was run without it—fixing the number of cell types to six (including irrelevant candidates) in the liver-brain-lung dataset. This yielded substantially degraded performance: proportion errors increased markedly, and correlations between estimated and true signatures dropped. Misidentification of cell types (e.g., assigning lung signal to intestine) occurred due to forced inclusion of spurious components. This highlights that accurate deconvolution critically depends on correctly inferring the true number of constituents.

### Example 4

A similar ablation test on the heart-brain dataset—again forcing six cell types—produced analogous results: inflated errors, reduced correlations, and biologically implausible assignments. The algorithm’s ability to prune irrelevant candidates is thus essential for fidelity.

### Example 5

Comparison with a standard NNLS-based deconvolution (which assumes known *k* and identities) revealed that even minor errors in guessing *k* (e.g., using five or six instead of three cell types) drastically reduced accuracy in the liver-brain-lung dataset. In contrast, the full algorithm—with its built-in cell-type determination—maintained high performance regardless of initial overestimation, proving its superiority in realistic scenarios where *k* is unknown.

## Discussion

Gene-expression analysis of heterogeneous tissues remains a cornerstone of biomedical research, yet its interpretation is fundamentally limited by unmeasured cellular heterogeneity. While single-cell technologies promise resolution at the individual cell level, their cost and complexity render them unsuitable for large-scale or retrospective studies. Computational deconvolution offers a pragmatic alternative, but existing methods are hamstrung by unrealistic demands for prior knowledge—knowledge rarely available for the vast archives of public microarray data. The present invention bridges this gap by introducing the first truly blind deconvolution algorithm capable of autonomously determining the number of cell types, identifying their biological identities, and accurately reconstructing both their expression signatures and sample-wise proportions.

Unlike prior art, which treats cell-type composition as a fixed input, the invention frames it as an inference problem solvable through iterative comparison of estimated signatures against a library of reference profiles. This approach leverages the abundance of purified cell-type data in public databases, transforming them into a reusable resource for dissecting any heterogeneous dataset. Validation across controlled and semi-controlled benchmarks confirms that the method matches or exceeds the accuracy of knowledge-dependent algorithms while operating under far more flexible assumptions. Its robustness to over-specified candidate lists, noisy references, and closely related cell types—enhanced by majority voting and class grouping—ensures reliable performance across diverse biological contexts.

Critically, ablation studies demonstrate that omitting the cell-type determination step leads to significant performance degradation, even with minor errors in assumed composition. This underscores the invention’s core innovation: the integration of hypothesis testing into the deconvolution workflow. By enabling accurate re-analysis of legacy datasets without new experiments or metadata curation, the method unlocks unprecedented opportunities for discovery in cancer biology, immunology, neuroscience, and beyond. In summary, the invention provides a powerful, general-purpose tool for transforming bulk gene-expression data into cell-type-resolved insights, democratizing access to high-resolution transcriptomics across the scientific community.
