# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND OF INVENTION

Organic light-emitting diodes (OLEDs) have emerged as a leading emissive display technology due to their high contrast ratios, wide viewing angles, fast response times, and compatibility with flexible substrates. Despite these advantages, OLEDs suffer from inherent limitations in light extraction efficiency. A significant fraction—typically more than 50%—of the photons generated within the emissive layer becomes trapped in waveguide and substrate modes due to total internal reflection at interfaces between layers of differing refractive indices. This phenomenon, commonly referred to as light trapping, arises primarily from the mismatch in refractive indices among the organic layers, transparent conductive oxides (such as indium tin oxide), glass substrates, and ambient air. Consequently, only a small portion of the internally generated light is emitted into the forward viewing direction, limiting the overall external quantum efficiency and luminous efficacy of conventional OLED architectures.

To address this limitation, researchers have explored various light extraction techniques, including the incorporation of microcavity structures that exploit optical interference effects to enhance directional emission. Microcavities formed by reflective electrodes can selectively amplify emission at specific wavelengths aligned with the cavity resonance condition, thereby improving both color purity and outcoupling efficiency. However, prior approaches relying on semitransparent metal electrodes—such as thin silver films—have been hindered by high optical absorption losses and excitation of surface plasmon polaritons at the organic/metal interface, which dissipate energy rather than radiate it. These drawbacks significantly reduce the net enhancement achievable through microcavity engineering, particularly for blue-emitting devices where material constraints further complicate optimization.

In response, dielectric distributed Bragg reflectors (DBRs) composed of alternating high- and low-refractive-index layers have been proposed as superior alternatives to metallic mirrors. DBRs offer high reflectivity with minimal absorption across targeted spectral bands, enabling stronger microcavity effects without parasitic losses. Nevertheless, existing fabrication methods for integrating DBRs into OLED stacks often require complex deposition processes or post-processing steps that are incompatible with standard manufacturing protocols. Moreover, while periodic microstructures such as gratings or photonic crystals have shown promise in extracting guided modes, their implementation typically involves lithographic patterning or etching procedures that increase cost and reduce yield.

Notably, Koo et al. previously demonstrated an approach involving buckled elastomeric substrates to create periodic corrugations that scatter trapped light into extractable modes. While effective in principle, this method relies on mechanical deformation of pre-stretched polymer films followed by transfer onto rigid supports—a process that introduces variability in feature size, limits scalability, and may compromise device integrity during thermal cycling or encapsulation. Furthermore, strictly periodic structures can induce diffraction artifacts or angular color shifts that degrade image quality in display applications.

There remains a critical need for an OLED architecture that enhances light output without introducing visual blur, maintaining compatibility with established fabrication techniques, and preserving color fidelity across viewing angles. In particular, quasi-periodic structures—those exhibiting statistical regularity without strict translational symmetry—offer a compelling solution. Such structures can efficiently couple guided modes into free space while suppressing coherent diffraction effects that lead to speckle or moiré patterns. The present invention addresses these challenges by integrating a quasi-periodic buckling morphology directly into the OLED stack through controlled thermal processing of a metal film deposited on an elastomeric underlayer, thereby achieving enhanced light extraction without departing from conventional vacuum-deposition workflows.

## BRIEF SUMMARY

The present invention provides an organic light-emitting diode (OLED) device incorporating a quasi-periodic buckling structure engineered to enhance light extraction efficiency while minimizing optical artifacts such as blurring or angular color shift. Unlike strictly periodic microstructures that produce coherent diffraction, the quasi-periodic nature of the buckling morphology ensures broadband and diffuse scattering of waveguided photons into the escape cone, resulting in higher luminance without compromising image sharpness. The device comprises two distinct hierarchical features: fine-scale buckles with sub-micron periodicity arising from intrinsic stress relaxation in ultrathin metal films, and gross-scale undulations induced by macroscopic thermal contraction mismatches between the metal layer and an underlying elastomeric substrate. Together, these dual-length-scale deformations create a multi-resonant optical environment that synergistically couples multiple waveguide modes across the visible spectrum.

This architecture enables substantial improvements in current efficiency—particularly for green-emitting phosphorescent OLEDs—by aligning the microcavity resonance with both the peak emission wavelength of the emitter and the maximum sensitivity of the human photopic response curve (~555 nm). Crucially, the quasi-periodic buckling is formed in situ during standard device fabrication, eliminating the need for additional patterning, etching, or transfer steps. As a result, the invention maintains full compatibility with industrial OLED manufacturing while delivering performance metrics unattainable with prior art approaches based on flat cavities or purely periodic textures.

## DETAILED DISCLOSURE

The present invention discloses an OLED device comprising a quasi-periodic grating structure formed by controlled buckling of a metal oxide layer situated within the light-emitting region of the device. This grating is not a lithographically defined pattern but rather a self-assembled topography resulting from differential thermal contraction between a deposited metal film and a compliant elastomeric underlayer. The resulting surface morphology exhibits quasi-periodicity—characterized by a dominant spatial frequency with stochastic variations in amplitude and phase—that effectively scatters trapped optical modes without generating coherent diffraction orders that would manifest as visual blur or rainbow effects in display applications.

The buckling structure consists of two superimposed components: fine buckling and gross buckling. Fine buckling refers to nanoscale ripples with periods ranging from approximately 200 to 800 nanometers, originating from compressive stresses developed during cooling after metal deposition. Gross buckling denotes larger undulations with periods exceeding 1 micrometer, arising from global strain release in the elastomer-metal bilayer system. Both features coexist on the same surface, as illustrated in FIG. 1 (fine buckling) and FIG. 2 (gross buckling), and collectively contribute to broadband light extraction enhancement.

In a preferred embodiment, the buckling occurs on the distal face of the OLED stack—that is, the side opposite the substrate—within the active light-emitting area. This configuration ensures that scattered light propagates directly toward the viewer without traversing additional lossy layers. The device is configured as a bottom-emission OLED, wherein light exits through the transparent substrate, although the principles apply equally to top-emission architectures with appropriate electrode modifications.

The OLED stack comprises, in sequence from the glass substrate upward: a distributed Bragg reflector (DBR) composed of alternating TiO₂ and SiO₂ quarter-wave layers tuned to the target emission color (e.g., 550 nm for green); a transparent anode of indium tin oxide (ITO); a hole injection layer of PEDOT:PSS; a hole transport layer of TAPC (1,1-bis[(di-4-tolylamino)phenyl]cyclohexane); an emissive layer containing a phosphorescent dopant such as Ir(ppy)₃ in a CBP host for green emission; an electron transport layer of 3TPYMB; an electron injection layer of LiF; and a cathode of aluminum. Critically, interposed between the DBR and ITO is a buckling-inducing layer stack consisting of a transparent elastomer film (e.g., polydimethylsiloxane, PDMS), a thin metal film (e.g., aluminum), and a metal oxide layer formed by oxidation of said metal.

The method of preparation begins with spin-coating a PDMS film onto a glass substrate and curing it to form a compliant base. A metal film of controlled thickness (e.g., 20–100 nm Al) is then thermally evaporated onto the PDMS under elevated temperature conditions. Upon cooling to room temperature, the greater thermal contraction of the metal relative to the elastomer induces compressive stress, triggering spontaneous buckling. Subsequent ultraviolet-ozone (UVO) treatment oxidizes the aluminum surface to form a transparent aluminum oxide layer, which serves as a stable foundation for subsequent ITO deposition. The remaining OLED layers are then sequentially deposited via standard thermal evaporation.

The degree of quasi-periodicity—and thus the spectral characteristics of light extraction—is tunable via the initial metal film thickness. Thicker films yield larger buckle amplitudes and longer correlation lengths, shifting the effective scattering spectrum. This parameter provides a simple, process-compatible knob for optimizing device performance per emission color. Advantages include enhanced outcoupling efficiency, improved current efficiency (up to 224 cd/A demonstrated for green devices), expanded color gamut due to narrowed emission spectra, and elimination of diffraction-induced visual artifacts—all achieved without modifying core OLED fabrication infrastructure.

### METHODS AND MATERIALS

In practicing the invention, PDMS precursor (Sylgard 184) was mixed at a 10:1 base-to-curing agent ratio, degassed, and spin-coated onto cleaned glass substrates at 500–2000 rpm to yield films of 5–50 μm thickness. Curing was performed at 80°C for 2 hours. Aluminum films of varying thicknesses (20, 50, and 100 nm) were deposited via thermal evaporation at a base pressure of <5×10⁻⁶ Torr and a deposition rate of 1 Å/s, with the substrate heated to 60°C during deposition to promote adhesion and stress development. Following deposition, samples were cooled to ambient temperature, allowing spontaneous buckling to occur due to thermal mismatch between Al (coefficient of thermal expansion ~23 ppm/K) and PDMS (~310 ppm/K).

To control buckle periodicity and enhance surface hydrophilicity for subsequent layer deposition, samples underwent ultraviolet-ozone (UVO) treatment using a Jelight Company Model 42 apparatus for durations of 5–30 minutes. This step partially oxidizes the aluminum surface to form a conformal Al₂O₃ layer while also modulating the elastic modulus of the near-surface PDMS, thereby influencing buckle wavelength. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy confirmed the presence of Al₂O₃ after 15 minutes of UVO exposure.

Full OLED devices were then fabricated on the buckled Al/Al₂O₃/PDMS/glass substrates. After UVO treatment, a 100-nm ITO layer was sputtered, followed by standard organic and metal layers as described in the Detailed Disclosure. Control devices were fabricated on flat glass/ITO substrates without buckling layers. All devices were encapsulated in a nitrogen glovebox using epoxy resin and glass lids.

Performance testing involved measuring current density–voltage–luminance (J–V–L) characteristics using a Keithley 2400 source meter and calibrated silicon photodiode. Electroluminescence spectra were acquired with an Ocean Optics HR4000 spectrometer. Devices incorporating quasi-periodic buckling consistently exhibited 1.4–2.4× higher current efficiency compared to flat controls, with the greatest enhancement observed in green-emitting devices whose microcavity resonance aligned with both the DBR peak reflectivity (550 nm) and the photopic luminosity maximum (555 nm). Angular emission profiles confirmed suppressed diffraction lobes and maintained color stability up to ±60° viewing angles, validating the quasi-periodic design’s superiority over strictly periodic alternatives.
