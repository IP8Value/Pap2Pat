# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

Programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1), also known as CD279, is a transmembrane receptor expressed primarily on activated T cells, B cells, natural killer T cells, and myeloid cells. First identified in 1992 by Tasuku Honjo and colleagues at Kyoto University, PD-1 functions as a critical immune checkpoint that modulates the intensity and duration of immune responses to prevent autoimmunity and maintain peripheral tolerance. Upon binding to its ligands—programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) or programmed death-ligand 2 (PD-L2)—which are often upregulated on tumor cells and antigen-presenting cells within the tumor microenvironment, PD-1 transmits inhibitory signals that suppress T-cell activation, proliferation, cytokine production, and cytotoxic activity. This mechanism enables tumors to evade immune surveillance, thereby facilitating disease progression. The pivotal role of the PD-1/PD-L1 axis in cancer immunology has led to the development of monoclonal antibodies targeting this pathway, which have revolutionized the treatment of numerous malignancies, including melanoma, non-small cell lung cancer, renal cell carcinoma, and others. These immune checkpoint inhibitors restore anti-tumor immunity by blocking PD-1–ligand interactions, thereby reinvigorating exhausted T cells. Despite their clinical success, not all patients respond to such therapies, underscoring the urgent need for reliable biomarkers capable of predicting therapeutic efficacy, stratifying patient populations, and guiding personalized treatment strategies.

## SUMMARY

The present disclosure identifies soluble programmed cell death protein-1 (sPD-1) as a clinically relevant biomarker with significant prognostic and predictive value in the context of cancer immunotherapy, particularly for patients receiving autologous dendritic cell vaccines (DCV). Unlike membrane-bound PD-1, sPD-1 is a circulating isoform detectable in serum or plasma, believed to arise from alternative splicing or proteolytic cleavage of the full-length receptor. The inventors have discovered that baseline levels of sPD-1, in combination with dynamic changes in sPD-1 following initial vaccine administration, can effectively stratify metastatic melanoma patients into subgroups with markedly different survival outcomes when treated with DCV. Specifically, patients exhibiting either very low baseline sPD-1 levels (<100 pg/mL) or intermediate baseline levels (<200,000 pg/mL) accompanied by a decrease in sPD-1 after three weekly vaccine doses demonstrate significantly improved long-term survival compared to those with high baseline sPD-1 (>200,000 pg/mL) or increasing sPD-1 levels post-vaccination. This dual-parameter classification system demonstrates strong predictive accuracy for three-year and five-year survival in DCV-treated cohorts, correctly identifying 80% of long-term survivors and 86% of short-term non-survivors. In contrast, this association is absent in patients treated with autologous tumor cell vaccines (TCV), highlighting a fundamental immunological distinction between these two vaccine platforms. Based on these findings, the invention discloses a method for selecting cancer patients suitable for DCV therapy by measuring sPD-1 levels in blood samples obtained before and after initial vaccination. Furthermore, the invention provides a method for predicting overall survival in vaccinated patients using defined sPD-1 thresholds and temporal response criteria. Additionally, the disclosure encompasses a method for treating cancer comprising administering a DCV to a patient whose sPD-1 profile meets predefined favorable criteria. Variations of these methods include integration with immune checkpoint inhibition therapy, application across multiple cancer types beyond melanoma, and adaptation to different classes of cancer vaccines that elicit Th1-polarized immune responses.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

Soluble PD-1 (sPD-1) represents an extracellular fragment of the PD-1 receptor that circulates in biological fluids and may modulate immune checkpoint signaling through competitive binding to PD-L1 or PD-L2, although its precise biological function remains incompletely understood. Emerging evidence suggests that sPD-1 levels reflect the state of systemic immune activation and regulation, particularly during Th1-type anti-tumor immune responses where interferon-gamma induces PD-1 expression on lymphocytes. Given its origin from activated immune cells, sPD-1 has been proposed as a potential surrogate marker for immune engagement in cancer. However, prior reports on sPD-1 in various malignancies have yielded inconsistent conclusions: in non-small cell lung cancer patients treated with erlotinib, higher sPD-1 correlated with improved progression-free and overall survival; in chronic hepatitis B virus infection, elevated sPD-1 predicted increased risk of hepatocellular carcinoma development; in established hepatocellular carcinoma, high sPD-1 was associated with worse prognosis; and in advanced pancreatic cancer, sPD-1 showed no correlation with clinical outcomes despite associations with systemic inflammation and T-cell infiltration. These discrepancies underscore the context-dependent nature of sPD-1 as a biomarker and highlight the necessity of evaluating it within specific therapeutic frameworks. The present disclosure focuses on metastatic melanoma patients enrolled in a randomized phase II trial comparing autologous dendritic cell vaccines (DCV) and autologous tumor cell vaccines (TCV), both derived from patient-specific short-term tumor cell cultures. The experimental design involved collecting paired serum samples at week-0 (one week before the first of three weekly vaccine injections) and week-4 (one week after the third injection) from 39 of 42 randomized patients, followed by long-term survival tracking up to five years. Analysis revealed that while baseline sPD-1 alone was not prognostic for the entire cohort, it exhibited striking predictive power within the DCV arm. Patients with baseline sPD-1 <100 pg/mL or <200,000 pg/mL with a subsequent decrease in sPD-1 demonstrated median survival exceeding four years, whereas those with baseline >200,000 pg/mL or increasing sPD-1 had median survival under two years (p=0.0152). Notably, all five patients with the lowest baseline sPD-1 (<100 pg/mL)—three in DCV and two in TCV—achieved prolonged survival without subsequent anti-PD-1 therapy, suggesting that minimal pre-existing immune suppression may favor vaccine responsiveness. Conversely, the two DCV-treated patients with sPD-1 >200,000 pg/mL succumbed within one year, indicating profound immune exhaustion refractory to DCV alone. Critically, a decline in sPD-1 after vaccination was strongly associated with survival only in DCV recipients (5/7 long-term survivors vs. 0/7 in TCV, p=0.021), reinforcing the unique immunobiology of dendritic cell-based vaccination. Blood samples were analyzed using a validated quantitative ELISA under GLP conditions, ensuring reproducibility. Survival results confirmed the superior efficacy of DCV over TCV in this trial (median OS 43.4 vs. 20.5 months), and the sPD-1 classification further refined patient selection within the DCV group. Threshold levels of 100 pg/mL and 200,000 pg/mL were empirically determined based on observed survival bifurcations and serve as operational criteria for patient stratification. The disclosed method for selecting patients involves measuring sPD-1 at baseline and week-4 and applying the composite rule: favorable candidates are those with sPD-1 <100 pg/mL at baseline OR sPD-1 between 100–200,000 pg/mL with a decrease from week-0 to week-4. Treatment entails subcutaneous administration of DCV, typically formulated with GM-CSF as an adjuvant, according to a multi-dose schedule (e.g., weeks 1, 2, 3, 8, 12, etc.). The invention also contemplates combining DCV with immune checkpoint inhibition therapy—defined herein as any agent that blocks the PD-1/PD-L1 axis, including monoclonal antibodies such as pembrolizumab, nivolumab, cemiplimab, or dostarlimab—for patients with intermediate sPD-1 profiles who might benefit from concurrent immune stimulation and checkpoint release. “Treating” or “treatment” is broadly construed to encompass diagnosis, prophylaxis, amelioration, and cure, including the administration of medicaments, cellular products, and biologics. Applicable cancer types extend beyond melanoma to any malignancy amenable to DCV therapy, including but not limited to renal cell carcinoma, glioblastoma, prostate cancer, and ovarian cancer. Similarly, the methods apply to various cancer vaccines that induce Th1 immunity, such as peptide-pulsed dendritic cells, RNA-transfected DCs, viral vector-based vaccines, and neoantigen-targeted formulations. The PD-1 checkpoint is defined as the inhibitory signaling pathway initiated by PD-1 ligation, and its inhibition refers to pharmacological or biological interruption of this interaction to enhance T-cell function.

### EXAMPLES

The study population comprised 39 metastatic melanoma patients enrolled in a randomized phase II clinical trial (NCT00948480) comparing autologous dendritic cell vaccine (DCV) and autologous tumor cell vaccine (TCV). Eligible patients had stage IV or recurrent stage III disease, had undergone surgical resection to establish short-term autologous tumor cell lines, and exhibited a Karnofsky performance status ≥70. Patient characteristics—including age, sex, disease stage, LDH levels, and prior therapies—were balanced between arms and previously published. Both vaccines utilized irradiated autologous tumor cells as the antigen source; DCV involved ex vivo loading of these antigens onto dendritic cells derived from leukapheresis, while TCV consisted of the irradiated tumor cells themselves. Vaccines were administered subcutaneously with GM-CSF at weeks 1, 2, 3, 8, 12, 16, 20, and 24. Blood samples were prospectively collected at week-0 (pre-vaccination) and week-4 (post-third dose) and cryopreserved. Soluble PD-1 levels were quantified using a multiplex ELISA (Raybiotech, Inc.) per GLP standards, with results reported in pg/mL. Statistical analyses employed non-parametric tests due to skewed distributions: Mann–Whitney U for group comparisons, Wilcoxon signed-rank for paired changes, Fisher’s exact test for proportions, and log-rank test for survival curves. Summary data revealed extreme variability in baseline sPD-1 (0–511,063 pg/mL; mean 54,566; median 119,845), far exceeding the normal range (200–1200 pg/mL). Graphical depictions (Figure 1) illustrated individual sPD-1 trajectories by treatment and survival status. Median and mean sPD-1 levels at week-0 and week-4, along with absolute and percent changes, were tabulated for the entire cohort and subgroups (Table 3). Baseline sPD-1 did not differ significantly between DCV and TCV arms or between 3-year survivors and non-survivors overall. When dichotomized at 1,200 pg/mL (near the cohort median of 1,509), survival outcomes remained comparable (3-year OS 47% vs. 35%, p=0.453), confirming lack of general prognostic utility. Similarly, directional changes in sPD-1 (increase vs. decrease) showed no association with survival across the entire population or within treatment arms (Figures 4A–B). However, deeper analysis by treatment arm uncovered critical insights. Among DCV-treated patients, baseline sPD-1 >200,000 pg/mL perfectly predicted short survival (<1 year; 0/2 survivors), whereas all 15 with <200,000 pg/mL survived >1 year (p=0.0074). Moreover, DCV patients with baseline <500 pg/mL exhibited a trend toward better 3-year survival (5/6 vs. 5/11, p=0.304). Strikingly, a decrease in sPD-1 post-vaccination correlated with long-term survival exclusively in the DCV group (5/7 vs. 0/7 in TCV, p=0.021). These observations led to a composite classification: “sPD-1 low/decreased” included patients with baseline <100 pg/mL OR baseline 100–200,000 pg/mL with a week-4 decrease; all others were “sPD-1 high/increased.” In DCV-treated patients, this model yielded 8/9 long-term survivors in the favorable group versus 2/8 in the unfavorable group (p=0.0152), with 80% sensitivity and 86% specificity for 3-year survival. Dot plots (Figure 6A) visualized individual classifications against survival duration. False positives (predicted survivor but died early) and false negatives (predicted non-survivor but lived >5 years) were rare and clinically explainable—e.g., one false negative received subsequent anti-PD-1 therapy. In stark contrast, the same classification failed in TCV-treated patients, correctly identifying only 1/6 long-term survivors (Figure 7). These data collectively demonstrate that sPD-1 dynamics are predictive only in the context of DCV, likely due to its superior ability to prime de novo T-cell responses. The predictive value of sPD-1 criteria supports its use as a prognostic marker (identifying DCV non-responders with high baseline sPD-1), a predictive marker (forecasting vaccine benefit based on sPD-1 trajectory), and potentially a surrogate endpoint in future DCV trials. Limitations include small sample size and retrospective design, necessitating prospective validation. Immunologically, low sPD-1 may signify an “immune desert” amenable to vaccine-induced priming, while high sPD-1 reflects an exhausted, checkpoint-dominated milieu requiring combinatorial blockade. The association between declining sPD-1 and survival in DCV recipients suggests successful immune activation without compensatory PD-1 upregulation.
