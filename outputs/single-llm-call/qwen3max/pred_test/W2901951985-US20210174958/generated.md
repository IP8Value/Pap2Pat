# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

Cancer remains a leading cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide, despite decades of public health initiatives aimed at early detection through population-level screening programs. A persistent challenge in oncology is the late diagnosis of malignancies, particularly for cancers such as colorectal and pancreatic cancer, where over half of all cases are identified only after regional or distant metastasis has occurred. This diagnostic delay significantly diminishes therapeutic efficacy and survival outcomes. Current standard-of-care screening modalities—such as colonoscopy for colorectal cancer—are often invasive, costly, logistically burdensome, and associated with suboptimal patient adherence. Moreover, many existing tests exhibit inadequate sensitivity for early-stage disease and precancerous lesions, thereby failing to fulfill the core objective of cancer screening: interception before irreversible progression.

In response to these limitations, significant scientific and commercial interest has emerged around blood-based “liquid biopsy” approaches that leverage circulating biomarkers for non-invasive cancer detection. Among these, cell-free DNA (cfDNA)—fragments of nucleic acid released into the bloodstream from both tumor and non-tumor cells—has garnered particular attention. Tumor-derived cfDNA, also known as circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA), carries somatic alterations including point mutations, structural variants, and copy number variations (CNVs) that are absent in healthy individuals. Additionally, ctDNA exhibits distinct biophysical properties, such as shorter fragment lengths, which may further aid discrimination. However, the clinical utility of ctDNA-focused strategies for population screening is constrained by biological realities: in early-stage cancer, ctDNA often constitutes less than 1% of total cfDNA, and in some premalignant conditions, it may be undetectable using current technologies. Technical challenges—including sequencing depth requirements, cost, blood volume constraints, and confounding phenomena like clonal hematopoiesis—further limit the feasibility of mutation-centric assays in asymptomatic screening populations.

An alternative paradigm shifts focus from tumor-specific mutations to broader, genome-wide signatures embedded within the entire cfDNA pool. Emerging evidence suggests that early-stage tumors induce systemic changes in host physiology, including alterations in immune cell populations, stromal interactions, and epigenetic states of circulating cells. These “tumor-educated” changes may manifest in cfDNA through shifts in fragmentation patterns, methylation profiles, and chromatin accessibility that reflect the cellular origins and physiological states of the contributing tissues. Critically, such signals may be detectable even when ctDNA fraction is extremely low, offering a complementary or superior route to early detection.

The analysis of these complex, high-dimensional cfDNA signals demands advanced computational methodologies capable of discerning subtle, multivariate patterns amidst biological and technical noise. Machine learning (ML) offers a powerful toolkit for this task, enabling data-driven discovery without prior assumptions about specific genomic loci or molecular features. However, the successful application of ML in diagnostics requires rigorous control of confounding variables—such as age, sex, sample processing batch, and institutional source—that can inadvertently correlate with disease status and lead to spurious model performance. Without explicit mitigation of these confounders, models risk learning artifacts rather than biology, resulting in poor generalizability beyond the training cohort.

Accordingly, there exists a pressing need for novel, robust, and scalable methods that integrate multi-analyte profiling of the non-cellular fraction of blood with machine learning frameworks specifically designed to account for real-world sources of variability. Such methods must be capable of detecting early-stage cancers with high sensitivity and specificity while remaining cost-effective, minimally invasive, and suitable for large-scale implementation. The present invention addresses this unmet need by providing a comprehensive system for cancer detection that leverages whole-genome cfDNA sequencing, multi-modal analyte integration, and confounder-aware machine learning to enable accurate, generalizable, and clinically actionable diagnoses.

## BRIEF SUMMARY

The present invention introduces a machine learning–based diagnostic method focused on the non-cellular portion of human circulation, particularly cell-free nucleic acids and other soluble biomolecules, to classify biological samples as indicative of a specified property, such as the presence or absence of cancer. The method begins by assaying a biological sample—typically plasma or serum—for multiple classes of molecules, including nucleic acids (e.g., cell-free DNA and RNA), polyamino acids (e.g., proteins and peptides), carbohydrates, and metabolites. From these assays, a set of quantitative measurements is obtained, which are then used to construct a feature vector representing the molecular profile of the sample.

This feature vector is input into a pre-trained machine learning model that has been optimized to distinguish between predefined classes, such as cancer versus non-cancer, or different cancer subtypes. The model processes the feature vector and outputs a classification, which may include a probability score, a binary decision, or a multi-class label. The classes of molecules assayed may include, for example, genomic DNA fragments, messenger RNA, microRNA, long non-coding RNA, histone-modified peptides, glycoproteins, and small-molecule metabolites. Assays employed may encompass next-generation sequencing (e.g., whole-genome, targeted, or bisulfite sequencing), immunoassays (e.g., ELISA, Luminex), mass spectrometry, PCR-based quantification, and carbohydrate-binding assays.

The invention further encompasses a system for performing such classifications, comprising a receiver module that accepts raw assay data, a feature module that constructs the feature vector, an analysis module that applies the machine learning model, a labeling module that assigns diagnostic labels, a comparator module that evaluates model confidence, a training module that refines model parameters using labeled training samples, and an output module that communicates results to clinicians or integrated health systems. In one embodiment, the system includes a classification circuit implemented in hardware or firmware for rapid, on-device inference. Additionally, the invention provides a non-transitory computer-readable medium encoded with instructions that, when executed by a processor, carry out the steps of the method.

The specified property may relate to any clinically-diagnosed disorder, including but not limited to colorectal cancer, pancreatic cancer, lung cancer, breast cancer, or non-malignant conditions such as inflammatory bowel disease or neurodegenerative disorders. The method supports continuous measurement of patient traits over time, enabling monitoring of treatment responsiveness, recurrence, or progression. By integrating diverse molecular analytes and employing robust, confounder-controlled machine learning, the invention delivers a highly sensitive, specific, and generalizable platform for early disease detection and management.

## TERMS

As used herein, the articles “a,” “an,” and “the” are intended to mean one or more unless the context clearly indicates otherwise. The term “or” is used in its inclusive sense, meaning “and/or,” unless explicitly stated otherwise. The phrase “based on” encompasses direct and indirect causation or dependency, and does not imply exclusivity unless specified. “Area under the curve” (AUC) refers to the area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve, a graphical plot illustrating the diagnostic ability of a binary classifier system as its discrimination threshold is varied; the ROC curve plots true positive rate (sensitivity) against false positive rate (1 − specificity). 

“Cancer” and “cancerous” denote malignant neoplasms characterized by uncontrolled cell growth, invasion, and potential metastasis. “Cancer-free” describes a subject without clinical or pathological evidence of malignancy. A “genetic variant” or “variant” is a deviation from a reference nucleotide sequence, which may be germline (inherited) or somatic (acquired). A “germline variant” is present in the germ cells and thus heritable. “Input features” or “features” are measurable properties of a biological sample used as inputs to a machine learning model. A “machine learning model” or “model” is a computational algorithm trained to map input features to output predictions; model training involves optimizing internal parameters to minimize prediction error on labeled training data.

A “marker” or “marker protein” is a molecule whose presence, absence, or quantity correlates with a biological state; marker detection may occur via binding assays, sequencing, or spectroscopy. “Non-cancerous tissue,” “normal tissue,” and “healthy tissue” refer to tissue without malignant pathology. “Polynucleotides,” “nucleotides,” “nucleic acids,” and “oligonucleotides” encompass DNA, RNA, and their analogs, composed of nucleotide monomers linked by phosphodiester bonds. A “polypeptide,” “protein,” or “peptide” is a polymer of amino acids, which may undergo post-translational modifications such as phosphorylation or glycosylation.

A “prediction” is a model-generated estimate of an unknown outcome; predictive methods include regression, classification, and clustering. “Prognosis” refers to the likely course of a disease. “Specificity” is the proportion of true negatives correctly identified, while “sensitivity” is the proportion of true positives correctly identified. A “structural variation” (SV) is a genomic alteration involving segments larger than 50 base pairs, such as deletions or duplications. A “subject” is a human or animal from whom a biological sample is obtained; subject characteristics include age, sex, and medical history.

A “training sample” is a labeled biological specimen used to train a model; a “training vector” is its corresponding feature representation. “Tumor,” “neoplasia,” “malignancy,” and “cancer” are used interchangeably. “Tumor burden” quantifies the total cancer mass in a subject. A “nucleic acid sample” comprises extracted DNA or RNA. A “barcode” is a unique nucleotide sequence appended to molecules during library preparation to enable multiplexing; barcode sequences are typically 6–12 nucleotides long. “Tagmentation” is a transposase-mediated fragmentation and adapter ligation reaction. Nucleic acid amplification generates multiple copies of a target sequence, yielding an “amplified product.”
