# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNICAL FIELD

The present invention pertains to the field of veterinary virology and vaccine development, specifically to recombinant viral vectors derived from Gallid herpesvirus 3 (GaHV-3), also known as Marek’s disease virus serotype 2 (MDV-2). More particularly, the invention relates to a recombinant GaHV-3 vector engineered to express heterologous antigens from avian pathogens, thereby serving as a multivalent vaccine platform for the prevention and control of multiple infectious diseases in poultry. The recombinant vector is based on the SB-1 strain of GaHV-3, a naturally attenuated virus with a long history of safe and effective use as a monovalent vaccine against Marek’s disease. The invention further encompasses methods for constructing such vectors, compositions comprising the recombinant virus, and their use in immunizing avian species against co-circulating pathogens such as infectious bursal disease virus (IBDV), avian influenza virus (AIV), Newcastle disease virus (NDV), infectious laryngotracheitis virus (ILTV), and avian infectious bronchitis virus (IBV). This technology addresses critical limitations in current recombinant vaccine strategies by providing a complementary vector system that avoids immune interference observed with homologous vector platforms, particularly those based on herpesvirus of turkeys (HVT).

## BACKGROUND

Marek’s disease (MD) is a highly contagious lymphoproliferative disorder of chickens caused by Gallid herpesvirus 2 (GaHV-2), also designated Marek’s disease virus serotype 1 (MDV-1). The disease is characterized by rapid-onset T-cell lymphomas, peripheral nerve paralysis due to lymphocytic infiltration, and immunosuppression, leading to significant economic losses in the global poultry industry. MDV-1 replicates primarily in feather follicle epithelial cells, from which it is shed into the environment as cell-free virions in dander, facilitating airborne transmission. Mortality rates in unvaccinated flocks typically range from 10% to 30% but can reach 100% depending on the virulence of the infecting strain, host genetics, and management conditions. Since the 1970s, MD has been effectively controlled through the widespread use of live attenuated vaccines. These include the naturally avirulent MDV-2 strain SB-1 (Gallid herpesvirus 3, GaHV-3), the herpesvirus of turkeys (HVT, Meleagrid herpesvirus 1), and the attenuated MDV-1 strain CVI988/Rispens.

Among these, HVT has emerged as the most successful recombinant viral vector platform for avian vaccines. Recombinant HVT expressing antigens from IBDV, NDV, AIV, and ILTV have demonstrated robust protective immunity, even in the presence of maternal antibodies. However, a major limitation of HVT-based vectors is the phenomenon of immune interference when multiple recombinant HVT vaccines are co-administered. This interference results in reduced efficacy of one or both components, leading manufacturers to advise against combining different HVT-vectored products. Furthermore, engineering HVT to express multiple heterologous antigens simultaneously has proven technically challenging due to genome size constraints and potential instability.

In contrast, the SB-1 strain of GaHV-3 has been used for decades in bivalent formulations with HVT (e.g., SB-1 + HVT vaccines), where it acts synergistically to provide superior protection against very virulent MDV strains compared to either component alone. This complementarity suggests that SB-1 could serve as an orthogonal vector platform—capable of delivering additional antigens without interfering with HVT-based immunity. Despite its potential, SB-1 has not been widely exploited as a recombinant vector, largely due to historical difficulties in genetic manipulation. Recent advances, including the cloning of the full-length SB-1 genome into a bacterial artificial chromosome (BAC), have enabled precise genome editing via recombineering techniques.

Concurrently, the control of infectious bursal disease virus (IBDV)—a birnavirus causing severe immunosuppression and mortality in young chickens—remains a persistent challenge. While live-attenuated and inactivated IBDV vaccines exist, they suffer from drawbacks including residual virulence, inability to overcome maternal antibody interference, and incompatibility with in ovo vaccination. Bivalent MD/IBD vaccines based on HVT (e.g., VAXXITEK® HVT+IBD) represent a significant advance, offering safe, single-dose, in ovo-compatible protection. Nevertheless, the reliance on HVT as the sole vector platform restricts the development of trivalent or quadrivalent vaccines due to interference concerns. Thus, there exists a compelling need for alternative, non-interfering vector systems capable of delivering IBDV and other avian pathogen antigens. The present invention fulfills this need by establishing recombinant SB-1 (GaHV-3) as a viable, high-efficacy vector for bivalent and multivalent poultry vaccines.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The invention provides a recombinant Gallid herpesvirus 3 (GaHV-3) vector, specifically derived from the SB-1 strain, engineered to express at least one heterologous polynucleotide encoding an antigen from an avian pathogen. The heterologous polynucleotide is inserted into a nonessential region of the GaHV-3 genome, preferably within intergenic loci such as between UL3 and UL4 or between UL21 and UL22, to minimize disruption of viral replication while enabling robust antigen expression. The expressed antigen may be derived from infectious bursal disease virus (IBDV), avian influenza virus (AIV), Newcastle disease virus (NDV), infectious laryngotracheitis virus (ILTV), or avian infectious bronchitis virus (IBV). In preferred embodiments, the antigen is the VP2 capsid protein of IBDV, or immunogenic fragments thereof, which elicits neutralizing antibodies and protective immunity.

The recombinant vector comprises an expression cassette containing the heterologous polynucleotide operably linked to a promoter functional in avian cells, such as the murine cytomegalovirus immediate early promoter (mCMV IE), and a transcription termination signal, such as the SV40 early polyadenylation sequence. The invention further encompasses a vaccine composition comprising the recombinant GaHV-3 vector in combination with a pharmaceutically or veterinarily acceptable carrier, excipient, or adjuvant. Optionally, the vaccine may include a second Marek’s disease virus vector, such as HVT or Rispens, to form a bivalent or multivalent formulation that leverages complementary immune stimulation without interference.

Also provided is an isolated DNA molecule encoding the recombinant GaHV-3 vector, which may be maintained in a bacterial artificial chromosome (BAC) for stable propagation and genetic manipulation in bacterial hosts. The vaccine is administered to avian species—particularly chickens—via routes including subcutaneous injection, in ovo inoculation, or spray application, and is effective in conferring protection against both Marek’s disease and the target avian pathogen. The invention additionally includes a method of treating or preventing disease in avian species by administering the recombinant vaccine, as well as intermediate vectors containing selectable markers (e.g., galK) to facilitate efficient insertion of expression cassettes via site-specific recombination or recombineering technologies.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention is directed to recombinant viral vectors, vaccine compositions, and methods of use, all centered on a genetically modified Gallid herpesvirus 3 (GaHV-3). As used herein, “animal” refers to any member of the kingdom Animalia, with emphasis on avian species, particularly domesticated birds raised for meat or egg production. “Poultry” denotes domesticated avian species including chickens (Gallus gallus domesticus), turkeys, ducks, geese, and quail, with chickens being the preferred host. The term “nucleic acid” encompasses DNA and RNA molecules, whether genomic, synthetic, or recombinant, and includes both single-stranded and double-stranded forms. A “polynucleotide” is a polymer of nucleotides, which may encode a functional product such as a protein or regulatory RNA. “Genomic DNA” refers to the complete DNA content of an organism’s chromosomes, while “genomic RNA” applies to RNA viruses whose genomes consist of RNA.

A “gene” is a segment of nucleic acid that encodes a polypeptide or functional RNA, including regulatory sequences necessary for expression. A “heterologous polynucleotide” is a nucleic acid sequence not naturally found in the host viral genome, introduced to confer new properties such as antigen expression. A “recombinant viral vector” is a virus whose genome has been altered by the insertion, deletion, or modification of genetic material to deliver therapeutic or immunogenic payloads. In this context, the “recombinant Gallid herpesvirus 3 vector” is a live, replication-competent virus derived from the SB-1 strain, modified to express foreign antigens without compromising its ability to replicate and induce immunity.

“Protein” refers to a polymer of amino acids linked by peptide bonds, which may be full-length or truncated. An “antigen” is any molecule capable of eliciting an immune response, while an “immunogen” specifically induces adaptive immunity. An “immunogenic protein or peptide” is an antigen that provokes a measurable humoral or cellular response. “Conservative variation” denotes amino acid substitutions that preserve biochemical properties (e.g., leucine to isoleucine), often maintaining function. An “epitope” or “antigenic determinant site” is the specific region of an antigen recognized by an antibody or T-cell receptor; it may be linear (continuous sequence) or conformational (formed by folded structure). “Blocking of antibody binding” refers to the inhibition of antibody-antigen interaction, used herein to confirm epitope specificity.

An “immunological response” includes both humoral (antibody-mediated) and cell-mediated immunity. “Avian pathogens” encompass viruses, bacteria, fungi, or parasites that infect birds, with viral pathogens being primary targets. An “insertion site” is a genomic locus where foreign DNA is integrated, preferably a “nonessential region” whose disruption does not abolish viral viability. An “intergenic locus” lies between coding sequences and is often tolerant of insertions. The “expression cassette” comprises a promoter, the heterologous gene, and a termination signal. A “promoter” is a DNA sequence that initiates transcription, and may be constitutive (e.g., mCMV IE) or inducible. A “transcription termination region” ensures proper mRNA processing.

“Percent identity” is calculated by aligning sequences and determining the proportion of identical residues over the aligned length. A “vaccine or immunogenic composition” contains an immunogen and a carrier. “Vaccinating” means administering such a composition to induce protective immunity. A “polyvalent vaccine” targets multiple pathogens or strains. “Isolated DNA” is separated from its natural environment. A “bacterial artificial chromosome (BAC)” is a cloning vector for large DNA inserts, enabling stable maintenance in E. coli. A “pharmaceutically acceptable carrier” includes buffers, stabilizers, and adjuvants suitable for veterinary use. An “antibody” is an immunoglobulin that binds specifically to an antigen.

Preferred insertion sites include the intergenic regions between UL3 and UL4 (e.g., genomic coordinates 19265–19266 of GenBank AB049735.1) and between UL21 and UL22 (e.g., 49807–49808). These loci support high-level antigen expression with minimal impact on viral fitness, as demonstrated by growth curve and gene expression analyses. The heterologous polynucleotide encodes an antigen from an avian pathogen, such as IBDV VP2 (SEQ ID NO: 10 or 6), ILTV glycoproteins E, I, or B (SEQ ID NOs: 31–33), NDV F or HN (SEQ ID NOs: 34–35), AIV HA (H5, H7, H9; SEQ ID NOs: 36–38) or NA (H5N1; SEQ ID NO: 39), or IBV S1/S2 (SEQ ID NO: 40). These sequences may include allelic variants or codon-optimized versions for enhanced expression in avian cells. The vector may carry multiple expression cassettes at distinct loci, enabling multivalent antigen delivery.

### Recombinant Gallid Herpesvirus 3 (GaHV3; MDV-2) Vector

The recombinant GaHV-3 vector of the invention is constructed by inserting one or more heterologous polynucleotides into the SB-1 viral genome at defined intergenic loci, specifically between UL3 and UL4 or between UL21 and UL22. These sites were selected based on empirical data showing tolerance to transgene insertion without catastrophic loss of replicative capacity. The heterologous polynucleotide encodes an immunogenic antigen from an avian pathogen, with infectious bursal disease virus (IBDV) being a primary target. The VP2 capsid protein of IBDV, particularly from strains such as Faragher 52/70 or UK661, is a preferred antigen due to its role in inducing neutralizing antibodies. The VP2 sequence may correspond to SEQ ID NO: 10 or SEQ ID NO: 6, or exhibit at least 95% amino acid identity thereto, including immunogenic fragments retaining conformational epitopes necessary for protection.

Additional preferred antigens include glycoproteins from infectious laryngotracheitis virus (ILTV), namely glycoprotein E (gE; SEQ ID NO: 31), glycoprotein I (gI; SEQ ID NO: 32), and glycoprotein B (gB; SEQ ID NO: 33), each exhibiting at least 90% sequence identity to reference sequences and capable of eliciting neutralizing responses. For Newcastle disease virus (NDV), the fusion (F) protein (SEQ ID NO: 34) and hemagglutinin-neuraminidase (HN) protein (SEQ ID NO: 35) are included, with similar identity thresholds. Avian influenza A virus antigens encompass hemagglutinin (HA) from H5, H7, and H9 subtypes (SEQ ID NOs: 36–38) and neuraminidase (NA) from H5N1 (SEQ ID NO: 39). For avian infectious bronchitis virus (IBV), the S1 or S2 subunits of the spike glycoprotein (SEQ ID NO: 40) serve as key immunogens.

All antigen sequences may be modified as allelic variants or subjected to codon optimization to enhance expression in chicken cells without altering immunogenicity. The recombinant vector may carry two or three distinct heterologous polynucleotides, each inserted at separate intergenic loci (e.g., UL3/4, UL10/11, UL21/22) and driven by independent expression cassettes. Each cassette includes a strong promoter such as mCMV IE and a polyadenylation signal like SV40 early, ensuring high-level, sustained antigen production during viral replication in avian hosts.

### An Intermediate Recombinant Gallid Herpesvirus 3 (GaHV3; MDV-2) Vector Comprising One or More Maker(s)

An intermediate product in the construction of the final recombinant vector is a GaHV-3 genome containing one or more selectable markers inserted into the target intergenic loci. These markers, such as the galactokinase (galK) gene from E. coli, enable positive and negative selection during recombineering. The galK marker allows growth on minimal media with galactose as the sole carbon source (positive selection) and counter-selection on media containing 2-deoxy-galactose (DOG), which is toxic when metabolized by galK-expressing cells. Other markers include fluorescent proteins (e.g., GFP), antibiotic resistance genes (e.g., kanamycin), or enzymatic reporters (e.g., lacZ). The marker facilitates the initial insertion step and is subsequently replaced by the antigen expression cassette via a second round of recombineering, yielding a marker-free final vector suitable for vaccine use.

### Methods for Producing a Recombinant Gallid Herpesvirus 3 (GaHV3; MDV-2) Vector

The recombinant GaHV-3 vector is produced using BAC-based reverse genetics. First, the full-length SB-1 genome is cloned into a BAC vector, enabling propagation and manipulation in E. coli. Using λ-Red recombineering, a galK selection cassette is inserted into the target intergenic locus (e.g., UL3/4). Positive clones are selected on galactose media. In a second recombineering step, the galK cassette is replaced with the antigen expression cassette (e.g., VP2 under mCMV IE promoter), and recombinants are selected on DOG-containing media. The resulting BAC DNA is transfected into primary chicken embryo fibroblasts (CEF), where infectious virus is reconstituted. The virus is amplified over three passages in CEF, titrated by plaque assay, and stored at −80°C in stabilizing buffers. Alternatively, site-specific recombinase systems (e.g., Cre-loxP) may be employed for cassette exchange. The final vector is confirmed by PCR and sequencing and used directly as a vaccine.

### Vaccines

The invention provides a vaccine composition comprising the recombinant GaHV-3 vector in a veterinarily acceptable carrier, which may include buffers (e.g., Tris, PBS), stabilizers (e.g., sucrose, gelatin), antioxidants (e.g., ascorbic acid), preservatives (e.g., thimerosal), and adjuvants (e.g., aluminum salts, oil emulsions). The vaccine may be monovalent (GaHV-3 vector only) or bivalent, further comprising a second MDV vector such as HVT or Rispens. Administration routes include subcutaneous injection in day-old chicks or in ovo inoculation at 18 days of embryonation. The formulation is lyophilized or liquid-stable, stored at 2–8°C or frozen, and reconstituted prior to use.

### Use of the Vaccine

The vaccine is used to immunize avian species, particularly chickens, against Marek’s disease and a second avian pathogen (e.g., IBDV). A prime-boost regimen may be employed, though single-dose administration is sufficient. Vaccination occurs at hatch or in ovo, with protection evident by four weeks post-vaccination. In experimental models, recombinant SB-1-UL3/4VP2 conferred 100% survival against lethal IBDV challenge, comparable to commercial HVT+IBD vaccines. The vaccine is compatible with maternal antibodies and does not interfere with co-administered HVT vectors, enabling flexible multivalent regimens.

## EXAMPLES

### Cell Culture and Virus Propagation

Primary chicken embryo fibroblasts (CEF) were prepared from 9–10-day SPF embryos and maintained in E199 medium with 5% FBS. DF-1 and DT40 cells were cultured in DMEM and RPMI-1640, respectively, with 10% serum. BAC DNA was transfected into CEF using Lipofectamine®, and recombinant viruses were amplified over three passages. Plaque assays on CEF confirmed titers of ~10⁶ PFU/mL.

### Virus Growth Curve Studies

Growth kinetics of parental SB-1 and recombinant viruses (SB-1-UL3/4VP2, etc.) were assessed by infecting CEF and harvesting at 0–120 hpi. Titers were determined by plaque assay. All recombinants showed slightly reduced replication at 72–120 hpi, with SB-1-UL3/4VP2 exhibiting the least attenuation.

### Serum Neutralization Test

Neutralizing antibodies were measured using IBDV D78 strain on DF-1 cells. Sera were heat-inactivated, serially diluted, incubated with 100 TCID₅₀ virus, and added to DF-1 monolayers. CPE was scored after 4 days; the highest dilution preventing CPE was the titer.

### Statistical Analysis

Data were analyzed by two-way ANOVA with multiple comparisons (growth curves, antibody titers) and Mantel-Cox test (survival). Significance was set at p < 0.05.

### Analysis of Different Loci to Insert an Expression Cassette

Three intergenic loci (UL3/4, UL10/11, UL21/22) were tested for VP2 expression. UL3/4 supported the highest VP2 expression and least adjacent gene disruption, making it optimal.

### Example 2

Recombinant plasmids pSB-1-UL3/4VP2, etc., were constructed. Growth curves showed UL3/4 insertion caused minimal replication defect, confirming its suitability.

### Example 3

Immunogenicity was tested in SPF chicks. All recombinants induced neutralizing antibodies by week 4, with UL3/4VP2 showing highest titers.

### Vaccine Development Against IBDV: Immunization Study

In Experiment 1, 40 chicks received recombinant vaccines or VAXXITEK®. Antibodies peaked at week 4. In Experiment 2, challenge with vvIBDV UK661 at 4 weeks post-vaccination resulted in 100% survival in all vaccinated groups versus 0% in controls. Clinical scores confirmed protection. An interference study showed no reduction in MD immunity when SB-1-VP2 was co-administered with HVT.

In summary, the invention provides a novel recombinant GaHV-3 vector platform that overcomes limitations of existing HVT-based systems, enabling effective, non-interfering multivalent vaccination against major avian diseases.
