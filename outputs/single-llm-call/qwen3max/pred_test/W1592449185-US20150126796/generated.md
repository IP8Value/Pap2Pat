# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates to the field of radiotherapy, and more particularly to systems and methods for ensuring accurate patient positioning during external beam radiation therapy. The invention provides a general-purpose patient safety system that enables continuous, real-time, and independent verification of patient setup accuracy across all anatomical treatment sites. This system is designed to complement existing image-guided radiotherapy (IGRT) technologies by offering an automated workflow that minimizes human intervention while safeguarding against gross geometric errors that could lead to misadministration of radiation dose.

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

Radiotherapy errors remain a persistent concern in modern oncology despite significant technological advancements. One of the most critical sources of such errors is geometric miss due to incorrect patient setup, which can result in severe clinical consequences including tumor recurrence from underdosing or normal tissue complications from overdosing. Reports indicate that setup-related incidents account for a substantial proportion of radiotherapy errors, with some studies showing that up to 37.5% of non-brachytherapy incidents stem from patient positioning inaccuracies. These errors are particularly dangerous in hypofractionated regimens like stereotactic body radiotherapy (SBRT), where high doses per fraction amplify the impact of even minor spatial discrepancies.

Geometric miss often arises from challenges in consistently localizing the patient to the exact treatment position established during simulation. Although immobilization devices with couch indexing capabilities provide initial approximate alignment, they do not eliminate the need for precise daily verification. Image-guided radiotherapy (IGRT) systems, especially cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT), have significantly improved setup accuracy by enabling visualization of internal anatomy and facilitating corrections through image registration. However, these systems possess inherent limitations that compromise their ability to guarantee error-free treatment delivery.

A primary limitation of radiographic IGRT systems like CBCT is their inability to continuously monitor patient position after image acquisition. Once imaging is complete, unintended couch movements—whether accidental or deliberate for clearance checks—can displace the patient from the intended treatment position. While modern linear accelerators include interlocks to prevent such scenarios, these safeguards can be overridden by therapists, potentially leading to catastrophic errors if done negligently. Furthermore, CBCT and similar radiographic systems cannot be employed during non-coplanar treatments, as the imaging geometry differs from the actual treatment configuration, thereby introducing uncertainty in patient localization.

Non-radiographic tracking systems such as optical surface imaging or infrared marker-based solutions have been developed to address some of these shortcomings. Commercially available systems like AlignRT, C-Rad Sentinel, and ExacTrac Optical-Tracking System offer continuous monitoring capabilities but are typically restricted to specific disease sites due to practical and technical constraints. For instance, surface imaging systems perform best on rigid, well-defined anatomical regions like the head and neck, while infrared tracking systems often require multiple markers affixed directly to the patient’s skin, increasing therapist workload and potential for placement errors. Moreover, these systems generally lack seamless integration with existing clinical workflows, necessitating manual data entry and frequent therapist intervention, which undermines their utility as universal safety mechanisms.

The absence of immediate, independent, and general-purpose position verification represents a critical gap in current radiotherapy practice. Existing tools either provide intermittent snapshots of patient position (e.g., CBCT) or are limited in anatomical applicability and workflow efficiency (e.g., optical trackers). Consequently, there remains a pressing need for a robust, automated patient localization system that operates independently of treatment machine, immobilization device, or internal anatomy, and that can reliably detect gross setup errors across all disease sites without imposing additional burden on clinical staff.

One known approach involves the use of fiducial markers placed on the patient during simulation, whose coordinates are later used as reference points for daily setup. However, this method suffers from several drawbacks. First, it requires consistent and accurate repositioning of the marker on the patient’s skin at each treatment session, which is prone to human error, especially when multiple tattoos exist from prior treatments. Second, skin-based markers are susceptible to motion artifacts, particularly in thoracic and abdominal regions affected by respiration. Third, the process lacks automation, demanding active therapist involvement in both marker placement and system initialization, thereby disrupting clinical workflow and increasing cognitive load. These limitations underscore the necessity for an improved solution that combines precision, generality, and operational simplicity.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention introduces a novel method for tracking patient position during radiotherapy that overcomes the limitations of existing approaches by integrating optical tracking with a fully automated clinical workflow. The method begins with the placement of a computed tomography ball bearing (CT BB) on the patient’s skin or immobilization device at a location proximal to the intended treatment area during the treatment-simulation CT scan. Following the scan, the CT BB is removed, and its position is either marked with a skin tattoo or permanently affixed to the immobilization device, depending on the treatment site.

The three-dimensional coordinates of the CT BB relative to the treatment isocenter are calculated within the treatment planning system (TPS) and exported alongside the approved treatment plan to a designated network location. Concurrently, the treatment plan is finalized in the record and verify (R&V) system, where beams and associated treatment sites are labeled. A data import module in the patient safety system (PSS) automatically correlates each treatment site with the corresponding CT BB coordinates, thereby predicting the room-based reference position of the marker for each treatment fraction.

At the time of treatment, the CT BB is replaced with an infrared-reflective marker (IRRM)—a flat-surfaced, disposable device designed for easy mounting and optimal visibility to ceiling-mounted optical cameras. In preferred embodiments, the IRRM is permanently mounted on the patient’s immobilization device (e.g., face mask or body mold) during the first treatment session, eliminating the need for daily reapplication and ensuring reproducibility. The PSS then continuously captures the real-time three-dimensional position of the IRRM using an optical tracking system comprising dual CCD cameras.

The system compares the detected IRRM position with the predicted reference coordinates stored in its database. Any deviation exceeding predefined thresholds triggers an alert displayed on a graphical user interface (GUI) accessible both in the treatment room and control area. Critically, the system accounts for non-coplanar setups by mathematically rotating the reference vector between isocenter and IRRM according to the actual couch angle, ensuring accurate comparison regardless of gantry or couch orientation. This real-time, independent verification enables immediate detection of gross setup errors—including unintended couch shifts, incorrect immobilization, or wrong-site treatment—before radiation delivery commences.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention is embodied in a comprehensive patient safety system (PSS) designed to enhance the accuracy and reliability of patient setup in external beam radiotherapy. The system functions as an independent layer of verification that complements, rather than replaces, existing IGRT modalities. Its core components include an optical tracking subsystem, a control and processing unit, a radiation delivery system interface, and a user-friendly graphical interface, all integrated into a seamless clinical workflow.

The optical tracking system comprises a pair of high-resolution CCD cameras (e.g., Polaris cameras from Northern Digital Inc.) mounted on the ceiling of the treatment room in a fixed, calibrated configuration. These cameras operate in the near-infrared spectrum and are capable of simultaneously tracking up to fifty infrared-reflective markers (IRRM) at a frame rate of approximately fifteen per second. Each camera includes a position sensor unit that detects reflected infrared light from the IRRMs and transmits raw positional data via serial and proprietary cables to a central control box.

The control box serves as the hardware intermediary between the cameras and the main computer. It houses signal conditioning circuitry and facilitates data synchronization, ensuring temporal coherence between the two camera feeds. The control box is connected to a dedicated computer running specialized software that performs real-time triangulation to compute the three-dimensional coordinates of each IRRM in the camera-native coordinate system. This internal processing unit executes algorithms based on epipolar geometry to resolve spatial positions from the stereo image pairs captured by the cameras.

To translate these native coordinates into the absolute room coordinate system aligned with the linear accelerator’s isocenter, a rigorous calibration procedure is performed. A calibration jig containing five precisely positioned IRRMs is scanned via CT to establish their relative geometry in a 5×3 matrix **A**. The same jig is then placed on the treatment couch and aligned to the imaging isocenter using CBCT guidance, with congruence between imaging and machine isocenters verified using a ball bearing phantom. The cameras capture the IRRM positions, forming matrix **B**. Using singular value decomposition (SVD), a rotation matrix **R** and translation vector **S** are computed to satisfy **A = BR + S**, thereby defining the transformation from camera space to room space.

The computer executing the PSS software includes standard components: a central processor, main memory (RAM), static memory (ROM or flash), a system bus, a display controller driving a high-resolution monitor, input devices (keyboard, mouse), a cursor control device, a non-volatile memory device (e.g., SSD), a signal generation device for alerts, and a network interface for communication with TPS and R&V systems. The system runs on a conventional operating system and stores executable instructions on a tangible computer-readable storage medium, enabling implementation via software, firmware, or dedicated hardware.

During treatment simulation, a CT BB is placed on the patient’s skin or immobilization device within the scan field. After CT acquisition, the BB is identified as a point of interest (POI) in the TPS, and its coordinates relative to each treatment isocenter are exported to a network folder. The approved plan is transferred to the R&V system, where beams are labeled by site, and the final plan is exported in RTP format to the same folder. The PSS imports this data, associating each beam with its corresponding POI to predict the IRRM’s room coordinates.

At treatment, the IRRM—a 6 mm diameter, 1 mm thick disc with one side covered in infrared-reflective tape and the other with medical-grade adhesive—is affixed either to the skin tattoo or, preferably, to the immobilization device. For head and neck patients using aquaplastic masks, the IRRM is mounted over the chin to ensure unobstructed visibility. For body mold patients, especially in abdominal-pelvic cases, an initial skin placement is used in the first session to guide permanent mounting on the mold at an optimal location.

The PSS automatically loads patient data when a treatment beam is selected in the R&V system, thanks to synchronization via external file triggers. Real-time tracking begins immediately upon IRRM detection, with deviations from reference displayed numerically and graphically. The GUI shows lateral, longitudinal, and anteroposterior offsets, a composite vector magnitude (VECT), and color-coded alarms when thresholds are exceeded. In advanced configurations, the system can trigger automatic beam shutoff or integrate with breath-hold devices for motion management.

Workflow automation is a cornerstone of the invention. By leveraging R&V integration and permanent IRRM mounting, therapist intervention is minimized—often eliminated after the first fraction. This not only reduces workload but also mitigates human error in data entry or marker placement. Daily QA is streamlined by mounting an IRRM on the institution’s standard QA phantom; alignment to isocenter via CBCT establishes a stable reference, and subsequent daily checks validate system consistency.

Extensive validation demonstrates the system’s accuracy and reliability. Phantom studies show sub-millimeter tracking precision under static conditions and less than 3 mm deviation even with 60° couch rotations. Clinical evaluations on head and neck patients reveal mean deviations below 2.5 mm relative to both couch indexing and CBCT systems, with residual errors after couch shifts averaging under 1 mm. For abdominal-pelvic patients, despite respiratory motion causing up to 17 mm of surface displacement, the system maintains sufficient accuracy to detect gross errors (>10 mm), especially when IRRMs are transferred to immobilization devices.

The PSS has proven effective in catching near-misses, including wrong indexing bar placement, machine-specific couch parameter errors, and post-imaging couch shifts. Its independence from internal anatomy, treatment machine, or immobilization type makes it universally applicable. Unlike commercial trackers requiring multiple markers or manual setup, this system uses a single, strategically placed IRRM and full automation, enabling widespread adoption in busy clinics.

In summary, the invention provides a robust, efficient, and general-purpose solution to the persistent problem of radiotherapy setup errors. By delivering continuous, independent, and automated position verification, it enhances patient safety without disrupting clinical workflow, thereby fulfilling a critical unmet need in modern radiation oncology.
