# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNICAL FIELD

The present invention relates to quantum computing optimization, and more specifically to methods, systems, and computer program products for solving mixed integer optimization problems using hybrid quantum-classical variational algorithms enhanced by Conditional Value-at-Risk (CVaR) as an aggregation function. The invention improves the convergence behavior and solution quality of existing quantum-classical hybrid algorithms such as the Variational Quantum Eigensolver (VQE) and the Quantum Approximate Optimization Algorithm (QAOA) when applied to combinatorial optimization problems that are encoded into diagonal Hamiltonians. By replacing the conventional expectation value with CVaR during the classical optimization loop, the invention enables more effective sampling of optimal or near-optimal solutions, particularly on noisy intermediate-scale quantum (NISQ) devices where circuit depth and fidelity are limited.

## BACKGROUND

Quantum mechanics provides a theoretical framework for understanding physical phenomena at atomic and subatomic scales, governed by principles such as superposition, entanglement, and measurement-induced collapse. Unlike classical physics, which describes deterministic trajectories, quantum mechanics employs probabilistic descriptions of system states through wave functions residing in Hilbert space. Classical computers, based on Boolean logic and binary bits, process information using transistors that represent either 0 or 1, enabling computation through sequential logic operations. Despite their success, classical computers face fundamental limitations in efficiently solving certain classes of problems, particularly those involving exponential complexity.

Quantum computing leverages quantum mechanical principles to perform computations in ways that may surpass classical capabilities for specific tasks. Central to quantum computing is the qubit, the quantum analog of the classical bit, which can exist in a superposition of |0⟩ and |1⟩ states, represented as α|0⟩ + β|1⟩ where |α|² + |β|² = 1. Multiple qubits can be entangled, creating non-local correlations that enable parallel processing of information. IBM’s quantum processor, among others, implements superconducting qubits controlled via microwave pulses, offering a platform for executing quantum circuits with limited coherence times and gate fidelities characteristic of NISQ devices.

Combinatorial optimization problems, such as maximum cut, number partitioning, and portfolio optimization, involve selecting the best configuration from a finite but exponentially large set of possibilities. These problems are often NP-hard and widely encountered in logistics, finance, and machine learning. Hybrid quantum/classical optimization algorithms, including VQE and QAOA, have emerged as promising approaches for tackling such problems on current quantum hardware. These algorithms use a parameterized quantum circuit to prepare a trial state, measure observables, and feed results back to a classical optimizer that adjusts parameters to minimize a cost function—typically the expected value of a problem-encoded Hamiltonian.

However, current methods suffer from significant limitations. The reliance on the sample mean as the objective function fails to prioritize high-quality solutions when the Hamiltonian is diagonal, as is the case for classical combinatorial problems. Classical algorithms for mixed integer optimization, such as branch-and-bound or cutting-plane methods, remain highly effective but do not exploit potential quantum speedups. There is a recognized need for a novel method that better aligns the quantum-classical feedback loop with the practical goal of heuristic optimization: finding good solutions with high probability, rather than minimizing average energy. Furthermore, the solution space explored by shallow-depth quantum circuits is often insufficiently expressive, especially in QAOA, which uses a fixed number of parameters independent of problem size, leading to “flat” output distributions that rarely sample optimal states.

## SUMMARY

The present invention introduces a method for solving mixed integer optimization problems using a hybrid quantum-classical framework enhanced by Conditional Value-at-Risk (CVaR) as the objective function. The method begins by generating decision variables corresponding to the combinatorial structure of the problem, such as binary assignments or integer selections. These variables are then mapped to a diagonal Hamiltonian whose ground state encodes the optimal solution. Quantum state parameters—including rotation angles and entangling gate strengths—are derived from this Hamiltonian and used to configure a parameterized quantum circuit.

The quantum processor is initiated to prepare the trial quantum state according to the derived parameters. Intermediate quantum states are measured multiple times to obtain a set of samples, each corresponding to a candidate solution and its associated objective value. Rather than averaging all samples, the invention evaluates only the worst-performing α-fraction (e.g., the lowest 10%) of samples, computing their conditional expectation—this is the CVaR. This aggregated value serves as the objective for the classical optimizer, which updates the quantum parameters to minimize CVaR iteratively.

The classical processor performs calculations to update parameters using gradient-based or derivative-free optimizers such as COBYLA. This loop continues until convergence criteria are met, yielding a quantum state with high probability of sampling near-optimal solutions. The invention provides a robust framework for mixed integer optimization, applicable to problems like portfolio selection, graph partitioning, and satisfiability. Additionally, the invention encompasses a computer program product comprising instructions stored on a non-transitory medium that, when executed, implement the described method on a hybrid computing system.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The optimization problem addressed by the present invention is a mixed integer optimization problem, which generalizes combinatorial optimization by allowing both discrete and continuous variables. A classical optimization problem is typically formulated as minimizing an objective function f(x) subject to constraints g_i(x) ≤ 0 and h_j(x) = 0, where x includes integer and real components. The objective function may be linear, quadratic, or nonlinear, and constraints can be equality or inequality conditions. In the context of quantum computing, such problems are often reformulated as Quadratic Unconstrained Binary Optimization (QUBO) or Ising models, enabling encoding into a Hamiltonian.

A hybrid classical-quantum computing system integrates a classical processor with a quantum processor, where the classical component handles high-level control, parameter updates, and data aggregation, while the quantum component prepares and measures quantum states. Current hybrid algorithms like VQE and QAOA exhibit limitations: they optimize the expected value of the Hamiltonian, which dilutes the impact of rare high-quality samples, and they often use insufficiently expressive ansätze, especially QAOA with fixed depth.

Linear equality constraints in the original problem are handled by introducing slack variables to convert them into equality constraints suitable for binary encoding. Inequality constraints are similarly transformed using auxiliary variables or penalty terms in the objective function. The invention extends the VQE approach by replacing the expectation minimization with CVaR minimization, thereby focusing the optimization on the tail of the solution distribution. Similarly, the QAOA approach is extended by redefining its classical subroutine to minimize CVaR instead of the mean.

The hybrid quantum/classical optimization algorithm operates as follows: a classical processor runs an optimization scheme (e.g., COBYLA) that derives quantum circuit angles and classical parameters based on prior measurements. The quantum processor prepares the corresponding state, executes the circuit, and measures outcomes to generate samples. These samples are evaluated by computing CVaR_α, which guides the next parameter update. The process repeats until convergence, defined by stagnation in CVaR improvement or a maximum iteration count.

Application components include a classical optimizer component that interfaces with the quantum system, a decision variable determination component that maps the problem to qubits, and modules that derive quantum angles and classical parameters.
