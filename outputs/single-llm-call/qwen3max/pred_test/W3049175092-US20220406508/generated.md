# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

The Dzyaloshinskii-Moriya interaction (DMI) is a fundamental antisymmetric exchange interaction that arises in magnetic systems lacking inversion symmetry, and it plays a pivotal role in stabilizing noncollinear spin textures such as chiral domain walls, spin spirals, and magnetic skyrmions. First proposed by Dzyaloshinskii based on phenomenological symmetry arguments and later explained microscopically by Moriya, the DMI originates from the interplay between spin-orbit coupling and broken spatial inversion symmetry, typically at interfaces or in noncentrosymmetric bulk crystals. In thin-film heterostructures, the DMI manifests most prominently at interfaces between ferromagnetic layers and adjacent nonmagnetic materials, where the structural asymmetry along the out-of-plane direction provides the necessary conditions for its emergence. The energy associated with the DMI is expressed as −**D**<sub>ij</sub> ⋅ (**S**<sub>i</sub> × **S**<sub>j</sub>), where **D**<sub>ij</sub> is the Dzyaloshinskii-Moriya vector whose sign dictates the handedness—left- or right-handed—of the resulting chiral spin configuration, while its magnitude influences the stability and spatial extent of these topological magnetic objects.

Extensive experimental efforts have been devoted to understanding and harnessing the DMI, particularly in the context of spintronic applications that rely on the controlled manipulation of chiral magnetic textures. Most studies have focused on ferromagnet/heavy metal bilayers or multilayers, leveraging the strong spin-orbit coupling inherent in heavy elements such as platinum, iridium, tantalum, and tungsten to generate sizable interfacial DMI. These systems have enabled the stabilization of Néel-type domain walls and skyrmions at room temperature, forming the basis for emerging technologies like racetrack memory and skyrmion-based logic devices. Additionally, DMI has been observed at ferromagnet/oxide interfaces, where electric-field control via voltage gating offers a pathway for low-power tuning of magnetic chirality. Despite these advances, the exploration of light elements—particularly those with low atomic numbers—as sources of DMI has remained limited, even though theoretical predictions suggest that charge transfer and hybridization at chemisorbed interfaces could induce significant DMI through mechanisms distinct from conventional heavy-metal-induced effects.

Current approaches to measuring and quantifying DMI often rely on indirect methods such as Brillouin light scattering, magneto-optical Kerr effect microscopy, or spin-wave spectroscopy, which infer DMI strength from dynamic or thermodynamic properties. Direct real-space imaging techniques like spin-polarized low-energy electron microscopy (SPLEEM) offer superior precision by enabling direct visualization of domain wall chirality, yet their application has been largely restricted to model systems under ultrahigh vacuum conditions. A critical limitation of existing methodologies is the lack of a tunable reference platform that allows unambiguous determination of unknown DMI contributions from newly introduced interfaces. Without such a platform, it becomes difficult to isolate and quantify the DMI induced by adsorbates, molecular layers, or other surface modifications, especially when multiple competing interactions are present.

Moreover, while heavy metals provide strong DMI, they are often expensive, scarce, and incompatible with certain integration schemes in semiconductor manufacturing. There exists a pressing need for alternative strategies to induce and control DMI using earth-abundant, lightweight, and chemically versatile species that can be reversibly adsorbed or desorbed. Such approaches would not only reduce material costs but also enable dynamic, in situ control of magnetic chirality through gas exposure, electrochemical gating, or thermal desorption—capabilities essential for next-generation reconfigurable spintronic devices. The absence of experimental validation for DMI induced by chemisorbed light elements like oxygen, despite compelling theoretical predictions linking DMI to electronegativity differences and interfacial dipole moments, underscores a significant gap in the field. Therefore, there is a clear and urgent need for improved techniques that combine tunable DMI platforms with direct, quantitative measurement of chirality evolution in response to controlled surface chemistry, thereby enabling the rational design of DMI-engineered magnetic heterostructures without reliance on heavy metals.

## SUMMARY

The present invention encompasses embodiments directed to the generation, control, and utilization of the Dzyaloshinskii-Moriya interaction (DMI) through the chemisorption of atomic or molecular species onto ferromagnetic surfaces, particularly at room temperature. In one aspect, the invention discloses that oxygen chemisorbed on ferromagnetic layers induces a substantial DMI, comparable in magnitude to that generated at interfaces with heavy transition metals such as platinum or palladium. This oxygen-induced DMI arises from charge transfer and orbital hybridization between the highly electronegative oxygen adatoms and the 3d orbitals of the underlying ferromagnet, creating a strong interfacial electric dipole that, in conjunction with spin-orbit coupling, gives rise to a robust antisymmetric exchange interaction. The magnitude and sign of this DMI depend critically on the oxygen coverage, enabling continuous and reversible tuning of magnetic chirality by controlling the amount of adsorbed oxygen.

The invention further describes the oxygen coverage–dependent evolution of domain wall chirality, wherein increasing oxygen coverage systematically shifts the magnetic texture from left-handed Néel walls through achiral Bloch walls to right-handed Néel walls, depending on the initial effective DMI of the underlying multilayer stack. This evolution demonstrates that chemisorbed oxygen can not only modulate but also reverse the chirality of spin textures, offering a powerful knob for magnetic design. The magnitude of the oxygen-induced DMI is quantified as approximately 0.63 ± 0.26 meV/atom at monolayer coverage, a value that rivals many heavy-metal interfaces and far exceeds that of other light-element systems such as graphene/Co.

An apparatus for generating controllable DMI according to the invention comprises a ferromagnetic layer deposited on a substrate, optionally separated by a nonmagnetic spacer layer, wherein the ferromagnetic layer is exposed to a controlled atmosphere of a chemisorbing substance such as oxygen or hydrogen. Suitable ferromagnet materials include nickel, cobalt, iron, and their alloys, particularly in ultrathin films exhibiting perpendicular magnetic anisotropy (PMA). The substance chemisorbed on the surface may be oxygen, hydrogen, or organic molecules such as bathocuproine (BCP), each capable of inducing DMI through interfacial charge redistribution. The DMI can be precisely controlled by varying the thickness of an intervening metal layer—such as palladium or tungsten—which itself contributes a competing DMI of opposite sign, thereby enabling fine-tuning of the net effective DMI. Additionally, the DMI is controlled by adjusting the thickness of the chemisorbed substance layer, as sub-monolayer variations in coverage produce measurable changes in chirality.

In a specific embodiment, skyrmions are generated or annihilated by modulating the oxygen coverage, demonstrating that chemisorption can directly write or delete topological magnetic bits. The invention also introduces perpendicular magnetic anisotropy (PMA) that is enhanced or suppressed by the same chemisorbed species, allowing simultaneous control of both DMI and PMA—two critical parameters for stabilizing chiral spin textures. PMA is controlled by the choice of chemisorbed substance and its thickness, with BCP shown to significantly enhance PMA in Ni/Co systems, shifting the spin reorientation transition to thicker ferromagnetic layers. A method for generating PMA thus involves depositing a chemisorbing organic molecule onto a ferromagnetic film, thereby modifying the surface electronic structure to favor out-of-plane magnetization.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The invention is founded on the discovery that chemisorbed species—particularly oxygen, hydrogen, and organic molecules—induce significant Dzyaloshinskii-Moriya interaction (DMI) and perpendicular magnetic anisotropy (PMA) at the interface with ferromagnetic thin films. This effect is demonstrated using spin-polarized low-energy electron microscopy (SPLEEM) to directly image the chirality of domain walls in Ni/Co-based multilayers grown on W(110) substrates with Pd spacer layers. Oxygen coverage–dependent evolution of domain wall chirality reveals a systematic transition from left-handed to right-handed Néel walls as oxygen coverage increases, confirming that chemisorbed oxygen generates a right-handed DMI that competes with the intrinsic DMI of the multilayer stack. The motivation for tuning the effective DMI by controlling the Pd spacer layer thickness stems from the opposing signs of DMI induced by tungsten (right-handed) and palladium (left-handed); by varying the Pd thickness, the net DMI can be swept through zero, creating an ideal platform to quantify unknown DMI contributions from surface adsorbates.

Experimental results summarize that chemisorbed oxygen induces a DMI of substantial magnitude, enabling its application in spintronics for chirality control without heavy metals. An experimental approach to measure unknown DMI contributions involves monitoring the domain wall spin texture as a function of Pd layer thickness, where chirality switching indicates a sign change in effective DMI. Measurements show that DW magnetization orientation, extracted from SPLEEM vector field analysis, shifts continuously with Pd thickness, with histograms of the chirality angle α revealing clear transitions near 2.5 monolayers of Pd. Compound SPLEEM images visually confirm the switching of Néel chirality, with color-coded in-plane magnetization overlaid on grayscale out-of-plane contrast.

Further examination of chemisorbed oxygen on Ni(111) leverages the well-established phase diagram of oxygen adsorption, where coverages up to 0.29 monolayers remain in a disordered lattice gas phase without oxide formation. Oxygen coverage–dependent chirality evolution on Ni/Co/Pd/W(110) shows that higher Pd thickness requires greater oxygen coverage to achieve chirality reversal, consistent with a linear dependence of DMI on Pd thickness. Quantification yields an oxygen-induced DMI of 0.63 ± 0.26 meV/atom, comparable to Pt/Ni and exceeding Ir/Ni or W/Ni interfaces. A phase diagram in d<sub>O</sub>-d<sub>Pd</sub> space maps regions of left-handed, achiral, and right-handed chirality, enabling predictive design of magnetic stacks.

Comparisons between Pd-induced and oxygen-induced DMI reveal that although oxygen is a light element, its high electronegativity drives significant charge transfer, generating an interfacial dipole that enhances DMI via spin-orbit coupling. Modeling of film thickness dependence supports the experimental chirality transitions, and DMI strength is determined through dipolar energy calculations calibrated against known systems. Summarized DM vector magnitudes show that oxygen/Ni interfaces produce DMI values rivaling those of Co/Pd (1.44 meV/atom) and exceeding Co/Ru or graphene/Co.

The invention extends to hydrogen chemisorption, which similarly induces reversible DMI. SPLEEM experiments on Ni(111), Co(0001), and Ni/Co/Pd/W(110) show hydrogen exposure–dependent chirality switching, with LEEM IV spectra and work function measurements confirming reversible adsorption/desorption. Hydrogen-induced DMI is estimated through analogous thickness-comparison methods, demonstrating that multiple light elements can serve as DMI sources. Reversible control is further illustrated by real-time SPLEEM sequences showing skyrmion creation and annihilation upon oxygen dosing and annealing, highlighting potential for rewritable magnetic memory.

Organic molecules like BCP also induce strong DMI and enhance PMA. SPLEEM observations confirm BCP-induced chirality switching and a shift in the spin reorientation transition, indicating increased PMA. Device embodiments incorporate reservoir layers that supply or absorb ions (e.g., H<sup>+</sup> or O<sup>2−</sup>), enabling electrochemical or gas-phase control of chemisorption. Applications span racetrack memories, artificial synapses for neuromorphic computing, and gas sensors where magnetic response correlates with analyte concentration. Sample preparation under ultrahigh vacuum ensures precise control of exposures, while time-dependent work function and domain wall analyses validate reversibility. Image denoising and statistical methods ensure robust chirality quantification. The scope of the invention includes any ferromagnet interfaced with a chemisorbing species that alters interfacial electronic structure, thereby enabling universal, heavy-metal-free DMI engineering.
