# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNICAL FIELD

The present invention resides in the technical field of cellular immunotherapy, particularly in the development and application of engineered immune cells for the treatment of cancer and other diseases. More specifically, the invention pertains to the generation of allogeneic, off-the-shelf gamma delta (γδ) T cell therapies derived from pluripotent human cells, including hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs), through gene engineering and in vitro differentiation techniques. The invention further encompasses methods for producing these engineered γδ T cells, compositions comprising such cells, and their therapeutic use in adoptive cell transfer. The technical domain also includes genetic engineering methodologies, such as viral vector-mediated transduction and CRISPR-Cas9-based gene editing, employed to confer desired functional properties—including enhanced tumor targeting, reduced immunogenicity, and safety controls—onto the resulting γδ T cell products. Additionally, the invention relates to the fields of molecular biology, immunology, and biopharmaceutical manufacturing, as it involves the design of recombinant nucleic acids encoding γδ T cell receptor (TCR) polypeptides, the formulation of cell culture media for ex vivo differentiation and expansion, and the development of scalable, feeder-free or feeder-dependent culture systems suitable for clinical-grade production.

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

Gamma delta (γδ) T cells represent a unique subset of T lymphocytes that bridge innate and adaptive immunity. Unlike conventional alpha beta (αβ) T cells, which recognize peptide antigens presented by major histocompatibility complex (MHC) molecules, γδ T cells can directly recognize stress-induced molecules, phosphoantigens, and lipid antigens in an MHC-unrestricted manner. This recognition capability allows γδ T cells to respond rapidly to infections, malignancies, and tissue damage without the need for classical antigen processing and presentation. Human γδ T cells, particularly those expressing the Vγ9Vδ2 TCR, are known to exhibit potent cytotoxic activity against a wide range of tumor cells and can secrete pro-inflammatory cytokines such as interferon-γ (IFN-γ) and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α). These attributes make γδ T cells attractive candidates for cancer immunotherapy.

Despite their therapeutic potential, the clinical application of γδ T cells has been hindered by several limitations. Primary among these is the low frequency of γδ T cells in peripheral blood, typically constituting only 1–5% of circulating T lymphocytes in healthy adults. Moreover, the ability to expand γδ T cells ex vivo from patient-derived blood is highly variable and often inefficient, especially in patients with advanced disease or prior chemotherapy. This variability poses a significant barrier to the reliable production of autologous γδ T cell products for adoptive therapy. Furthermore, even when expanded, primary γδ T cells may exhibit functional exhaustion or limited persistence in vivo, reducing their therapeutic efficacy.

Conventional methods for generating γδ T cells for therapeutic use have largely relied on the isolation and expansion of endogenous γδ T cells from peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) using phosphoantigens like zoledronate (ZOL) or synthetic analogs. While this approach has shown promise in early-phase clinical trials, it remains constrained by donor-to-donor variability, the presence of contaminating αβ T cells that carry a risk of graft-versus-host disease (GvHD) in allogeneic settings, and the logistical challenges associated with autologous manufacturing. These limitations underscore the need for a more robust, scalable, and standardized method to produce γδ T cells for widespread clinical use.

The growing demand for “off-the-shelf” allogeneic cell therapies—products that can be manufactured in large batches from a single donor and administered to multiple patients—has further motivated the development of novel platforms for γδ T cell production. Such therapies offer significant advantages over autologous approaches, including reduced cost, faster availability, and consistent product quality. However, realizing this vision requires overcoming the inherent biological constraints of primary γδ T cells and developing engineering strategies that ensure both safety and potency. The present invention addresses this unmet need by providing a method to generate engineered, allogeneic γδ T cells from pluripotent human cells, thereby enabling the production of high-quality, off-the-shelf cellular therapeutics with enhanced tumor-targeting capabilities and reduced immunogenicity.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides engineered, off-the-shelf gamma delta (γδ) T cell therapies derived from pluripotent human cells, particularly hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs), through a combination of gene engineering and controlled in vitro differentiation. The invention introduces a platform for producing allogeneic γδ T cells that are not only highly pure and functionally potent but also amenable to further genetic modification to enhance their therapeutic properties. Central to this platform is the transduction of pluripotent human cells with exogenous nucleic acid molecules encoding specific γ and δ chain polypeptides that form a functional γδ T cell receptor (TCR). These transduced cells are then differentiated in vitro into mature, functional γδ T cells using optimized culture conditions, including artificial thymic organoid (ATO) systems or feeder-free media formulations.

The invention encompasses compositions of matter comprising the engineered γδ T cells, as well as the polynucleotides and vectors used to express the γδ TCR chains. Specifically, the γ and δ chain polypeptides may be derived from naturally occurring or optimized sequences, such as the Vγ9Vδ2 TCR, and are encoded by recombinant nucleic acids delivered via viral vectors, including lentiviral or retroviral constructs. The method of producing engineered functional γδ T cells involves the transduction of pluripotent human cells—such as CD34+ HSCs isolated from cord blood or mobilized peripheral blood—with these vectors, followed by in vitro differentiation under conditions that promote T lineage commitment and γδ T cell maturation.

A key aspect of the invention is the ability to differentiate transduced pluripotent cells in vitro through a multi-stage process that mimics thymic development. This includes initial culture in an ATO system expressing Notch ligands, which supports early T cell progenitor differentiation, followed by expansion in cytokine-rich media supplemented with factors such as IL-7, IL-15, and phosphoantigens like ZOL. The invention also contemplates co-culturing transduced cells with stromal feeder cells or using serum-free, xeno-free media formulations to support robust expansion while maintaining functional integrity. In some embodiments, the differentiated γδ T cells are further expanded in vitro to achieve clinically relevant cell numbers, and optional steps such as cell sorting or magnetic-activated cell sorting (MACS) may be employed to enrich for desired phenotypes.

The engineered γδ T cells of the invention exhibit distinct characteristics that enhance their therapeutic utility. These include a CD8 single-positive or double-negative phenotype, high expression of memory and NK cell markers (e.g., CD161, CD45RO), and elevated levels of inflammatory homing receptors (e.g., CCR5, CXCR3), which facilitate tumor infiltration. Critically, the cells display reduced surface expression of HLA class I and II molecules, rendering them less immunogenic and more resistant to host-mediated allorejection. Additionally, the invention enables the engraftment of these cells in vivo following adoptive transfer, where they demonstrate long-term persistence and antitumor activity in preclinical models.

The applications of the engineered γδ T cells are broad, encompassing the treatment of various cancers, including hematological malignancies and solid tumors, as well as infectious diseases. The cells can be further engineered to express chimeric antigen receptors (CARs), bispecific engagers, or immunomodulatory cytokines, thereby enhancing their tumor-targeting specificity and potency. The invention also incorporates safety features, such as suicide genes (e.g., herpes simplex virus thymidine kinase, HSV-TK), which allow for controlled elimination of the cells in the event of adverse reactions. In summary, the invention provides a scalable, robust, and versatile platform for generating off-the-shelf γδ T cell therapies with superior functional properties, reduced immunogenicity, and enhanced safety profiles.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

Gamma delta (γδ) T cells are a specialized subset of T lymphocytes that play a critical role in immune surveillance due to their ability to recognize a wide array of non-peptide antigens, including phosphoantigens and stress-induced molecules, without the requirement for major histocompatibility complex (MHC) presentation. This MHC-unrestricted recognition endows γδ T cells with the capacity to target a broad spectrum of tumor cells and infected cells, making them highly attractive for cancer immunotherapy. However, the natural scarcity of γδ T cells in peripheral blood and the inefficiency of ex vivo expansion from patient samples have historically limited their clinical application. The present invention addresses these challenges by introducing a novel method for generating engineered, allogeneic γδ T cells from pluripotent human cells, thereby enabling the production of high-quality, off-the-shelf cellular therapeutics.

The motivation for developing engineered γδ T cells stems from the limitations of current methods, which rely on the isolation and expansion of endogenous γδ T cells from peripheral blood. These methods are plagued by donor variability, low yields, and the risk of contamination with conventional αβ T cells, which can cause graft-versus-host disease (GvHD) in allogeneic settings. The invention’s solution is to bypass these limitations by genetically engineering pluripotent human cells—such as hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs)—to express defined γδ T cell receptors (TCRs) and then differentiating these cells in vitro into functional γδ T cells. This approach ensures a consistent, scalable, and pure product that is free from GvHD risk.

Pluripotent human cells, including CD34+ HSCs derived from cord blood or granulocyte-colony-stimulating factor (G-CSF)-mobilized peripheral blood, possess the multipotentiality to differentiate into all hematopoietic lineages, including T cells. The CD34 marker, a well-established surface antigen for identifying HSCs and progenitor cells, is used to isolate these starting populations. An unexpected discovery underpinning the invention is that γδ T cell development can be efficiently recapitulated in vitro from these engineered HSCs, yielding cells with a phenotype and functionality that closely resemble or even surpass those of endogenous human γδ T cells. This in vitro differentiation is critical because it allows for the production of mature, functional γδ T cells in a controlled, scalable manner, independent of patient-specific factors.

The engineered γδ T cells of the invention can be further modified to co-express disease-targeting molecules, such as chimeric antigen receptors (CARs), which enhance their specificity and potency against particular tumor antigens. The method of making engineered functional γδ T cells begins with the transduction of pluripotent human cells with recombinant vectors encoding the γ and δ chain polypeptides of a selected γδ TCR. This transduction is typically achieved using lentiviral or retroviral vectors, which integrate the transgenes into the host genome, ensuring stable expression throughout differentiation and expansion. Following transduction, the cells are differentiated in vitro using culture systems that mimic the thymic microenvironment, such as artificial thymic organoids (ATOs) expressing Notch ligands, which are essential for T cell lineage commitment.

The differentiation process is followed by expansion of the resulting γδ T cells in cytokine-supplemented media, often containing interleukin-7 (IL-7) and interleukin-15 (IL-15), to achieve clinically relevant cell numbers. Optional steps may include cell sorting to enrich for specific subsets, co-culturing with feeder cells to support growth, or engrafting the cells in vivo in preclinical models to assess functionality. The engineered γδ T cells exhibit a distinct gene expression profile characterized by high levels of transcription factors associated with innate-like T cell function (e.g., ZBTB16/PLZF), Th1-polarizing cytokines (e.g., IFN-γ, TNF-α), and cytotoxic molecules (e.g., perforin, granzyme B). They also express elevated levels of NK cell activating receptors (e.g., NKG2D, DNAM-1) and inflammatory homing markers (e.g., CCR5, CXCR3), while displaying reduced expression of HLA class I and II molecules, which contributes to their low immunogenicity.

The invention further encompasses the use of exogenous nucleic acid molecules to encode therapeutic gene products, such as CARs, cytokines (e.g., IL-15), or suicide genes (e.g., HSV-TK). Compositions of matter include the γδ T cells themselves, as well as the polynucleotides and vectors encoding the γ and δ chain polypeptides. These vectors may be designed for constitutive or inducible expression and can incorporate additional elements, such as reporter genes (e.g., truncated EGFR) for tracking. The engineered γδ T cells can be administered to patients via adoptive transfer, either alone or in combination with other therapies, and have demonstrated potent antitumor efficacy in preclinical models of melanoma, leukemia, and multiple myeloma.

Materials and methods relating to the γ and δ chain polypeptides include the cloning of TCR genes from human donors or synthetic optimization for enhanced affinity and stability. The construction of lentiviral vectors involves the insertion of these TCR genes into backbone plasmids, followed by virus production in packaging cells. Functional characterization of cloned γδ TCRs confirms their ability to confer antigen-specific reactivity upon transduction. Long-term in vivo provision of transgenic γδ T cells has been demonstrated in immunodeficient mouse models, where the cells persist and mediate tumor regression. The invention also describes the in vitro generation of allogeneic HSC-engineered γδ T cells (AlloHSC-γδT cells) using both feeder-dependent and feeder-independent cultures, with the latter offering advantages for clinical scalability.

The scale of AlloHSC-γδT cell production is substantial, with yields sufficient to generate hundreds to thousands of patient doses from a single donor. Phenotypic and functional analyses confirm that these cells closely resemble endogenous human γδ T cells, with superior cytotoxicity and cytokine production. Figures referenced in the invention illustrate key findings: FIG. 8 shows the phenotype and functionality of AlloHSC-γδT cells, demonstrating their similarity to endogenous cells; FIG. 9 establishes an in vitro tumor cell killing assay, revealing potent activity against multiple tumor lines; FIG. 10 evaluates in vivo antitumor efficacy, showing superiority over PBMC-NK cells; FIG. 11 studies CAR-engineered AlloBCAR-γδT cells, highlighting dual CAR-dependent and -independent killing pathways; FIG. 12 and FIG. 13 demonstrate enhanced in vivo efficacy of AlloBCAR-γδT cells compared to conventional CAR-T cells, even in high-tumor-burden settings; FIG. 14 characterizes IL-15-enhanced variants with improved persistence; and FIG. 15 and FIG. 16 confirm low immunogenicity and favorable comparison to existing methods.

Additional features include the incorporation of suicide genes, such as viral thymidine kinase (TK), which can be activated by prodrugs like ganciclovir (GCV) for safety control. The lack of surface HLA-I/II expression is achieved through gene editing technologies like CRISPR-Cas9, using guide RNAs to disrupt B2M and CIITA genes. Serum-free media formulations, containing components like B-27 supplement, vitamins, and proteins, support robust differentiation and expansion. Cell populations are produced from single batches, frozen in solutions containing dextrose, electrolytes, albumin, dextran, and DMSO, and remain stable upon thawing. The invention is broadly applicable, with embodiments covering various culture conditions, genetic modifications, and therapeutic applications, ultimately providing a transformative platform for off-the-shelf γδ T cell therapy.

### EXAMPLES

The invention is exemplified through a series of experimental demonstrations that validate its core principles and therapeutic potential. First, human Vγ9Vδ2 TCRs were cloned from donor γδ T cells and inserted into lentiviral vectors for gene delivery. These vectors were used to transduce CD34+ hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs) isolated from cord blood or mobilized peripheral blood, initiating the process of γδ T cell engineering. The transduced HSCs were then differentiated into transgenic γδ T cells using a two-stage in vitro culture system: an initial artificial thymic organoid (ATO) phase to support T lineage commitment, followed by expansion in cytokine-rich media supplemented with zoledronate (ZOL) to selectively expand γδ T cells. This method reliably generated high-purity populations of engineered γδ T cells, termed AlloHSC-γδT cells, with yields sufficient for clinical-scale production.

Next, the AlloHSC-γδT cells were further engineered to express additional targeting molecules, such as chimeric antigen receptors (CARs), to create AlloBCAR-γδT cells. For instance, a second-generation BCMA-targeting CAR (BCAR) was introduced into AlloHSC-γδT cells via retroviral transduction, resulting in cells that retained their native γδ TCR functionality while gaining CAR-mediated specificity for multiple myeloma cells. These dual-targeting cells exhibited enhanced tumor killing in vitro and in vivo, leveraging both CAR-dependent and CAR-independent (i.e., γδ TCR- and NK receptor-mediated) mechanisms to overcome antigen escape. The utility of AlloHSC-γδT cells as a platform for off-the-shelf disease-targeting cellular therapy was thus firmly established.

To further enhance therapeutic efficacy, AlloHSC-γδT cells were engineered to overexpress genes encoding T cell stimulatory factors, such as interleukin-15 (IL-15), resulting in Allo15BCAR-γδT cells with improved in vivo persistence and antitumor responses. Conversely, genes encoding T cell inhibitory factors, such as PD-1, were disrupted using CRISPR-Cas9 to prevent exhaustion and maintain effector function. The invention also demonstrated the in vivo generation of HSC-γδT cells by adoptively transferring γδ TCR gene-engineered HSCs into immunodeficient mice, where they reconstituted functional γδ T cell compartments—a proof-of-concept for HSC-based adoptive therapy.

For allogeneic applications, HSCs were genetically modified to express a selected γδ TCR gene and then differentiated ex vivo into AlloHSC-γδT cells using either feeder-dependent or feeder-free culture systems. Feeder-dependent methods employed stromal cells expressing Notch ligands, while feeder-free approaches utilized serum-free media formulations, both achieving robust expansion and high purity. The resulting AlloHSC-γδT cells were shown to target multiple diseases, including hematological cancers like leukemia and solid tumors like melanoma, with superior efficacy compared to conventional NK or CAR-T cells. Critically, these cells exhibited no graft-versus-host disease (GvHD) in preclinical models and displayed low immunogenicity due to reduced HLA expression, making them ideal for off-the-shelf use. The novel features and advantages of AlloHSC-γδT cells—scalability, purity, multifunctionality, and safety—were conclusively demonstrated, providing a compelling proof of principle for their clinical translation.

## ILLUSTRATIVE VECTOR SEQUENCES

The practice of the present invention employs recombinant nucleic acid constructs that are either novel or derived from established molecular biology techniques, as described in standard references such as Sambrook et al., Molecular Cloning: A Laboratory Manual (3rd ed., Cold Spring Harbor Laboratory Press, 2001), and Ausubel et al., Current Protocols in Molecular Biology (John Wiley & Sons, 2003). Key terms used herein are defined in accordance with their ordinary meaning in the art, as further clarified by these references. For example, “pluripotent human cells” refer to cells capable of differentiating into any cell type of the hematopoietic lineage, including HSCs marked by CD34 expression; “gamma delta T cell receptor” denotes a heterodimeric receptor composed of gamma and delta chain polypeptides that confer antigen specificity; and “off-the-shelf” signifies allogeneic cell products manufactured in advance for immediate therapeutic use. The vector sequences utilized, such as lentiviral backbones (e.g., pMNDW) and retroviral constructs (e.g., pMP71), are well-known in the field and have been adapted herein to encode the specific γδ TCR genes and auxiliary elements (e.g., suicide genes, reporters) as detailed in the Examples.
