# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD

The present invention relates to differentiable rendering, and more particularly to methods, systems, and non-transitory computer-readable media for generating differentiable two-dimensional renderings from three-dimensional surface representations, including explicit meshes, parametric surfaces, and implicit isosurfaces. The invention enables the computation of smooth, accurate derivatives of rendered images with respect to underlying scene parameters—even at occlusion boundaries—by combining a non-differentiable rasterization step with a differentiable splatting operation. This approach supports end-to-end optimization in inverse graphics, pose estimation, shape reconstruction, and neural rendering applications without requiring custom gradient implementations or specialized hardware.

## BACKGROUND

Contemporary differentiable rendering techniques face significant limitations when applied to real-world surface representations such as triangle meshes, parametric splines, or implicit isosurfaces derived from volumetric fields. Traditional mesh-based renderers produce undefined or discontinuous gradients at occlusion boundaries, where visibility changes abruptly, thereby hindering gradient-based optimization in tasks like 3D reconstruction or pose estimation. While some approaches attempt to circumvent this by replacing gradients heuristically or by explicitly sampling occluding edges, these methods often suffer from convergence instability, high computational cost that scales with mesh complexity, or incompatibility with complex shading models and textures. Other strategies reparameterize the rendering integral for path tracing contexts, but these are not applicable to standard rasterization pipelines. Volumetric representations such as Neural Radiance Fields (NeRF) offer naturally differentiable rendering through volume integration, but they require expensive ray marching and are inefficient when the underlying geometry is well-approximated by a surface. Surface splatting methods treat geometry as point clouds, which avoids mesh processing but introduces sampling artifacts during optimization due to fixed or poorly adapted point distributions. Implicit surface renderers often assume user-provided occlusion masks or fail to handle self-occlusions, while Marching Cubes-based isosurface extraction is non-differentiable due to topological ambiguities near grid-cell singularities. Consequently, there remains a critical need for a general-purpose differentiable rendering framework that supports diverse surface representations, provides smooth derivatives at occlusion boundaries, operates efficiently on standard hardware, and integrates seamlessly with automatic differentiation systems without custom gradient code.

## SUMMARY

The present invention introduces a novel method for differentiable rendering that overcomes the limitations of existing approaches by decoupling visibility resolution from derivative computation. The method begins by obtaining a three-dimensional mesh or surface representation, which may be explicit (e.g., a triangle mesh), parametric (e.g., a B-spline surface), or implicit (e.g., an isosurface of a density field). This three-dimensional mesh is then rasterized using a conventional, non-differentiable graphics pipeline to produce screen-space geometry buffers containing per-pixel surface attributes such as positions, normals, or texture coordinates; this step efficiently resolves occlusions using depth buffering or depth peeling without requiring derivative propagation. Initial color values for each pixel are determined by applying a differentiable shading function—such as texture lookup, Phong shading, or a neural radiance field—to the rasterized attributes in a deferred shading pass. Subsequently, the invention constructs splats: localized, differentiable kernels centered at the projected screen-space positions of the surface samples, each carrying the computed initial color. These splats are designed with smooth falloff profiles, typically Gaussian, and their centers are computed via perspective division and viewport transformation to preserve geometric derivatives. Updated color values for each pixel are then determined by aggregating contributions from nearby splats, weighted based on proximity and depth-aware layering to correctly handle occlusions and disocclusions. The resulting two-dimensional rendering is fully differentiable with respect to the original scene parameters, enabling gradient-based optimization. The invention further encompasses a computing system comprising one or more processors and memory storing instructions that, when executed, perform the aforementioned steps of obtaining a three-dimensional mesh, rasterizing it, determining initial color values, constructing splats, and determining updated color values. Additionally, the invention includes non-transitory computer-readable media encoded with program instructions that, when executed by a computing device, cause the device to carry out the same sequence of operations, thereby facilitating integration into machine learning frameworks and computer vision pipelines.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

### Overview

Differentiable rendering refers to the process of generating a two-dimensional image from a three-dimensional scene in such a way that the image’s pixel values are differentiable functions of the scene’s underlying parameters, such as vertex positions, camera pose, or material properties. The present invention achieves this through a splat-based forward rendering pipeline that separates non-differentiable visibility determination from differentiable color aggregation. The input to the system is a three-dimensional mesh or surface, which may originate from a variety of sources including scanned geometry, procedural generation, or machine-learned models. Rasterization converts this 3D representation into a two-dimensional raster by projecting vertices into screen space, interpolating attributes across triangles or surface patches, and resolving visibility using a Z-buffer; this step is intentionally treated as non-differentiable, allowing the use of highly optimized, off-the-shelf graphics hardware or software rasterizers. For each pixel covered by the surface, the system generates screen-space coordinates and determines an initial color value by applying a differentiable shading scheme—such as texture mapping, lighting models, or neural network evaluations—to the interpolated surface attributes. A splat is then constructed for each such pixel: a localized kernel centered at the pixel’s projected position, colored by the initial color, and defined with a smooth spatial falloff (e.g., a narrow-variance Gaussian). Critically, the splat center is computed from the original 3D geometry through perspective projection and viewport transformation, ensuring that derivatives flow back to the scene parameters. The system preserves derivatives for vertices by expressing all operations after rasterization within an automatic differentiation framework. Updated color values for each output pixel are computed by aggregating splats from neighboring pixels, with weights determined by Euclidean distance in screen space and normalized to ensure stability. This produces a differentiable two-dimensional rendering that exhibits smooth derivatives even at occlusion boundaries, where traditional rasterization yields discontinuities. The three-dimensional mesh itself may be generated by a machine-learned model, such as a neural network trained to predict vertex positions or implicit surface parameters from images or other inputs. The rasterization scheme may employ multi-layer depth peeling to capture multiple surface intersections per pixel, enabling correct handling of transparency or overlapping geometry. The initial color value may incorporate complex shading and texturing schemes, including deferred shading where multiple attribute buffers are combined in a post-rasterization pass. Each splat is constructed with a smooth falloff function to ensure continuous gradients, and its weight is computed based on the distance between the splat center and the target pixel location. In multi-layer scenarios, only a subset of splats—those corresponding to visible or near-visible surface layers—is selected for aggregation, with weights further modulated by depth relationships to avoid spurious contributions from occluded geometry. The final rendering is utilized in downstream tasks such as training machine-learned models, where smooth derivatives enable stable and efficient optimization. During training, a loss function comparing the rendered output to ground truth data is evaluated, and model parameters are adjusted via backpropagation through the differentiable rendering pipeline. Specific applications include a machine-learned pose estimation model that infers object orientation from images, and a machine-learned three-dimensional mesh generation model that reconstructs shape from observations. Compared to point-based rendering, the present method leverages true surface representations, avoiding under- or over-sampling issues. Unlike pure rasterization, which fails to provide derivatives at occlusion edges, the splatting step reintroduces continuity precisely where it is needed. The technical effects and benefits include the ability to generate fully differentiable renderings from arbitrary surface types, reduced computational resource requirements due to image-space operations independent of mesh complexity, and provision of accurate, smooth derivatives that facilitate robust convergence in inverse rendering tasks.

### Example Model Arrangements

To illustrate the splat construction process, consider a one-dimensional line segment rendered onto a pixel grid. Barycentric coordinates define the position of a sample along the segment, and a splat is constructed at the corresponding splat position in screen space. The pixel color value is updated by blending the splat’s color with existing contributions, weighted by proximity. Derivatives are generated from the splat by differentiating the weighted sum with respect to scene parameters. The derivative of the splat’s weight with respect to a vertex position v2, for example, depends on the relative location of the splat center and the pixel; its sign indicates whether increasing v2 would increase or decrease the splat’s influence. If the splat moves closer to the pixel, its weight increases; if it moves away, the weight decreases. This mechanism ensures that gradients correctly guide optimization toward alignment with target images. A data flow diagram of the rendering method shows the sequence: obtain a three-dimensional mesh, rasterize it to produce a two-dimensional raster, determine initial color values by applying shading and/or texture data, construct a splat for each pixel using computed screen-space coordinates, determine updated color values by aggregating splats, and generate the final two-dimensional differentiable rendering. This pipeline excels at finding smooth derivatives at occlusion boundaries, where traditional methods fail. Another data flow diagram depicts model training: obtain training data comprising two-dimensional renderings and corresponding 3D meshes, generate derivatives for respective splats during forward pass, process the rendering with a machine-learned model to produce output (e.g., pose or shape), evaluate a loss function against ground truth, adjust model parameters via backpropagation, and iterate until convergence. The entity represented by the three-dimensional mesh may be any physical or virtual object, such as a human hand, a toy airplane, or a NeRF-reconstructed Lego brick, demonstrating the generality of the approach.

### Example Methods

An exemplary method according to the invention begins by obtaining a three-dimensional mesh, which may be generated using machine-learning techniques such as a neural network trained on 3D scans. The mesh is rasterized to obtain a two-dimensional raster, resolving visibility via depth buffering. For each pixel in the raster, an initial color value is determined by applying shading and/or texture data—this could involve sampling a texture map using interpolated UV coordinates or evaluating a neural radiance field at the surface point. A splat is constructed for each pixel: its center point is computed by applying perspective division and viewport transformation to the 3D surface position, ensuring geometric fidelity. Updated color values are then determined by weighting splats based on proximity to each output pixel; specifically, a subset of nearby splats is selected, their weights are computed as a function of distance (e.g., Gaussian kernel), normalized to sum to at least one, and used to blend colors. This generates a two-dimensional differentiable rendering with smooth derivatives at occlusion boundaries. The rendering is processed by a machine-learned model to generate output such as pose estimates or reconstructed geometry. A loss function quantifies the discrepancy between this output and ground truth, and model parameters are adjusted accordingly. The method supports training of both machine-learned pose estimation models and three-dimensional mesh generation models. For instance, a second pose or orientation may be used to generate synthetic image data for self-supervised learning, or a second three-dimensional mesh may be predicted and compared to a reference. These processes rely on servers, databases, and software applications that exchange information over networks; actions include sending mesh data to a renderer, receiving gradients from a loss module, and updating model weights. The flexibility of computer-based systems allows for numerous variations: splat kernels may be elliptical instead of circular, depth-aware compositing may use more than three layers, or shading may incorporate global illumination approximations. All such embodiments remain within the scope of the invention.
