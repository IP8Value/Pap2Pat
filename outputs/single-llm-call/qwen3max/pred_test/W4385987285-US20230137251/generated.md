# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

### 1. Field of the Invention

The present invention relates generally to the field of biological sensors and, more specifically, to a wirelessly magnetically coupled LC (inductor-capacitor) sensor system for the rapid detection of antibiotic susceptibility in bacterial cultures. The invention provides a novel phenotypic antimicrobial susceptibility testing (AST) platform that enables real-time monitoring of bacterial growth and response to antibiotics without requiring species-specific reagents, complex sample preparation, or optical or genomic analysis. The system is designed for integration into standard laboratory formats such as 96-well microtiter plates and is particularly suited for high-throughput clinical diagnostics where speed, simplicity, and compatibility with complex biological matrices are critical.

### 2. Description of the Related Art

Drug-resistant infections represent a growing global health crisis, with antimicrobial resistance (AMR) contributing to millions of infections and over one million deaths annually worldwide. In intensive care units, up to 30% of patients in high-income countries—and even higher proportions in low- and middle-income countries—are affected by AMR-related complications. Timely and accurate identification of effective antibiotics is essential for patient survival, particularly in cases of sepsis, where delays in appropriate therapy beyond 12 hours can reduce survival rates from 80% to below 15%. Despite this urgency, conventional methods for determining antibiotic susceptibility remain slow and labor-intensive.

Current clinical workflows typically rely on culture-based pathogen detection, which requires a median of 13 hours to yield sufficient biomass (10⁷–10⁸ CFU/mL) for downstream susceptibility testing. Traditional phenotypic AST methods—including broth microdilution, agar disk diffusion, and E-test gradient strips—require an additional 16 to 48 hours to produce interpretable results. While genotypic approaches such as quantitative PCR (qPCR), whole-genome sequencing, and MALDI-TOF mass spectrometry offer faster turnaround times (hours rather than days), they are limited by their dependence on pre-existing knowledge of resistance-associated genetic markers. Consequently, these methods cannot detect novel or uncharacterized resistance mechanisms, which are increasingly common in evolving microbial populations.

In response to these limitations, several next-generation AST platforms have emerged. These include optical imaging systems that track single-cell division in microfluidic chambers, pH-sensitive electrodes that monitor metabolic byproducts of bacterial growth, bioluminescent ATP assays, magnetic biosensors functionalized with antibodies, and electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) devices coated with peptides or capture molecules. Although promising in terms of sensitivity and specificity, these technologies often demand sophisticated instrumentation, extensive data processing, stringent environmental controls, or complex surface chemistry. Moreover, single-cell approaches struggle with polymicrobial samples, where mixed populations obscure individual strain responses.

There remains a critical unmet need for a rapid, label-free, high-throughput phenotypic AST method that operates at the population level, functions in clinically relevant media (including those containing host proteins), and integrates seamlessly into existing laboratory infrastructure. The present invention addresses this need through a novel application of wirelessly coupled LC resonant sensors that exploit the intrinsic dielectric properties of growing bacterial cultures to report real-time changes in permittivity—a proxy for biomass accumulation and metabolic activity. Unlike prior art, this system requires no enrichment, labeling, or species-specific modifications, and delivers actionable susceptibility results within 30 minutes of inoculation.

## BRIEF SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a system and method for rapid phenotypic antimicrobial susceptibility testing using a wirelessly magnetically coupled LC sensor. The system comprises an LC sensor embedded within a standard 96-well plate, where each well contains a bacterial culture exposed to one or more antibiotics. The sensor includes an interdigitated capacitor (IDC) and an inductive coil fabricated on a flexible polyimide substrate, coated with a thin, biocompatible polyurethane layer to prevent fouling. A detection coil, positioned beneath the plate and connected to an impedance analyzer, wirelessly couples with the sensor coil via magnetic induction. As bacteria grow or respond to antibiotics, changes in the complex permittivity of the surrounding medium alter the capacitance of the IDC, thereby shifting the resonant frequency of the LC circuit. This shift is detected in real time by the analyzer, enabling continuous monitoring of bacterial growth dynamics.

The system calculates the complex permittivity of the culture from measured resonant and zero-reactance frequencies using derived analytical equations that incorporate sensor geometry and electromagnetic coupling parameters. By analyzing the slope of the permittivity-time curve over the first 30 minutes of incubation, the system generates a "sensitivity score" that correlates strongly with traditional minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) values. The method has been validated across multiple Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacterial strains—including *Escherichia coli*, *Staphylococcus aureus*, and *Pseudomonas aeruginosa*—and against diverse antibiotic classes such as β-lactams, fluoroquinolones, glycopeptides, and aminoglycosides. Critically, the system remains functional in media supplemented with up to 2% fetal bovine serum, demonstrating robustness in protein-rich environments that mimic diluted clinical specimens. The invention thus enables rapid, automated, and scalable AST without reliance on genomic databases, optical components, or complex fluidics.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

The invention encompasses a complete system for bacterial treatment monitoring based on passive, wireless LC resonant sensing. The core of the system is a miniaturized LC sensor integrated into individual wells of a standard 96-well microtiter plate. Each sensor consists of a planar spiral inductor and an interdigitated capacitor (IDC) patterned onto a flexible polyimide film. The entire structure is conformally coated with a thin layer of oil-based polyurethane to provide chemical stability, prevent biofouling, and ensure electrical insulation while allowing dielectric interaction with the surrounding medium. The sensor is inserted vertically into the well wall to minimize sedimentation artifacts and maximize exposure to the culture volume.

To enable wireless interrogation, a receiver coil is positioned beneath the plate, aligned with each sensor. This coil is connected to an impedance analyzer (e.g., Keysight E4990A-20) that sweeps a frequency range (e.g., 1–12 MHz) to identify the resonant frequency of the coupled LC system. Magnetic coupling between the receiver and sensor coils transmits energy without physical contact, eliminating the need for onboard power or wiring. The resonant frequency depends on the inductance (L) of the coil and the capacitance (C) of the IDC, the latter of which is modulated by the complex permittivity (ε) of the bacterial culture in proximity to the IDC fingers.

The complex permittivity ε is calculated using a derived equation:  
\[
\varepsilon = \frac{C_{1}}{k\varepsilon_{0}} - \varepsilon_{\mathit{sub}} + \frac{1}{kR_{1}\omega_{zero - inductance}\varepsilon_{0}}
\]  
where \( \varepsilon_0 \) is the permittivity of free space, \( \varepsilon_{\mathit{sub}} \) is the substrate permittivity, \( C_1 \) and \( R_1 \) are the effective capacitance and resistance of the IDC, and \( k \) is the cell constant defined by sensor geometry:  
\[
k = \frac{\text{l}(N - 1)K\left[1 - \left( \frac{D}{D + W} \right)^2\right]^{1/2}}{2K\left[\frac{D}{D + W}\right]}
\]  
Here, \( N \) is the number of digits, \( \text{l} \) is digit length, \( D \) is the gap between digits, \( W \) is digit width, and \( K[\cdot] \) denotes the complete elliptic integral of the first kind. The frequencies \( \omega_{\mathit{resonant}} = 1/\sqrt{LC} \) and \( \omega_{zero - inductance} = \sqrt{1/(LC) - 1/(R^2C^2)} \) are extracted from impedance spectra by locating the peak magnitude and zero-phase points, respectively.

Signal processing is performed via a MATLAB script that automates frequency sweeps every 5 minutes, identifies resonant features, and computes permittivity in real time. Electromagnetic simulations (e.g., COMSOL) confirm that standard polystyrene 96-well plate bottoms are magnetically transparent, enabling efficient coupling across the 1.2 mm separation between coils. Experimental validation shows that the system detects bacterial growth within 30 minutes—far earlier than optical density (OD600) measurements, which typically require 5 hours to exit lag phase.

The system was tested using low-salt Luria-Bertani (LB) medium (0.5 g/L NaCl) to minimize ionic interference, though future iterations may accommodate other media. Bacterial samples were prepared by diluting overnight cultures to OD600 ≈ 0.001 and inoculating 300 µL into sensor-equipped wells. Growth curves for *E. coli* MG1655, *S. aureus* ALC2085, and *P. aeruginosa* PAO1 all exhibited rapid permittivity increases within 30 minutes, plateauing after ~3.5 hours due to surface saturation. Antibiotic susceptibility testing with ampicillin clearly differentiated sensitive (*E. coli* MG1655) and resistant (*E. coli* MG1655 ASV) strains within the same timeframe: sensitive strains showed suppressed permittivity slopes, while resistant strains exhibited near-normal growth kinetics.

Dose-response experiments confirmed linear correlation between permittivity slope and antibiotic concentration, with sensitivity scores accurately predicting MIC values established by broth microdilution (e.g., ampicillin MIC = 12.8 µg/mL for *E. coli* MG1655). The system remained functional in media containing 1–2% fetal bovine serum (FBS), simulating diluted clinical samples, though performance declined at 5% FBS due to protein fouling. To address this, the invention contemplates optimization of coating thickness and IDC geometry to increase the "saturation thickness" \( d_{\mathit{sat}} \), defined by:  
\[
d_{\mathit{sat}} = - \frac{D}{a_{1}}\ln\left( \frac{0.005}{a_{2}} \right)
\]  
with \( a_1 \) and \( a_2 \) as cubic functions of \( W + D \). Increasing digit spacing or width expands the sensing volume, enhancing tolerance to complex matrices.

For high-throughput deployment, the system can be integrated with robotic plate handlers and multi-coil arrays. A reference library of slope-MIC correlations enables automated MIC prediction without user interpretation. Advantages over competing technologies include: (1) no requirement for species identification prior to testing; (2) compatibility with polymicrobial samples; (3) minimal sample preparation (dilution only); (4) low cost due to printed circuit fabrication; and (5) scalability to 96- or 384-well formats. Future work includes clinical validation with patient-derived isolates, integration with microfluidics for continuous perfusion, and lithographic miniaturization for point-of-care use.
