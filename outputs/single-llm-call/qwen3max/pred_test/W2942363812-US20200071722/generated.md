## BACKGROUND

Genetic engineering has undergone transformative advances through the development of programmable nucleases such as zinc finger nucleases (ZFNs), transcription activator-like effector nucleases (TALENs), and CRISPR-Cas systems. These technologies enable targeted genome modifications by inducing double-strand breaks at specific genomic loci, which are subsequently repaired by endogenous cellular mechanisms such as non-homologous end joining (NHEJ) or homology-directed repair (HDR). Despite their widespread utility, these methods suffer from significant limitations. Double-strand breaks can trigger p53-mediated DNA damage responses, leading to cell cycle arrest or apoptosis, particularly in primary and non-dividing cells. Moreover, error-prone NHEJ often results in unintended insertions or deletions (indels), chromosomal translocations, and other genomic rearrangements that compromise the fidelity and safety of genome editing. These drawbacks are especially problematic in therapeutic contexts where precision and predictability are paramount. Consequently, there remains a critical need for alternative genome engineering strategies that circumvent reliance on double-strand break formation and endogenous DNA repair pathways.

## SUMMARY

Genome modification holds immense potential for treating genetic disorders, enabling precise correction, insertion, or deletion of DNA sequences without collateral genomic damage. Among the most promising agents for such applications are site-specific recombinases (SSRs), which catalyze direct, scarless recombination between defined DNA target sites without generating double-strand breaks. Unlike programmable nucleases, SSRs operate through a concerted mechanism involving cleavage, strand exchange, and ligation, thereby avoiding activation of DNA damage response pathways and functioning efficiently even in non-dividing cells. However, the utility of natural SSRs is severely constrained by their fixed substrate specificities, which rarely align with therapeutically relevant genomic loci. Although programmable nucleases can be retargeted through modular DNA-binding domains, analogous efforts to re-engineer SSRs have met with limited success due to the complex and poorly understood determinants of their DNA recognition.

Site-specific recombinases, particularly those of the tyrosine and serine families, recognize extended DNA sequences—often 30–50 base pairs in length—through a combination of direct protein–DNA contacts, shape complementarity, and indirect readout mechanisms. This intricate recognition code resists rational redesign, as mutations intended to alter specificity frequently abolish activity or yield unpredictable cross-reactivity. Early attempts to evolve recombinases with new specificities required hundreds of rounds of directed evolution and yielded variants with only modest changes in target sequence preference. More recent approaches have fused recombinase catalytic domains to programmable DNA-binding proteins, but these chimeric systems often suffer from reduced efficiency, steric constraints, and context-dependent activity.

To overcome these challenges, the present invention introduces evolved recombinases whose DNA specificities have been systematically redirected toward non-native target sequences using phage-assisted continuous evolution (PACE). PACE enables rapid, high-throughput selection of functional recombinase variants by linking recombinase activity to the production of infectious bacteriophage particles. In this system, a selection phage carries a gene encoding a mutant recombinase and a recombination-dependent reporter cassette; successful recombination excises a transcriptional terminator, allowing expression of a phage gene essential for infectivity. Only recombinases that recognize the desired target sequence support phage propagation, enabling continuous enrichment of functional variants over days rather than months.

The invention further provides methods for identifying the precise DNA target site preferences of recombinases using Rec-seq, a high-throughput in vitro profiling technique. Rec-seq employs libraries of partially randomized DNA substrates flanked by hairpin structures; upon recombination, the product becomes resistant to exonuclease digestion, enabling selective amplification and deep sequencing of bona fide substrates. This approach reveals nucleotide-resolution specificity profiles, including long-range compensatory interactions and asymmetric binding preferences not evident from crystal structures.

Evolved recombinases generated by the disclosed methods recognize non-native target sequences—including safe harbor loci such as Rosa26—with high efficiency and specificity. These recombinases enable precise integration, excision, or inversion of transgenes in mammalian genomes without double-strand breaks. Libraries of nucleic acid molecules containing degenerate recombinase target sites facilitate comprehensive assessment of off-target activity, allowing prediction of genomic pseudosites and optimization of specificity.

The advantages of evolved recombinases include: (1) scarless, directional genome modifications; (2) activity in non-dividing cells; (3) minimal induction of DNA damage responses; (4) high efficiency exceeding HDR-based methods; and (5) programmability via continuous evolution. Current recombinases are limited by their immutable native specificities and poor tolerance for target site variation. The present invention addresses this need by providing a platform for evolving recombinases with tailored specificities for any genomic locus of interest.

Potential applications include gene therapy (e.g., targeted insertion of corrective transgenes into safe harbors), cell engineering (e.g., conditional knockout or lineage tracing), synthetic biology (e.g., logic gates based on recombinase-mediated DNA inversion), and functional genomics (e.g., high-throughput screening via recombinase-activated reporters). In conclusion, the invention provides a robust, scalable framework for converting natural recombinases into precision genome engineering tools with customizable DNA recognition capabilities.

## DEFINITIONS

An accessory plasmid is a plasmid introduced into host cells that supplies one or more gene products required for the production of infectious viral particles but is not encoded by the selection phage genome. The function of the accessory plasmid is to complement genetic deficiencies in the selection phage, thereby linking a biological activity of interest—such as recombinase-mediated DNA recombination—to phage propagation.

A cellstat is a bioreactor system that maintains a constant volume of culture by continuously removing spent medium and replenishing it with fresh medium, thereby enabling steady-state growth conditions for continuous evolution experiments.

Continuous evolution process refers to an experimental methodology wherein genetic diversity is continuously introduced into a population of replicating entities, and functional variants are continuously selected over time without manual intervention. The steps of the continuous evolution process include: (1) introducing a library of genetic variants into a replicating vector; (2) propagating the vector in host cells under conditions that link a desired activity to vector replication; (3) continuously supplying mutagenic agents to generate diversity; and (4) maintaining selective pressure over multiple generations to enrich functional variants.

Flow of host cells describes the movement of bacterial host cells into and out of a lagoon chamber during PACE. The characteristics of this flow include a constant influx of fresh host cells and efflux of culture medium, ensuring a stable population density and continuous supply of uninfected cells for phage propagation.

Fresh host cells are bacterial cells that have not been previously exposed to phage infection and are continuously supplied to the lagoon to sustain phage replication during PACE.

A gene of interest is a nucleic acid sequence encoding a protein or functional RNA whose activity is to be evolved or selected. Examples of genes of interest include those encoding site-specific recombinases, nucleases, polymerases, or transcription factors.

A helper phage is a bacteriophage that provides structural or replication proteins in trans to complement a defective selection phage. The function of the helper phage is to supply missing gene products necessary for virion assembly or genome packaging, thereby enabling propagation of the selection phage only when a linked activity is functional.

High copy number plasmids are plasmids that replicate to yield tens to hundreds of copies per bacterial cell, such as pUC19. Low copy number plasmids replicate to yield one to ten copies per cell, such as pSC101.

A host cell is a prokaryotic or eukaryotic cell that supports the replication of a viral vector or plasmid. Characteristics of host cells used in PACE include susceptibility to phage infection, compatibility with mutagenesis systems, and the ability to express recombinant proteins.

An infectious viral particle is a complete virion capable of infecting a host cell and initiating a productive infection cycle. Characteristics include a packaged genome, structural proteins forming a capsid, and, in some cases, envelope proteins.

A lagoon is a chemostat-like vessel used in PACE to house host cells and selection phage under continuous flow conditions. Its function is to maintain a stable environment for phage propagation while enabling continuous selection.

A mutagen is an agent that increases the frequency of genetic mutations. Examples include error-prone DNA polymerases, chemical mutagens like ethyl methanesulfonate, and mutagenesis plasmids encoding error-prone replication machinery. The function of mutagens in PACE is to introduce genetic diversity into the evolving gene of interest.

A mutagenesis plasmid is a plasmid encoding components of an error-prone DNA replication system, such as the E. coli translesion synthesis polymerase V (umuDC operon). Its function is to increase the mutation rate of phage genomes during replication. Examples include pGA101 and derivatives thereof.

Nucleic acid refers to DNA or RNA, including single-stranded, double-stranded, linear, circular, or hybrid forms.

A phage is a virus that infects bacteria. Phage vectors are engineered bacteriophages used to deliver and evolve genetic material in bacterial hosts.

Phage-assisted continuous evolution (PACE) is a laboratory technique that couples protein function to bacteriophage fitness, enabling rapid, automated evolution of proteins over many generations without manual selection steps. PACE technology involves a selection phage, accessory plasmid, mutagenesis plasmid, and continuous culture system.

A promoter is a DNA sequence that initiates transcription of a downstream gene. Types include constitutive promoters (e.g., lac promoter), inducible promoters (e.g., arabinose-inducible pBAD), and conditional promoters regulated by recombinase activity.

A protein is a polymer of amino acids folded into a three-dimensional structure that performs biological functions. Protein structure includes primary (sequence), secondary (α-helices, β-sheets), tertiary (folded domain), and quaternary (multimeric assembly) levels.

A replication product is a newly synthesized copy of a phage genome generated during infection.

A selection phage is a genetically engineered bacteriophage whose ability to produce infectious progeny depends on the activity of a gene of interest. Components include a phage genome deficient in a gene required for infectivity, a gene encoding the evolving protein, and a recombination-dependent reporter cassette.

A small molecule is an organic compound of low molecular weight (<900 daltons) that can modulate biological processes. Characteristics include membrane permeability and specific binding to protein targets.

A turbidostat is a bioreactor that maintains constant optical density by adjusting medium inflow based on real-time turbidity measurements, ensuring exponential-phase growth.

A vector is a DNA molecule used to deliver genetic material into a host cell. Vector function includes replication, selection, and expression of inserted genes.

The viral life cycle comprises stages including attachment, genome entry, replication, assembly, and release. A viral particle is an individual virion capable of initiating infection.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

Recombinase technology enables precise, scarless genome modifications by catalyzing recombination between specific DNA target sites. Unlike nuclease-based editors, recombinases do not rely on error-prone DNA repair pathways, making them ideal for therapeutic applications requiring high fidelity. The present invention leverages phage-assisted continuous evolution (PACE) to overcome the historical challenge of altering recombinase specificity. Selection strategies for PACE experiments link recombinase activity to phage infectivity: a selection phage carries a gene encoding a mutant recombinase and a recombination substrate flanking a transcriptional terminator that blocks expression of a phage gene essential for virion assembly (e.g., gene III of M13 phage). Only when the recombinase recognizes the target site and excises the terminator can infectious phage be produced, enabling continuous enrichment of functional variants.

Methods for assessing recombinase specificity include Rec-seq, an in vitro high-throughput profiling technique. Rec-seq uses libraries of DNA hairpins containing randomized recombinase target sites; successful recombination generates a double-hairpin product resistant to exonuclease digestion. Deep sequencing of enriched products reveals nucleotide preferences across the entire target site. Directed evolution strategies involve iterative rounds of PACE followed by Rec-seq validation to refine specificity.

Suitable recombinases for evolution include tyrosine-family members (Cre, Dre, VCre) and serine integrases (Bxb1, φC31). Evolved recombinases enable integration of heterologous nucleic acid sequences into safe harbor loci such as Rosa26, AAVS1, or CCR5, minimizing risks of insertional mutagenesis. Recombinase-mediated excision allows removal of selectable markers or pathogenic sequences. Additional applications include conditional gene activation, chromosome engineering, and synthetic gene circuits.

Prior art includes U.S. Pat. Nos. 7,803,599 (Cre-loxP system) and 9,637,755 (PACE technology). Safe harbor loci vary by species: Rosa26 in mice, AAVS1 in humans, and H11 in rats are well-characterized. Recombinase technology also enables large-scale genomic deletions, inversions, and translocations for functional genomics studies.

### Phage-Assisted Continuous Evolution

PACE technology accelerates protein evolution by coupling function to phage fitness. The PACE process involves a lagoon containing E. coli host cells continuously infused with fresh medium and infected with selection phage. M13 phage biology is exploited due to its filamentous structure, single-stranded DNA genome, and non-lytic life cycle, permitting continuous production of progeny without host cell lysis. The M13 genome is manipulated to delete gene III, rendering it dependent on accessory plasmid-supplied pIII for infectivity.

The accessory plasmid carries gene III under control of a conditional promoter activated only upon successful recombination. Stringency of selective pressure is tuned by promoter strength: weak promoters demand high recombinase activity, while strong promoters permit lower activity. Low copy number accessory plasmids reduce background expression, enhancing selection fidelity; high copy variants may be used for difficult-to-evolve activities.

Alternative accessory plasmid designs include split-gene systems or protease-dependent activation. Modified viral vectors may lack additional genes (e.g., gene VIII) to increase dependency on host functions. Host cell requirements include F-pilus expression for M13 infection, typically provided by F′ episomes in E. coli strains like NEB Turbo or ER2738. The genotype of host cells includes mutations enhancing phage propagation (e.g., ΔendA) and compatibility with mutagenesis plasmids.

Prior art includes Liu et al., Nature 2011 (PACE foundational paper). Modified viral vectors lacking gene III are complemented by helper constructs expressing pIII. Host cells for M13-PACE must express F-pili; common strains include Top10F′ and DH12S. Genotypes are optimized for high transformation efficiency, phage resistance, and mutagenesis compatibility.

### Methods for Evolving Recombinases

Methods for evolving recombinases begin by cloning a library of recombinase variants into a selection phagemid. Host cells harboring an accessory plasmid are contacted with the phagemid and grown in a lagoon under continuous flow. Incubation under conditions permitting mutation—via co-expression of error-prone polymerases from a mutagenesis plasmid—introduces diversity. Replicated phage vectors are isolated from the outflow and used to infect fresh host cells, perpetuating selection.

The expression construct in host cells includes the accessory plasmid with a recombination-dependent promoter driving gene III. Recombination of target sequences—engineered into the accessory plasmid—excises a transcriptional terminator, enabling pIII expression. Mutated recombinases with higher efficiency on the desired target propagate more phage, enriching functional variants. Negative selection counters undesired activity: unwanted recombination activates expression of a dominant-negative pIII protein (pIIIneg), reducing phage fitness. Continuous replenishment of host cells maintains selection pressure over days.

### Evolved Recombinases

Evolved recombinases are variants derived from natural SSRs through directed evolution to recognize non-native target sequences. They possess amino acid sequences differing from wild-type by one or more substitutions, insertions, or deletions. Specific mutations—such as Q90R, R259K, or E262G in Cre—alter DNA contact networks. The recombinase target sequence is a non-canonical DNA site, e.g., RosaLox (derived from the Rosa26 locus), differing from loxP at multiple positions. Canonical target sequences like loxP (ATAACTTCGTATAATGTATGC TATACGAAGTTAT) are replaced by evolved sites of 30–40 bp length.

The type of recombinase includes tyrosine (e.g., Cre) or serine (e.g., Bxb1) families. Mutations in amino acid sequence are concentrated in DNA-binding regions (helices B, D, J-K loop). Recognition occurs via altered hydrogen bonding, van der Waals contacts, or indirect readout. Target sequences are located in safe harbor genomic loci, enabling therapeutic transgene integration. Pharmaceutical compositions comprise evolved recombinase mRNA or protein formulated for delivery to cells. Administration routes include intravenous injection or ex vivo cell treatment. Uses include correcting monogenic diseases by inserting functional cDNA into safe harbors.

### Methods For Recombinase-Mediated Genetic Engineering

The method for engineering nucleic acid molecules involves contacting a DNA substrate containing two recombinase target sites with an evolved recombinase. Recombination between target sequences—differing from canonical sites—results in deletion, inversion, or integration. The composition is administered to cells via lipid nanoparticles or viral vectors. Uses include creating knock-in cell lines or therapeutic genome editing.

### Methods for Evaluating the Specificity of Recombinases

The method identifies target sites by providing a recombinase and contacting it with a library of candidate nucleic acid molecules. Each candidate contains a partially randomized target site flanked by hairpins. Recombination yields exonuclease-resistant products, which are PCR-amplified and sequenced. Enriched sequences reveal preferred nucleotides at each position. Specificity is evaluated by calculating enrichment scores and generating sequence logos.

### Libraries for Assessing Recombinase Target Site Preferences

The library comprises nucleic acid molecules with hairpin structures and randomized half-sites. Each candidate contains a loop sequence connecting inverted repeats. Libraries include >10^11 variants with 2–7 mutations per half-site. Use includes high-throughput specificity profiling and off-target prediction.

### Vectors and Reagents

Selection phage carries an M13 genome deficient in gene III, with a multiple cloning site for recombinase genes. Helper phage supplies pIII in trans. Mutagenesis plasmids encode E. coli polymerase V (umuDC) and deoxyadenosine methylase (dam) with hemimethylated-GATC binding domains to enhance mutagenesis.

### Expression Constructs

Nucleic acids encoding recombinases are operably linked to heterologous promoters (e.g., T7, CMV). Constructs include plasmids, viral vectors, or linear DNA. Regulatory sequences (promoters, enhancers, terminators) control expression in cells, tissues, or organisms. Cells expressing evolved recombinase are used for genome engineering.

### Host Cells

Host cells for PACE include E. coli strains (NEB Turbo, Top10F′) with F′ plasmids for M13 infection. Accessory plasmids supply phage functions. Modified viral vectors lack essential genes, complemented by helper constructs. Prokaryotic (bacterial) and eukaryotic (yeast, mammalian) cells support different vector systems. Phage-host combinations are optimized for efficiency.

## EXAMPLES

PACE selection was developed to alter Cre’s DNA specificity. A Cre deletion-dependent Accessory Plasmid (AP) was generated, where gene III expression required excision of a terminator flanked by loxP variants. Host cells harboring AP were infected with a Cre-selection phagemid. Validation showed enrichment of active Cre variants. Inactive Cre mutants were evolved to recognize native loxP, confirming system functionality.

Selection for recombinases recognizing the Rosa26 locus employed a retargeting strategy: the AP contained RosaLox sites. PACE yielded Cre variants with activity on RosaLox. Evolution toward asymmetric RosaLox identified clones with converged mutations (e.g., Q90R, R259K). Activity assays showed retained loxP recombination but enhanced RosaLox activity in mammalian cells.

Negative PACE selection linked off-target activity to pIIIneg expression. Unwanted recombination on loxP produced dominant-negative pIII, reducing phage output. Stringency was adjusted using weak promoters (e.g., pTet). Results showed suppression of loxP activity while maintaining RosaLox recognition.

Rec-seq measured specificity in vitro. Library oligos with randomized half-sites were extended, recombined, and treated with exonucleases. PCR amplification and sequencing generated specificity profiles. Sequence logos revealed relaxed specificity at distal bases and key contacts at position 8. Profiling of evolved Cre mutants showed loss of position 8 preference and unique binding modes at positions 12–13.

Single-mutant analysis identified residues like Glu262 as long-range specificity determinants. Profiling of Cre relatives (Dre, VCre, Bxb1) demonstrated Rec-seq’s broad applicability, uncovering asymmetric recognition in VCre and dual substrate modes in Bxb1.
