# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates to image-guided radiation therapy (IGRT), and more particularly to a non-invasive method and system for real-time tumor tracking during radiation therapy without the use of implanted fiducial markers or external surrogates. The invention enables accurate localization and continuous monitoring of moving tumors—especially those in the lung and abdominal regions that exhibit significant respiratory-induced motion—by leveraging orthogonal X-ray imaging sequences and a novel regression-based tracking framework grounded in Riemannian geometry and machine learning principles. This approach facilitates precise delivery of therapeutic radiation doses while minimizing exposure to surrounding healthy tissues, thereby improving treatment efficacy and patient safety.

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

Image-guided radiation therapy has become a cornerstone of modern oncology, allowing clinicians to deliver high-dose radiation precisely to malignant tumors while sparing adjacent critical structures. A key challenge in IGRT, particularly for thoracic and abdominal malignancies, is the substantial displacement of tumors caused by respiration. Without accurate real-time tracking, large safety margins must be added around the clinical target volume to ensure complete tumor coverage throughout the breathing cycle. These margins inevitably irradiate healthy tissue, increasing the risk of complications and limiting dose escalation. Consequently, robust tumor tracking is essential not only for margin reduction but also for enabling techniques such as respiratory-gated or continuous-beam radiation delivery, which can significantly shorten treatment duration and improve patient comfort.

Conventional tumor tracking approaches have relied heavily on internal fiducial markers—typically small metallic implants placed near or within the tumor via invasive procedures such as CT-guided needle insertion. While these markers provide high-contrast reference points visible in X-ray fluoroscopy, their implantation carries significant risks, including pneumothorax, hemorrhage, infection, and tissue damage. Moreover, fiducials may migrate, rotate, or become dislodged between or during treatment fractions, introducing uncertainty into the spatial correspondence between the marker and the actual tumor. This instability undermines the reliability of long-term tracking and necessitates frequent recalibration or re-implantation.

Alternative strategies have employed external surrogates, such as reflective markers placed on the patient’s chest or abdomen, whose motion is optically tracked and correlated with internal tumor movement through statistical or biomechanical models. However, the relationship between external surface motion and internal tumor dynamics is often nonlinear, patient-specific, and prone to disruption due to changes in breathing patterns, posture, or tissue compliance. As a result, surrogate-based methods frequently suffer from poor generalization and degraded accuracy over time, especially in patients with irregular respiration or obesity.

Markerless approaches have also been explored, including template matching, optical flow, and particle filtering. Template matching methods compare a reference tumor image patch across frames but fail in low-contrast regions where tumor boundaries are indistinct—a common scenario in soft-tissue imaging. Optical flow algorithms estimate dense motion fields but are computationally intensive, sensitive to noise, and prone to drift without strong texture cues. Particle filter-based trackers maintain a distribution of possible tumor states but require large numbers of hypotheses and expensive likelihood evaluations, making them unsuitable for real-time clinical deployment. Furthermore, many existing methods assume offline training or patient-specific model adaptation, which is impractical in routine clinical workflows. Thus, there remains a critical unmet need for a fast, accurate, fully automatic, and non-invasive tumor tracking solution that operates without fiducials, external markers, or prior patient data.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a method for tracking moving tumors in real time during image-guided radiation therapy without the use of implanted fiducial markers or external surrogates. The method formulates tumor tracking as an online regression problem that directly maps image features extracted from orthogonal X-ray video sequences to underlying 3D tumor motion parameters. By modeling tumor motion as an affine transformation lying on a Riemannian manifold and employing geodesic distances for regression fitting, the invention achieves high tracking accuracy even for low-contrast tumors in noisy, low-resolution imaging conditions. The regression model is learned instantaneously from the initial pair of orthogonal X-ray images where the tumor location is known—such as through initial patient setup alignment—and requires no offline training, manual annotation, or patient-specific calibration. During subsequent frames, tumor position is estimated through a single matrix multiplication between a concatenated feature vector and the precomputed regression matrix, enabling real-time performance at speeds exceeding clinical requirements. The invention supports both a unified 3D regression framework that jointly processes both X-ray views and a dual 2D regression approach that tracks each view independently before back-projecting to 3D space, offering flexibility based on computational constraints and imaging geometry.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE PREFERRED EMBODIMENTS

The tumor tracking method of the present invention begins with the acquisition of two simultaneous orthogonal X-ray image sequences—typically in the coronal and sagittal planes—captured using a standard radiotherapy imaging system such as a linear accelerator equipped with dual flat-panel detectors. At the initial time point (t = 0), the 3D location of the tumor center is assumed to be known, either from pre-treatment planning CT registration, table alignment, or manual initialization by a clinician. This initial position serves as the anchor for all subsequent tracking operations.

Non-invasive tumor tracking is achieved by treating the temporal evolution of tumor position as a sequence of incremental affine transformations applied to an initial support window enclosing the tumor. Unlike segmentation-based methods that attempt to delineate tumor boundaries—which is infeasible for low-contrast lesions—the present invention tracks only the centroid or representative position of the tumor region, which is sufficient for beam targeting in IGRT.

The core of the method lies in regression model fitting. A regression function Ω is learned online at t = 0 that maps image-derived feature vectors to motion parameters representing the tumor’s displacement. This regression is formulated in a Riemannian geometric framework to respect the intrinsic structure of the space of affine transformations, which form a Lie group. Specifically, motion hypotheses are represented as matrices, and distances between them are computed via the matrix logarithm and exponential maps along geodesics, rather than through naive Euclidean vectorization.

Image feature extraction is performed using Histograms of Oriented Gradients (HOG), a descriptor known for its sensitivity to local shape and edge orientation while being robust to illumination changes. Within a candidate tracking window centered on the current tumor estimate, the image is divided into 5×5 pixel blocks, each yielding an 8-bin histogram of gradient orientations. These histograms are concatenated into a single column vector h_t, forming a compact yet discriminative representation of the local tumor appearance.

Motion constraints are incorporated during the training phase to reflect known biomechanical properties of respiratory motion. For instance, tumor motion is predominantly translational along the cranial-caudal axis, with limited rotation or scaling. By restricting the random sampling of motion hypotheses ΔM_i to physically plausible ranges—e.g., maximum translation of ±15 mm and rotation of ±5 degrees—the regression model becomes more stable and less susceptible to outliers or jitter.

The 3D setup parameters, including source-to-isocenter distance, detector orientation, and imaging geometry, are used to project 3D motion hypotheses onto the two orthogonal 2D image planes. This ensures that the generated synthetic tumor appearances in the training data are consistent with the actual imaging configuration.

The initial object location and shape are defined by a bounding box or ellipsoid centered at the known tumor position at t = 0. While the exact boundary is not tracked, the size of this initial window influences feature extraction and is refined through an adaptive window selection process described below.

Alignment of the initial set of sequences is implicit in the orthogonal imaging setup; no inter-frame registration is required because the method operates directly on raw X-ray pairs without relying on temporal smoothness or inter-slice correspondence.

3D motion parameters are encoded as elements of a 4×4 affine transformation matrix, capturing translation, rotation, scaling, and shear in three dimensions. For computational efficiency, only the independent parameters (typically 12) are used, though the full matrix may be retained for projection purposes.

A training module generates a large set (e.g., n = 600) of random 3D affine motion hypotheses ΔM_i* around the initial pose. Each hypothesis is projected onto both X-ray views, and corresponding HOG feature vectors h_0,i^1 and h_0,i^2 are extracted from the warped image regions. These are concatenated to form a joint feature vector.

The tracking module, once trained, operates in real time: for each new X-ray pair at time t, it extracts the current feature vector h_t, multiplies it by Ω, applies the matrix exponential to obtain the incremental motion ΔM_t, and updates the tumor pose via M_t = M_{t−1} ⋅ ΔM_t.

The regression function Ω is a matrix learned via ridge regression to solve an overcomplete least-squares problem: Ω = (X^⊤X + λI)^{−1}X^⊤Y, where X contains concatenated training features and Y contains the matrix logarithms of the motion hypotheses. Tikhonov regularization (λ > 0) ensures numerical stability.

Feature extraction, as noted, uses HOG descriptors computed within adaptively sized windows. The motion parameters estimation proceeds by applying the learned Ω to the current feature vector and mapping the result back to the manifold via the exponential map.

Motion constraints application occurs during hypothesis generation, ensuring that only biomechanically feasible motions are considered, thereby improving generalization and reducing tracking drift.

Window update is performed dynamically using a self-similarity analysis to determine the optimal tracking window size for each patient and view. Candidate window sizes are evaluated based on the discriminative power of their HOG features within a local search region.

3D regression tracking (3DR) learns a single Ω* that maps joint 2D features from both views directly to 3D motion, maintaining geometric consistency across views. In contrast, 2D regression tracking (2DR) trains separate models for each view and intersects the resulting rays in 3D space.

3D motion representation uses affine matrices in SE(3) or its affine extension, enabling compact encoding of complex displacements. The affine transformation is applied to a canonical unit square (in 2D) or cube (in 3D) to generate deformed image regions for feature sampling.

Feature vector extraction follows the HOG protocol, with normalization across blocks to enhance robustness. Training set construction involves random sampling of motion hypotheses conditioned on respiratory constraints, ensuring coverage of likely motion states without overfitting.

Regression function training is completed in under 0.05 seconds on standard hardware, making it suitable for intra-fraction use. Tumor motion prior probability is implicitly encoded through constrained hypothesis sampling, reflecting empirical knowledge of respiratory kinematics.

Linear motion constraint approximations are valid under normal breathing without hysteresis, though the full affine model accommodates more complex motion when necessary. Motion prior probability estimation is not performed explicitly; instead, the hypothesis distribution embeds prior knowledge.

Optimal tracking window selection is critical for performance. The method evaluates multiple window sizes by computing the mean of the smallest 20% of pairwise HOG distances within a local neighborhood. Higher values indicate greater discriminability, guiding selection of the best-performing window.

Self-similarity in the tumor region is low by design—tumors are distinct from surrounding tissue—but in low-contrast cases, the optimal window balances inclusion of discriminative edges against background contamination.

HOG distance computation uses the ℓ² norm between feature vectors, providing a differentiable and efficient similarity measure. Discriminative-power score computation aggregates these distances to rank candidate window sizes.

2D regression tracking operates independently on each view, then combines results via back-projection. If the two rays do not intersect exactly—as is typical due to noise—the midpoint of the shortest line segment connecting them is taken as the 3D tumor estimate.

### Effect of the Invention

The present invention achieves exceptional tracking accuracy, with average 3D errors of approximately 1.05 pixels (for 2DR) and 1.16 pixels (for 3DR) on challenging low-resolution, low-contrast digitally reconstructed radiograph sequences derived from real patient 4DCT data. This corresponds to a tumor positioning error of only 7.5% relative to the average tumor displacement magnitude of 13.86 pixels, far surpassing the performance of state-of-the-art alternatives such as optical flow (3.57 pixels), ℓ¹-based particle filtering (5.01 pixels), and HOG-based particle filtering (5.68 pixels). The method maintains robustness across diverse tumor locations, shapes, and breathing patterns—including irregular respiration—without requiring temporal smoothing or dynamical models.

In terms of speed, the invention operates in real time, with 3DR processing each frame in less than 0.03 seconds and 2DR in under 0.06 seconds on a standard CPU. This is orders of magnitude faster than optical flow methods, which require nearly 3 minutes per frame, and significantly quicker than particle filter approaches. The computational efficiency stems from the absence of iterative optimization, exhaustive search, or per-frame model updates; tracking reduces to feature extraction and a single matrix multiplication.

Compared with prior art, the invention eliminates the need for invasive fiducials, external markers, offline training, manual annotation, or patient-specific adaptation. It functions reliably in low-contrast, noisy, and low-resolution imaging conditions typical of clinical X-ray systems, where traditional template matching or boundary-based methods fail. By operating directly on raw orthogonal X-ray pairs without registration or segmentation, the method integrates seamlessly into existing IGRT workflows.

The key advantages of the invention include: (1) non-invasiveness, avoiding surgical risks and fiducial migration; (2) real-time performance suitable for continuous beam delivery; (3) high accuracy even for invisible or low-contrast tumors; (4) minimal setup requirements—only an initial tumor location is needed; (5) geometric consistency through Riemannian-aware regression; and (6) adaptability to both 2D and 3D regression paradigms. These attributes collectively enable significant reduction of planning target volume margins—from centimeters to millimeters—thereby minimizing radiation exposure to healthy tissues and improving therapeutic outcomes in image-guided radiation therapy.
