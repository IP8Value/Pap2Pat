## BACKGROUND

Light-emitting diode (LED) volumes have become a cornerstone of modern virtual production workflows in cinematic and television content creation. These LED volumes consist of large arrays of RGB LED panels arranged to form immersive cylindrical or cubic environments that surround actors and physical set elements. The primary purpose of these volumes is twofold: first, to serve as an in-camera background display that renders high-resolution, perspective-correct virtual environments in real time; and second, to function as a dynamic lighting source that reproduces the illumination characteristics of real-world scenes through the display of high dynamic range image-based lighting (HDR IBL) maps. This dual functionality enables filmmakers to capture complex visual effects shots with realistic lighting and reflections directly in-camera, thereby reducing reliance on post-production compositing and chroma keying.

Despite these advantages, conventional LED panel systems suffer from significant limitations related to color rendition fidelity. Specifically, the spectral emission characteristics of standard RGB LEDs—typically narrowband emitters with pronounced gaps in the visible spectrum—differ substantially from the broad, continuous spectra of natural and artificial broad-spectrum illuminants such as daylight, incandescent, or white LED lighting. As a result, when RGB LED panels are used to simulate broad-spectrum lighting environments, materials with non-uniform reflectance spectra (e.g., skin tones, orange fabrics, cyan pigments) exhibit perceptual color shifts that do not match their appearance under the target illumination. For instance, lighter skin tones often appear unnaturally pink, darker skin tones shift toward red, yellow materials darken, and cyan objects take on a bluish cast. These errors arise because the human visual system and camera sensors integrate light across the full visible spectrum, and the absence of energy in certain wavelength bands—particularly between the green and red peaks of typical RGB LEDs—leads to metameric failure: two illuminants that appear identical in color (metamers) produce different color responses when reflected off spectrally selective surfaces.

Current calibration workflows for LED volumes are primarily designed to ensure accurate color reproduction of the displayed background imagery as seen by the camera. These methods typically involve capturing images of pure red, green, and blue patches displayed on the LED panels and computing a 3×3 color correction matrix that maps input RGB values to output values such that the camera records the intended colors. While this approach successfully aligns the in-camera background with the source content, it does not address the fundamental issue of poor color rendition for foreground subjects illuminated by the same LED panels. Because the calibration assumes that matching the three primaries ensures correct reproduction of all colors—a valid assumption only for neutral or spectrally flat materials—it fails to account for the spectral mismatch between the LED emission and the target illuminant when lighting colored or textured objects. Consequently, existing systems optimize for display fidelity at the expense of lighting fidelity, leading to compromised visual realism in final footage.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides a novel color correction method for optimizing color rendition in RGB LED-based virtual production stages. The method employs a multi-matrix framework comprising a pre-correction matrix, a post-correction matrix, and a second pre-correction matrix, each serving distinct roles in balancing in-camera background accuracy with foreground lighting fidelity. The first step involves generating a pre-correction matrix M that maps target scene pixel colors to LED panel drive values to ensure metameric matching of the illumination as observed by the motion picture camera. This matrix is derived from calibration images of pure red, green, and blue patches displayed on the in-camera-frustum portion of the LED volume, with out-of-frustum panels disabled to avoid contamination from interreflections.

Next, a post-correction matrix Q is generated to improve the color rendition of foreground subjects—such as actors, costumes, and props—illuminated by the out-of-camera-frustum LED panels. This matrix is computed by simulating the appearance of a color chart under the LED-reproduced HDR IBL environment using calibration data captured under individual LED spectral channels. The simulation leverages the superposition principle of light and incorporates a scale factor that accounts for geometric differences between the calibration setup and a full spherical illumination environment. The post-correction matrix Q is solved via least-squares optimization to minimize the color error between the simulated chart appearance and the ground-truth appearance recorded in the original real-world scene.

To preserve the accuracy of the in-camera background while applying the post-correction Q to the entire recorded image, a second pre-correction matrix N is generated, defined as N = M Q⁻¹. This matrix is applied exclusively to the in-camera-frustum content rendered on the LED panels, effectively pre-compensating for the post-correction that will be applied later in the imaging pipeline. This ensures that background pixels, after undergoing the global post-correction Q, retain their intended color appearance, while foreground subjects benefit from improved color rendition due to the optimized lighting from out-of-frustum panels.

The correction matrices are then applied in a spatially aware manner: matrix M is applied to out-of-frustum content, matrix N to in-frustum content, and matrix Q to the final recorded image. Additionally, an RGB offset is computed and applied to in-frustum pixel values to compensate for black level elevation caused by ambient light reflecting off the LED panels themselves—a phenomenon resulting from the non-zero albedo of the panel surfaces. This offset is determined empirically by measuring the average camera response when only out-of-frustum content is active and the in-frustum display is turned off.

The method further includes solving for the first 3×3 matrix M by inverting the spectral sensitivity-LED emission coupling matrix [SL], which is constructed from averaged pixel values of the primary color patches. A color calibration image is generated by displaying these patches and capturing them with the principal photography camera. Average pixel values are sampled from this image to populate [SL]. Similarly, the second 3×3 matrix Q is solved using a set of three calibration images, each showing a color chart illuminated by a single LED channel (red, green, or blue), scaled by a geometric factor β to approximate omnidirectional diffuse illumination. Calibration imagery is generated under controlled conditions to isolate the contribution of each spectral channel.

In a system embodiment, the color rendition optimization system is integrated into a virtual production stage comprising LED panels, a motion picture camera with real-time tracking, and a rendering engine such as Unreal Engine. The system executes computer-readable instructions stored on non-transitory media that implement the matrix generation and application logic. The disclosed method combines features from multiple embodiments—including spatially varying pre-correction, global post-correction, and black level compensation—to achieve simultaneous optimization of both background display fidelity and foreground lighting color accuracy.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF EXEMPLARY EMBODIMENTS

Virtual production stages utilizing LED volumes have revolutionized media production by enabling real-time rendering of photorealistic backgrounds and dynamic lighting. These stages typically consist of cylindrical or cubic arrangements of high-resolution RGB LED panels that envelop performers and set pieces. The panels display high dynamic range image-based lighting (HDR IBL) environments, which serve both as visual backdrops and as programmable light sources that mimic the illumination of real-world locations. However, the color rendition properties of these systems are fundamentally limited by the spectral characteristics of the constituent RGB LEDs, which emit light in narrow bands with significant gaps in the visible spectrum. This leads to perceptual color errors in illuminated materials, particularly those with complex reflectance spectra such as human skin, dyed fabrics, and painted surfaces.

Prior color calibration workflows for LED volumes focus exclusively on ensuring that the colors displayed on the panels appear correct to the camera. This is achieved by calibrating the three primary colors—red, green, and blue—and assuming that linear combinations of these primaries will reproduce all other colors accurately. While this assumption holds for neutral or spectrally flat materials, it fails for colored objects under broad-spectrum illumination, resulting in hue shifts and saturation errors that degrade visual realism. The present invention addresses this limitation by introducing a color rendition optimization system that decouples the requirements for background display accuracy and foreground lighting fidelity.

The system operates by generating and applying three distinct 3×3 color correction matrices in a coordinated pipeline. First, a pre-correction matrix M is computed to map input RGB values to LED drive signals such that the camera observes the correct color for the three primaries. This matrix is derived from a calibration image showing pure red, green, and blue patches displayed on the in-camera-frustum portion of the LED volume. The average pixel values from these patches form the columns of a spectral coupling matrix [SL], which is inverted to yield M = [SL]⁻¹. This ensures that when the HDR IBL environment is displayed using matrix M, the white point of the illumination—as measured by the white square of a color chart—matches that of the original scene, achieving a metameric match.

However, metameric matching alone does not guarantee accurate color rendition for non-neutral materials. To address this, a post-correction matrix Q is computed to adjust the final recorded image so that a color chart illuminated by the LED-reproduced environment matches its appearance in the real world. This matrix is derived from three additional calibration images, each showing the same color chart illuminated by only one LED channel (red, green, or blue) at a time. These images are captured with a 1m × 1m LED panel segment placed 1m from the chart, oriented to provide near-Lambertian illumination. The resulting pixel values for each chart square under each spectral channel are assembled into a material-specific matrix [SRL]_j. Using the diffuse integral w_avg of the target HDR IBL environment—extracted from the white square of the real-world color chart—the system simulates the chart’s appearance under LED lighting as (1/β)[SRL]_j M w_avg, where β ≈ 0.311 is a scale factor accounting for the solid angle difference between the calibration setup and a full hemisphere. The matrix Q is then solved in a least-squares sense to minimize the error between this simulated appearance and the ground-truth chart colors.

Applying Q globally to the recorded image would distort the in-camera background, which was calibrated using M. To prevent this, a second pre-correction matrix N = M Q⁻¹ is applied exclusively to the in-camera-frustum content. This pre-compensation ensures that after the global post-correction Q is applied, the background pixels revert to their intended colors. The system dynamically determines which pixels belong to the in-camera frustum based on real-time camera tracking data, allowing on-the-fly classification of pixels as either in-frustum or out-of-frustum.

The final imagery captured by the camera thus exhibits optimized color rendition for both foreground and background. The color rendition optimization system receives data from the LED panels (e.g., current display content, panel type) and the camera (e.g., position, orientation, lens parameters) and transmits corrected pixel values back to the panels and processing server. The system assumes panel and camera linearity, radiometric alignment across different panel types (e.g., wall, ceiling, floor), and the ability to display the full dynamic range of the HDR IBL map without clipping.

Three calibration images are generated: one for in-camera-frustum primary calibration, and two or more for out-of-frustum color rendition calibration. The first pre-correction matrix M enables metameric illuminant matching, while the post-correction matrix Q improves color rendition by simulating how a color chart would appear under uniform illumination and adjusting accordingly. The system constructs a cube map environment to compute the diffuse convolution for the frontal direction, scales calibration images by β, and focuses on the diffuse integral of the HDR IBL map, equating it to the white square’s pixel value adjusted for reflectance.

Figures 1 through 4 illustrate the system architecture, environmental integration, component details, and method flow, respectively. The system can be implemented in various virtual production stages, including those with mixed panel types, and may be executed on a server comprising a physical processor and memory storing executable instructions. The joint optimization approach, while theoretically possible, is avoided in favor of sequential matrix computation to maintain on-set lighting neutrality and avoid out-of-gamut content. The invention concludes with a robust, practical solution that significantly enhances color fidelity in LED-based virtual production without requiring spectral measurements or complex hardware modifications.

### EXAMPLE EMBODIMENTS

Example 1 describes a computer-implemented method for optimizing color rendition in an RGB LED virtual production stage. The method includes generating a first pre-correction matrix M by capturing a calibration image of red, green, and blue patches displayed on in-camera-frustum LED panels and solving M = [SL]⁻¹, where [SL] is formed from average pixel values of the patches. A post-correction matrix Q is generated by capturing three calibration images of a color chart illuminated individually by red, green, and blue LED channels, simulating the chart’s appearance under the target HDR IBL using (1/β)[SRL]_j M w_avg, and solving for Q via least-squares minimization. A second pre-correction matrix N = M Q⁻¹ is then computed. During filming, M is applied to out-of-frustum content, N to in-frustum content, and Q to the final recorded image.

Example 2 details the solving of the pre-correction matrix M. A calibration image is captured with pure red, green, and blue patches displayed on the in-camera-frustum panels while out-of-frustum panels are off. Average RGB values from each patch are extracted and arranged as columns of [SL]. Matrix inversion yields M, ensuring that input RGB values produce the correct camera-observed colors.

Example 3 explains the solving of the post-correction matrix Q. Three calibration images are captured, each with a 1m × 1m LED panel segment emitting only one color channel, illuminating a color chart at 1m distance. Pixel values from each chart square under each channel form [SRL]_j. Given w_avg from the real-world chart’s white square, the system simulates LED-lit chart appearance and solves Q to minimize color error.

Example 4 describes generating the second pre-correction matrix N as N = M Q⁻¹, ensuring that in-frustum content, when post-corrected by Q, retains its intended color.

Example 5 specifies generating the in-camera-frustum LED panel color calibration image by displaying primary color patches and capturing them with the principal photography camera under controlled lighting.

Example 6 reiterates solving for Q using the simulated chart appearance and real-world reference.

Example 7 covers generating calibration imagery for both primary and color rendition calibration under standardized geometric conditions.

Example 8 involves adjusting in-frustum pixel values by an RGB offset derived from a black level measurement: with out-of-frustum content active and in-frustum content off, the average camera response b_camera is recorded, and the offset is computed as b_camera / w_camera, where w_camera is the response to a [1,1,1] patch.

The system embodiment comprises a color rendition optimization system integrated into a virtual production stage, including a pre-correction matrix manager, post-correction matrix manager, and calibration manager. The non-transitory computer-readable medium embodiment stores instructions for executing the method. Key terms are defined: “color rendition optimization system” refers to the integrated hardware and software that performs the multi-matrix correction; “virtual production stage” denotes an LED-enclosed filming environment; “color chart” is a standardized reference with known color patches; “calibration data” includes the four required images; “pre-correction matrix M” maps scene colors to LED drives for metameric match; “post-correction matrix Q” adjusts final image for accurate material colors; and “second pre-correction matrix N” compensates in-frustum content for Q. The invention optimizes color rendition while maintaining background fidelity, overcoming limitations of prior art through a novel, practical, and measurement-efficient approach.
