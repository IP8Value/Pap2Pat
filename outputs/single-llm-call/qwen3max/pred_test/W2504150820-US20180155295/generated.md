## FIELD

The present invention relates to novel small molecule modulators of the chemokine receptor CXCR4. More specifically, the invention provides compounds that function as agonists or antagonists of CXCR4, pharmaceutical compositions comprising such compounds, and methods of using these compounds for the treatment or modulation of diseases and conditions mediated by CXCR4 activity, including but not limited to cancer, inflammatory disorders, stem cell mobilization, and immune-related pathologies.

## BACKGROUND

CXCR4 is a G protein-coupled receptor (GPCR) that binds its endogenous ligand, stromal cell-derived factor-1 (SDF-1), also known as CXCL12. This receptor-ligand pair plays a pivotal role in numerous physiological processes, including embryonic development, hematopoiesis, immune cell trafficking, and organogenesis. Under pathological conditions, aberrant CXCR4 signaling contributes to tumor metastasis, HIV entry into T cells, chronic inflammation, and autoimmune diseases. The CXCR4-CXCL12 axis is particularly critical in directing the homing of hematopoietic stem cells to the bone marrow niche and in facilitating the dissemination of cancer cells to distant organs that express high levels of CXCL12.

Physiologically, CXCR4 activation by CXCL12 triggers intracellular signaling cascades involving Gαi proteins, leading to inhibition of adenylate cyclase, mobilization of intracellular calcium, activation of mitogen-activated protein kinases (MAPKs) such as ERK1/2, and recruitment of β-arrestins, which mediate receptor internalization and non-canonical signaling. These pathways collectively regulate cell migration, survival, proliferation, and adhesion.

To date, only one CXCR4-targeting drug has received regulatory approval: plerixafor (AMD3100), a bicyclam compound that functions as a CXCR4 antagonist. Plerixafor is clinically used to mobilize hematopoietic stem cells from the bone marrow into peripheral blood for collection and subsequent autologous transplantation in patients with non-Hodgkin’s lymphoma and multiple myeloma. Despite its utility, AMD3100 exhibits limitations, including modest potency, short half-life, and potential cardiac side effects, underscoring the need for improved CXCR4-modulating agents.

Chemokine signaling through GPCRs like CXCR4 involves complex conformational dynamics. Upon ligand binding, the receptor undergoes structural rearrangements that enable coupling to intracellular effectors. The large, multi-domain binding site of CXCR4—originally characterized through crystal structures with the cyclic peptide CVX15 and the small molecule IT1t—reveals distinct sub-pockets that can accommodate diverse chemical entities. However, designing small molecules that effectively mimic or block the actions of the large protein ligand CXCL12 remains a significant challenge.

In development, CXCR4 is essential for cardiac septation, cerebellar development, and vascular formation; knockout mice lacking CXCR4 die perinatally due to severe defects. In oncology, CXCR4 overexpression correlates with poor prognosis in numerous cancers, including breast, prostate, pancreatic, and melanoma, where it drives metastatic spread to CXCL12-rich organs such as the lungs, liver, and bone marrow. Consequently, CXCR4 inhibition represents a promising therapeutic strategy to block metastasis and sensitize tumors to conventional therapies.

Despite extensive efforts, the discovery of new CXCR4-acting agents has been hampered by the receptor’s structural complexity and the lack of robust high-throughput screening paradigms capable of identifying both antagonists and, more critically, agonists. Prior approaches have largely focused on antagonist development using structure-activity relationship (SAR) studies of known scaffolds like bicyclams, tetrahydroisoquinolines, and peptidomimetics. Small molecule agonists of CXCR4 have remained elusive, with no prior reports of orally bioavailable, drug-like compounds capable of selectively activating CXCR4 in a manner comparable to CXCL12.

Thus, there exists a compelling and unmet need for novel, potent, selective, and metabolically stable small molecule modulators of CXCR4—particularly agonists—that can serve as research tools to dissect CXCR4 signaling pathways and as potential therapeutics for conditions where controlled activation or desensitization of CXCR4 is beneficial.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention is directed to small molecule modulators of CXCR4, a key GPCR involved in development, immunity, and disease. While antagonists of CXCR4 have been explored, small molecule agonists represent a novel class of pharmacological tools with significant therapeutic potential. The identification of such agonists has been historically challenging due to the large binding interface of the endogenous protein ligand CXCL12 and the conformational flexibility of GPCRs.

Using a dual in silico screening approach, we combined ligand-based pharmacophore modeling—derived from known CXCR4 antagonists—with structure-based virtual docking into the CXCR4 crystal structure (PDB: 3ODU). This strategy yielded a set of 15 compounds subjected to biological evaluation. Surprisingly, several compounds, notably NUCC-390, exhibited robust agonist activity across multiple functional assays.

### EXPERIMENTAL

Biological and computational methods employed to identify and validate CXCR4 modulators are described below.

### Annotated Database Creation

A GPCR-focused library of ~13,000 compounds (ChemBridge) was processed using Discovery Studio 3.1. Low-energy conformers (<10 kcal/mol above global minimum) were generated via ConFirm/CatConf with CHARMm force field refinement and poling-enhanced sampling, yielding ~100 conformers per compound.

### EXAMPLE 2

A common-feature pharmacophore model was built using 10 training compounds selected from 162 CXCR4 antagonists (ChEMBL v13). Cluster analysis grouped antagonists into five clusters; two representatives per cluster formed the training set. The optimal hypothesis contained two hydrophobic (Hy), two hydrogen bond acceptor (HBA), and one positive ionizable (PI) feature. Screening the annotated database yielded 26 hits (>85% fit); six were acquired for testing.

### EXAMPLE 3

CXCR4 crystal structures (3ODU, 3OE0) were prepared using Schrödinger Prime and Sybyl ProtPrep. Glide (grid-based) and Surflex (fragment-based) docking engines were employed. A 12 Å³ grid centered on IT1t defined the binding site. Docking of known antagonists validated protocols; library screening identified 52 (Glide) and 48 (Surflex) hits. Twenty-two consensus hits underwent filtering for drug-likeness and availability; nine were purchased.

### EXAMPLE 4

Docking of 20 known antagonists confirmed interactions with key residues (e.g., Glu288, Asp97, Tyr116). The GPCR library was docked using identical parameters. Hits with Glide score <−6.0 or Surflex total score >6.0 and favorable residue contacts were prioritized. NUCC-397 exemplified strong predicted binding.

### EXAMPLE 5

Calcium imaging in C8161 melanoma cells measured intracellular Ca²⁺ flux via fura-2. Compounds were tested at 10 μM. Agonists (e.g., NUCC-390) induced Ca²⁺ transients blocked by AMD3100. Antagonists (e.g., NUCC-388) inhibited SDF-1 responses. Dose-response curves yielded IC₅₀ values: NUCC-388 (0.3 μM), NUCC-392 (1 μM), NUCC-397 (3 μM), NUCC-51420 (1 μM).

### EXAMPLE 6

NUCC-390 induced ERK1/2 phosphorylation in C8161 cells, comparable to SDF-1, confirming downstream pathway activation.

### EXAMPLE 7

In HEK293 cells expressing CXCR4-YFP, NUCC-390 induced receptor internalization indistinguishable from SDF-1, blocked by AMD3100. Antagonists promoted membrane retention, suggesting inverse agonism. In Boyden chamber assays, NUCC-390 stimulated C8161 chemotaxis equivalent to SDF-1. Binding studies showed NUCC-390 did not compete with ¹²⁵I-SDF-1α, indicating non-identical binding sites. Molecular modeling revealed agonists form H-bonds with Tyr255, whereas antagonists bind Glu288—mirroring IT1t.

Synthesis of representative compounds followed general procedures: amide coupling (5a, 5b), reductive amination (6a, 6b), and cyclization (7a–7d). Intermediates like tert-butyl 3-(2-ethoxy-2-oxoacetyl)-4-oxopiperidine-1-carboxylate were prepared and characterized by MS and ¹H NMR. Final compounds (e.g., 10–16) were >95% pure by LC/MS.

The scope of the invention includes all stereoisomers, tautomers, and prodrugs of the disclosed compounds, as well as methods of use not limited to those explicitly tested. Limitations include exclusion of compounds with reactive or toxicophoric groups inconsistent with pharmaceutical use.
