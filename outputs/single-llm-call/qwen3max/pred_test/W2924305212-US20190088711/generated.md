# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

### Technical Field

The present invention relates to the field of magnetic memory devices, and more particularly to spin-orbit torque (SOT)-based magnetoresistance effect elements and magnetic memory systems incorporating such elements. The invention further pertains to methods and structures for reducing reversal current density in SOT-type magnetic memory devices, thereby enhancing endurance, reliability, and energy efficiency for applications in next-generation non-volatile memory technologies, including edge computing and artificial intelligence hardware.

### Description of Related Art

Giant magnetoresistance (GMR) elements have long served as foundational components in magnetic sensing and memory applications. A GMR element typically consists of two ferromagnetic layers separated by a non-magnetic conductive spacer layer. The electrical resistance of the GMR structure varies depending on the relative orientation of magnetization in the two ferromagnetic layers—parallel alignment yields low resistance, while antiparallel alignment yields high resistance. Although GMR elements enabled significant advances in hard disk drive read heads and early magnetic random-access memory (MRAM), their relatively modest magnetoresistance ratio limited scalability and performance in high-density memory applications.

Tunneling magnetoresistance (TMR) elements emerged as a superior alternative to GMR, employing an insulating barrier—typically magnesium oxide (MgO)—between two ferromagnetic layers to enable quantum mechanical tunneling of electrons. TMR elements exhibit significantly higher magnetoresistance ratios, often exceeding 100%, which makes them ideal for high-performance MRAM cells. In conventional TMR-based MRAM, data is written via spin-transfer torque (STT), wherein a spin-polarized current passing perpendicularly through the magnetic tunnel junction (MTJ) exerts torque on the free layer’s magnetization, inducing switching. While STT-MRAM offers non-volatility and good scalability, it suffers from high write currents, especially at short pulse widths, which accelerates degradation of the tunnel barrier and limits write endurance to approximately 10^12 cycles—a threshold insufficient for cache-class memory applications.

Magnetic random-access memory (MRAM) based on STT has been successfully embedded in CMOS processes and used to replace embedded flash memory due to its speed, endurance, and non-volatility. However, for MRAM to serve as a last-level cache or even SRAM replacement in AI and IoT edge devices, it must achieve not only ultra-high endurance (>10^12 write cycles) but also sub-nanosecond write speeds with minimal energy consumption. The fundamental limitation of STT-MRAM lies in the trade-off between write speed and current density: shorter pulses require exponentially higher current densities, which induce electromigration, dielectric breakdown, and thermal stress in the MgO barrier, ultimately degrading device reliability.

To address these challenges, spin-orbit torque (SOT) writing methods were developed. In SOT-MRAM, a separate heavy metal layer—such as tungsten (W), tantalum (Ta), or platinum (Pt)—is placed adjacent to the MTJ’s free layer. When an in-plane current flows through this heavy metal layer, the spin Hall effect generates a transverse pure spin current that injects spins with a specific polarization into the adjacent ferromagnetic layer, exerting torque and enabling magnetization reversal without passing current through the tunnel barrier. This three-terminal architecture decouples read and write paths, theoretically eliminating stress on the MgO layer and enabling faster switching (<1 ns) with improved endurance.

However, the SOT writing method introduces its own limitations. First, conventional SOT switching requires an external magnetic field or structural asymmetry to break symmetry and enable deterministic switching, complicating integration into dense memory arrays. Second, even in field-free designs, the required current density remains high—on the order of 10^7 to 10^8 A/cm²—leading to Joule heating in the SOT line, particularly when using high-resistivity materials like tungsten. As demonstrated in recent studies, such heating can cause physical failure modes, including bursting or migration of the SOT wiring material, resulting in electrical opens or shorts. These failures occur not in the MTJ itself but in the SOT line, undermining the presumed reliability advantage of SOT-MRAM. Thus, despite its architectural benefits, the SOT writing method remains constrained by high reversal current density and associated thermal-electromigration effects, limiting its practical endurance and scalability.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

### Motivate Reducing Reversal Current Density

A primary objective of the present invention is to reduce the reversal current density required for magnetization switching in SOT-type magnetoresistance effect elements. Lowering this current density mitigates Joule heating in the spin-orbit torque wiring, thereby preventing material degradation, electromigration, and catastrophic failure modes such as electrical opens or shorts. This enhancement directly translates to improved write endurance exceeding 10^12 cycles, making the device suitable for high-performance memory applications in AI accelerators, edge computing, and cache-class storage.

### SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a spin-orbit torque (SOT) type magnetoresistance effect element configured to achieve efficient, reliable, and field-free magnetization reversal at reduced current densities. A key problem with conventional SOT elements is the high current density needed for switching, which induces thermal stress and material failure in the SOT wiring layer, particularly when fabricated from high-resistivity heavy metals like tungsten. To solve this, the invention introduces an inserted layer strategically positioned within the ferromagnetic stack or adjacent to the SOT wiring to modulate spin current injection and enhance torque efficiency.

In a first aspect, the invention discloses a spin current magnetization rotational element comprising a spin-orbit torque wiring layer made of a non-magnetic heavy metal, a first ferromagnetic layer exhibiting in-plane or perpendicular magnetization, and an inserted layer interposed between the SOT wiring and the ferromagnetic layer or within the ferromagnetic stack itself. The inserted layer is composed of a material with a spin Hall angle of opposite polarity to that of the SOT wiring, thereby amplifying the effective spin torque through constructive interference of spin currents.

In a second aspect, the inserted layer may be formed from a magnetic metal or topological insulator that enhances spin polarization or modifies interfacial spin-orbit coupling, further reducing the threshold current for reversal. The thickness and composition of the inserted layer are engineered to optimize spin transparency and minimize damping, ensuring efficient transfer of angular momentum.

In a third aspect, the invention encompasses a spin-orbit torque magnetoresistance effect element integrating the spin current magnetization rotational element with a magnetic tunnel junction, where the first ferromagnetic layer serves as the free layer of the MTJ. The pinned layer may be a synthetic antiferromagnet (SAF) to provide strong exchange bias while minimizing stray fields that cause switching asymmetry.

In a fourth aspect, the invention includes a magnetic memory array comprising multiple such SOT-MRAM cells arranged in a cross-point architecture with independent word lines, source lines, and read lines, enabling high-speed, three-terminal read and write operations with enhanced endurance and reliability.

### Spin Current Magnetization Rotational Element

The spin current magnetization rotational element is a core component of the invention, designed to enable low-current, field-free magnetization reversal through engineered spin-orbit interactions. In a first embodiment, the element comprises a spin-orbit torque wiring layer formed from a non-magnetic heavy metal such as tungsten (W), tantalum (Ta), or β-phase tantalum. This layer exhibits a strong spin Hall effect, wherein an in-plane charge current generates a transverse pure spin current polarized perpendicular to both the charge current direction and the spin flow direction. For example, a current flowing in the x-direction through a W layer generates a spin current in the y-direction with spins polarized along the z-axis.

The spin-orbit torque wiring is selected for its high spin Hall angle and compatibility with CMOS fabrication. Tungsten, though resistive, offers a large negative spin Hall angle, making it effective for generating spin currents that can torque adjacent ferromagnetic layers. Pure spin current generation occurs without net charge transfer into the ferromagnet, minimizing Ohmic losses and barrier stress.

Adjacent to the SOT wiring is a first ferromagnetic layer, which may consist of CoFeB, Fe, or other alloys tailored for in-plane or perpendicular magnetic anisotropy. In the disclosed experimental device (Type-Y), the free layer exhibited in-plane magnetization orthogonal to the write current direction due to shape anisotropy and film thickness. The magnetization direction of this layer can be reversed by the spin torque exerted by the injected spin current.

To enhance efficiency, an inserted layer is introduced between the SOT wiring and the ferromagnetic layer. This inserted layer is composed of a material with a spin Hall angle of opposite polarity—for instance, if the SOT wiring is W (negative spin Hall angle), the inserted layer may be Pt (positive spin Hall angle). The opposing polarities create a spin current gradient that amplifies the effective field acting on the ferromagnet, reducing the critical current density for reversal. Alternatively, the inserted layer may be a magnetic metal such as Co or Fe, which modifies the spin mixing conductance at the interface, or a topological insulator like Bi₂Se₃, which generates highly spin-polarized surface currents with near-unity efficiency.

The thickness of the inserted layer is precisely controlled—typically between 0.3 nm and 2 nm—to balance spin transparency against interfacial scattering. Too thick, and the layer attenuates the spin current; too thin, and it fails to modulate the spin torque effectively.

In operation, a write current applied to the SOT wiring generates a spin current that traverses the inserted layer and injects into the ferromagnetic layer. The resulting spin-orbit torque overcomes the magnetic anisotropy energy, rotating the magnetization. The inserted layer enhances this process by either reinforcing the spin accumulation or tailoring the interfacial Rashba effect, which arises from structural inversion asymmetry and contributes additional spin-orbit fields.

A second embodiment employs SOT wiring and inserted layer of the same polarity but exploits magnetic coupling between multiple ferromagnetic constituent layers. For example, a trilayer stack of CoFeB/Ru/CoFeB forms a synthetic antiferromagnet where the two ferromagnetic layers are antiferromagnetically coupled. Spin current injection into one layer induces correlated dynamics in the other, lowering the net switching barrier.

In perpendicular magnetization systems, SOT alone cannot deterministically switch magnetization without a symmetry-breaking field. However, in in-plane magnetized films oriented along the x- or y-direction relative to current flow, the SOT-induced effective field lies along the desired rotation axis, enabling full 180° switching without external fields. The invention leverages this principle in Type-Y devices, where the free layer’s magnetization is orthogonal to current flow, allowing field-free reversal.

The production method involves sputter deposition under high vacuum to ensure clean interfaces, followed by photolithography and ion milling to define the MTJ pillar and SOT line. Annealing treatments crystallize the MgO barrier and enhance TMR, while oxidation steps may be used to passivate surfaces. The entire process is compatible with back-end-of-line CMOS integration.

In a fourth embodiment, an 8×8 magnetic memory array is constructed using these optimized cells. Each cell connects to a word line (SOT write line), a source line, and a bit line (read line). Write operations use nanosecond pulses through the word line, while read operations sense resistance via the bit line. Endurance testing confirms >10^12 cycles with minimal TMR degradation, and failure analysis reveals that defects originate in the SOT line—not the MTJ—validating the decoupled architecture.
