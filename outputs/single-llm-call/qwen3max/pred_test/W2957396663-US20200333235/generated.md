# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates generally to the field of biomedical diagnostics and, more specifically, to systems and methods for classifying biological particles—particularly cancer cells—based on their electrical impedance characteristics across multiple frequencies. The invention further encompasses label-free, rapid, and cost-effective techniques for assessing cell viability in response to targeted therapeutic agents, thereby enabling personalized cancer treatment strategies. By integrating microfluidic impedance cytometry with machine learning algorithms, the invention provides a platform suitable for point-of-care applications that obviates the need for optical labeling, complex instrumentation, or large sample volumes. This technology is particularly applicable to the evaluation of drug efficacy in epithelial cancers where surface markers such as activated matriptase are overexpressed, allowing clinicians to determine patient-specific responses to antibody-conjugated antineoplastic therapies prior to systemic administration.

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

Circulating tumor cells (CTCs) and other biological particles derived from solid tumors represent critical biomarkers for cancer diagnosis, prognosis, and therapeutic monitoring. These cells, shed from primary or metastatic lesions into the bloodstream or other bodily fluids, carry molecular and phenotypic information reflective of the originating tumor. Their detection and characterization can inform clinical decisions regarding treatment selection, disease progression, and recurrence risk. However, CTCs are exceedingly rare—often present at concentrations as low as one cell per billion blood cells—posing significant analytical challenges.

Current detection methods for CTCs and viable cancer cells predominantly rely on immunomagnetic separation, fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS), or microscopy-based approaches employing fluorescent dyes such as trypan blue or propidium iodide. While these techniques offer reasonable sensitivity under controlled laboratory conditions, they suffer from several limitations. First, they typically require cell labeling with antibodies or dyes, which not only alters the native state of the cells but also precludes subsequent molecular analyses such as genomic or proteomic profiling. Second, many of these methods demand relatively large sample volumes (often 0.5–1 mL or more), which may be impractical in pediatric or serial-monitoring contexts. Third, the instrumentation involved—such as flow cytometers or high-resolution microscopes—is bulky, expensive, and not amenable to deployment in resource-limited or point-of-care settings.

The importance of accurate and rapid CTC or cancer cell viability analysis cannot be overstated. In the era of precision oncology, determining whether a patient’s tumor cells express specific surface markers (e.g., activated matriptase) and respond to targeted therapies is essential for optimizing treatment outcomes and minimizing unnecessary toxicity. Traditional viability assays like the MTS or trypan blue exclusion provide population-level averages but lack single-cell resolution and real-time capability. Moreover, they do not distinguish between different mechanisms of cell death or subtle changes in membrane integrity that may precede overt necrosis or apoptosis.

Existing CTC detection technologies include platforms such as CellSearch®, which uses EpCAM-based immunomagnetic capture followed by fluorescent staining, and microfluidic devices like the CTC-iChip, which combines size-based sorting with inertial focusing. While innovative, these systems remain constrained by reliance on known surface markers, limited throughput, or complex operational protocols. Furthermore, none integrate real-time, label-free biophysical assessment of cell state with computational intelligence for dynamic classification.

Challenges in CTC and cancer cell detection extend beyond rarity and heterogeneity. Biological variability, sample preparation artifacts, and background interference from leukocytes and erythrocytes further complicate analysis. Additionally, the dielectric properties of cells—governed by membrane capacitance, cytoplasmic conductivity, and nuclear morphology—are highly sensitive to physiological state, yet conventional single-frequency impedance measurements fail to capture this multidimensional information.

Given these constraints, there exists a compelling need for improved methods that enable rapid, label-free, single-cell resolution analysis of cancer cell viability and type using minimal sample volumes, portable instrumentation, and intelligent data interpretation. Such a system would bridge the gap between laboratory research and clinical decision-making, facilitating timely and individualized cancer therapy.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention introduces a novel system for classifying biological particles, particularly cancer cells, based on their multifrequency impedance response. This system measures the electrical impedance of individual cells as they pass through a microfluidic channel embedded with electrodes, capturing both amplitude and phase changes across a range of alternating current (AC) frequencies. From this impedance response data, physical properties such as membrane integrity, cytoplasmic conductivity, and overall dielectric behavior are determined.

The invention further incorporates machine learning algorithms to classify biological particles according to their viability or type. Specifically, features extracted from impedance measurements—including amplitude change and phase shift at discrete frequencies such as 500 kHz, 20 MHz, 25 MHz, and 30 MHz—are used to train supervised classifiers, notably Support Vector Machines (SVMs) with Gaussian kernels. These models distinguish live from dead cells with high accuracy, enabling quantitative assessment of drug-induced cytotoxicity without labels or stains.

In another aspect, the invention provides a system for determining the biological particle type by measuring impedance response across multiple frequencies, determining relevant physical properties from the resulting data, and applying a trained machine learning model to assign classification labels. This approach is particularly effective for identifying cancer cells expressing specific surface markers, such as activated matriptase, and evaluating their response to antibody-conjugated therapeutics.

Additionally, the invention discloses a method of classifying biological particles comprising: (a) flowing a suspension of biological particles through a microfluidic channel containing integrated electrodes; (b) applying an AC voltage across the electrodes at multiple discrete frequencies simultaneously; (c) measuring the resulting impedance response for each particle; (d) extracting amplitude change and phase change features from the response data; and (e) inputting these features into a machine learning classifier to determine particle viability or identity. This method enables rapid, automated, and cost-effective analysis suitable for point-of-care diagnostics and personalized medicine applications.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

### A. Methods and Systems for Classifying Biological Particles

The invention provides comprehensive methods and systems for classifying biological particles, especially cancer cells, based on their intrinsic electrical properties. At the core of this approach is a microfluidic impedance cytometer capable of measuring the frequency-dependent impedance response of single cells in suspension. The system comprises a microfabricated chip with gold electrodes patterned on a glass substrate, bonded to a polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) microfluidic channel. Cells are introduced into the channel via gravity-driven flow, minimizing electronic noise associated with mechanical pumps.

Key components of the system include: (i) a microfluidic channel with defined geometry (e.g., 100 μm width, 30 μm height); (ii) interdigitated gold electrodes (20 μm wide, 25 μm spacing) fabricated using photolithography and electron beam evaporation; (iii) a multifrequency lock-in amplifier (e.g., Zurich Instruments®) capable of simultaneous excitation and detection at multiple frequencies; and (iv) software for data acquisition, post-processing, and machine learning-based classification.

Biological particles amenable to classification include mammalian cells, particularly human epithelial cancer cells such as T47D breast carcinoma cells, as well as white blood cells, platelets, and other suspended cellular entities. In exemplary embodiments, the system analyzes cancer cells treated with anti-matriptase-conjugated drugs to assess therapeutic efficacy. Cell collection is performed from culture media or dissociated tumor biopsies, followed by resuspension in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) at concentrations of approximately 400 cells/μL.

Impedance response measurement involves applying an AC voltage across the electrodes while cells traverse the sensing zone. The system records both real and imaginary components of the current, enabling calculation of amplitude and phase changes induced by each cell. These changes reflect perturbations in the local electric field due to differences in cellular dielectric properties between live and dead states.

Physical properties are determined through post-processing algorithms that detrend and denoise raw signals, then extract two primary features per frequency: (1) amplitude change—the difference between baseline and peak voltage during cell transit; and (2) phase change—the angular deviation in the excitation signal caused by the cell’s presence. These features are compiled into a feature matrix for machine learning analysis.

Machine learning models, particularly SVMs with Gaussian kernels, are trained on labeled datasets comprising impedance features from 100% live and 100% dead cells (validated by Vi-CELL™). Over 1,000 single-cell events are used to ensure robustness against overfitting. Alternative classifiers such as logistic regression, K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN), and neural networks may also be employed.

In cancer diagnosis applications, the system evaluates tumor cell viability following exposure to targeted therapeutics. It is particularly suited for epithelial carcinomas, B-cell lymphomas, and multiple myeloma where activated matriptase is overexpressed. Advantages over existing methods include label-free operation, minimal sample volume (~tens of microliters), single-cell resolution, and compatibility with downstream molecular analysis.

The label-free and cost-effective nature of the approach eliminates the need for fluorescent dyes or antibodies, reducing reagent costs and simplifying workflow. Moreover, the compact design supports point-of-care deployment in clinics or field settings. Multi-frequency impedance cytometry enables probing of both membrane (low-frequency) and intracellular (high-frequency) properties, yielding richer biophysical signatures than single-frequency methods.

Experiments were conducted using T47D cells at frequencies ranging from 300 kHz to 30 MHz, with simultaneous measurements at four discrete frequencies including 500 kHz. Cell preparation involved centrifugation (290 g, 5 min) and resuspension in 1× PBS immediately after viability confirmation via Vi-CELL™. Impedance cytometry measurements captured transient signals corresponding to individual cell passages.

An equivalent circuit model was used to interpret the electrode–electrolyte interface, comprising double-layer capacitances (Cdl) at each electrode, solution resistance (Rs), and coupling capacitance (Ccell) representing the cell’s influence. Data post-processing included detrending, denoising, and feature extraction. Amplitude change and phase change were computed for each cell at all measured frequencies.

Scatter plots of amplitude change (e.g., at 500 kHz vs. 20 MHz) revealed distinct clusters for live and dead cells, as did phase change plots. Machine learning analysis utilized the Classification Learner Toolbox in MATLAB® or equivalent platforms. Feature matrices were constructed from extracted amplitude and phase values, normalized to unit variance, and used to train classifiers.

Neural network architectures, including feedforward networks with hidden layers, were explored for enhanced nonlinearity handling. Unsupervised techniques such as k-means clustering provided exploratory insights into natural groupings within the data. Comparative analysis showed SVM with Gaussian kernel outperformed logistic regression and KNN in classification accuracy.

White blood cell subtypes were also classified using similar methodology, demonstrating broad applicability. Electrode fabrication employed standard photolithography: wafer cleaning, photoresist spin-coating, UV exposure through chromium masks, development, hard bake, followed by 10 nm Cr adhesion layer and 100 nm Au deposition via electron beam evaporation, and liftoff.

Microfluidic channels were fabricated via soft lithography: SU-8 patterning on silicon wafers created master molds; PDMS (10:1 ratio) was cast, cured at 80°C for 2 hours, peeled, and punched for inlet/outlet. Oxygen plasma treatment enabled irreversible bonding to electrode chips, followed by 70°C bake.

Cell culture used RPMI 1640 media with 10% FBS for T47D cells. Cytotoxicity assays involved 72-hour drug incubation, followed by MTS or Vi-CELL™ validation. Computer systems comprised client-server architecture over communications networks, with data modules storing impedance traces and computing modules executing classification algorithms. Internal computer structure included system bus, I/O interfaces, and program products encoded on non-transitory media.

### B. Definitions

As used herein, the terms “subject” and “patient” refer to any vertebrate organism, including humans, non-human primates, rodents, canines, felines, and livestock, from whom a biological sample is obtained for analysis. A “normal,” “control,” or “reference” subject denotes an individual without diagnosed disease or exhibiting baseline physiological parameters.

A “sample,” “test sample,” or “patient sample” encompasses any biological material collected from a subject, including whole blood, serum, plasma, urine, saliva, cerebrospinal fluid, bone marrow aspirate, fine-needle aspirates, tumor biopsies, and cell cultures. Sample preparation may involve centrifugation, filtration, dilution, or enzymatic dissociation to yield single-cell suspensions.

A “biological sample” includes cells, tissues, or bodily fluids containing analyzable biological particles. Cell types include epithelial cells, leukocytes, erythrocytes, platelets, stem cells, and neoplastic cells. Methods for collecting and processing samples follow standard clinical or research protocols.

The terms “determining,” “measuring,” “assessing,” and “assaying” denote quantitative or qualitative evaluation of a parameter using instrumental, computational, or observational means. “Diagnosis” refers to identification of a disease state based on analytical findings, while “prognosis” involves prediction of disease course or treatment outcome.

Singular forms include plurals unless context dictates otherwise. The terms “including,” “comprising,” “containing,” and “having” are open-ended and permit additional elements. Phrases such as “in one embodiment” indicate optional implementations. “And/or” denotes inclusive disjunction. “Substantially” implies sufficient similarity to achieve functional equivalence. “Approximately” or “about” allows ±10% variation. Ranges (e.g., “from X to Y”) include endpoints. “Each” refers to every member of a collection. Examples are illustrative, not limiting.
