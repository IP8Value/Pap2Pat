# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNICAL FIELD

The present invention pertains to the field of molecular biotechnology and, more specifically, to methods for facilitating the genetic manipulation of bacterial strains that are recalcitrant to conventional transformation techniques. The invention provides a novel pipeline for mimicking the native DNA methylation patterns of target bacteria in a modified *Escherichia coli* host strain, thereby enabling the efficient introduction of exogenous DNA into otherwise difficult-to-transform bacterial species. This approach is particularly relevant for bacterial strains that harbor multiple restriction-modification (R-M) systems, which typically degrade foreign DNA lacking the appropriate methylation signatures. The invention encompasses recombinant vectors, engineered bacterial hosts, and methods for co-expressing DNA-methyltransferase-encoding genes to produce methylation-modified plasmid DNA suitable for high-efficiency transformation of target bacteria.

## BACKGROUND ART

Restriction-modification (R-M) systems are ubiquitous defense mechanisms in prokaryotes that protect cells from invading genetic elements such as bacteriophages and plasmids. These systems consist of two enzymatic components: a restriction endonuclease (REase) that cleaves DNA at specific recognition sequences, and a DNA methyltransferase (MTase) that modifies the same or overlapping sequences by adding methyl groups to adenine or cytosine residues, thereby preventing cleavage by the cognate REase. R-M systems are broadly classified into four types—Type I, II, III, and IV—based on their subunit composition, cofactor requirements, cleavage site positions relative to recognition sequences, and sequence specificity. Type I, II, and III systems typically target unmethylated DNA, while Type IV systems recognize and cleave DNA containing foreign methylation patterns, such as those introduced by Dam or Dcm methylases in *E. coli*. The presence of multiple R-M systems in a single bacterial strain creates a formidable barrier to the introduction of exogenous DNA, especially when such DNA is prepared in standard laboratory *E. coli* strains that carry their own methylation signatures.

To overcome these barriers, various techniques have been developed. In vitro modification of exogenous DNA involves treating plasmid DNA with purified MTases prior to transformation, but this method is limited by the availability of active enzymes, the cost of large-scale reactions, and the difficulty of modifying multiple sites simultaneously. In vivo modification strategies rely on expressing heterologous MTases in *E. coli* to methylate plasmid DNA during propagation. However, this approach is often hindered by the toxicity of foreign methylation patterns to the *E. coli* host, particularly when the introduced MTases modify chromosomal DNA in ways that activate endogenous restriction systems such as Mrr, McrA, and McrBC, which target methylated DNA with non-native patterns. Another strategy involves inactivating the R-M systems of the target bacterium through gene knockout, but this is labor-intensive, may compromise cellular fitness, and is impractical for strains with numerous or poorly characterized R-M systems.

Existing techniques suffer from several limitations. First, many bacterial strains cannot be transformed efficiently even with unmethylated DNA due to the presence of Type IV R-M systems. Second, the expression of heterologous MTases in wild-type *E. coli* often leads to poor cell growth or plasmid instability because the host’s restriction machinery recognizes the newly introduced methylation as foreign. Third, incomplete knowledge of the full complement of MTases in a target organism makes it difficult to design a comprehensive methylation strategy. Finally, current methods do not provide a universal platform that can be readily adapted to diverse bacterial species with varying genomic architectures and R-M system compositions. Collectively, these challenges have restricted genetic manipulation to a narrow subset of model organisms, leaving many industrially, environmentally, or clinically relevant bacteria genetically intractable.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The object of the present invention is to provide a robust and universally applicable method for introducing exogenous DNA molecules into bacterial strains that are resistant to conventional transformation due to the presence of multiple restriction-modification systems. The invention achieves this by employing a specialized *Escherichia coli* host strain, designated EC135, which has been genetically engineered to lack all known endogenous restriction-modification systems and orphan methyltransferases. This strain serves as a neutral platform for the in vivo modification of exogenous plasmid DNA without unintended methylation or degradation.

The method comprises co-expressing one or more DNA-methyltransferase-encoding genes from the target bacterium in the EC135 host. These genes are cloned into an inducible expression vector, such as pBAD43 or pWYE724, under the control of an arabinose-inducible promoter. Upon induction, the expressed MTases modify the exogenous plasmid DNA propagated in EC135, thereby mimicking the native methylation pattern of the target bacterium. The resulting methylation-modified plasmid DNA is then extracted and introduced into the target bacterium via standard transformation protocols, such as electroporation.

The recombinant vector used in this process contains multiple MTase genes arranged in operons, optimized with ribosome binding sites for efficient expression in *E. coli*. The construction of such vectors is facilitated by in vivo assembly in *Saccharomyces cerevisiae*, which enables rapid and precise integration of multiple DNA fragments. The target bacterium may be any prokaryotic species that exhibits resistance to transformation due to R-M barriers, including but not limited to *Bacillus amyloliquefaciens*, *Bacillus cereus*, and *Nitrobacter hamburgensis*.

The *E. coli* EC135 strain is derived from the TOP10 background and carries deletions in the *dam*, *dcm*, *hsdRMS*, *mcrA*, *mcrBC*, and *mrr* loci, rendering it incapable of methylating DNA at common sites or restricting foreign methylation patterns. The exogenous DNA molecule to be introduced may be a shuttle plasmid, an integration plasmid, or any other replicon suitable for the target organism. The DNA-methyltransferase-encoding genes are identified through bioinformatic analysis of the target genome, followed by experimental validation of their activity using dot blot assays with antibodies against N6-methyladenine (m6A), N4-methylcytosine (m4C), and 5-methylcytosine (m5C). Predicted genes are cloned and expressed individually in EC135, and their methylation activity is confirmed by immunodetection or liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS).

The invention offers several advantages over existing methods. By eliminating all endogenous R-M activity in the host, it ensures that plasmid DNA is modified only by the introduced MTases, without interference from competing methylation systems. The co-expression of multiple MTases allows for the faithful replication of complex methylation patterns found in bacteria with numerous R-M systems. Furthermore, the use of an inducible system prevents constitutive expression, which could be toxic to the host. The resulting plasmid DNA exhibits dramatically enhanced transformation efficiency in the target bacterium, enabling applications such as gene knockouts, reporter gene expression, and metabolic engineering in previously intractable strains.

## SPECIFIC EMBODIMENTS

### Example 1

The present invention is exemplified by the construction of the *E. coli* strain EC135, a derivative of the commercially available TOP10 strain, which serves as the foundational host for the MoDMP (Mimicking of DNA Methylation Patterns) pipeline. To generate EC135, competent cells were first prepared from *E. coli* TOP10 using standard calcium chloride methods. The temperature-sensitive plasmid pKD46, which expresses the λ Red recombinase system under the control of an arabinose-inducible promoter, was then transformed into these cells. A chloramphenicol resistance gene flanked by homology arms targeting the *recA* locus was amplified by PCR and electroporated into the pKD46-containing cells to restore the wild-type *recA* allele, as the *dam recA* double mutant is inviable. Recombinants were selected on chloramphenicol plates and verified by PCR. The pKD46 plasmid was subsequently eliminated by growing the cells at 37°C, which exceeds the plasmid’s replication temperature limit. Following *recA* reversion, the *dam* gene was knocked out using a kanamycin resistance cassette amplified with homology arms, and recombinants were selected and verified. The kanamycin cassette was then removed using FLP recombinase expressed from plasmid pCP20. The resulting strain, lacking both *dam* and *dcm* and carrying a functional *recA* gene, was designated EC135 and validated for the absence of endogenous methylation and restriction activity.

### Example 2

The efficacy of the MoDMP pipeline was demonstrated using *Bacillus amyloliquefaciens* TA208, an industrial guanosine-producing strain refractory to standard transformation. Putative DNA-methyltransferase-encoding genes were predicted from the TA208 genome using the REBASE database and verified by cloning into pBAD43 and expressing in EC135. Active MTases were identified via dot blot assays detecting m6A, m4C, and m5C modifications. A recombinant bacterium, EC135/pM.Bam, was constructed by co-expressing all four active MTase genes in a single operon on plasmid pWYE724, assembled in *S. cerevisiae* DAY414. Shuttle plasmids—including pAD123, pMK3, pMK4, pHCMC02, pHCMC04, and pDG—were transformed into EC135/pM.Bam, induced with arabinose, and extracted. These plasmids were then transformed into TA208, yielding transformation efficiencies up to 3×10⁶ CFU/µg DNA, a 10⁴-fold increase over plasmids from EC135 alone. Notably, plasmids pAD123 and pDG, which were untransformable when prepared in TOP10 or EC135, became transformable via MoDMP. Additionally, the integration plasmid pWYE748, designed to disrupt the *upp* gene, was successfully introduced into TA208 only when prepared in EC135/pM.Bam, yielding chloramphenicol-resistant colonies. PCR and sequencing confirmed correct integration, and phenotypic assays showed that the resulting strain BS043 grew on 5-fluorouracil, confirming *upp* inactivation. Repeated attempts to transform pWYE748 prepared in EC135 or TOP10 failed, underscoring the necessity of the MoDMP host.

### Example 3

The MoDMP pipeline was further validated in *Bacillus cereus* ATCC 10987, a strain with four characterized R-M systems. Putative MTase genes were predicted and verified as in Example 2. Five active genes were cloned into pBAD43 and expressed in EC135, with activity confirmed by dot blot and LC-MS. The recombinant strain EC135/pM.Bce was constructed by assembling all five genes into pWYE724 in *S. cerevisiae*. Shuttle plasmids transformed into EC135/pM.Bce, induced, and extracted showed transformation efficiencies in *B. cereus* up to 2×10⁷ CFU/µg DNA, a 10³-fold improvement over controls. Plasmids from EC135 and TOP10 showed similar low efficiencies, indicating that Type IV restriction is not a major barrier in this strain, but that methylation by native MTases is essential for evading Type I–III systems. Dot hybridization of total DNA from EC135/pM.Bce confirmed the presence of expected methylation marks, and restriction protection assays demonstrated that co-expressed MTases conferred broad resistance to REase digestion, matching the native strain’s pattern.

### Example 4

The pipeline was extended to *Nitrobacter hamburgensis* X14, a chemoautotrophic bacterium with eleven putative R-M systems and no prior reports of genetic transformation. MTase genes were predicted from the X14 genome, and four were verified as active in EC135. Recombinant strains EC135/DNA1–DNA10 were constructed by expressing individual or combined MTases. Dot blot and LC-MS confirmed m6A modification by Nham_0569, Nham_0582, and Nham_3225. The full set of active MTases was assembled into pM.Nham in *S. cerevisiae* and transformed into EC135. The shuttle plasmid pBBR1-MCS5-PNham3450-GFP, carrying a *gfpmut3a* reporter under a native X14 promoter, was methylated in EC135/pM.Nham and transformed into X14. After enrichment, green fluorescent cells were observed by confocal microscopy, and flow cytometry revealed 50.37% of cells were fluorescent, confirming successful transformation. Plasmid rescue into *E. coli* and restriction digestion verified plasmid integrity. This represents the first genetic transformation of any *Nitrobacter* species, demonstrating the MoDMP pipeline’s applicability to phylogenetically and physiologically diverse bacteria.
