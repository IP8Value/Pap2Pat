# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

Custom optical systems have long been the backbone of experimental atomic, molecular, and optical (AMO) physics, particularly in the context of trapped ion quantum information processing. These systems typically involve a large number of discrete optical components—such as mirrors, lenses, beam splitters, modulators, and fibers—that are mounted individually onto optical tables using posts, clamps, and kinematic mounts. While this approach provides considerable flexibility during the prototyping phase, it suffers from several critical limitations that impede scalability, reproducibility, and long-term operational stability. Chief among these limitations is mechanical and thermal drift, which arises due to environmental perturbations such as temperature fluctuations, air currents, acoustic noise, and vibrations inherent to laboratory settings. Over time, even sub-micron displacements or angular misalignments of optical elements can degrade the fidelity of quantum operations by introducing phase noise, beam pointing instability, or intensity fluctuations in laser delivery paths. Furthermore, the manual alignment process required for such systems is labor-intensive, non-reproducible across different setups, and highly sensitive to operator skill. In cryogenic environments—where trapped ion experiments increasingly operate to achieve ultrahigh vacuum (UHV) conditions and reduce motional heating—the challenges are exacerbated by thermal contraction mismatches between materials, limited physical access, and stringent requirements for vibration isolation. Traditional optical breadboards lack standardized mounting interfaces that ensure precise, repeatable positioning of optical modules relative to one another or to the ion trap itself. Consequently, integrating multiple optical functions—such as state detection, Doppler cooling, Raman gate addressing, photoionization loading, and frequency stabilization—into a single coherent system becomes an exercise in constant realignment and troubleshooting rather than robust engineering. These limitations represent a fundamental barrier to transitioning trapped ion platforms from laboratory curiosities into reliable, field-deployable quantum computing systems. There exists a pressing need for an integrated optomechanical architecture that combines the precision of monolithic machining with the modularity required for functional specialization, all while maintaining compatibility with cryogenic operation and UHV constraints.

## SUMMARY

The present invention introduces a novel optomechanical system designed specifically for high-fidelity trapped ion quantum information processing under cryogenic and ultrahigh vacuum conditions. Central to this system is the concept of the optical block—a precision-machined plate that serves as a self-contained unit for implementing a specific optical function. Each optical block is fabricated from a low-stress, dimensionally stable material such as MIC-6 cast aluminum and features a grid of tapped holes arranged at a regular pitch, typically 2 inches, conforming to standard optical mounting conventions. Critically, each tapped hole is accompanied by two precision dowel pin holes spaced symmetrically around it, enabling repeatable, micron-level alignment when the block is mounted onto a larger support structure. The dowel pin hole arrangement follows a consistent geometric pattern across all blocks and supporting plates, ensuring interchangeability and positional fidelity throughout the system. Optical elements—including mirrors, lenses, acousto-optic modulators (AOMs), electro-optic modulators (EOMs), fiber couplers, and beam shaping optics—are directly mounted onto the optical block using complementary dowel pins and fasteners, thereby fixing their spatial relationship relative to the block’s reference frame. This design eliminates cumulative alignment errors that plague traditional post-and-clamp assemblies. The invention further encompasses a hierarchical optomechanical system comprising a base plate, an intermediate plate, and one or more optical blocks. The base plate, which forms the foundation of the entire apparatus, is rigidly coupled to a cryostat’s cold stage and includes its own array of tapped holes and dowel pin holes matching those on the intermediate plate. The intermediate plate, in turn, provides a thermally stable platform for mounting optical blocks and interfaces directly with the cryostat via precision dowel pins and screws, ensuring common-mode vibration response between the optical system and the ion trap. Optical coupling between components is achieved primarily through single-mode optical fibers, allowing each optical block to be independently tested, calibrated, and replaced without disturbing the rest of the system. The optical elements supported by this architecture include continuous wave (CW) lasers for cooling and state detection, frequency-stabilized reference cavities, Raman laser modulation units for quantum logic gates, ion imaging optics, and ablation beam delivery systems for isotope-selective ion loading. Integrated within this framework is a cryo-package assembly—a compact, differentially pumped vacuum chamber that houses the ion trap, getter material, and ablation target. This cryo-package consists of a hermetically sealed package, typically a 100-pin ceramic pin grid array (CPGA), and a custom-machined lid made of oxygen-free copper. The lid incorporates an imaging window fabricated from sapphire or fused silica, side viewports made of N-BK7 glass, and a meandering passageway that serves as a narrow pumping channel during initial evacuation. A getter cavity within the lid contains activated charcoal or other cryosorbent materials to achieve ultrahigh vacuum upon cooldown. A ring frame brazed onto the CPGA mates with a corresponding groove in the lid, with indium wire providing both a compliant seal and enhanced thermal conduction. An internal ground shield wall, machined beneath the imaging window, minimizes stray electric fields from dielectric surfaces while preserving optical access. Together, these innovations enable unprecedented mechanical, thermal, and optical stability in a scalable, modular platform suitable for advanced quantum computing applications.

The drawings are arranged to progressively disclose the system from macro to micro: Figure 1 presents an overview of the entire setup; Figures 2–5 detail internal cryostat and cryo-package structures; Figures 6–10 show experimental characterization data validating performance. Turning to the optomechanical system 100, it comprises a module plate 102 serving as the primary mounting surface for multiple optical blocks 118. Each optical block implements a distinct optical function—such as CW beam delivery, Raman modulation, or fluorescence imaging—and interacts with others via fiber optic links, minimizing free-space propagation and associated instabilities. System 100 offers significant advantages over prior art, including reduced drift, enhanced reproducibility, simplified maintenance, and compatibility with cryogenic environments. Module plate 102 is machined from MIC-6 aluminum for its excellent flatness, low internal stress, and machinability. It features a uniform grid of ¼-20 tapped holes at 2-inch pitch, each flanked by two 2-mm dowel pin holes arranged perpendicular to the grid axes. Optical block 118, similarly fabricated from MIC-6, includes matching tapped and dowel pin holes on its underside, allowing it to be aligned and secured to module plate 102 with micron-level precision using dowel pins and screws. Optical elements are mounted directly onto the top surface of optical block 118 using the same dowel-and-tapped-hole paradigm, ensuring fixed alignment relative to the block’s coordinate system. Assembly begins with computer-aided design (CAD) modeling to define exact hole positions and optical paths. During manufacturing, CNC machining ensures dimensional accuracy. To assemble, an optical block is positioned over the module plate using dowel pins inserted into corresponding holes; once aligned, screws are tightened through the tapped holes to secure the block. Alternative arrangements may use magnetic bases or kinematic mounts, but the dowel-and-screw method provides superior rigidity. Multiple optical blocks can be attached sequentially, each referencing the same global coordinate system defined by the module plate grid. In operation, system 100 delivers laser light via fibers to each block, where free-space optics condition the beam before it exits toward the cryostat. The entire structure is enclosed to suppress air turbulence. When integrated with a cryostat, an intermediate plate couples the optical blocks to the cryogenic environment. This intermediate plate features tapped holes matching those on the optical blocks and dowel pin holes aligning with a base plate affixed to the cryostat’s 5-K stage. The base plate, also machined with a compatible hole pattern, anchors the entire optical stack to the cryostat. The cryostat itself includes a sample chamber with radiation shields, temperature sensors, and optical viewports. Upon installation, the optomechanical system remains co-aligned with the ion trap despite thermal cycling, as all components share a common mechanical reference. Experiments confirm exceptional stability: vibrational displacement is less than 3 nm RMS, and optical coherence times exceed 300 ms. Applications span quantum computing, precision metrology, and fundamental AMO physics. The cryo-package assembly further enhances performance. It consists of a CPGA package housing the ion trap and an interposer with filtering capacitors, sealed by a copper lid. The lid includes a sapphire imaging window, N-BK7 side windows, a meandering evacuation channel, and a getter cavity filled with activated charcoal. A ring frame on the CPGA engages a groove in the lid, with indium wire providing a compliant, thermally conductive seal. An internal ground shield wall surrounds the imaging path to mitigate patch potentials. During assembly, the lid is bonded to the package in a cleanroom, then mounted to the sample mount via eight tapped holes on its top surface. After installation into the cryostat, the chamber is evacuated at room temperature through the meandering passageway, then cooled. Cryopumping by the getter achieves pressures below 10⁻¹¹ torr, as inferred from zig-zag ion chain flip rates. Temperature and pressure are monitored indirectly via ion dynamics. Motional heating rates as low as 13 quanta/s demonstrate minimal electric field noise. Variations include alternative lid materials (e.g., titanium), package configurations (e.g., multi-chamber), window materials (e.g., fused silica), getter types (e.g., non-evaporable getters), and cryopumping methods (e.g., cryogenic panels). In summary, the optomechanical system provides a stable, modular, and scalable platform for trapped ion quantum technologies, overcoming the limitations of traditional optical setups through precision mechanical integration and cryogenic optimization.
