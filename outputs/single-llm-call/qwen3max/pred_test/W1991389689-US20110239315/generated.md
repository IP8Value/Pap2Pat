# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNICAL FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention resides in the field of molecular biology, genetic engineering, and synthetic biology, specifically relating to programmable DNA-binding proteins and their applications in targeted gene regulation and genome modification. More particularly, the invention provides novel polypeptides comprising engineered transcription activator-like (TAL) effector repeat domains that enable sequence-specific recognition of DNA target sites, and further encompasses methods for constructing such polypeptides, nucleic acids encoding them, host cells expressing them, and their use in modulating gene expression in a wide range of organisms including plants, mammals, and other eukaryotes. The invention also relates to modular cloning strategies for the rapid and reliable assembly of custom TAL effector architectures with defined DNA-binding specificities, as well as fusion constructs wherein the TAL repeat domain is operably linked to functional effector domains such as transcriptional activators, repressors, nucleases, or chromatin-modifying enzymes. These engineered molecules represent powerful tools for precise manipulation of genomic loci, enabling applications in basic research, biotechnology, agriculture, and therapeutic development.

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

Phytopathogenic bacteria of the genus *Xanthomonas* employ a suite of virulence factors known as transcription activator-like (TAL) effectors to subvert host plant defenses and promote infection. These bacterial proteins are translocated into plant cells via type III secretion systems and function by binding to specific promoter sequences of host genes, thereby activating transcription of susceptibility genes that facilitate pathogen colonization. Central to this activity is a unique DNA-binding domain composed of tandem repeats, each typically 34 amino acids in length, which collectively confer sequence-specific recognition of target DNA. This architecture distinguishes TAL effectors from other classes of DNA-binding proteins and underpins their utility as programmable molecular tools.

The DNA-binding specificity of TAL effectors is determined by a modular array of repeat units, wherein each repeat recognizes a single nucleotide in the target DNA sequence. Critical to this recognition code are two hypervariable amino acid residues at positions 12 and 13 within each repeat, commonly referred to as the repeat-variable diresidue (RVD). Empirical studies have established a direct correlation between specific RVDs and their cognate DNA bases—for example, the HD RVD preferentially binds cytosine, while NI recognizes adenine. This simple and predictable code enables rational design of TAL effectors with customized DNA-binding specificities simply by arranging repeat units in an order corresponding to the desired target sequence.

This capacity for selective gene expression through programmable DNA recognition has motivated significant interest in adapting TAL effectors for biotechnological applications beyond their native phytopathogenic context. In particular, the ability to engineer proteins that bind any user-defined DNA sequence offers unprecedented opportunities for targeted modulation of gene expression, site-specific genome editing, and epigenetic reprogramming. Prior to the discovery of the TAL effector code, zinc finger (ZF) proteins represented the primary platform for engineering custom DNA-binding domains. However, ZF design is complicated by context-dependent effects between adjacent fingers, making prediction of binding specificity less reliable and construction more laborious.

Current methods for identifying and assembling custom zinc finger arrays often rely on phage display, bacterial selection systems, or modular assembly based on pre-characterized finger motifs. Despite advances, these approaches remain technically challenging, time-consuming, and prone to reduced affinity or off-target binding due to inter-finger cooperativity. Moreover, the repetitive nature of both ZF and TAL coding sequences presents inherent difficulties for conventional cloning techniques, necessitating specialized strategies to avoid recombination or misassembly during plasmid propagation.

These limitations underscore the need for improved methodologies that simplify the construction of custom DNA-binding proteins while ensuring high fidelity and functionality. The present invention addresses this need by leveraging the intrinsic modularity and predictability of TAL effector repeat domains, combined with an optimized cloning framework that enables rapid, scarless, and scalable assembly of designer polypeptides with precisely defined DNA-recognition capabilities.

## BRIEF SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The invention provides a method for producing a polypeptide capable of selectively recognizing a predetermined DNA sequence, the method comprising assembling a repeat domain from a plurality of repeat units, each repeat unit comprising a conserved structural scaffold and a hypervariable region that dictates base-pair specificity. The repeat domain is constructed using a modular approach wherein individual repeat units are selected based on their known recognition code to match the target DNA sequence in a linear, one-to-one correspondence. This method yields a polypeptide that specifically binds to the intended DNA sequence with high affinity and selectivity.

The polypeptide produced by this method comprises an N-terminal domain, a central repeat domain composed of tandem repeat units, and a C-terminal domain that may include an effector moiety such as a transcriptional activation domain, repression domain, nuclease domain, or other functional module. The invention further encompasses isolated polynucleotide molecules encoding such polypeptides, recombinant expression vectors containing these nucleic acids, and host organisms—including bacterial, yeast, insect, mammalian, and plant cells—engineered to express the polypeptides.

In addition, the invention discloses a method for constructing polypeptides with custom DNA-binding specificities through a hierarchical cloning strategy that utilizes type IIS restriction enzymes to enable directional, scarless ligation of repeat modules. This “Golden TAL Technology” facilitates the rapid assembly of TAL effector derivatives with variable numbers of repeats (from 1 to over 20), diverse terminal domains, and integrated tags or reporters.

The invention also includes methods for targeted modulation of gene expression in a cell by introducing a polypeptide that binds to a regulatory region of a target gene and either activates or represses its transcription, depending on the nature of the fused effector domain. Furthermore, the invention provides methods for directed modification of a target DNA sequence by fusing the TAL repeat domain to a nuclease domain (e.g., FokI), thereby inducing double-strand breaks that stimulate homology-directed repair or non-homologous end joining, enabling precise genome editing.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

The invention is described herein with reference to the accompanying figures, which illustrate exemplary embodiments of the disclosed technology. In certain embodiments, the invention provides polypeptides comprising a repeat domain derived from TAL effectors of *Xanthomonas* species, particularly those belonging to the AvrBs3 family. A “repeat domain” refers to a contiguous stretch of amino acids composed of multiple tandem “repeat units,” each approximately 34 residues long, sharing a conserved backbone structure but differing at key hypervariable positions that determine DNA base recognition. Each “repeat unit” corresponds to a single nucleotide in the target DNA, and the sequential arrangement of repeat units defines the full target sequence recognized by the polypeptide.

The “recognition code” governing this interaction is defined by the identity of the two hypervariable amino acids at positions 12 and 13 (the RVD) within each repeat unit. For instance, an HD RVD specifies binding to cytosine, NG to thymine, NI to adenine, and NN to guanine or adenine. This code allows accurate prediction of DNA-binding specificity from protein sequence and vice versa. An “effector” as used herein denotes any functional domain fused to the TAL repeat domain to confer biological activity upon DNA binding. Effector proteins may include transcriptional activators (e.g., VP16, GAL4), repressors (e.g., KRAB), nucleases (e.g., FokI, I-SceI), chromatin modifiers (e.g., histone acetyltransferases, deacetylases), or fluorescent tags.

“Naturally occurring” refers to molecules or sequences found in nature without human intervention. “Modulating expression” encompasses any alteration in the level of gene transcription, including “inhibiting expression” (reducing mRNA production) and “activating expression” (increasing mRNA production). Assays for modulation include reporter gene assays (e.g., luciferase, GUS), quantitative RT-PCR, and Western blotting. A “regulatory domain” is a protein segment that influences transcription, sourced from natural transcription factors such as p53, NF-κB, or synthetic activators like VP16. Examples of associated modifiers include kinases (e.g., MAPK), phosphatases, oncogenes (e.g., Myc), DNA repair enzymes (e.g., BRCA1), and chromatin-associated proteins (e.g., HP1).

Histone deacetylase functions as a transcriptional repressor by removing acetyl groups from histones, promoting chromatin condensation, whereas histone acetyltransferase acts as an activator by facilitating open chromatin states. A “gene” includes coding sequences, promoters, enhancers, and untranslated regions. A “target gene” is any gene whose expression is intended to be modulated. “Plant” includes monocots and dicots, with tissues such as leaves, roots, and meristems being amenable to transformation. “Modulating expression in plant cells” involves delivering the polypeptide or its encoding nucleic acid into plant protoplasts, callus, or whole plants via *Agrobacterium*-mediated transformation, particle bombardment, or viral vectors.

Target DNA sequences are typically located in promoter regions upstream of transcription start sites and may include minimal promoters, enhancers, or silencers. Polynucleotide molecules of the invention include DNA and RNA analogs with modified backbones (e.g., phosphorothioates) or linkages, and may exist as linear, circular, single- or double-stranded forms. “Specifically binds to a target DNA sequence” means the polypeptide associates with the intended sequence with significantly higher affinity than with non-target sequences. “Endogenous” refers to native genomic sequences, while “exogenous” denotes introduced foreign DNA. A “genetically modified plant” contains stably integrated transgenes.

A “minimal promoter” drives basal transcription but requires enhancer elements for robust activity. Repressor and activator proteins mediate “repression” and “activation,” respectively. A “derivative” is a modified version retaining core function. Repeat units “derived from TAL effectors” originate from natural or engineered variants of AvrBs3-like proteins, which share >60% amino acid identity across their repeat domains. The TAL effector family exhibits a conserved N-terminal domain involved in translocation and nuclear localization, followed by the central repeat domain and a C-terminal activation domain.

The modular architecture permits combinatorial assembly: by selecting repeat units with appropriate RVDs, one can design polypeptides that bind any user-specified DNA sequence beginning with a 5′ thymine. This principle enables construction of artificial transcription factors, nucleases, or epigenetic editors. For genome editing, the TAL repeat domain is fused to a FokI endonuclease domain; dimerization of FokI induces double-strand breaks, which are repaired by cellular machinery via homologous recombination or error-prone NHEJ. Alternatively, I-SceI or other rare-cutting endonucleases may be employed.

Polynucleotides encoding these polypeptides are codon-optimized for expression in target hosts and cloned into expression cassettes with appropriate promoters (constitutive, inducible, tissue-specific), terminators, and selectable markers (e.g., antibiotic or herbicide resistance). Delivery into plant cells uses *Agrobacterium*, biolistics, or PEG-mediated transformation; into mammalian cells via lipofection, electroporation, or viral vectors. The invention further covers fragments, variants, and mutants of the core sequences, provided they retain DNA-binding specificity. Variants may include conservative substitutions, insertions, or deletions evaluated by sequence alignment algorithms such as BLAST or ClustalW.

Stringent hybridization conditions ensure specificity in library screening, with wash stringency adjusted based on melting temperature (Tm) calculations. Expression cassettes are designed with optimized 5′ UTRs (e.g., TMV omega leader) to enhance translation. Transformation methods yield stable or transient expression, and regenerated plants are propagated to homozygosity. Non-plant hosts (yeast, insect, mammalian cells) are also contemplated for protein production or functional assays. Ultimately, the invention enables precise, programmable control over genomic function across diverse biological systems.

## EXAMPLES

### Example 1

The basis for DNA specificity of TAL effectors was elucidated through analysis of AvrBs3, revealing that each 34-amino-acid repeat contains hypervariable residues at positions 12 and 13 (RVDs) that dictate recognition of a single DNA base pair. Correlation of repeat types with specific bases—HD for C, NI for A, NG for T—led to a predictive model of DNA recognition. This model was validated using an AvrBs3 derivative lacking three repeats, which activated only promoters containing the truncated target sequence. Analysis of UPA-boxes in AvrBs3-induced pepper genes confirmed in vivo relevance. Prediction of target sequences for other *Xanthomonas* TAL effectors (Hax3, Hax4) was achieved by inspecting promoter regions of known target genes, identifying matching sequences that adhered to the 5′ T requirement. Experimental validation showed Hax3 and Hax4 activated reporters only when their cognate boxes were present. Permutation of the Hax4-box demonstrated strict dependence on the third base (recognized by an NG repeat), confirming specificity. Similarly, an IG repeat in Hax2 was shown to recognize thymine. Artificial effector ArtX1 was designed based on predicted RVD–base pairing and activated only its intended target. Influence of repeat number was tested by constructing effectors with 0.5 to 15.5 HD repeats; maximal activation required at least 10.5 repeats. Artificial effectors with novel specificities were successfully generated, demonstrating the robustness of the code. Further embodiments include AvrBs3-family effectors modified at the C-terminus for expression in non-plant systems, functioning as activators or repressors when fused to appropriate domains. Repeat domains also serve as affinity tags for DNA pulldown or imaging. Fusion to endonucleases enables targeted cleavage. Custom repeat orders are assembled via Golden Gate cloning. Effectors can repress viral replication by binding essential viral promoters. The invention covers isolated nucleic acids, transformed plants, seeds, and host cells expressing these effectors, along with associated promoters and vectors. Materials and methods detail bacterial strains, plant inoculations, artificial effector construction using GATEWAY-compatible ENTRY vectors, GUS reporter assays, transgenic *Arabidopsis*, β-glucuronidase quantification, DNaseI footprinting with fluorescent PCR products, and plasmid sequencing.

### Example 2

A TAL repeat unit capable of binding guanine was identified through functional analysis of hypervariable region (HVR) motifs. Bioinformatic screening of natural TAL effectors revealed underrepresented RVDs potentially targeting G. Synthetic repeats with candidate RVDs (e.g., NH, NK) were tested in reporter assays in human cells. Only specific combinations yielded activation of G-containing target promoters, establishing new entries in the recognition code. This expands the repertoire of targetable sequences, particularly GC-rich regions previously difficult to address.

### Example 3

The highly repetitive nature of TAL coding sequences impedes conventional cloning. To overcome this, Golden Gate cloning was adopted, utilizing type IIS restriction enzymes like BsaI and BpiI that cut outside their recognition sites, enabling seamless, directional assembly. Repeat units are cloned into entry plasmids flanked by BpiI sites, generating unique overhangs that dictate positional order. In a first ligation, up to six repeats are assembled into an intermediate vector. These 6-mers are then released via BpiI and ligated into an acceptor vector flanked by BsaI sites, which also accommodates N-terminal, C-terminal, and tag modules. A single-tube reaction with BsaI and ligase simultaneously digests and ligates all components, yielding full-length effectors in one step. This method, schematized in FIG. 19A-D, allows rapid generation of effectors with user-defined specificities without PCR, minimizing errors and enabling high-throughput construction.

### Example 4

TAL-FokI nuclease fusions were produced in vitro by co-expressing TAL repeat domains and FokI in rabbit reticulocyte lysate. Purified proteins were incubated with plasmid substrates containing target sites. Cleavage products were resolved by agarose gel electrophoresis, showing specific double-strand breaks only when both TAL binding sites were present in proper orientation and spacing, confirming functional nuclease activity dependent on correct DNA recognition.

### Example 5

Additional RVD–base recognition specificities were determined by constructing DNA-binding domains using Golden Gate Cloning and testing activation of reporters with systematically varied target sequences. Results, depicted in FIGS. 20–27, revealed that certain RVDs (e.g., NN) exhibit dual specificity (G/A), while others (e.g., HG) show preference for G. These expanded codes enhance design flexibility and accuracy. The data validate the use of these specificities in engineering methods and compositions for precise genomic targeting.
