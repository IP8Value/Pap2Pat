# DESCRIPTION

The present invention provides a novel method for diagnosing schizophrenia in human subjects by assessing the expression levels of specific molecular markers—namely, the dopamine D2 receptor (DRD2) and the inwardly rectifying potassium channel Kir2.3—in peripheral biological samples. Schizophrenia is a complex neuropsychiatric disorder that manifests through disturbances in cognition, emotion, and motor function, and its diagnosis has historically relied on clinical observation and psychiatric evaluation, which can be subjective and delayed. The identification of objective, quantifiable biomarkers that correlate with the disease state offers a significant advancement in both early detection and ongoing management of schizophrenia. The inventors have discovered that DRD2 and Kir2.3 are consistently overexpressed at the mRNA level in peripheral blood lymphocytes (PBLs) of drug-naïve or drug-free individuals diagnosed with schizophrenia, as compared to healthy controls. This finding is particularly robust because it eliminates confounding effects from antipsychotic medication, thereby reflecting the intrinsic pathophysiology of the disorder. The method described herein enables clinicians and researchers to obtain a biological sample—preferably peripheral blood—from a subject, isolate nucleic acids or proteins therefrom, and quantitatively assess the expression levels of DRD2 and/or Kir2.3 using established molecular techniques such as quantitative real-time PCR (QRT-PCR), microarray hybridization, or immunoassays. By comparing these measured levels to a predetermined baseline derived from healthy individuals, a diagnostic conclusion regarding the presence or likelihood of schizophrenia can be drawn. This approach not only enhances diagnostic accuracy but also supports personalized treatment strategies and longitudinal monitoring of disease progression or therapeutic response.

## BACKGROUND ART

Schizophrenia is a chronic and severe mental disorder affecting approximately 1% of the global population, characterized by positive symptoms (e.g., hallucinations, delusions), negative symptoms (e.g., social withdrawal, anhedonia), and cognitive impairments. Despite decades of research, its etiology remains incompletely understood, though dysregulation of the dopaminergic system is widely implicated as a central pathophysiological mechanism. Genetic studies have consistently demonstrated a heritable component, with genome-wide linkage analyses pointing to multiple susceptibility loci. Prior art has explored the use of genetic markers for schizophrenia diagnosis, focusing largely on neurotransmitter-related genes. For instance, Johnston-Wilson et al. (WO 0144269) disclosed differentially expressed genes in postmortem brain tissue of schizophrenic patients, laying groundwork for molecular profiling. Cochran et al. (EP 1 348 963 A1) identified altered expression of myelin-related genes, suggesting oligodendrocyte dysfunction. WO02400661 described an animal model involving Kir2.3 knockout mice exhibiting behavioral abnormalities, hinting at the channel’s relevance to neuropsychiatric conditions. Dopamine receptor dysfunction, particularly involving the D2 subclass, has long been hypothesized to underlie psychotic symptoms; indeed, all effective antipsychotics antagonize D2 receptors. Horn et al. (U.S. Pat. No. 4,931,270) developed radioligand assays for dopamine receptors but did not apply them to peripheral diagnostics. Subsequent research confirmed expression of neurotransmitters and dopamine receptors—including DRD2, DRD3, and DRD4—in immune cells, notably peripheral blood lymphocytes (PBLs), raising the possibility that peripheral expression mirrors central nervous system status. DRD3 was proposed as a peripheral marker due to its upregulation in schizophrenic PBLs, independent of medication status. Separately, Fuchs’s group reported reduced α7 nicotinic acetylcholine receptor (α7AChR) mRNA in PBLs of schizophrenic patients. However, none of these prior approaches combined DRD2 with Kir2.3 or validated their co-expression in drug-naïve cohorts. The persistent need for reliable, objective, and non-invasive diagnostic tools motivates the search for new molecular markers that offer high sensitivity and specificity, especially in early or prodromal stages of illness.

## BRIEF DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

The invention provides a method for diagnosing schizophrenia in a subject by assessing the expression level of Kir2.3 and/or DRD2 in a biological sample obtained from the subject. The method comprises determining the expression level of Kir2.3 and/or DRD2 and comparing it to a baseline expression level established from healthy control subjects. In a preferred embodiment, the expression level of Kir2.3 is assessed using nucleic acid-based techniques such as quantitative real-time PCR or microarray hybridization. In another preferred embodiment, the expression level of DRD2 is similarly assessed using these molecular methods. A hybridization probe specific for Kir2.3 and/or DRD2 may be employed, wherein the probe is designed to bind selectively to the target mRNA sequence under stringent conditions. A preferred embodiment of such a probe comprises a nucleotide sequence complementary to at least a portion of the coding region of human Kir2.3 (KCNJ4 gene) or DRD2 mRNA. Microarray analysis may be used wherein the biological sample’s nucleic acids are labeled and hybridized to an array containing immobilized probes for Kir2.3 and/or DRD2. Alternatively, PCR methods, particularly quantitative real-time PCR using SYBR Green or TaqMan chemistry, may be employed for precise quantification. Biological samples may include whole blood, serum, plasma, or isolated peripheral blood lymphocytes, with PBLs being the preferred source due to their established expression of neurotransmitter receptors. In a parallel protein-level embodiment, the method involves assessing the protein level of Kir2.3 and/or DRD2 in the biological sample using immunoassays such as ELISA or Western blotting, followed by comparison to a baseline protein level. Preferred embodiments include using monoclonal antibodies specific for Kir2.3 or DRD2 proteins. The invention further encompasses a diagnostic agent comprising a detectably labeled probe or antibody specific for Kir2.3 and/or DRD2, as well as a diagnostic kit containing such agents, along with instructions for use, buffers, and controls for performing the diagnostic assay.

## DEFINITIONS

As used herein, the term “subject” refers to a human individual suspected of having or at risk for schizophrenia, including those exhibiting prodromal symptoms or with a family history of the disorder. A “biological sample” means any specimen obtained from a subject that contains nucleic acids or proteins, including but not limited to whole blood, plasma, serum, saliva, urine, cerebrospinal fluid, or isolated cells such as peripheral blood lymphocytes (PBLs). “Nucleic acid obtained from a biological sample” refers to RNA or DNA extracted or isolated from said sample, typically total RNA enriched for mRNA. An “immune cell” includes lymphocytes, monocytes, and other white blood cells, with PBLs being a primary example. “Isolation” means the physical separation of a component (e.g., RNA, protein) from other cellular constituents, achieved through chemical, enzymatic, or mechanical means. A “probe” is a labeled nucleic acid molecule capable of hybridizing specifically to a complementary target sequence under defined conditions. “Expression level” denotes the quantity of mRNA or protein corresponding to a specific gene product in a given sample, measurable by techniques such as QRT-PCR or immunoassay. “Specific hybridization” occurs when a probe binds to its intended target sequence with sufficient complementarity to distinguish it from non-target sequences under stringent washing conditions. “DRD2” refers to the human dopamine D2 receptor, encoded by the DRD2 gene (GenBank accession NM_000795), including all splice variants. “Kir2.3” refers to the human inwardly rectifying potassium channel 2.3, encoded by the KCNJ4 gene (GenBank accession NM_002240). A “part of a protein or nucleic acid” means a fragment retaining sufficient sequence identity or epitope structure to enable specific detection or binding.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

The present invention is directed to biochemical methods for diagnosing schizophrenia based on the differential expression of two key molecular markers: the dopamine D2 receptor (DRD2) and the inwardly rectifying potassium channel Kir2.3. Both are encoded by human genes—DRD2 (chromosome 11q23) and KCNJ4 (chromosome 2q31–q32)—and play critical roles in neuronal excitability and signal transduction. DRD2 is a G-protein-coupled receptor that inhibits adenylyl cyclase and modulates ion channel activity, while Kir2.3 mediates potassium influx, stabilizing resting membrane potential. Evidence suggests functional coupling between D2-class receptors and inwardly rectifying potassium channels in neurons, where dopamine activation leads to channel modulation, influencing network synchrony and information processing—processes disrupted in schizophrenia. The inventors have discovered that both DRD2 and Kir2.3 are significantly upregulated at the mRNA level in PBLs of drug-naïve schizophrenic patients, indicating a systemic molecular signature of the disease. In one embodiment, the method involves isolating RNA from a biological sample (preferably PBLs), converting it to cDNA, and quantifying DRD2 and/or Kir2.3 transcript levels via QRT-PCR or microarray. The measured expression level is then compared to a baseline—a statistically derived reference range established from age- and sex-matched healthy controls. In another embodiment, protein levels of DRD2 and Kir2.3 are assessed using specific antibodies in immunoassays, with results similarly benchmarked against control baselines. The invention emphasizes combining these novel markers with previously known ones (e.g., DRD3, α7AChR) to enhance diagnostic sensitivity and specificity. The baseline expression level is defined as the mean ± standard deviation of expression in healthy individuals, with thresholds set above the maximum observed in controls (e.g., >2.56-fold for DRD2 by SYBR Green). Quantitative detection methods include real-time PCR with SYBR Green intercalation or TaqMan hydrolysis probes, ensuring high precision. “In situ” expression assays may also be adapted for single-cell resolution. Antibodies for protein detection are produced by immunizing hosts with recombinant Kir2.3 or DRD2 peptides, followed by purification and validation. Antibody binding signals are normalized to housekeeping proteins (e.g., β-actin). Receptor or channel proteins may be isolated via affinity chromatography for functional assays. Activity measurements, such as potassium flux or ligand binding, provide additional diagnostic layers. A threshold value for diagnosis is determined empirically from cohort studies, optimized using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis. The method is validated using samples from confirmed schizophrenic and control subjects, ensuring robustness across demographics.

### EXAMPLES

The following examples illustrate the materials and methods employed to validate the diagnostic utility of DRD2 and Kir2.3 expression in schizophrenia. Patient recruitment occurred at the Department of Psychiatry, University of Szeged, with informed consent obtained per the Declaration of Helsinki. Each participant underwent comprehensive medical examination, including neurological assessment and urine drug screening to exclude substance-induced psychosis. Diagnosis of schizophrenia or schizophreniform psychosis was confirmed using the Mini International Neuropsychiatric Interview Plus (MINI Plus) according to DSM-IV criteria. Psychiatric symptom severity was quantified using the Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale (PANSS), Clinical Global Impression (CGI), and Global Assessment of Functioning (GAF) scales, with ratings performed independently by two trained psychiatrists. Demographic and medical data were collected for 13 drug-naïve or drug-free patients (7 female, 6 male; mean age 34 ± 12 years) and 10 healthy controls (5 female, 5 male; mean age 38 ± 10 years). Peripheral blood lymphocytes (PBLs) were isolated from 10 mL EDTA-anticoagulated blood by lysing erythrocytes with red blood lysis buffer (5 mM MgCl₂, 10 mM NaCl, 10 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.0). Total RNA was extracted using a Macherey-Nagel RNA isolation kit per manufacturer instructions and stored at −80°C with RNase inhibitor. RNA integrity and concentration were verified by agarose gel electrophoresis and NanoDrop spectrophotometry. For microarray analysis, cDNA was prepared from pooled patient and control RNA using the Genisphere Expression Array 350 Detection Kit, labeled, and hybridized to custom human cDNA microarrays containing 3,200 clones. Hybridizations were performed in a Ventana station using FGL2 and Chiphyb buffers, followed by Cy3/Cy5 capture reagent incubation. Slides were washed, dried, and scanned; data were analyzed using established bioinformatics pipelines. Real-time PCR was conducted on individual RNA samples. First-strand cDNA synthesis used SuperScript II reverse transcriptase with random hexamers. QRT-PCR employed either SYBR Green dye (ABGene mix, 150 nM primers) or TaqMan probes (300 nM primers, 250 nM FAM/Dabsyl-labeled probes), run on a RotorGene instrument. Primer and probe sequences were designed with Primer Express software and synthesized by Bioneer, with mass spectrometry confirming purity. Non-template controls ensured absence of genomic DNA contamination. Data were analyzed via the Pfaffl method, normalizing to β-actin or HPRT. Statistical analysis used unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-tests and agglomerative hierarchical clustering (group average linkage, Euclidean distance) in R software. Results showed DRD2 and Kir2.3 mRNA levels were significantly elevated in patients: DRD2 averaged 21.4-fold (SYBR) and 7.8-fold (TaqMan) increases (p < 0.0023), while Kir2.3 showed 22-fold (SYBR) and 8.2-fold (TaqMan) increases (p = 0.0001). Thresholds for positivity were set above the highest control value (e.g., >2.56 for DRD2 by SYBR). Clustering correctly segregated patients from controls, except for one outlier (F5), confirming the markers’ diagnostic power.
