# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNICAL FIELD

The present invention relates generally to the field of robotics and prosthetics, and more specifically to multi-modal tactile sensing systems for use in robotic and prosthetic hands. The invention provides a novel fingertip sensor architecture that integrates multiple sensing modalities—including proximity, contact detection, and force measurement—within a compact, durable, and biologically inspired form factor suitable for integration into prosthetic digits or robotic end-effectors. The technical domain encompasses microelectromechanical systems (MEMS), embedded electronics, elastomeric materials engineering, signal processing, machine learning for sensor fusion, and human-machine interfaces. The invention is particularly directed toward enabling closed-loop sensory feedback in upper limb prostheses by providing rich, real-time tactile information that mimics or exceeds the functional capabilities of natural human fingertips, thereby supporting intuitive control, object manipulation, and sensory restoration through neural interfaces.

## BACKGROUND

Robotic and prosthetic systems have advanced significantly in recent decades, particularly in the design of dexterous multi-articulated hands capable of performing complex grasping and manipulation tasks. Modern prosthetic hands now incorporate up to six independent actuators, achieve closing speeds under one second, and generate grip forces exceeding 100 newtons, enabling users to perform many activities of daily living. Concurrently, feedforward control strategies—especially myoelectric pattern recognition algorithms—have improved user intent decoding, allowing more intuitive and simultaneous control of multiple degrees of freedom. Despite these advances, a critical limitation remains: the absence of effective sensory feedback mechanisms that inform the user about interactions between the prosthesis and the environment. Natural human manipulation relies heavily on somatosensory input, including proprioception (awareness of limb position) and tactile sensation (detection of contact, pressure, slip, and texture). Without such feedback, prosthetic users must rely exclusively on visual monitoring, which is cognitively demanding, slow, and ineffective in low-visibility conditions.

Traditional tactile sensors used in prosthetics—such as force-sensitive resistors (e.g., Tekscan FlexiForce)—offer only limited functionality, typically measuring normal forces within a narrow range (e.g., 0–4 N) and lacking spatial resolution or angular sensitivity. These sensors cannot detect off-center contacts, tangential forces, or pre-contact proximity, all of which are essential for stable and adaptive grasping. In robotics, researchers have explored various tactile sensing approaches, including capacitive arrays, piezoresistive skins, optical waveguides, and strain gauges. While some provide high spatial resolution, they often suffer from fragility, complex wiring, susceptibility to electromagnetic interference, or incompatibility with the constrained volume and power budgets of self-contained prosthetic hands. Moreover, few existing solutions integrate multiple sensing modalities into a single, robust fingertip structure that can be overmolded with soft, skin-like materials without degrading performance.

Object manipulation—particularly in unstructured environments—requires not only knowledge of *whether* contact has occurred but also *where*, *how*, and *with what orientation* the contact is made. During natural grasping, fingertips conform to object geometry, resulting in contact forces that vary in magnitude, location, and angle relative to the fingertip surface. A reliable tactile system must therefore capture this multidimensional interaction data to enable both reflexive adjustments (e.g., preventing slip) and higher-level perceptual inference (e.g., object identification). The inability of conventional sensors to resolve spatial and angular variations in loading severely limits their utility in sensory restoration paradigms, where precise, repeatable, and physiologically relevant feedback is required to interface with neural stimulation systems such as peripheral nerve electrodes. Thus, there exists a pressing need for a multi-modal fingertop sensor that is compact, durable, computationally efficient, and capable of delivering rich tactile information—including proximity, zero-force contact detection, calibrated force measurement, and spatial/angular localization of impact—to support next-generation prosthetic and robotic manipulation.

## SUMMARY

The present invention introduces a multi-modal fingertip sensor designed to overcome the limitations of traditional tactile sensing systems in prosthetics and robotics. This sensor integrates at least two distinct sensing elements within a single fingertip housing: an infrared (IR) proximity sensor and a barometric pressure sensor. Together, these components enable the simultaneous measurement of object proximity, initial contact events (even at near-zero force), and calibrated normal force over a wide dynamic range (e.g., 0–50 N). The IR proximity sensor detects changes in reflected infrared light intensity as an object approaches or contacts the fingertip surface, providing high sensitivity to light touches and pre-contact spatial awareness. The barometric pressure sensor, sealed within an elastomer-overmolded cavity, measures internal air pressure changes caused by deformation of the elastomeric shell during loading, yielding a linear and stable force response at higher loads.

The sensor further includes integrated circuitry for signal conditioning, analog-to-digital conversion, and digital communication. Both sensing elements are equipped with onboard instrumentation amplifiers and analog-to-digital converters, and communicate with a central controller via a standard I²C bus protocol, minimizing wiring complexity and enabling multiplexing of multiple sensors across a prosthetic hand. A microcontroller or dedicated logic circuit manages sensor polling, performs real-time calibration (including temperature compensation for the barometer), computes derived signals such as contact onset, and transmits fused sensory data to higher-level control or neural interface systems.

In some embodiments, the invention includes computer-readable storage media encoded with instructions that, when executed by a processor, cause the system to perform methods for sensor fusion, contact detection, force calibration, and spatial/angular classification of applied loads. These instructions may implement Gaussian process regression for force estimation, support vector machines or convolutional neural networks for supervised classification tasks, and real-time signal filtering and event detection algorithms. The resulting system provides a comprehensive tactile perception suite that supports advanced prosthetic control, sensory restoration, and robotic manipulation.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The fields of robotics and prosthetics continue to advance toward systems that replicate the dexterity and sensory richness of the human hand. However, despite significant progress in actuation and control, most prosthetic and robotic hands remain functionally "numb," lacking the tactile feedback necessary for robust, adaptive interaction with the environment. Traditional tactile sensors—such as force-sensitive resistors, piezoelectric films, or simple strain gauges—suffer from narrow dynamic ranges, poor spatial resolution, fragility, or inability to detect non-normal or off-center forces. These limitations hinder the development of closed-loop control systems and prevent meaningful sensory restoration for amputees using neural interfaces.

To address these shortcomings, the present invention provides a multi-modal fingertip sensor that combines complementary sensing principles to deliver a richer, more informative tactile signal. The core innovation lies in the synergistic integration of an infrared (IR) emitter-detector pair and a microelectromechanical systems (MEMS)-based barometric pressure sensor within a single, compact fingertip assembly. The IR sensor excels at detecting proximity and very light contact forces (down to near-zero newtons), while the barometer provides accurate, linear force measurements at higher loads (up to 50 N or more). By fusing these signals, the system overcomes the individual weaknesses of each modality—such as the IR sensor’s dependence on surface reflectivity or the barometer’s insensitivity to sub-threshold deformations.

This multi-modal sensor is particularly well-suited for integration into prosthetic fingers, where space, weight, and durability are critical constraints. The sensor assembly is embedded within a cavity formed in a 3D-printed finger structure, which is then overmolded with a soft, compliant elastomer (e.g., Dragon Skin 10 silicone). This elastomeric layer serves as both a protective covering and a mechanical transducer that converts external forces into measurable internal pressure changes and surface deformations. The integration of standard I²C communication between the sensors and a central controller ensures reliable, low-noise data transmission with minimal wiring, a crucial feature for multi-digit prosthetic hands.

The synthesis of multi-modal sensory information is achieved through a combination of signal processing and machine learning techniques. Raw data from the IR and barometer sensors are first filtered and segmented into discrete loading-unloading cycles. Gaussian process (GP) regression is then employed to calibrate the combined sensor readings into a continuous, accurate estimate of applied force, achieving R² values exceeding 0.99 in experimental validation. For spatial and angular localization tasks, the problem is framed as supervised classification: given a time-series segment of multi-modal sensor data, the system predicts one of several discrete spatial locations (e.g., center, distal, proximal, medial, lateral) or angular orientations (e.g., 0°, ±20°) of the applied load. Both classical machine learning models (e.g., support vector machines with handcrafted features) and deep learning architectures (e.g., small convolutional neural networks operating on raw sensor data) have been successfully deployed, with SVMs achieving up to 96% accuracy in spatial classification and 89% in angular classification.

The technical effects and advantages of the invention include: (1) extended dynamic range of force sensing through modality fusion; (2) detection of zero-force contact events via IR derivative analysis; (3) robust spatial and angular localization of impacts without dense sensor arrays; (4) compatibility with standard prosthetic manufacturing processes (3D printing, overmolding); (5) low wiring complexity via I²C multiplexing; and (6) real-time operation suitable for closed-loop control. In some embodiments, the sensor is implemented as special-purpose hardware, such as an application-specific integrated circuit (ASIC) or a custom printed circuit board (PCB) with embedded firmware. In other embodiments, the functionality is realized through programmable circuitry, such as a field-programmable gate array (FPGA) or a microcontroller executing stored instructions.

The invention may also be embodied in a non-transitory machine-readable medium storing program code for performing the described methods. Phrases such as “in some embodiments” are used throughout this description to indicate that specific features or configurations are optional and may be combined or omitted without departing from the core inventive concept.

Referring to FIG. 1, a prosthetic hand is depicted with multi-modal tactile sensors integrated into each fingertip. FIG. 3 illustrates a single prosthetic digit containing the sensor assembly within an internal cavity. FIG. 4 shows a portion of a thumb with similar integration. The cavity is precisely formed during 3D printing of the finger substrate to accommodate the PCB-mounted IR and barometer sensors along the midline. After sensor placement, liquid silicone elastomer is vacuum-degassed and poured into a mold surrounding the finger, creating a seamless, durable outer layer.

A logic circuit or microcontroller (e.g., Arduino-based) multiplexes I²C signals from multiple fingers, reducing the number of required communication lines. Firmware running on this controller performs real-time calculations, including IR proximity estimation (using manufacturer-provided algorithms), barometer temperature compensation, and contact event detection via differentiation of the IR signal. A custom LabVIEW program may be used for visualization and data logging during development and calibration.

Experimental characterization was performed using an Instron material testing machine applying calibrated loads (1–50 N) at controlled spatial offsets (±2.5 mm) and angular orientations (0°, ±20°). Data collection involved dynamic loading-unloading cycles, with signals segmented and standardized for machine learning. Sensor fusion via GP regression enabled highly accurate force estimation. Supervised learning models were trained to classify probing direction and impact location, with preprocessing including peak detection, windowing, and normalization.

Flowcharts illustrate key operational sequences: (1) raw pressure and distance data are collected, filtered, calibrated, and converted into force, proximity, and binary contact signals; (2) contact events are detected by computing the derivative of the distance signal and comparing it to a threshold; (3) raw sensor data are communicated to a machine learning engine that outputs total force, impact position, and force angle; and (4) biomimetic responses are generated upon detection of initial contact, potentially triggering neural stimulation patterns.

These capabilities enable applications in shared autonomy, slip prevention, object recognition, and sensory restoration. While limitations exist—such as IR sensitivity to surface properties and GP computational cost—the multi-modality inherently provides redundancy and cross-validation. Future integration with neural interfaces could map calibrated force to naturalistic percepts and proximity to novel sensory channels, significantly enhancing user embodiment and control.
