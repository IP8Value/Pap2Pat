# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

Cancer remains one of the leading causes of morbidity and mortality worldwide, with significant unmet medical needs despite advances in surgical, chemotherapeutic, and radiation-based interventions. The development of effective cancer immunotherapies has emerged as a critical frontier in oncology, aiming to harness the body’s immune system to recognize and eliminate malignant cells. While certain immunotherapeutic strategies have demonstrated clinical benefit, such as immune checkpoint inhibitors and chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T-cell therapies, these approaches are often limited by high costs, complex manufacturing requirements, and variable efficacy across tumor types and patient populations. Dendritic cell (DC)-based vaccines represent a promising avenue for personalized cancer immunotherapy due to their ability to present tumor-associated antigens to naïve T cells and initiate adaptive immune responses. However, current DC vaccine platforms face substantial limitations that hinder their broad clinical applicability.

One major limitation of existing DC-based therapies is their reliance on defined tumor antigens, which restricts their use to cancers with well-characterized immunogenic targets, such as prostate cancer in the case of Sipuleucel-T. This approach is not feasible for tumors lacking known or consistently expressed antigens, including many aggressive subtypes like triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC). Alternative strategies involving neoantigen identification and mRNA delivery to DCs have shown promise but remain resource-intensive, requiring extensive genomic sequencing, bioinformatic prediction, and individualized vaccine formulation—processes that are both time-consuming and costly. Furthermore, approaches utilizing whole tumor lysates or irradiated tumor cells engineered to secrete immunostimulatory cytokines, such as GVAX, suffer from inconsistent antigen presentation, poor control over cytokine dosing, and unintended immunosuppressive effects, including the expansion of myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) that can dampen anti-tumor immunity. These challenges underscore a pressing need for novel therapeutic platforms that can deliver a broad repertoire of tumor antigens in a highly immunogenic format while simultaneously providing controlled, localized immune stimulation without systemic toxicity.

Recent research has highlighted the potential of particulate antigen carriers, particularly those in the size range of 100–1000 nanometers, to enhance uptake by antigen-presenting cells (APCs), including dendritic cells. Such particles mimic natural pathogens and are efficiently internalized via phagocytosis or endocytosis, facilitating robust antigen processing and presentation. Additionally, co-delivery of adjuvants directly on or within these particles can promote DC maturation and cytokine production, thereby bridging innate and adaptive immunity. Despite these insights, no current platform effectively integrates autologous tumor-derived membranes with precisely anchored immunostimulatory molecules in a manner that maximizes both antigen diversity and immune activation while minimizing off-target effects. The invention described herein addresses these gaps by introducing a dendritic cell vaccine platform based on tumor membrane vesicles (TMVs) engineered to display glycosyl phosphatidylinositol (GPI)-anchored immunostimulatory molecules (ISMs), offering a scalable, adaptable, and potent strategy for treating a wide spectrum of cancers, including those without defined target antigens.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides a novel dendritic cell-based cancer therapy that utilizes tumor membrane vesicles (TMVs) as a source of diverse tumor antigens, combined with surface-anchored immunostimulatory molecules to enhance dendritic cell activation and anti-tumor immunity. Specifically, the invention encompasses a method of treating cancer in a subject comprising administering dendritic cells that have been pulsed ex vivo with TMVs incorporating at least one glycosyl phosphatidylinositol (GPI)-anchored immunostimulatory molecule, wherein said molecule comprises a cytokine fused to a GPI anchor domain. In preferred embodiments, the cytokine is granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor (GM-CSF) or interleukin-12 (IL-12), and the TMVs may contain both GPI-GM-CSF and GPI-IL-12 to synergistically enhance dendritic cell uptake, maturation, and cytokine production.

The tumor membrane vesicles are derived from tumor tissue or cultured tumor cells and retain native tumor-associated antigens on their surface, including but not limited to HER2 in HER2-positive cancers. These vesicles typically range from 200 to 600 nanometers in diameter, a size optimized for efficient internalization by dendritic cells. The GPI anchor enables stable incorporation of the immunostimulatory cytokine onto the outer leaflet of the TMV lipid bilayer without requiring genetic modification of the tumor cells, thereby preserving the native antigenic profile while adding potent adjuvant activity. The dendritic cells used in the therapy may be autologous or allogeneic and are preferably bone marrow-derived dendritic cells (BMDCs) generated in the presence of GM-CSF.

The invention further includes various embodiments of the TMVs, such as those prepared from frozen tumor biopsies, primary tumor resections, or established tumor cell lines, making the platform applicable even when only limited tumor material is available. The method is particularly suited for treating carcinomas, including breast cancer (both HER2-positive and triple-negative subtypes), prostate cancer, lung cancer, melanoma, and head-and-neck cancers. Moreover, the dendritic cell vaccine may be administered alone or in combination with other therapeutic modalities, such as chemotherapy, radiation, immune checkpoint inhibitors, or targeted small-molecule agents, to achieve enhanced anti-tumor efficacy. This combinatorial approach leverages the ability of the DC vaccine to prime tumor-specific T cells while adjunctive therapies modulate the tumor microenvironment to support immune cell infiltration and function.

### Tumor Membrane Vesicles (TMVs)

Tumor membrane vesicles (TMVs) are nano-scale particles derived from the plasma membranes of tumor cells, designed to serve as a comprehensive source of tumor-associated antigens for dendritic cell-based immunotherapy. These vesicles retain the native lipid bilayer architecture and surface protein composition of the parent tumor cells, thereby presenting a broad array of potential T-cell epitopes without the need for prior antigen identification. TMVs typically exhibit a diameter ranging from approximately 200 to 600 nanometers, a size range that has been empirically demonstrated to facilitate efficient uptake by dendritic cells and other antigen-presenting cells through phagocytic and endocytic pathways. The composition of TMVs includes integral and peripheral membrane proteins, glycolipids, cholesterol, and phospholipids, all of which contribute to their structural integrity and biological functionality.

A key innovation of the present invention lies in the incorporation of glycosyl phosphatidylinositol (GPI)-anchored immunostimulatory molecules (ISMs) onto the surface of TMVs via a protein transfer methodology. GPI anchoring allows for the stable insertion of recombinant cytokines or co-stimulatory molecules into the outer leaflet of the TMV membrane without disrupting its native antigenic landscape. This is achieved by fusing the immunostimulatory protein—such as granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor (GM-CSF) or interleukin-12 (IL-12)—to a C-terminal GPI attachment signal sequence, which directs post-translational modification and membrane anchoring during recombinant expression in eukaryotic cells. The purified GPI-anchored protein is then incubated with pre-formed TMVs under controlled conditions, enabling spontaneous insertion into the lipid bilayer through hydrophobic interactions mediated by the GPI moiety.

The resulting cytokine-adjuvanted TMVs function as integrated vaccine platforms that simultaneously deliver tumor antigens and localized immune signals to dendritic cells. Upon pulsing, dendritic cells internalize these vesicles, leading to enhanced antigen processing and presentation via both MHC class I and II pathways. Critically, the surface-displayed GPI-GM-CSF promotes dendritic cell maturation, as evidenced by upregulated expression of co-stimulatory molecules such as CD86 and MHC-II, while GPI-IL-12 drives the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines like interferon-gamma (IFN-γ) and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), thereby polarizing T-cell responses toward a Th1 phenotype conducive to anti-tumor immunity. Additional immunostimulatory agents, such as GPI-anchored B7-1 (CD80) or B7-2 (CD86), may also be incorporated to provide direct co-stimulation to T cells.

The TMV-based vaccine approach is particularly advantageous for cancers lacking defined target antigens, such as triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC), as it leverages the entire repertoire of tumor membrane proteins as potential immunogens. For antigen-defined cancers like HER2-positive breast cancer, TMVs derived from HER2-expressing tumor cells naturally present HER2 on their surface, enabling antigen-specific immune targeting. TMVs are prepared through mechanical and biochemical disruption of tumor tissue or cell pellets, followed by differential and density gradient centrifugation to isolate plasma membrane fractions. This process preserves membrane integrity while excluding cytosolic contaminants, although controlled inclusion of cytoplasmic proteins and RNA may further enhance immunogenicity by engaging intracellular pattern recognition receptors.

In therapeutic application, dendritic cells—preferably autologous bone marrow-derived dendritic cells—are pulsed ex vivo with TMVs containing GPI-ISMs, then administered to the subject via subcutaneous, intradermal, or intranodal routes. This strategy has been shown in murine models to inhibit primary tumor growth, reduce metastatic burden (particularly in the lungs in the 4T1 TNBC model), and increase infiltration of CD4+ and CD8+ T cells as well as MHC-II+ CD11c+ dendritic cells into the tumor microenvironment. The platform is compatible with combination regimens involving chemotherapy, radiation, or immune checkpoint blockade, offering a versatile and potent modality for cancer immunotherapy across diverse malignancies.

### TMV Preparation, Characterization, and GPI-ISM Incorporation

Tumor membrane vesicles (TMVs) are prepared from either fresh or frozen tumor tissue specimens or from cultured tumor cell pellets, enabling flexibility in sourcing material even from limited biopsy samples. The preparation process begins with mechanical homogenization of the tumor tissue using a Dounce homogenizer or similar device to disrupt cellular architecture while preserving membrane integrity. Alternatively, cultured tumor cells are harvested and subjected to repeated cycles of freezing and thawing to induce cell lysis. The resulting homogenate or lysate is then subjected to differential centrifugation to remove nuclei, unbroken cells, and large debris. The crude membrane fraction is further purified by ultracentrifugation through a sucrose density gradient, typically at 41% w/w sucrose, which separates plasma membrane-derived vesicles from organelle membranes and soluble proteins based on buoyant density. The TMV-enriched fraction appears as a distinct lipid ring at the interface, which is collected and washed extensively in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) to remove residual sucrose.

Following isolation, TMVs are characterized for size distribution and polydispersity using dynamic light scattering instruments such as the Zetasizer Nano ZS, which confirms that the vesicles fall within the optimal 200–600 nm range for dendritic cell uptake. Surface antigen expression, including tumor-specific markers like HER2, is verified by flow cytometry using fluorophore-conjugated antibodies. To incorporate glycosyl phosphatidylinositol-anchored immunostimulatory molecules (GPI-ISMs), purified recombinant GPI-GM-CSF and/or GPI-IL-12 proteins are mixed with TMVs at a defined mass ratio—typically 25 µg of GPI-ISM per milligram of TMV protein—and incubated at 37°C for four hours under constant rotation. This facilitates spontaneous insertion of the GPI anchor into the outer leaflet of the TMV lipid bilayer. Unbound GPI-ISMs are removed by ultracentrifugation and PBS washes, and successful incorporation is confirmed by flow cytometry using cytokine-specific antibodies. The resulting cytokine-adjuvanted TMVs are stable, sterile-filtered if necessary, and ready for use in dendritic cell pulsing experiments or therapeutic administration.

### TMVs Containing the Tumor Antigen HER2 and Protein Transferred GPI-ISMs on Their Surface

Characterization of tumor membrane vesicles (TMVs) derived from HER2-positive tumor cells, such as the D2F2/E2 murine breast cancer line, confirms the retention of the HER2 tumor antigen on the vesicle surface. Flow cytometric analysis demonstrates high-level HER2 expression on both parental D2F2/E2 cells and their corresponding TMVs, indicating that the vesicle preparation process preserves native membrane protein topology. Concurrently, TMVs prepared without GPI-anchored immunostimulatory molecules (plain TMVs) show no detectable surface expression of GM-CSF or IL-12. In contrast, TMVs subjected to protein transfer with recombinant GPI-GM-CSF and GPI-IL-12 exhibit robust surface staining for both cytokines, confirming successful incorporation. Size analysis via Zetasizer reveals a mean particle diameter of approximately 200 nm with a polydispersity index of 0.31, consistent with monodisperse nanoparticle formulations suitable for efficient dendritic cell internalization. These data collectively validate that the engineered TMVs present both tumor-specific antigens and exogenous immunostimulatory signals in a particulate format optimized for immune recognition.

### Uptake of TMVs by DCs is Potentiated by GPI-ISMs

The uptake of tumor membrane vesicles (TMVs) by dendritic cells (DCs) is significantly enhanced when the vesicles are surface-modified with glycosyl phosphatidylinositol-anchored immunostimulatory molecules (GPI-ISMs). To assess internalization, TMVs are labeled with pHrodo Deep Red, a pH-sensitive fluorescent dye that exhibits minimal fluorescence at neutral pH but becomes brightly fluorescent in the acidic environment of phagolysosomes, thereby specifically marking internalized vesicles. Bone marrow-derived dendritic cells (BMDCs) are incubated with labeled plain TMVs or TMVs incorporating GPI-GM-CSF, GPI-IL-12, or both. Flow cytometry analysis at 30, 90, and 180 minutes post-pulsing reveals that while plain TMVs are internalized to a baseline extent, TMVs bearing GPI-GM-CSF induce the highest percentage of pHrodo-positive DCs, followed by those containing both GPI-ISMs and GPI-IL-12 alone. Quantification of median fluorescence intensity (MFI) further demonstrates that DCs pulsed with dual-GPI-ISM TMVs exhibit the greatest amount of internalized material per cell. These findings indicate that GPI-anchored cytokines, particularly GM-CSF, act as functional ligands that engage receptors on DCs to potentiate phagocytic uptake, thereby increasing antigen load and subsequent immune activation.

### DC Vaccine Therapy Reduces Tumor Growth

The therapeutic efficacy of dendritic cells pulsed with cytokine-adjuvanted tumor membrane vesicles (DC vaccine) was evaluated in a HER2-positive murine breast cancer model using D2F2/E2 tumor cells. Mice bearing established subcutaneous tumors were treated with a single dose of 1 × 10⁶ DCs administered subcutaneously in the contralateral flank seven days post-tumor inoculation. Control groups received phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), unpulsed DCs, or DCs pulsed with plain TMVs lacking GPI-ISMs. Only mice treated with the DC vaccine—comprising DCs pulsed with TMVs incorporating both GPI-GM-CSF and GPI-IL-12—exhibited significant inhibition of tumor growth compared to all control groups. Tumor volume measurements over time revealed sustained suppression in the DC vaccine group, whereas control tumors progressed rapidly. Survival analysis demonstrated a marked extension in median survival from 35 days in control groups to 57 days in the DC vaccine group, representing a statistically significant improvement. These results establish that the co-delivery of tumor antigens and GPI-anchored cytokines via DCs is essential for generating a potent anti-tumor response in an antigen-defined cancer model.

### DC Vaccine Inhibits 4T1 Tumor Growth, Reduces Lung Metastasis, and Improved Immune Profile of Tumors

The versatility of the DC vaccine platform was further demonstrated in the highly aggressive and metastatic 4T1 triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) model, which lacks a defined tumor-associated antigen. Mice inoculated with 4T1 cells and treated with DCs pulsed with 4T1-derived TMVs containing both GPI-GM-CSF and GPI-IL-12 showed significant inhibition of primary tumor growth compared to PBS, unpulsed DC, or plain TMV-pulsed DC controls. Given the spontaneous metastatic nature of 4T1 tumors, lung metastasis was quantified 20 days post-inoculation by digesting lung tissue with collagenase IV, selecting for tumor cells using 6-thioguanine-containing media, and counting viable colonies. The DC vaccine group exhibited a two- to three-fold reduction in metastatic burden relative to controls. Additionally, flow cytometric analysis of tumor-infiltrating leukocytes revealed a two-fold increase in MHC-II⁺ CD11c⁺ dendritic cells and significant elevations in both CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ T-cell populations within the tumor microenvironment. These findings confirm that the DC vaccine not only controls primary tumor growth but also mitigates metastasis and reshapes the immune landscape toward a more favorable anti-tumor state, even in the absence of a known target antigen.

### Bone Marrow-Derived Dendritic Cell (BMDC) Production

Bone marrow-derived dendritic cells (BMDCs) used in the disclosed methods are generated from the femurs of female BALB/c mice aged 6–8 weeks. Following euthanasia, femurs are aseptically removed, cleared of muscle tissue, and flushed with RPMI-1640 medium using a 22-gauge needle to harvest bone marrow cells. Red blood cells are lysed using ammonium-chloride-potassium (ACK) lysis buffer, and the remaining nucleated cells are resuspended in complete RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum, L-glutamine, penicillin-streptomycin, and 20 ng/mL recombinant murine GM-CSF (rmGM-CSF). Cells are plated at a density of 2 × 10⁵ cells/mL and cultured at 37°C in a humidified 5% CO₂ incubator. rmGM-CSF is replenished on days 3, 6, and 8 to support DC differentiation and survival. By day 10, non-adherent and loosely adherent cells exhibiting typical dendritic morphology are harvested, washed, and used for TMV uptake assays, pulsing experiments, or therapeutic administration. This protocol yields a homogeneous population of immature BMDCs suitable for antigen loading and subsequent in vivo application.

### 4T1 Lung Metastasis Assay

Quantification of lung metastasis in the 4T1 murine breast cancer model is performed by harvesting lungs from tumor-bearing mice 20 days after subcutaneous inoculation with 5 × 10⁴ 4T1 cells. Lungs are processed under sterile conditions: minced into small fragments, digested in 1 mg/mL collagenase IV for two hours at 37°C with gentle rotation, and passed through a 70-µm cell strainer to generate a single-cell suspension. The suspension is washed and resuspended in complete DMEM selection medium containing 60 µM 6-thioguanine (6-TG), which selectively kills non-tumor lung fibroblasts while sparing 4T1 tumor cells that express hypoxanthine-guanine phosphoribosyltransferase (HGPRT) and are thus resistant to 6-TG. Cells are plated in multi-well plates and cultured until tumor colonies become visible. Once confluency is reached in at least one well, all wells are trypsinized, and viable tumor cells are counted using an automated cell counter such as the Cellometer T4. This method provides a quantitative measure of metastatic burden and enables comparison of therapeutic efficacy across experimental groups.
