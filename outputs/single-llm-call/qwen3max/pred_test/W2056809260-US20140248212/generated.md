# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

Gene and cell-based therapies represent a transformative frontier in modern medicine, offering the potential to treat or even cure diseases that are refractory to conventional pharmacological interventions. These therapeutic modalities involve the delivery of functional genes or living cells—engineered to perform specific biological functions—into patients to correct genetic defects, modulate immune responses, or replace damaged tissues. Despite their promise, the clinical translation of such therapies has been hindered by significant challenges, particularly the inability to non-invasively monitor the fate, distribution, viability, and functional activity of therapeutic genes and cells after administration. Conventional methods for assessing therapeutic cell engraftment or gene expression rely heavily on invasive biopsies, which are not only limited by sampling error but also pose risks to patient safety and preclude longitudinal monitoring.

This limitation underscores a critical unmet need for robust, non-invasive imaging technologies capable of providing whole-body, quantitative, and serial assessments of therapeutic cell kinetics and transgene expression. Positron emission tomography (PET) reporter gene imaging has emerged as a powerful solution to this problem. In this approach, therapeutic cells are engineered to co-express a therapeutic transgene along with a PET reporter gene (PRG). The PRG encodes a protein that selectively accumulates or metabolically traps a radiolabeled PET reporter probe (PRP), enabling detection via PET imaging. This strategy allows clinicians and researchers to track the location, magnitude, and duration of therapeutic cell activity without repeated surgical interventions.

Conventional PET reporter gene systems have largely relied on viral enzymes, most notably Herpes Simplex Virus type 1 thymidine kinase (HSV1-tk) and its optimized mutant HSV1-sr39tk. These PRGs phosphorylate nucleoside analogs such as 18F-labeled fluoroganciclovir (FGCV), 9-[4-18F-3-(hydroxymethyl)butyl]guanine (18F-FHBG), 2′-deoxy-2′-18F-5-ethyl-1-β-D-arabinofuranosyluracil (18F-FEAU), and 2′-deoxy-2′-18F-5-iodo-1-β-D-arabinofuranosyluracil (18F-FIAU). Among these, the HSV1-tk/18F-FHBG system has been successfully used in human clinical trials, demonstrating proof-of-concept for non-invasive cell tracking. However, a major drawback of HSV1-tk is its immunogenicity. As a foreign viral protein, HSV1-tk can elicit cytotoxic T-cell responses that lead to the immune-mediated destruction of the very therapeutic cells intended for long-term engraftment—a phenomenon documented in multiple clinical studies.

To circumvent this issue, researchers have explored “second-generation” PRGs derived from human genes. One such candidate is the human norepinephrine transporter (hNET), which mediates cellular uptake of radiolabeled meta-iodobenzylguanidine (MIBG). While hNET is non-immunogenic, its sensitivity is significantly lower than that of HSV1-tk due to the absence of enzymatic signal amplification. Another promising avenue involves human nucleoside kinases, such as deoxycytidine kinase (dCK) and mitochondrial thymidine kinase 2 (TK2). A double mutant of dCK (dCK-AA) and a truncated form of TK2 (lacking the mitochondrial targeting sequence) have been developed as PRGs for 18F-FEAU. Although these human-derived systems reduce immunogenic risk, they often retain substantial activity toward endogenous nucleosides like thymidine and deoxycytidine, potentially perturbing intracellular nucleotide pools and raising concerns about genotoxicity.

Human mitochondrial thymidine kinase 2 (htk2) presents a compelling alternative. Naturally localized to mitochondria, htk2 phosphorylates thymidine and deoxycytidine as part of the mitochondrial nucleotide salvage pathway. When truncated to remove its N-terminal mitochondrial targeting signal, htk2 becomes cytosolic and accessible to systemically administered nucleoside analogs. Importantly, htk2 exhibits low inherent immunogenicity due to its human origin. However, wild-type htk2 suffers from strong feedback inhibition by thymidine triphosphate (dTTP), which fluctuates during the cell cycle and can suppress PRP phosphorylation, thereby reducing imaging sensitivity. Moreover, its native substrate specificity limits efficient phosphorylation of non-natural L-enantiomer probes such as L-[18F]FMAU.

These limitations highlight the need for novel PET reporter gene/reporter probe systems that combine high sensitivity, minimal immunogenicity, metabolic inertness, and compatibility with favorable biodistribution profiles. The present invention addresses this need through the rational engineering of an enhanced human thymidine kinase 2 variant—specifically, the TK2-N93D/L109F double mutant—that exhibits reduced feedback inhibition, diminished activity toward endogenous nucleosides, and markedly increased catalytic efficiency for L-configured thymidine analogs like L-[18F]FMAU. This engineered PRG, paired with its optimized PRP, constitutes a next-generation platform for safe, sensitive, and clinically translatable molecular imaging of cell and gene therapies.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides novel systems, materials, and methods for non-invasive imaging of mammalian cells using positron emission tomography (PET) or single-photon emission computed tomography (SPECT). Central to the invention is a highly sensitive and non-immunogenic reporter gene derived from human mitochondrial thymidine kinase 2 (htk2), engineered to overcome the limitations of prior art reporter systems. Specifically, the invention features an enhanced version of htk2—designated TK2-N93D/L109F—comprising amino acid substitutions at positions 93 (asparagine to aspartate) and 109 (leucine to phenylalanine) relative to the full-length human TK2 sequence. This mutant polypeptide exhibits reduced susceptibility to thymidine triphosphate (dTTP)-mediated feedback inhibition, decreased phosphorylation of endogenous nucleosides (thymidine and deoxycytidine), and significantly enhanced catalytic activity toward non-natural L-enantiomer thymidine analogs, particularly 2-deoxy-2-[18F]-5-methyl-1-β-L-arabinofuranosyluracil (L-[18F]FMAU).

The invention further encompasses novel radiolabeled probes, including L- and D-enantiomers of fluorinated thymidine analogs such as L-[18F]FMAU and L-[18F]FEAU, which serve as PET reporter probes (PRPs) specifically trapped by the engineered TK2 polypeptide. These probes enable whole-body molecular imaging applications with improved signal-to-noise ratios due to favorable biodistribution profiles—characterized by low background accumulation in non-target tissues and efficient renal and hepatobiliary clearance.

Embodiments of the invention include nucleic acid molecules encoding the human thymidine kinase 2 polypeptide with the N93D and L109F substitutions, optionally further comprising a deletion of the first 50 amino acids to eliminate mitochondrial localization. These nucleic acids may be incorporated into expression vectors suitable for delivery into therapeutic cells via viral (e.g., lentiviral, retroviral, adenoviral) or non-viral (e.g., plasmid, transposon-based) methods. Host cells—such as T lymphocytes, stem cells, or tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes—transfected or transduced with such vectors express the engineered TK2 reporter gene and can be tracked in vivo following administration of the corresponding radiolabeled probe.

The invention also provides kits for imaging mammalian cells using PET or SPECT, comprising the engineered TK2 expression vector, instructions for use, and optionally the radiolabeled probe or precursors for its synthesis. These kits facilitate standardized, reproducible cell labeling and imaging across preclinical and clinical settings.

A key advantage of the invention is its biological inertness: the TK2-N93D/L109F polypeptide exhibits reduced activity toward endogenous nucleosides, minimizing perturbations to cellular nucleotide pools and lowering the risk of genotoxicity—critical considerations for long-term therapeutic cell persistence. Additionally, because the reporter gene is of human origin, it avoids the immune rejection associated with viral PRGs like HSV1-tk, thereby preserving therapeutic cell viability.

Applications of the invention span monitoring adoptive T-cell therapies, tracking stem cell engraftment, evaluating oncolytic virotherapy, and assessing gene therapy vector biodistribution. The system is compatible with clinical PET/CT and PET/MRI platforms and supports quantitative estimation of therapeutic cell numbers in vivo. Furthermore, the same reporter gene can be co-expressed with a “safety switch” gene (e.g., inducible caspase) to enable ablation of malfunctioning cells if adverse events occur.

In summary, the invention introduces a human-derived, metabolically benign, and highly sensitive PET reporter system that overcomes the immunogenicity and metabolic interference issues of existing technologies, thereby enabling safer and more reliable longitudinal monitoring of advanced cell and gene therapies.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

Gene therapy refers to the introduction of genetic material into a patient’s cells to treat or prevent disease. Therapeutic genes (TGs) encode proteins that correct defective pathways, kill malignant cells, or modulate immune responses. Similarly, therapeutic cells (TCs)—such as chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T cells, mesenchymal stem cells, or induced pluripotent stem cell (iPSC)-derived lineages—are administered to restore function or target pathology. A major challenge in both approaches is the inability to non-invasively monitor gene expression kinetics or cell trafficking post-administration. Conventional treatments like chemotherapy or radiation lack precision and cause systemic toxicity, whereas gene and cell therapies offer targeted action but require verification of delivery, persistence, and function.

PET reporter gene imaging addresses this gap by enabling real-time, whole-body visualization of TG or TC activity. In this paradigm, therapeutic cells are engineered to express a PET reporter gene (PRG) alongside the therapeutic transgene. The PRG encodes a protein that interacts with a systemically administered PET reporter probe (PRP)—a small molecule labeled with a positron-emitting radionuclide such as fluorine-18 ([18F]). Upon entering PRG-expressing cells, the PRP is metabolically trapped (e.g., via phosphorylation), leading to its accumulation and detectable signal on PET scans.

The present invention centers on a PRG-PRP system wherein the PRG is a mutated human thymidine kinase 2 (TK2) polypeptide and the PRP is a radiolabeled L-enantiomer thymidine analog, preferably L-[18F]FMAU. Wild-type TK2 is a mitochondrial enzyme involved in the salvage pathway for pyrimidine nucleotides. However, for use as a PRG, the mitochondrial targeting sequence (first 50 amino acids) is deleted, resulting in cytosolic localization and accessibility to circulating probes. Further, two point mutations—N93D and L109F—are introduced to enhance performance.

The TK2-N93D/L109F mutant was developed through structure-guided enzyme engineering. The N93D substitution reduces feedback inhibition by dTTP by destabilizing the closed, active conformation of the enzyme, thereby maintaining activity even during S-phase when dTTP levels peak. The L109F mutation, located near the nucleoside-binding pocket, increases catalytic turnover (kcat) for L-configured analogs while decreasing affinity for natural D-thymidine. Together, these mutations yield a kinase that is orthogonal to wild-type TK2: it phosphorylates L-[18F]FMAU efficiently but shows reduced activity toward endogenous thymidine and deoxycytidine, minimizing metabolic interference.

The invention includes the human thymidine kinase 2 polypeptide comprising amino acid substitutions N93D and L109F, with or without the N-terminal truncation (Δ1–50). Also provided is a nucleic acid molecule encoding this polypeptide, operably linked to a promoter (e.g., CMV, EF1α, or tissue-specific promoters) in an expression vector. Suitable vectors include retroviral, lentiviral, adenoviral, adeno-associated viral (AAV), or non-viral plasmids. Host cells—primary human T cells, hematopoietic stem cells, or immortalized lines—can be transfected or transduced to stably express the reporter gene.

Compositions of matter encompass non-naturally occurring analogs of thymidine, particularly L-enantiomers such as L-[18F]FMAU, L-[18F]FEAU, and their D-counterparts, labeled with [18F], [124I], or other PET/SPECT isotopes. These probes are substrates for the engineered TK2 but not significantly metabolized by endogenous kinases, ensuring specificity.

The imaging system comprises: (1) a mammalian cell expressing the TK2-N93D/L109F reporter gene; (2) a radiolabeled PRP (e.g., L-[18F]FMAU); and (3) a PET or SPECT scanner. The method involves introducing the reporter gene into target cells, administering the PRP intravenously, allowing uptake and phosphorylation, and detecting the accumulated radioactivity via tomographic imaging.

Kits for implementation include the expression vector, packaging plasmids (for viral systems), transfection reagents, and optionally the PRP or synthesis modules. The TK2 polypeptide may also be fused to heterologous sequences (e.g., fluorescent proteins) for multimodal imaging.

Preclinical data demonstrate that TK2-N93D/L109F-expressing tumors accumulate L-[18F]FMAU at levels comparable to HSV1-sr39tk-expressing tumors imaged with [18F]FHBG, confirming high sensitivity. Biodistribution studies in mice and humans show favorable clearance, though human myocardial and hepatic uptake necessitates probe optimization for certain applications. Radiation dosimetry indicates acceptable exposure levels for clinical use.

Applications include monitoring CAR-T cell trafficking in oncology, tracking neural stem cells in neurodegenerative disease, and evaluating gene therapy vector spread. The system’s human origin ensures low immunogenicity, while its metabolic inertness supports long-term safety—key advantages for regulatory approval and clinical adoption.

## EXAMPLES

### Example 1

PET reporter probe (PRP) systems rely on the specific interaction between a PET reporter gene (PRG) and its cognate probe to enable non-invasive imaging. Traditional PRG-PRP pairs, such as HSV1-tk with [18F]FHBG, suffer from immunogenicity and suboptimal biodistribution. To address these issues, we evaluated L-[18F]FMAU and L-[18F]FEAU as candidate PRPs due to their L-configuration, which is poorly recognized by endogenous human nucleoside kinases, potentially reducing background signal. In vitro kinase assays confirmed that wild-type human TK2 phosphorylates L-FMAU, albeit with low efficiency. Cell culture studies in L1210 murine leukemia cells transduced with TK2 variants showed that L-[18F]FMAU uptake correlated with PRG expression. In vivo microPET imaging in tumor-bearing mice demonstrated specific accumulation of L-[18F]FMAU in TK2-expressing xenografts.

To mitigate potential immunogenicity of the hTK2-N93D PRG, we analyzed MHC class I epitopes presented by human cells expressing the mutant kinase. Using predictive algorithms, we identified HLA-A2-restricted epitopes and designed additional mutations (e.g., surface residue substitutions) to abolish binding without compromising enzymatic function. We also evaluated other human nucleoside kinases—such as thymidine kinase 1 (TK1) and deoxyguanosine kinase—as alternative PRGs, but none matched the substrate flexibility and low endogenous competition of TK2.

Motivated by TK2’s ability to phosphorylate both D- and L-nucleosides, we truncated its mitochondrial signal peptide (residues 1–50), yielding a cytosolic enzyme accessible to systemic probes. Introducing the N93D mutation reduced dTTP feedback inhibition: in kinase assays, WT TK2 activity dropped by 55% at 100 µM dTTP, whereas TK2-N93D declined by less than 5%. Catalytic activity toward L-[18F]FMAU increased 1.5-fold in cell uptake assays and nearly 2-fold in vivo. Comparative imaging showed TK2-N93D/L1210 tumors accumulated significantly more L-[18F]FMAU than WT TK2 tumors, validating the engineering strategy.

### Example 2

Preclinical studies were conducted in two models: a hepatic colorectal cancer (CRC) metastasis model and a melanoma immunotherapy model. In the CRC model, human LS174T cells were injected into nu/nu mice to establish liver metastases. An adenoviral vector (Ad.CMV) carrying the TK2-N93D/L109F PRG under a Cox2 promoter—active in inflamed/tumor microenvironments—was delivered intravenously. Tumor burden was monitored by optical imaging, and PRP ([18F]FMAU) was administered before microPET/CT scanning. PRG expression was confirmed by PCR and enzyme activity assays, showing selective activation in metastatic lesions.

In the Pmel-1/B16 melanoma model, adoptive transfer of T cells transduced with TK2-N93D/L109F enabled tracking of T-cell infiltration into tumors. Mice received CD8+ T cells expressing the PRG, followed by L-[18F]FMAU injection and PET imaging. Signal intensity correlated with anti-tumor efficacy, demonstrating utility in immunotherapy monitoring. Potential caveats include variable transduction efficiency and probe metabolism in different tissues; alternative approaches involve using stronger promoters or dual-reporter systems.

### Example 3

Commercialization efforts focus on developing kits for preclinical and clinical cell labeling. Leveraging the ELIXYS automated radiochemistry platform, we established GMP-compliant production of L-[18F]FMAU. Lentiviral vectors encoding TK2-N93D/L109F were produced at high titer for efficient transduction of human therapeutic cells (e.g., CD8+ T cells, hMSCs). Non-viral delivery kits based on ΦC31 integrase-mediated genomic integration were also developed for transient or stable expression.

Conversion factors—relating PET signal intensity to cell number—were determined by injecting known quantities of labeled mouse CD8+ T cells or hMSCs into colon cancer xenografts, followed by L-[18F]FMAU PET/CT. PRG expression per cell was quantified by flow cytometry (using co-expressed YFP) and correlated with radiotracer uptake. These calibration curves enable absolute cell quantification in vivo, a critical feature for dose-response studies.

### Example 4

An exploratory Investigational New Drug (eIND) submission was prepared based on comprehensive preclinical data. Biodistribution and dosimetry studies in rodents and non-human primates confirmed low toxicity and acceptable radiation exposure. Chemistry, manufacturing, and controls (CMC) documentation detailed vector production, probe synthesis, and quality testing. Contingency plans included backup probes (e.g., L-[18F]FEAU) and alternative PRG mutants if immunogenicity emerged. Whole-body PET/CT in a healthy volunteer showed L-[18F]FMAU accumulation in liver, kidneys, and myocardium, informing future protocol adjustments.

### Example 5

Gene and cell therapies demand precise pharmacokinetic monitoring. We developed L-[18F]FMAU as a novel PRP and evaluated its biodistribution in mice and humans. Rational mutagenesis of human TK2 yielded the N93D mutant with reduced dTTP sensitivity and enhanced L-FMAU phosphorylation. Conclusions affirm L-[18F]FMAU as a viable PRP when paired with engineered TK2, supporting clinical translation.

### Example 6

Current cell therapies lack real-time monitoring tools. We developed the TK2-N93D/L109F PRG-PRP system to overcome HSV1-tk immunogenicity. Biodistribution of L-[18F]FMAU in mice showed low GI uptake versus [18F]FHBG. Structure-guided engineering generated the double mutant, which exhibited 2.6-fold higher sensitivity than TK2-N93D and matched HSV1-sr39tk performance. Human studies revealed myocardial uptake, prompting probe modifications. The system meets key PRG criteria: sensitivity, specificity, inertness, and human origin.

### Example 7

L-2-O-[(Trifluoromethyl)sulfonyl]-1,3,5-tri-O-benzoyl was prepared as a precursor. No-carrier-added [18F]-fluoride was trapped on an anion exchange cartridge, released with K2.2.2./K2CO3, and reacted with the triflate precursor at 85°C for 10 min. The crude product was purified by HPLC, deprotected, and formulated in saline for injection.

### Example 8

Ideal PET probes are metabolically stable, rapidly cleared from non-target tissues, and efficiently trapped by the PRG. L-[18F]FMAU meets these criteria: it is a poor substrate for endogenous kinases, shows favorable murine biodistribution, and is phosphorylated selectively by TK2-N93D/L109F. Human studies support its potential, pending optimization for cardiac uptake.
