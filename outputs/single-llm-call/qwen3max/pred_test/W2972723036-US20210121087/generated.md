# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD

The present invention relates generally to the field of medical diagnostics and monitoring, particularly to systems and methods for noninvasive estimation of intracranial pressure (ICP) in human subjects. More specifically, the invention pertains to a computational framework that leverages physiological models of cerebral hemodynamics in conjunction with Bayesian statistical inference to estimate both mean intracranial pressure and intracranial pressure pulse amplitude from minimally invasive or noninvasive measurements of arterial blood pressure and cerebral blood flow velocity. The technology is designed for use in clinical settings such as neurocritical care units, operating rooms, emergency departments, and ambulatory environments where continuous, real-time monitoring of intracranial dynamics is required without the risks associated with invasive transducer placement. The invention further encompasses software implementations, computer-readable media, and integrated hardware-software systems capable of executing the described estimation algorithms on standard computing platforms, thereby enabling widespread deployment across diverse healthcare infrastructures.

## BACKGROUND

Intracranial pressure (ICP) is a critical physiological parameter that reflects the pressure exerted by the brain, cerebrospinal fluid, and blood within the rigid confines of the skull. Normal ICP values in adults typically range from 7 to 15 mmHg, while values exceeding 20–25 mmHg are considered pathological and may indicate life-threatening conditions such as traumatic brain injury (TBI), hydrocephalus, intracranial hemorrhage, or malignant cerebral edema. Sustained elevation of ICP can compromise cerebral perfusion, leading to ischemic brain damage, herniation syndromes, and death if not promptly diagnosed and managed. Consequently, accurate and continuous monitoring of ICP is essential for guiding therapeutic interventions, assessing treatment efficacy, and predicting neurological outcomes in critically ill patients.

Despite its clinical importance, current gold-standard methods for ICP measurement remain highly invasive. These include external ventricular drains (EVDs), which involve insertion of a catheter into the lateral ventricle, and intraparenchymal microtransducers such as Codman or Camino sensors implanted directly into brain tissue. While these techniques provide high-fidelity, real-time ICP data, they carry significant risks, including intracranial hemorrhage (occurring in up to 1–2% of cases), infection (with reported rates of 5–10% for EVDs), and mechanical complications such as catheter occlusion or malposition. Moreover, due to these risks, invasive ICP monitoring is typically reserved for patients with severe neurological impairment, thereby excluding a large population of individuals who might benefit from early detection of elevated ICP but do not meet stringent criteria for invasive intervention.

Noninvasive alternatives have long been sought, yet existing approaches suffer from substantial limitations. Techniques based on transcranial Doppler ultrasound, optic nerve sheath diameter measurement, or tympanic membrane displacement lack sufficient accuracy, reproducibility, or temporal resolution for continuous monitoring. Other methods attempt to correlate ICP with surrogate signals such as otoacoustic emissions or retinal vein pulsation, but these remain investigational and are not validated for routine clinical use. Model-based computational approaches that infer ICP from systemic arterial blood pressure (ABP) and cerebral blood flow velocity (CBFV)—measurable via radial artery catheterization and transcranial Doppler ultrasound, respectively—have shown promise but are often hampered by sensitivity to signal misalignment, dependence on patient-specific calibration, and inability to account for dynamic changes in cerebrovascular autoregulation.

A key limitation of prior model-based methods is their assumption of perfect temporal alignment between ABP and CBFV waveforms, despite the fact that physiological delays, measurement device latencies, and beat-to-beat variability introduce unknown time offsets that significantly degrade estimation accuracy. Furthermore, many existing algorithms rely on fixed or population-averaged model parameters, failing to adapt to individual patient physiology or evolving pathophysiological states. This rigidity limits generalizability across diverse patient populations and clinical scenarios. There remains a pressing need for a robust, noninvasive ICP estimation system that overcomes these shortcomings by integrating physiological modeling with adaptive statistical inference, enabling accurate, real-time, and patient-specific ICP monitoring without reliance on invasive calibration or prior knowledge of individual hemodynamic parameters.

## SUMMARY

In one embodiment, the invention provides a system for noninvasive intracranial pressure estimation comprising a data acquisition interface configured to receive signals representing arterial blood pressure (ABP) and cerebral blood flow velocity (CBFV) from a patient; a processor communicatively coupled to the data acquisition interface; and a memory storing processor-executable instructions that, when executed, cause the processor to estimate an initial ICP value by applying a statistical model to the ABP and CBFV signals, obtain updated ABP and CBFV data over a subsequent time period, estimate an updated ICP value based on changes in the ABP and CBFV data relative to the initial estimate, and output the updated ICP value to a display or clinical decision support system. The system dynamically accounts for unknown time offsets between the ABP and CBFV waveforms and incorporates a Bayesian framework to fuse model predictions with observed data, thereby enhancing robustness against noise and physiological variability.

In another embodiment, the invention provides a software product embodied in a non-transitory computer-readable medium, comprising instructions for performing a noninvasive ICP estimation method. The software acquires time-series data of ABP and CBFV from input devices or stored files, computes an initial ICP estimate by evaluating a likelihood distribution derived from a first-order finite impulse response (FIR) model of cerebral hemodynamics across a range of candidate ICP values and time offsets, establishes a baseline ICP by combining this likelihood with a prior distribution of physiologically plausible ICP values, and subsequently tracks changes in ICP over time using a uniform prior and a Kalman filter-like update mechanism that fuses observed ICP changes with predictions from an autoregressive (AR) process model of ICP dynamics. The software outputs a sequence of updated ICP estimates that reflect both instantaneous hemodynamic changes and longer-term trends, enabling continuous monitoring without recalibration.

Both the system and software embodiments leverage a subject-specific model of cerebral vasculature that treats CBFV as the output of a two-tap FIR filter driven by cerebral perfusion pressure (CPP), defined as the difference between central ABP and ICP. By scanning over plausible ICP values and time offsets, the method generates a joint likelihood distribution that quantifies the compatibility of each candidate ICP with the observed ABP-CBFV relationship. The initial estimate is refined through Bayesian inference, while subsequent updates minimize dependence on the initial prior by employing a tracking algorithm that adaptively weights model-predicted and data-derived ICP changes according to their respective uncertainties. This dual-stage approach ensures both high initial accuracy and long-term stability, making it suitable for extended monitoring in dynamic clinical environments.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

Conventional noninvasive ICP estimation techniques suffer from fundamental inaccuracies stemming from oversimplified assumptions about cerebral physiology and inadequate handling of real-world signal characteristics. Many prior methods assume a fixed, known relationship between arterial blood pressure (ABP) and cerebral blood flow velocity (CBFV), neglecting the fact that this relationship is mediated by intracranial pressure (ICP) and modulated by cerebrovascular resistance and compliance—parameters that vary significantly across individuals and over time. Moreover, these techniques often fail to account for the critical influence of temporal misalignment between ABP and CBFV waveforms, which arises from physiological conduction delays, differences in sensor placement, and beat-to-beat variability in cardiac cycle timing. Such misalignment introduces systematic errors in model-based ICP estimates, as even small time shifts (on the order of tens of milliseconds) can drastically alter the inferred hemodynamic relationship.

The core limitation of mapping ABP and CBFV directly to ICP lies in the ill-posed nature of the inverse problem: multiple combinations of ICP and vascular parameters can produce similar CBFV responses, leading to ambiguity in estimation. Traditional approaches that fix model parameters or ignore time offsets exacerbate this ambiguity, resulting in estimates that are either biased or highly sensitive to noise. To address these challenges, the present invention introduces a novel computational framework grounded in a physiologically plausible model of cerebral hemodynamics and enhanced by advanced statistical inference techniques.

The invention employs a statistical model that represents the cerebral vasculature as a first-order, subject-specific system wherein CBFV is modeled as the output of a two-tap finite impulse response (FIR) filter whose input is cerebral perfusion pressure (CPP)—the difference between central ABP and ICP. Recognizing that direct measurement of central ABP is often impractical, the system utilizes radial ABP (rABP) as a surrogate, with appropriate corrections for hydrostatic pressure differences. Critically, the model incorporates time offsets as explicit parameters, allowing the system to evaluate the ABP-CBFV relationship across a range of plausible temporal alignments constrained by physiological principles: specifically, that CBFV systolic peaks should precede rABP peaks, and diastolic points should be approximately aligned, reflecting the Windkessel-like behavior of the cerebral circulation.

The estimation process begins by generating a set of candidate CPP waveforms by subtracting a range of physiologically plausible mean ICP values (e.g., from 0 to mean rABP in 1 mmHg increments) from the rABP signal. For each candidate ICP and each admissible time offset, the system computes optimal FIR filter coefficients (α and β) in a least-squares sense to minimize the prediction error between the modeled and actual CBFV. The resulting residual error norms are transformed into a joint likelihood distribution L(I, d) over ICP and time offset, where higher likelihood corresponds to smaller prediction errors. This likelihood is then marginalized over time offsets to yield a one-dimensional likelihood distribution over ICP alone.

To establish a baseline ICP estimate, the system combines this likelihood with a prior distribution that reflects clinical knowledge of ICP ranges—typically a mixture of truncated Gaussian distributions emphasizing both normal (e.g., ~13.6 mmHg) and pathological (e.g., >30 mmHg) values—to form a posterior distribution. The median of this posterior serves as the initial ICP estimate, computed over multiple consecutive data windows (e.g., five 20-beat segments) and averaged to enhance robustness. Subsequently, the system transitions to a tracking phase that uses a uniform prior to reduce dependence on initial assumptions. However, to mitigate the increased variance associated with a flat prior, the invention implements a Kalman filter-like update mechanism that fuses observed ICP changes (derived from the likelihood) with predictions from a first-order autoregressive (AR) model of ICP dynamics. This AR model, characterized by a white-noise process with tunable variance, allows the system to anticipate gradual ICP trends while remaining responsive to abrupt changes.

The framework explicitly addresses data quality by incorporating preprocessing steps including bandpass filtering (0.5–16 Hz) to remove out-of-band noise, baseline correction for transducer height differences, and signal conditioning to ensure waveform fidelity. Low-quality time periods, identified through signal integrity metrics, are excluded from estimation. Optimization techniques, such as grid search over ICP and time offset ranges followed by pseudo-inverse computation of model parameters, ensure computational efficiency and numerical stability. The system also enables noninvasive estimation of ICP pulse pressure by leveraging the same likelihood distribution to compute frequency-domain reconstructions of the ICP waveform from ABP and CBFV harmonics, without requiring pretraining or calibration.

In operation, the system continuously acquires rABP and CBFV data, segments them into non-overlapping estimation windows (e.g., 20 cardiac beats), and applies the model-based Bayesian routine to each window. The final ICP estimate is derived from the posterior distribution in the baseline phase and from the filtered change estimates in the tracking phase, ensuring both accuracy and adaptability. The entire pipeline—from data acquisition to ICP output—is implemented in software executable on standard computing hardware, with potential integration into bedside monitors, mobile devices, or cloud-based analytics platforms. This approach not only achieves estimation accuracy comparable to invasive methods (e.g., bias <1 mmHg, RMSE <4 mmHg) but also provides interpretable, patient-specific insights into cerebral autoregulation through the estimated model parameters (α, β, or equivalently, resistance R and compliance C). By overcoming the limitations of prior art through rigorous physiological modeling and adaptive statistical inference, the invention enables safe, reliable, and continuous noninvasive ICP monitoring for a broad spectrum of neurological and surgical applications.
