# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates to novel monoclonal and polyclonal antibodies specifically reactive with MCR proteins, particularly MCR-1 and MCR-2, which are phosphoethanolamine transferases encoded by plasmid-borne mcr genes responsible for conferring bacterial resistance to the antibiotic colistin. The invention further encompasses immunoassay methods and diagnostic kits utilizing these antibodies for the direct detection of MCR proteins in biological and environmental samples, including food matrices such as ground meat. These tools enable rapid, specific, and sensitive identification of colistin-resistant bacteria without reliance on nucleic acid amplification or lengthy culture-based susceptibility testing, thereby addressing a critical need in public health surveillance, clinical diagnostics, and food safety monitoring.

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

Antibiotics have revolutionized modern medicine by enabling effective treatment of bacterial infections that were once fatal. However, the widespread and often indiscriminate use of antibiotics in human medicine and agriculture has led to the emergence and global dissemination of antimicrobial resistance, posing a severe threat to public health. Among the last-resort antibiotics for treating multidrug-resistant Gram-negative bacterial infections is colistin, also known as polymyxin E. Colistin exerts its bactericidal effect by binding to the anionic lipopolysaccharide (LPS) in the outer membrane of Gram-negative bacteria, displacing divalent cations and disrupting membrane integrity, ultimately leading to cell lysis and death. Historically, colistin resistance was attributed to chromosomal mutations affecting lipid A modification, which limited its horizontal spread. However, this paradigm shifted dramatically in 2015 with the discovery of the plasmid-mediated mcr-1 gene in Escherichia coli isolated from food animals and humans in China. This gene encodes the MCR-1 protein, a phosphoethanolamine transferase that modifies lipid A by adding a cationic phosphoethanolamine group, thereby reducing the net negative charge of the bacterial surface and preventing colistin binding.

The identification of mcr-1 marked the first report of a horizontally transmissible colistin resistance determinant, raising global alarm due to its potential to spread rapidly across bacterial species and geographic regions. Since then, additional variants—mcr-2 through mcr-8—have been identified, exhibiting significant sequence homology to mcr-1, particularly within the C-terminal catalytic domain responsible for enzymatic activity. These genes have been detected in diverse sources, including livestock, retail meat, environmental samples, and clinical isolates worldwide, often co-occurring with carbapenem resistance genes in so-called “pan-drug-resistant” pathogens. The convergence of resistance to both colistin and carbapenems severely compromises therapeutic options, heightening the urgency for robust detection methods.

Current approaches to detect colistin resistance fall into phenotypic and genotypic categories. Phenotypic methods, such as broth microdilution for minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) determination, suffer from poor reproducibility due to colistin’s polycationic nature, adsorption to laboratory plastics, and variable expression of resistance mechanisms. Genotypic methods, including PCR and whole-genome sequencing (WGS), offer speed but are indirect—they detect the presence of mcr genes rather than the functional MCR protein. PCR assays are prone to false negatives due to inhibitors in complex samples or low target abundance, and false positives due to non-functional gene fragments or cryptic homologous sequences. WGS, while comprehensive, requires specialized infrastructure and bioinformatics expertise, limiting its accessibility in resource-constrained settings. Consequently, there exists a pressing need for reliable, direct, protein-based detection methods that confirm the actual production of the resistance-conferring enzyme. The present invention fulfills this unmet need by providing high-affinity antibodies and immunoassays capable of specifically detecting MCR-1 and MCR-2 proteins with high sensitivity and tolerance to complex sample matrices.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides monoclonal and polyclonal antibodies that specifically bind to MCR proteins, particularly MCR-1 and MCR-2, which are key mediators of plasmid-encoded colistin resistance in Gram-negative bacteria. These antibodies are produced by hybridoma cell lines and immunized animals, respectively, using recombinant catalytic domains of MCR-1 and MCR-2 as immunogens. The invention further encompasses immunoassays, particularly sandwich enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays (ELISAs), that utilize these antibodies to directly detect MCR proteins in bacterial lysates, culture supernatants, and complex food samples such as ground beef, chicken, and pork. The monoclonal antibodies may be labeled with detectable moieties, including enzymes, fluorophores, biotin, or radioisotopes, to facilitate signal generation in various assay formats. A method for detecting MCR proteins in a sample is disclosed, comprising contacting the sample with a capture antibody specific for an MCR protein, followed by detection with a labeled detection antibody, and measuring the resulting signal. Additionally, the invention includes diagnostic kits containing one or more of these antibodies, along with necessary reagents, buffers, and instructions for use in detecting colistin-resistant bacteria. The key advantages of the invention include direct detection of the functional resistance protein, high specificity and sensitivity (with limits of detection as low as 0.01 ng/mL for cMCR-1), excellent precision (intra- and inter-assay coefficients of variation within regulatory guidelines), and robust performance in complex food matrices. These features enable rapid, unambiguous identification of viable mcr-harboring bacteria, supporting applications in clinical diagnostics, food safety surveillance, and antimicrobial resistance monitoring. The scope of the invention extends to all MCR protein variants sharing sufficient epitope homology with MCR-1 and MCR-2, particularly within the conserved catalytic domain.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

For purposes of the present invention, technical terms are defined as follows. The articles “a,” “an,” and “the” are used interchangeably with “at least one” and “one or more.” The term “about” means plus or minus ten percent of the indicated value. An “antibody” refers to an immunoglobulin molecule capable of specifically binding to an antigen, including full-length antibodies, antigen-binding fragments (e.g., Fab, F(ab′)₂, scFv), and engineered variants thereof. A “monoclonal antibody” is an antibody produced by a single clone of B cells or hybridoma cells, exhibiting uniform binding specificity and affinity. The “catalytic domain” of an MCR protein refers to the C-terminal soluble region (approximately residues 214–541 in MCR-1) responsible for phosphoethanolamine transferase activity. “Chimeric” antibodies contain portions derived from different species, such as murine variable regions fused to human constant regions. The phrase “consisting essentially of” excludes additional elements that would materially affect the basic and novel characteristics of the claimed invention. “Detecting the formation of the immunological complex” means identifying the binding event between an antibody and its target antigen, typically via a detectable label. An “effective amount” is a quantity sufficient to produce a desired biological or diagnostic effect. “Humanized” antibodies are engineered to reduce immunogenicity in humans by grafting complementarity-determining regions (CDRs) from a non-human antibody onto a human antibody framework. “Isolated” means separated from its natural environment, such as an antibody purified from serum or culture supernatant. An “MCR protein” encompasses any protein encoded by an mcr gene (e.g., mcr-1 to mcr-8) that confers colistin resistance via lipid A modification. “MCR-1 protein” and “MCR-2 protein” refer specifically to the proteins encoded by mcr-1 and mcr-2, respectively. “Resistance to colistin” denotes a phenotype wherein a bacterium survives exposure to colistin concentrations that inhibit susceptible strains, mediated by MCR proteins.

Monoclonal antibodies of the invention were developed by immunizing mice with recombinant catalytic domains of MCR-1 (cMCR-1) and fusing splenocytes with myeloma cells to generate hybridomas. Nine stable hybridoma lines (MCR-1-1 to MCR-1-9) were selected based on high ELISA titers and specificity. Characterization revealed that six antibodies (e.g., MCR-1-7) cross-react with both MCR-1 and MCR-2 due to epitope conservation in the catalytic domain, while three (e.g., MCR-1-9) are MCR-1-specific, recognizing divergent epitopes. These antibodies can be used in various immunoassays, including ELISA, Western blotting, and lateral flow devices. Genetic analysis shows high conservation among mcr variants, particularly in the catalytic domain (87.4% identity between MCR-1 and MCR-2), supporting broad reactivity of cross-reactive antibodies. Recombinant MCR-1 and MCR-2 proteins, including full-length and catalytic domain constructs, were expressed in E. coli with N-terminal His-tags and purified via immobilized metal affinity chromatography, with catalytic domains further purified by size-exclusion chromatography. Antibody production involved immunizing rabbits with cMCR-1 to generate polyclonal IgG, and mice for monoclonal antibody development via hybridoma technology. A sandwich ELISA was established using polyclonal IgG as the capture antibody and monoclonal antibody MCR-1-7 as the detection antibody, demonstrating high sensitivity (LOD: 0.01 ng/mL for cMCR-1; 0.1 ng/mL for cMCR-2) and linearity over defined ranges. The assay exhibited excellent precision, with intra-day %CV <5% and inter-day %CV <15%, meeting FDA CBER guidelines.

Antibodies may be conjugated to labels for detection. Labels include enzyme-conjugated moieties (e.g., horseradish peroxidase, alkaline phosphatase), biotin-coupled reagents (detected via streptavidin-enzyme conjugates), fluorochromes (e.g., FITC, Alexa Fluor dyes), radioisotopic labels (e.g., ¹²⁵I), and chemiluminescent substrates (e.g., luminol derivatives). Binding of labels to antibodies is achieved via standard chemical conjugation techniques, preserving antigen-binding activity. Kits for MCR protein detection comprise one or more anti-MCR antibodies, optionally labeled, along with buffers, standards, controls, and written instructions. Containers may include microtiter plates, vials, or lateral flow strips. Numerical ranges provided (e.g., LOD values) are exemplary and may vary based on assay conditions. Specific embodiments include the use of MCR-1-7 for broad detection of MCR variants and MCR-1-9 for MCR-1-specific tracking.

### Materials and Methods

All animal procedures complied with USDA ARS Directive #130.4.v.3 and were approved by the WRRC IACUC; mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation. Bacterial strains included mcr-1-positive E. coli AR-Bank #346 and #349, and mcr-1-negative ATCC25922 and ATCC29425. Full-length and catalytic domain genes of mcr-1 and mcr-2 were PCR-amplified and cloned into pET His6 TEV LIC vector, expressed in E. coli BL21(DE3) induced with IPTG at 16°C, and purified via HisTrap FF chromatography; catalytic domains underwent TEV cleavage and size-exclusion chromatography. Polyclonal antibodies were generated in rabbits immunized with cMCR-1 and purified via Protein-A affinity. Monoclonal antibodies were produced by immunizing Balb/cJ mice with cMCR-1, fusing splenocytes with SP2/0 myeloma cells, screening hybridomas by ELISA, and purifying IgG via Protein-G affinity. ELISAs involved coating plates with antigen or capture antibody, blocking with 5% NFDM/TBST, incubating with primary and HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies, and detecting chemiluminescence. LOD was defined as mean background + 3SD. Statistical analysis used Student’s t-test; standard curves were generated in GraphPad Prism. PAGE and Western blotting used NuPAGE gels, PVDF transfer, and ECL detection. Meat samples (25g) were spiked with ~10 CFU of bacteria, enriched in TSB + colistin, lysed with B-PER, and tested by ELISA.

### Results and Discussion

Recombinant MCR-1 and MCR-2 proteins were successfully expressed and purified, with catalytic domains yielding major bands at ~38.4 kDa. Mass spectrometry confirmed full-length MCR-1 integrity. Polyclonal antibodies from immunized rabbits showed high titers (≥1:8,000) and specific binding to MCR-1 in Western blots. Nine monoclonal antibodies were characterized: six cross-reactive with MCR-1/MCR-2 (e.g., MCR-1-7, IgG2b), three MCR-1-specific (e.g., MCR-1-9, IgG1). MCR-1-7 exhibited highest binding affinity in direct ELISA. A sandwich ELISA using pAb capture and MCR-1-7 detection achieved LODs of 0.01 ng/mL (cMCR-1) and 0.1 ng/mL (cMCR-2), with linear ranges of 0.01–4 ng/mL and 0.1–6 ng/mL, respectively. Precision met FDA criteria (%CV <5% intra-day, <15% inter-day). The assay detected MCR-1 in bacterial cultures at ≥0.4×10⁶ CFU/mL and in spiked meat samples (beef, chicken, pork) with signals significantly above background, despite matrix effects reducing signal intensity to 7–13% of aqueous controls. No false positives occurred with mcr-1-negative strains. This validates the ELISA for real-world food safety applications, with an effective LOD of <0.4 CFU/g post-enrichment. Immunoassays offer direct, functional detection of resistance, overcoming limitations of genotypic methods. The sandwich ELISA’s simplicity, reliability, and matrix tolerance make it suitable for routine screening. While embodiments focus on MCR-1/MCR-2, the invention’s scope includes detection of other MCR variants sharing conserved epitopes, particularly within the catalytic domain, enabling comprehensive surveillance of mobile colistin resistance.
