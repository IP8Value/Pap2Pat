# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates generally to proximity sensors, and more particularly to a multi-channel capacitive proximity sensor array that utilizes frequency-domain multiplexing through resonant structures to detect and distinguish the presence, location, and type of proximate objects. The invention enables the simultaneous detection of multiple proximity events occurring at different spatial locations by embedding each capacitive sensing element in a resonant circuit tuned to a distinct resonant frequency, thereby allowing independent monitoring of each sensing channel through frequency-selective interrogation. The disclosed proximity sensor is especially useful in applications requiring spatial discrimination, such as human-machine interfaces, industrial automation, robotics, and security systems, where conventional single-frequency capacitive sensors lack the capability to resolve multiple concurrent or spatially distributed proximity events.

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

Conventional capacitive proximity sensors operate by detecting changes in capacitance caused by the approach of a target object. These sensors are typically excited by a fixed-frequency oscillating signal, and the resulting change in signal amplitude or phase is used to infer the presence of an object. However, this approach suffers from several limitations. First, because only a single frequency is used, such sensors can only indicate whether an object is present or absent, without providing information about the object’s location if multiple sensing elements are employed. Second, when multiple capacitive elements are arranged in an array, traditional time-division multiplexing is required to read out each element sequentially, which reduces temporal resolution and increases system complexity. Third, conventional designs do not exploit the full frequency-dependent response of the capacitive structure, thereby limiting the richness of information that can be extracted from the sensor. Prior art has attempted to address some of these issues through frequency sweeping for gesture recognition or through the use of metamaterial-based resonators for enhanced sensitivity. However, these approaches either fail to provide spatial isolation among multiple sensing channels or require complex post-processing to deconvolve overlapping responses. Furthermore, existing commercial proximity sensors exhibit limited sensing range relative to their physical size and are generally incapable of distinguishing between metallic and non-metallic objects without additional hardware or algorithmic overhead. Thus, there remains a need for a proximity sensor architecture that combines high spatial resolution, multi-object detection capability, material discrimination, and efficient readout—all within a compact and scalable design.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention overcomes the limitations of conventional proximity sensors by introducing a resonant structure-based capacitive sensor array in which each sensing element is coupled to a unique inductive component to form a distinct LC resonance. This configuration enables frequency-domain multiplexing, wherein each sensor operates independently at its own resonant frequency, allowing simultaneous and non-interfering detection of multiple proximity events. A key innovation lies in the detection of phase changes in the transmission or reflection coefficient near each resonant frequency, which provides a sensitive and robust indicator of object proximity. Critically, the phase response differs characteristically depending on whether the proximate object is metallic or non-metallic: metallic objects induce a phase shift in one direction due to eddy current effects, while non-metallic (dielectric) objects produce a phase shift in the opposite direction due to capacitive coupling. This differential phase behavior enables the sensor to distinguish between object types without additional hardware. The invention further discloses a method for determining object type by analyzing the sign and magnitude of the phase change at the relevant resonant frequency. In a preferred embodiment, the proximity sensor comprises a microstrip transmission line integrated with multiple capacitive patches, each connected to an inductor—either surface-mounted, on-chip meander-type, or metamaterial-based split-ring resonators—to establish isolated resonances across a broad frequency band. The entire array is interrogated by a swept-frequency or multi-tone RF source, and the return signal is processed to extract both amplitude and phase information at each resonant frequency. The processor then identifies which sensor element is activated, estimates the distance to the object based on the magnitude of the response, and classifies the object as metallic or non-metallic based on the phase signature. This approach yields a highly efficient, scalable, and intelligent proximity sensing system capable of multi-directional, multi-object, and material-discriminative detection.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE PREFERRED EMBODIMENTS

The invention is motivated by the recognition that embedding capacitive sensing elements within resonant structures enables frequency-domain isolation of multiple sensing channels. Each capacitive patch, when combined with an inductor of specific inductance, forms an LC tank circuit with a unique resonant frequency \( f_c = 1/(2\pi\sqrt{LC}) \). By selecting inductors with sufficiently different values, the resonances are spaced apart in frequency such that the response of one sensor does not overlap with others, even in the presence of moderate quality factor limitations. Phase change detection is employed as a primary sensing modality because it offers higher sensitivity and noise immunity compared to amplitude-only measurements. When an object approaches a sensor, it alters the effective capacitance, shifting the resonant frequency and inducing a measurable phase shift in the transmission coefficient \( S_{21} \) at frequencies near resonance. Metallic objects, due to their high conductivity, generate opposing magnetic fields via eddy currents, which result in a negative phase shift (lagging phase), whereas non-metallic dielectric objects increase the local capacitance without significant conductive losses, leading to a positive phase shift (leading phase). This fundamental distinction allows reliable object-type determination.

In a four-element multi-directional sensor array embodiment, circular copper patches are fabricated on a three-layer PCB with FR-4 substrate, each connected via vias to a common microstrip feed line on the bottom layer, with a shared ground plane in the middle layer. Surface-mounted inductors of 100 nH, 68 nH, 33 nH, and 10 nH yield resonant frequencies at approximately 174 MHz, 211 MHz, 303 MHz, and 550 MHz, respectively. As shown in FIG. 1, the resonant frequency shifts downward as an object approaches, due to increased capacitance. FIG. 2 illustrates the simulated capacitance increase as a wooden block approaches the patch, confirming the sensing principle. FIGS. 3A and 3B depict the circuit model and simulated transmission responses, demonstrating that perturbation of one capacitor affects only its corresponding stopband. FIG. 4 shows the physical assembly of the sensor array into a cubic configuration, enabling omnidirectional sensing. The detecting unit, as shown in FIG. 5, includes an RF source, a vector network analyzer or equivalent impedance-measuring circuit, and a processor that analyzes phase and magnitude data. The processor executes an algorithm that compares measured phase shifts against calibrated thresholds to classify object type and determines active channels based on frequency-specific deviations.

FIG. 6 presents experimental results: FIG. 6a shows resonant frequency shifts only in the channel facing the object, while FIG. 6b demonstrates that monitoring \( S_{21} \) magnitude at fixed frequencies near each resonance yields directional sensitivity with sub-millimeter resolution. Multiple resonant frequencies enable dense channel packing; higher quality factors—achieved using split-ring resonators (SRRs) as shown in FIGS. 5 and 6—further enhance channel isolation. In the SRR-coupled embodiment (FIGS. 13–14), quality factors exceed 90, compared to 5–12 for surface-mounted inductors, allowing operation in narrower bandwidths (e.g., 2.6–3.3 GHz) with greater channel density. The detecting and processing unit continuously sweeps or steps through the resonant frequencies, computes phase derivatives, and applies decision logic to output object position, distance, and material type. This architecture supports linear arrays (FIGS. 8–12) for position tracking and multi-object scenarios, as demonstrated by simultaneous loading of adjacent sensors without cross-talk. The invention thus provides a versatile, high-performance proximity sensing platform adaptable to diverse industrial and consumer applications.
