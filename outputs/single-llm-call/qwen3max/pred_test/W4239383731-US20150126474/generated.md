## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates to the field of anti-infective therapeutics, specifically to small-molecule modulators of bacterial quorum sensing (QS) systems. More particularly, the invention provides novel compounds that activate QS in Vibrio species by inhibiting the ATPase activity of LuxO, a central response regulator in the QS signal transduction cascade. These compounds function as virulence repressors and represent a new class of anti-virulence agents for the prophylactic and therapeutic treatment of infections caused by pathogenic vibrios, including Vibrio cholerae, Vibrio parahaemolyticus, Vibrio vulnificus, and related species.

## BACKGROUND

Quorum sensing (QS) is a cell-density-dependent mechanism of bacterial communication that enables coordinated regulation of group behaviors such as bioluminescence, sporulation, biofilm formation, and virulence factor production. This process relies on the synthesis, release, detection, and population-wide response to extracellular signaling molecules known as autoinducers. In many Gram-negative pathogens, QS circuits are integrated into two-component signal transduction systems that link environmental cues to transcriptional outputs governing infection dynamics.

In pathogenic bacteria, QS often controls the expression of virulence determinants, making it an attractive target for anti-infective strategies that aim to disarm rather than kill the pathogen. Such anti-virulence approaches are hypothesized to exert less selective pressure for resistance development compared to conventional bactericidal antibiotics. While much effort has focused on disrupting acylhomoserine lactone (HSL)-based QS systems common in Proteobacteria, numerous globally significant pathogens—including Vibrio cholerae—utilize non-HSL autoinducers and distinct regulatory architectures, necessitating alternative intervention strategies.

Vibrio cholerae, the causative agent of cholera, employs a sophisticated QS system that integrates signals from two autoinducers: CAI-1 ((S)-3-hydroxytridecan-4-one) and AI-2 ((2S,4S)-2-methyl-2,3,3,4-tetrahydroxytetrahydrofuran borate). These molecules are detected by the transmembrane histidine kinase receptors CqsS and LuxPQ, respectively. At low cell density (LCD), in the absence of sufficient autoinducer, these receptors autophosphorylate and transfer phosphate to the response regulator LuxO. Phosphorylated LuxO (LuxO∼P) activates transcription of four small regulatory RNAs (Qrr1–4), which, in concert with the RNA chaperone Hfq, destabilize the mRNA of the master QS regulator HapR while stabilizing that of AphA. Consequently, AphA dominates at LCD, promoting expression of virulence genes required for intestinal colonization, including those encoding cholera toxin (CT) and the toxin co-regulated pilus (TCP).

At high cell density (HCD), autoinducer binding switches CqsS and LuxPQ to phosphatase mode, leading to dephosphorylation and inactivation of LuxO. This halts Qrr sRNA production, allowing HapR accumulation. HapR then represses virulence and biofilm genes while activating others involved in environmental survival and dissemination. Thus, unlike many chronic pathogens that upregulate virulence at HCD, V. cholerae represses it—a strategy aligned with its acute, diarrheal disease profile that facilitates rapid host-to-environment transmission.

Given this regulatory logic, pharmacological activation of QS in V. cholerae represents a rational anti-virulence strategy: forcing premature entry into the HCD state should suppress virulence factor expression. Moreover, because LuxO and its downstream circuitry are highly conserved across pathogenic Vibrio species—including V. parahaemolyticus, V. vulnificus, and V. alginolyticus—compounds targeting LuxO could offer broad-spectrum utility against vibriosis.

LuxO belongs to the NtrC family of σ⁵⁴-dependent transcriptional activators, characterized by an N-terminal receiver domain, a central AAA+ ATPase domain, and a C-terminal DNA-binding domain. Activation requires phosphorylation-induced oligomerization, ATP hydrolysis, and interaction with RNA polymerase-σ⁵⁴ holoenzyme to catalyze open complex formation at target promoters. Despite the essential role of NtrC-type proteins in bacterial physiology and virulence, no small-molecule modulators of their ATPase activity had been reported prior to this work.

Efforts to develop inhibitors of two-component systems (TCSs) have historically faced challenges, including poor selectivity, membrane toxicity, and limited cell permeability—particularly when targeting the conserved kinase domains of histidine kinases. While a few inhibitors of non-NtrC response regulators (e.g., WalR, DevR, AlgR1) have been described, they typically interfere with phosphorylation or DNA binding. Targeting the ATPase domain of NtrC-family proteins offers a novel and potentially generalizable approach, yet remains largely unexplored due to perceived difficulties in achieving specificity within this conserved enzymatic module.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention discloses novel strategies for activating quorum sensing in Vibrio species through targeted inhibition of LuxO, a central NtrC-type response regulator. Specifically, the invention provides small-molecule compounds that function as potent and selective inhibitors of LuxO ATPase activity, thereby inducing premature quorum sensing responses and repressing virulence gene expression.

The invention encompasses virulence inhibitor molecules belonging to a chemical class characterized by a 5-thio-6-azauracil core substituted with hydrophobic alkyl side chains, wherein optimal activity is conferred by branched, bulky terminal groups such as tert-butyl carbinol. These compounds, exemplified by compounds 11 and 12, activate QS in both autoinducer-deficient and constitutively active LuxO backgrounds, confirming their site of action downstream of receptor signaling.

The invention further provides methods for developing anti-infective agents based on pro-quorum sensing (pro-QS) principles, wherein administration of LuxO inhibitors shifts bacterial populations into a low-virulence, high-cell-density physiological state. This strategy is particularly suited for acute infections like cholera, where virulence is expressed at low cell density and repressed upon QS activation.

Also disclosed are specific compounds useful for QS activation, including neopentyl, isobutyl, cyclobutylmethyl, cyclopropylmethyl, 3-methylbutan-2-yl, (trimethylsilyl)methyl, 3,3-dimethylbutan-2-yl, and 2-((3,5-dioxo-2,3,4,5-tetrahydro-1,2,4-triazin-6-yl)thio)alkyl esters of 5-thio-6-azauracil derivatives.

The invention includes a method for inhibiting virulence in Vibrio species by administering an effective amount of a LuxO ATPase inhibitor to a subject infected with or at risk of infection by a pathogenic Vibrio. This method results in reduced production of key virulence factors such as TcpA in V. cholerae and VopD in V. parahaemolyticus, leading to attenuated pathogenicity.

Additionally, the invention provides methods for prophylactic treatment of vibrio infections and for inhibiting biofilm formation through systemic or topical application of LuxO inhibitors, either alone or in combination with other anti-infectives. These approaches exploit the natural role of QS in repressing colonization factors, offering a resistance-minimizing alternative to traditional antibiotics.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

The present invention identifies and characterizes a novel class of quorum sensing-activating molecules that function by inhibiting the ATPase activity of LuxO, a conserved response regulator in Vibrio species. These molecules, designated herein as Class 2 compounds, share a 5-thio-6-azauracil heterocyclic core and exhibit structure-dependent potency modulated by the steric and hydrophobic properties of their alkyl side chains.

Class 2 compounds are distinguished from Class 1 molecules, which act as agonists of the CqsS autoinducer receptor, by their ability to activate QS in strains harboring a constitutively active LuxO D47E mutant—thereby demonstrating a target downstream of the membrane receptors. Among the Class 2 compounds, those with branched, bulky terminal groups (e.g., compound 12 bearing a tert-butyl carbinol moiety) display enhanced potency, with EC₅₀ values in the low micromolar range in bioluminescence-based reporter assays.

A key embodiment of the invention is the identification of LuxO inhibitors that specifically block ATP hydrolysis without affecting LuxO phosphorylation status or DNA-binding affinity. Biochemical analyses confirm that compounds 11 and 12 function as uncompetitive inhibitors of LuxO ATPase, binding to the pre-formed LuxO–ATP complex and reducing both Vₘₐₓ and Kₘ in a manner consistent with allosteric inhibition. This mechanism prevents LuxO-mediated activation of qrr sRNA transcription, leading to derepression of HapR and induction of the high-cell-density gene expression program.

Genetic studies support this mechanism: random mutagenesis of luxO D47E followed by selection for resistance to compound 12 yielded four point mutants (I211F, L215F, L242F, V294L) that retain ATPase activity but are refractory to inhibition. Structural mapping of these residues onto homologous NtrC proteins places them adjacent to the Walker B motif and the GAFTGA domain—regions critical for ATP hydrolysis and σ⁵⁴ interaction—suggesting that the inhibitors bind an allosteric pocket that modulates catalytic conformation.

The invention demonstrates that LuxO inhibitors activate QS across multiple Vibrio species. In V. harveyi, compounds 11 and 12 induce >10,000-fold increases in bioluminescence in a luxO D47E background. In V. parahaemolyticus, they convert translucent (low-OpaR) colonies to opaque (high-OpaR), phenocopying a ΔluxO mutant. This cross-species efficacy arises from the high conservation of LuxO’s ATPase domain among vibrios, enabling broad-spectrum pro-QS activity irrespective of autoinducer identity (HSL, CAI-1, or AI-2).

Critically, LuxO inhibitors function as anti-virulence agents. In V. cholerae, compound 12 reduces TcpA production while increasing HapR levels. In V. parahaemolyticus, it suppresses both intracellular and secreted levels of the TTSS-1 effector VopD. In ex vivo cytotoxicity assays using HeLa cells, pretreatment of a hyper-virulent V. parahaemolyticus luxO* strain with compound 12 significantly delays host cell lysis, confirming functional attenuation of virulence.

The invention contemplates the use of LuxO inhibitors as prophylactics—for example, in outbreak settings or for travelers to endemic regions—where early administration could prevent establishment of virulence programs during initial colonization. Because these compounds do not affect bacterial growth, they are expected to impose minimal selective pressure for resistance.

Furthermore, the invention recognizes that LuxO belongs to the NtrC family of AAA+ ATPases, suggesting that the disclosed compounds may serve as scaffolds for developing broader inhibitors of NtrC-type response regulators. Although E. coli NtrC is insensitive to these compounds despite sequence conservation, the structural insights gained—particularly regarding the allosteric site near Walker B—provide a foundation for engineering pan-NtrC inhibitors targeting virulence, nitrogen metabolism, or motility in diverse pathogens.

Finally, the invention highlights the potential of targeting AAA+ ATPase domains in bacterial signaling proteins, a strategy that may be extendable to other microbial or even eukaryotic AAA+ systems involved in protein homeostasis, organelle dynamics, or DNA repair.

### Materials and Methods

Bacterial strains used in this invention include derivatives of Vibrio cholerae C6706str, Vibrio harveyi BB120, and Vibrio parahaemolyticus LM4476 and LM9688, along with standard Escherichia coli cloning strains such as DH5α and BL21(DE3). Cultures were grown in Luria-Bertani (LB) or Lennox broth (LM) media at 30°C or 37°C as appropriate, with antibiotics added at standard concentrations (e.g., ampicillin 100 mg/L, tetracycline 10 mg/L).

Plasmids and strain genotypes are detailed in supporting documentation, including reporter constructs carrying the V. harveyi lux operon under HapR control and plasmids expressing wild-type or mutant LuxO variants. Screening for V. cholerae QS-activating molecules was performed using a high-throughput whole-cell assay in 384-well plates, wherein LCD-locked strains (ΔcqsA ΔluxS or luxO D47E) carrying a lux reporter were exposed to ~20 µM of each compound from a 90,000-member library; hits were defined as those inducing >100-fold luminescence over baseline.

Bioluminescence assays for V. cholerae and V. harveyi involved diluting overnight cultures 20-fold in appropriate media, adding test compounds dissolved in DMSO, and measuring luminescence and optical density after 4 hours of incubation at 30°C with shaking. Protein purification of His₆-tagged LuxO D47E was achieved by cloning the luxO D47E allele into pET28b, expressing it in E. coli BL21(DE3), lysing cells in phosphate buffer with imidazole, and purifying via nickel-affinity chromatography.

ATPase activity was measured using a coupled-enzyme assay wherein LuxO-mediated ATP hydrolysis generates ADP, which is converted to pyruvate by pyruvate kinase, and then to lactate by lactate dehydrogenase, with concomitant oxidation of NADH monitored at 340 nm. DNA binding was assessed by electrophoretic mobility shift assays and fluorescence anisotropy using labeled qrr4 promoter fragments.

A genetic screen for LuxO mutants resistant to compound 12 involved random mutagenesis of luxO D47E using error-prone PCR, cloning into a conjugative plasmid, introduction into a V. cholerae ΔluxO background, and selection for clones that remained dark in the presence of 100 µM compound 12. Resistant mutants were sequenced and validated by site-directed mutagenesis.

Western blot analysis quantified TcpA in V. cholerae grown under virulence-inducing AKI conditions and VopD in V. parahaemolyticus cultured in LM with Mg²⁺ and oxalate. Cytotoxicity was measured by infecting HeLa cells with bacteria pretreated with compound 12 and quantifying lactate dehydrogenase release using a commercial kit.

Analytical methods included nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy for structural confirmation, high-resolution mass spectrometry for molecular weight verification, and standard organic chemistry techniques for compound characterization. All materials, including solvents and reagents, were obtained from commercial suppliers and used without further purification unless specified.

### Chemical Reactions.

Reaction conditions for the synthesis of the disclosed compounds employed anhydrous solvents under inert atmosphere when necessary, with temperatures ranging from 0°C to reflux depending on the transformation. Purification was typically achieved by flash chromatography on silica gel using ethyl acetate/hexane gradients or by preparative reverse-phase HPLC.

The general procedure for synthesizing the 5-thio-6-azauracil esters involved coupling the thiol-containing heterocycle with appropriately functionalized alkyl halides or alcohols under Mitsunobu or basic conditions. Neopentyl 2-((3,5-dioxo-2,3,4,5-tetrahydro-1,2,4-triazin-6-yl)thio)acetate was prepared by reacting 6-mercapto-3,5-dioxo-2,3,4,5-tetrahydro-1,2,4-triazine with neopentyl bromoacetate in the presence of potassium carbonate in DMF at room temperature.

Isobutyl 2-((3,5-dioxo-2,3,4,5-tetrahydro-1,2,4-triazin-6-yl)thio)acetate was synthesized analogously using isobutyl bromoacetate. Cyclobutylmethyl and cyclopropylmethyl analogs were prepared using the corresponding bromomethylcycloalkanes under identical conditions. The 3-methylbutan-2-yl ester was obtained by Mitsunobu reaction between the thiol and 3-methylbutan-2-ol using triphenylphosphine and diisopropyl azodicarboxylate.

The (trimethylsilyl)methyl derivative was synthesized from (trimethylsilyl)methyl bromide, while the 3,3-dimethylbutan-2-yl analog used 3,3-dimethylbutan-2-ol in a Mitsunobu protocol. Finally, the unsubstituted alkyl chain variant, 2-((3,5-dioxo-2,3,4,5-tetrahydro-1,2,4-triazin-6-yl)thio)acetic acid, was prepared by base hydrolysis of a methyl ester precursor followed by acidification.

All compounds were characterized by ¹H and ¹³C NMR and high-resolution mass spectrometry, confirming structural integrity and purity suitable for biological testing.

## Results

The invention identifies eight Class 1 and three Class 2 small molecules that activate quorum sensing in Vibrio cholerae, as determined by induction of bioluminescence in LCD-locked reporter strains. Class 1 compounds act selectively on the CqsS receptor, while Class 2 compounds activate QS in both ΔcqsA ΔluxS and luxO D47E backgrounds, indicating a target downstream of receptor signaling.

Investigation of the targets of QS-activating compounds revealed that Class 2 molecules, exemplified by compound 11, reduce transcription from a qrr4-gfp fusion without affecting other σ⁵⁴-dependent genes such as vpsR, thereby implicating LuxO as the specific target. Structure-activity relationship studies of Class 2 compounds demonstrated that modifications to the alkyl side chain of the conserved 5-thio-6-azauracil core dramatically influence potency, with increased steric bulk—particularly a tert-butyl carbinol group in compound 12—enhancing activity threefold over lead compound 11.

Biochemical characterization confirmed that Class 2 compounds inhibit LuxO ATPase activity in a dose-dependent, uncompetitive manner, as evidenced by parallel Lineweaver-Burk plots showing reduced Vₘₐₓ and Kₘ. Electrophoretic mobility shift and fluorescence anisotropy assays demonstrated no effect on LuxO–DNA binding affinity, confirming that inhibition is specific to the ATP hydrolysis step required for transcriptional activation.

A genetic screen for LuxO mutants resistant to compound 12 yielded four point mutations (I211F, L215F, L242F, V294L) that map to regions adjacent to the Walker B motif and GAFTGA domain in structural models. These mutants retain ATPase function but are insensitive to inhibition, supporting an allosteric mechanism of action.

Broad-spectrum activation of Vibrio QS was demonstrated by treating V. harveyi luxO D47E and V. parahaemolyticus luxO* strains with compounds 11 and 12, resulting in robust induction of bioluminescence and conversion to opaque colony morphology, respectively. These phenotypes mirror those of ΔluxO mutants, confirming functional conservation of LuxO inhibition across species.

Finally, the invention establishes that LuxO inhibitors function as anti-virulence agents: compound 12 reduces TcpA production in V. cholerae and VopD secretion in V. parahaemolyticus. In HeLa cell cytotoxicity assays, pretreatment with compound 12 significantly delays host cell lysis by a hyper-virulent V. parahaemolyticus strain, providing ex vivo validation of therapeutic potential.

### Investigation of the Targets of the QS Activating Compounds

Testing of Class 1 compounds against receptor deletion mutants confirmed exclusive dependence on CqsS, as none activated QS in a ΔcqsS background. Class 2 compounds, by contrast, activated both screening strains, prompting focused analysis on cytoplasmic QS components. The reduction in qrr4-gfp expression by compound 11, coupled with unchanged vpsR transcription, localized the target to LuxO rather than σ⁵⁴ or downstream RNA effectors.

### Structure-Activity-Relationship of Class 2 Compounds

A focused library of 5-thio-6-azauracil derivatives was synthesized with systematic variation of the ester side chain. Bioluminescence assays in the V. cholerae luxO D47E reporter strain revealed that linear or small alkyl groups conferred weak activity, whereas branched, hydrophobic termini—especially the 3,3-dimethylbutan-2-yl group in compound 12—maximized potency, yielding an EC₅₀ of ~5 µM.

### Class 2 Compounds Inhibit the LuxO ATPase Activity

Whole-cell assays confirmed QS activation by compounds 11 and 12 in strains expressing either wild-type or D47E LuxO, excluding effects on phosphorylation. DNA-binding assays showed unchanged affinity for qrr promoter DNA. In contrast, coupled-enzyme ATPase assays demonstrated dose-dependent inhibition, with kinetic analysis confirming uncompetitive inhibition. A strong correlation (R² > 0.9) between ATPase inhibition and cellular EC₅₀ validated the biochemical mechanism.

### A Genetic Screen to Identify LuxO Mutants Resistant to Compound 12

Random mutagenesis of luxO D47E followed by phenotypic screening yielded four resistant clones. Sequencing identified single amino acid substitutions, all of which, when reconstructed in wild-type LuxO, conferred similar resistance. Purified LuxO D47E/I211F protein exhibited normal ATPase activity that was unaffected by compound 12, confirming loss of inhibitor binding rather than compensatory hyperactivity.

### Broad Spectrum Activation of Vibrio QS

Compounds 11 and 12 induced bioluminescence in V. harveyi luxO D47E by >10⁴-fold and converted V. parahaemolyticus luxO* colonies from translucent to opaque—phenotypes indistinguishable from ΔluxO controls. These results confirm that LuxO inhibition bypasses upstream signaling diversity and directly modulates the conserved core of the Vibrio QS circuit.

### New Chemical Tools for Controlling Virulence in Vibrios

Western blots showed that compound 12 increases HapR and decreases TcpA in V. cholerae, and reduces both intracellular and secreted VopD in V. parahaemolyticus. Cytotoxicity assays demonstrated that HeLa cell lysis by V. parahaemolyticus luxO* is significantly delayed (from ~100% to ~30% at 3 hours) by compound 12 pretreatment, establishing a direct link between LuxO inhibition, virulence repression, and host protection.

## Discussion Of Results

The invention reports the discovery of two classes of molecules that activate quorum sensing in V. cholerae, with Class 2 compounds representing the first known small-molecule inhibitors of an NtrC-family response regulator’s ATPase activity. These molecules serve as valuable chemical probes for dissecting QS signal transduction and as leads for anti-virulence drug development.

Class 1 molecules, acting as CqsS agonists, expand the repertoire of non-native ligands for this promiscuous receptor, though their species selectivity limits broad applicability. In contrast, Class 2 LuxO inhibitors exploit a deeply conserved node in Vibrio QS, enabling pan-vibrio activity.

The uncompetitive inhibition mechanism—binding the LuxO–ATP complex—is unprecedented among response regulator inhibitors, which typically target phosphorylation or DNA binding. The identification of resistance mutations adjacent to the Walker B motif provides a structural roadmap for future inhibitor optimization.

Notably, despite high sequence conservation, E. coli NtrC is insensitive to these compounds, suggesting that subtle conformational differences can be leveraged for selectivity. This finding encourages efforts to develop pathogen-selective NtrC inhibitors.

By repressing virulence without affecting growth, LuxO inhibitors align with anti-virulence paradigms designed to minimize resistance. Their efficacy in reducing cytotoxicity ex vivo supports their potential as prophylactics or adjunctive therapies for vibrio infections.

Finally, the 5-thio-6-azauracil scaffold represents a promising starting point for developing inhibitors of other AAA+ ATPases, potentially extending the impact of this work beyond bacterial signaling to fundamental cellular processes across biology.
