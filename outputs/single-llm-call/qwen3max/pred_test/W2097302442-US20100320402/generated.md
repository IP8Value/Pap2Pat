# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates generally to the field of radiation oncology and, more specifically, to a novel method and system for delivering high-dose spatially fractionated radiotherapy in three dimensions. The invention provides an advanced form of GRID radiotherapy—termed 3D dose lattice radiotherapy—that enables precise concentration of high radiation doses at multiple discrete locations within a tumor volume while minimizing exposure to surrounding healthy tissues. This approach leverages modern radiotherapy technologies, including intensity-modulated radiation therapy (IMRT), robotic radiosurgery systems, and charged particle beam delivery, to create a three-dimensional array of high-dose “vertices” or “islands” embedded within the clinical target volume. The invention is particularly suited for the treatment of bulky, hypoxic, or radioresistant tumors where conventional uniform-dose radiotherapy may be suboptimal, and it represents a significant evolution from historical two-dimensional GRID techniques that suffer from limitations in dose conformity and normal tissue sparing.

## BACKGROUND

High-dose GRID radiotherapy, also known as spatially-fractionated GRID radiotherapy (SFGRT), has been employed since the early 1930s as a specialized technique for treating advanced, bulky tumors. In its original form, 2D GRID therapy utilized orthovoltage X-ray beams passed through a physical grid—a shielded plate with an array of apertures typically circular or square in shape, ranging from 0.5 to 1.5 cm in diameter and spaced approximately 1.8 cm apart. This configuration produced a spatially alternating pattern of high-dose “peaks” and low-dose “valleys” across the irradiated field. The rationale was that the high-dose regions would induce direct tumor cell kill, while adjacent unirradiated or low-dose skin regions would facilitate tissue repair, thereby reducing acute toxicity despite the delivery of very high peak doses (often exceeding 15–20 Gy). Although clinical reports have documented dramatic tumor regressions following 2D GRID therapy, even in cases refractory to conventional treatment, the technique has remained niche due to practical and dosimetric limitations.

Early applications of 2D GRID were constrained by the physics of orthovoltage beams, which deposit maximal dose at the skin surface and exhibit rapid attenuation with depth. Consequently, the highest radiation doses often occurred superficially—outside the actual tumor volume—particularly when treating deep-seated lesions. Even with the transition to megavoltage (MV) X-rays, which offer better depth penetration, the fundamental geometry of 2D GRID results in cylindrical “pipes” of high dose extending through the patient, exposing significant volumes of normal tissue along the beam path to unnecessary radiation. Moreover, because the grid pattern is planar and fixed relative to the skin surface, it cannot conform to the three-dimensional shape of irregular tumors, leading to suboptimal dose distribution within the target and increased risk to critical organs.

Recent advances in radiobiology have provided compelling evidence that the therapeutic efficacy of GRID therapy stems not only from direct cellular damage in high-dose regions but also from non-targeted effects such as the bystander effect and abscopal response. These phenomena involve the release of signaling molecules—including TNF-α, TRAIL, and ceramide—from irradiated cells, which then propagate cell death signals to neighboring unirradiated tumor cells and even to distant metastatic sites. This biological insight suggests that spatially fractionated radiotherapy may be uniquely effective against hypoxic and radioresistant tumors, which are typically less responsive to conventional uniform-dose regimens. However, to fully exploit these mechanisms while minimizing toxicity, a more sophisticated delivery method is required—one that confines high-dose regions strictly within the tumor volume in three dimensions. This need motivates the development of 3D dose lattice radiotherapy, a paradigm that overcomes the inherent limitations of traditional 2D GRID by leveraging contemporary radiotherapy platforms to sculpt a volumetric lattice of high-dose vertices embedded entirely within the target.

## SUMMARY

The present invention introduces a method and system for forming a three-dimensional (3D) dose lattice within a tumor volume, representing a significant advancement over conventional two-dimensional (2D) GRID radiotherapy. In this approach, multiple localized high-dose regions—referred to as vertices or islands—are strategically distributed throughout the three-dimensional space of the clinical target volume, separated by low-dose valleys that facilitate biological bystander signaling while sparing normal tissue. The 3D dose lattice achieves superior dose conformity compared to 2D GRID, ensuring that peak doses are confined exclusively within the tumor and that dose fall-off outside the target is rapid and pronounced.

The invention offers several key advantages over prior art 2D GRID techniques. First, it eliminates the deposition of maximum dose in superficial or non-target tissues, a common drawback of planar grids. Second, it reduces integral dose to surrounding healthy structures by concentrating radiation only at selected internal points rather than along entire beam paths. Third, it enables customization of lattice geometry—such as vertex number, spacing, size, and dose level—based on tumor characteristics, thereby optimizing the peak-to-valley dose ratio for maximal biological effect.

Three primary technical approaches are disclosed for implementing 3D dose lattice radiotherapy. The first employs multileaf collimator (MLC)-based intensity-modulated radiation therapy (IMRT) or volumetric modulated arc therapy (VMAT), wherein dynamic MLC motion during gantry rotation shapes multiple arcs to converge dose at predefined vertices. The second utilizes a robotic linear accelerator capable of delivering numerous non-coplanar, focused photon beams from diverse angles to each vertex, as exemplified by the CyberKnife platform. The third approach, though not yet clinically implemented by the inventors, envisions the use of heavy charged particle beams—such as protons or carbon ions—with spot-scanning nozzles to deposit Bragg peaks precisely at each lattice vertex.

Treatment planning for 3D dose lattice radiotherapy involves defining the tumor volume, selecting lattice parameters (e.g., number and location of vertices, inter-vertex separation, and prescribed peak dose), and employing an optimization algorithm to generate a deliverable plan that maximizes peak-to-valley contrast while respecting normal tissue constraints. The process may be automated using inverse planning software integrated into commercial treatment planning systems.

In summary, the invention provides a clinically feasible, biologically rational, and technologically advanced method for delivering spatially fractionated radiotherapy in three dimensions. It enhances therapeutic ratio by combining physical dose localization with emerging radiobiological principles, offering a promising strategy for treating complex, bulky, or resistant malignancies with reduced toxicity.

## DESCRIPTION OF INVENTION

The term “lattice,” as used herein, refers to a three-dimensional arrangement of discrete, localized high-dose regions—termed “vertices”—distributed within a tumor volume, without requiring strict geometric symmetry or periodicity. A “dose lattice” denotes the resulting spatial dose distribution characterized by alternating peaks (at vertices) and valleys (in intervening regions), with peak doses typically ≥12 Gy and valley doses ≤3 Gy. The formation of a 3D dose lattice is achieved by converging radiation beams from multiple directions to focus high dose at each vertex while allowing rapid dose fall-off between them.

In contrast, traditional 2D GRID radiation therapy employs a single incident beam passing through a physical grid, producing a planar array of high-dose columns that extend through the patient regardless of tumor depth or shape. This results in non-conformal dose deposition, with peak doses often occurring outside the target volume. The 3D dose lattice overcomes this by embedding all high-dose regions entirely within the gross tumor volume (GTV), as demonstrated in clinical planning examples using both lung and pancreatic tumors.

One embodiment utilizes non-coplanar focused beams delivered by a robotic linear accelerator, such as the CyberKnife system. In this approach, each vertex serves as an isocenter, and 40–50 non-coplanar beams, collimated to small fields (e.g., 7.5 mm), are directed sequentially to each isocenter from diverse angles. The convergence of these beams creates a near-spherical high-dose island at each vertex, with inter-vertex separation of approximately 2–2.5 cm, yielding a peak-to-valley dose ratio of 3:1 to 5:1.

Alternatively, MLC-based IMRT or VMAT (e.g., RapidArc) can generate a 3D dose lattice using dynamic multileaf collimation during gantry rotation. Multiple arcs—typically six—are optimized to deposit dose at predefined vertices within the tumor. This method leverages existing linear accelerator hardware without modification and is compatible with respiratory gating or real-time tumor tracking systems to maintain lattice integrity during motion.

A third envisioned embodiment employs heavy charged particle beams, such as protons or carbon ions, delivered via spot-scanning technology. In this approach, the scanning nozzle is programmed to place a Bragg peak—or a minimally spread-out Bragg peak—at each lattice vertex, exploiting the inherent sharp distal fall-off of particle beams to enhance peak-to-valley contrast and further spare distal normal tissues.

The peak-to-valley dose characteristic is central to the invention’s mechanism, enabling both direct tumor kill in high-dose regions and indirect killing via bystander signaling in low-dose valleys. An automatic treatment planning process is employed, wherein an optimization algorithm iteratively adjusts beam weights, MLC positions, or scanning parameters to maximize dose heterogeneity within the target while minimizing dose to organs at risk. The objective function prioritizes high peak dose, low valley dose, and steep dose gradients.

The invention is applicable to photon-based systems, including conventional LINACs and robotic platforms, and may be extended to rotating radioisotope assemblies or other emerging modalities. It incorporates by reference all standard radiotherapy planning and delivery techniques known in the art. The scope encompasses any method that creates a 3D array of high-dose vertices within a tumor, regardless of beam type, delivery geometry, or lattice symmetry. Variations include lattices with one or two vertices for small tumors, non-spherical vertex shapes, or adaptive re-planning based on tumor response. Equivalents include any system achieving similar peak-to-valley dose distributions using alternative beam arrangements or technologies. Modifications may involve integration with immunotherapy or chemotherapy. In sum, the invention’s scope includes all embodiments and equivalents that realize the core principle of 3D-conformal, spatially fractionated high-dose radiotherapy.

## EQUIVALENTS

The invention is not limited to the specific embodiments described herein. Those skilled in the art will recognize that numerous equivalents may be employed to achieve the same functional result of a three-dimensional dose lattice within a tumor volume. Such equivalents include, but are not limited to, alternative beam delivery systems (e.g., tomotherapy, helical IMRT, or MRI-guided LINACs), different collimation methods (e.g., custom blocks, cone-based focusing, or variable aperture devices), varied particle types (e.g., helium or oxygen ions), or computational planning algorithms that produce comparable peak-to-valley dose distributions. Any modification that preserves the essential feature—localized high-dose vertices embedded in 3D within the target, separated by low-dose regions, and sparing normal tissue—falls within the scope of the claimed invention.
