# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

Unstructured data constitutes a vast and rapidly growing portion of information in the medical domain, encompassing textbooks, clinical guidelines, electronic medical records, scientific journal articles, and patient narratives. Unlike structured data, which is organized in predefined formats such as relational databases or standardized ontologies, unstructured data exists in free-form natural language, making it inherently difficult to process, query, or integrate into computational systems. The sheer volume and velocity of this unstructured content present significant challenges for healthcare professionals who must synthesize relevant knowledge from disparate sources to support timely and accurate clinical decision-making. This challenge has motivated the development and application of natural-language processing (NLP) techniques to extract structured, actionable knowledge from unstructured text. NLP aims to bridge the gap between human language and machine-readable representations by identifying entities, relationships, and events embedded within textual content. However, despite substantial progress in general-domain NLP, existing techniques face critical limitations when applied to the medical domain. These limitations include insufficient coverage of domain-specific terminology, high sensitivity to syntactic variation, poor generalization from sparse labeled data, and an inability to leverage the rich semantic structure encoded in medical ontologies. Among the various NLP tasks, relation extraction—defined as the identification of semantic relationships between named entities—plays a pivotal role in transforming unstructured text into structured knowledge. Relation extraction enables downstream applications such as clinical question answering, evidence-based decision support, and knowledge base construction. Traditional approaches to relation extraction fall into three broad categories: rule-based methods that rely on handcrafted linguistic patterns; feature-based supervised learning models that encode lexical, syntactic, and semantic cues into fixed-dimensional vectors; and kernel-based methods that operate over structured representations like dependency or constituency parse trees. More recently, distant supervision has emerged as a scalable paradigm for generating training data by aligning external knowledge bases with text corpora, though this approach introduces significant noise due to the assumption that co-occurrence of entity pairs implies a known relation. Despite these advances, the importance of relation extraction in the medical domain cannot be overstated, as it directly facilitates the conversion of narrative clinical text into computable assertions that can inform diagnosis, treatment selection, and prognosis. The ability to automatically extract relations such as “treats,” “causes,” or “manifests” from millions of clinical documents represents a foundational capability for next-generation clinical decision support systems.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides a method for semantic relation extraction from unstructured medical text using a manifold-based learning framework that effectively leverages both labeled and unlabeled data. The method begins by identifying a set of clinically relevant “super-relations”—high-level semantic relations that are critical for clinical decision-making, such as “treats,” “contraindicates,” “causes,” and “manifests.” Training data for each super-relation is collected through a combination of distant supervision from the Unified Medical Language System (UMLS) knowledge base and strategic manual annotation of representative sentence instances. To address the scarcity and noise inherent in labeled data, the invention employs a manifold model that preserves the intrinsic geometric structure of the data distribution. This model assigns similar scores to semantically similar relation instances, whether labeled or unlabeled, thereby mitigating overfitting and improving generalization. A key advantage of the manifold model is its closed-form solution, which enables efficient computation and scalability to large corpora. Furthermore, the model incorporates label weights to reflect confidence in noisy labels derived from distant supervision or crowdsourced annotations. By integrating domain-specific parsing, UMLS semantic typing, and manifold regularization, the invention achieves superior performance over conventional linear and kernel-based classifiers in extracting medical relations from real-world clinical text. The resulting relation extractors are not only highly accurate but also computationally efficient, enabling the construction of large-scale medical knowledge bases that complement and extend existing resources like UMLS.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The invention introduces manifold models for semantic relation extraction, specifically tailored to the medical domain. Central to this approach is the concept of “super-relations”—a curated set of high-level semantic relations that are essential for clinical reasoning and decision-making. These include, but are not limited to, “treats,” “contraindicates,” “causes,” “manifests,” “diagnoses,” “prevents,” and “located in.” Each super-relation subsumes multiple fine-grained relations from the UMLS Metathesaurus, providing a generalized yet clinically meaningful abstraction. The training data gathering phase begins with a large medical corpus comprising 80 million sentences drawn from medical textbooks, journals, MEDLINE abstracts, and Wikipedia. From this corpus, example data for each super-relation is obtained by identifying sentences containing pairs of terms associated with known CUI (Concept Unique Identifier) pairs in UMLS that instantiate the target relation. This corpus is defined as the collection of all unstructured medical text processed by the system, and the example data consists of candidate sentence instances potentially expressing a given super-relation. To minimize manual annotation effort, a subset of these candidates is selected for labeling using K-medoids clustering, where cluster centers represent the most prototypical or representative instances. Labeled data refers to sentence examples manually annotated as positive or negative for a specific relation, while unlabeled data comprises the remaining unvetted candidates. The output of this phase is a hybrid training dataset containing both labeled and unlabeled examples for each super-relation.

Medical relation extraction is motivated by the urgent need to convert unstructured clinical narratives into structured knowledge for decision support. However, it faces unique challenges, including the ambiguity of medical terminology, the sparsity of labeled data, and the complexity of entity recognition in real-world settings where perfect entity mentions are unavailable. To address these, the invention introduces a manifold model for medical relation extraction that integrates domain-specific parsing and semantic typing. The system employs MedicalESG, a medical-adapted English Slot Grammar parser, which is ten times faster than MetaMap while maintaining high accuracy in identifying medical concepts. Semantic types from UMLS—133 categories such as “Disease or Syndrome,” “Pharmacologic Substance,” and “Antibiotic”—are assigned to each relation argument. When an entity maps to multiple semantic types (e.g., “Hepatitis B” as both a disease and a vaccine), all types are retained, and the model learns context-sensitive weighting during training. Label weight is considered to account for uncertainty in distant supervision labels; for instance, if a relation has a 97% noise rate, its positive labels are assigned low confidence. The manifold model admits a closed-form solution, ensuring computational efficiency.

The identification of super-relations is grounded in empirical analysis of 5,000 clinical questions from the American College of Physicians’ Doctor Dilemma dataset, which revealed that seven relations cover over 60% of clinically relevant queries. Training data for these super-relations is collected by first mapping UMLS relations to super-relations, then retrieving sentences containing CUI pairs linked by those relations. Parsing and detecting medical entity mentions is performed using MedicalESG, which generates dependency parse trees and associates words with CUIs. Semantic types are assigned via UMLS lookup, and multiple types per word are integrated into the feature representation. Candidate examples are cleaned by clustering with K-medoids and manually annotating cluster centers. The K-medoids algorithm partitions sentence instances into k clusters (k = 3,000–6,000) based on bag-of-words similarity of dependency paths, and cluster centers are selected for annotation. Annotators label each instance as positive or negative, yielding a noise rate per super-relation (e.g., 16% for “treats,” 97% for “contraindicates”). The negative training set is expanded by adding representative examples from unrelated UMLS relations.

Parse tree generation is performed by MedicalESG, which outputs grammatical dependencies. Words are associated with CUIs using UMLS, and semantic types are assigned accordingly. The training data collection process involves identifying key relations, obtaining example data, annotating a subset, and outputting the final dataset. For relation extraction, each example is represented by eight feature groups: semantic types of both arguments, dependency path features, incoming/outgoing link features, and topic and bag-of-words features derived from LSI over the medical corpus. These features are mapped into a single vector space, enabling the use of linear classifiers. The relation extraction problem is formalized as learning a mapping function f that projects feature vectors to scores approximating true labels (+1 or –1) while preserving manifold topology. The manifold model constructs a graph Laplacian matrix L from a weight matrix W, where W_ij = exp(–||x_i – x_j||²). A cost function C(f) = Σ α_i (f^T x_i – y_i)² + µ f^T X L X^T f is minimized, balancing label fidelity and manifold smoothness. The first term penalizes deviation from labeled examples, weighted by α_i (label confidence); the second enforces similarity among neighboring instances. Label confidence is estimated from noise rates or annotator agreement. Theorem 1 states that f = (X(A + µL)X^T)^+ X A V^T minimizes C(f), where A is diagonal with α_i, and V is the label vector. The proof follows from Lagrange multipliers and matrix calculus.

In a QA framework, the extracted relations support question and topic analysis, hypothesis generation, candidate answer generation, scoring, and synthesis. Learned models from the manifold approach are used in final confidence merging and ranking. The system comprises a processing system with one or more processors, system memory, I/O and network adapters, mass storage, and software implementing the manifold model. A screen, user interface adapter, and display adapter enable interaction. Technical effects include improved accuracy, reduced overfitting, and scalability. Benefits include enhanced clinical decision support, automated knowledge base construction, and efficient use of unlabeled data.
