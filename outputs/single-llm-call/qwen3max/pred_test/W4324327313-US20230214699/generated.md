# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

### 1. Technical Field

The present invention relates generally to the field of quantum computing, and more specifically to methods and systems for simulating stochastic processes on quantum computing devices. The invention provides a novel framework for representing and generating quantum states that encode trajectories of stochastic processes—particularly Brownian motion and its generalizations such as fractional Brownian motion—using analog quantum encodings that exploit the spectral properties of these processes. The disclosed techniques enable highly efficient preparation of quantum states corresponding to stochastic trajectories with significantly reduced gate complexity and circuit depth compared to conventional digital simulation approaches. These methods are particularly advantageous for applications in quantum-enhanced Monte Carlo estimation, financial derivative pricing, and statistical analysis of anomalous diffusion phenomena.

### 2. Description of Related Art

Stochastic processes are mathematical models used to describe systems that evolve randomly over time, with applications spanning physics, engineering, biology, and finance. In computational settings, simulating such processes typically involves generating sample trajectories according to their probabilistic laws. Classical Monte Carlo methods estimate expectations of functions of these processes by averaging over many independently generated samples, requiring Θ(1/ε²) samples to achieve additive error ε. Quantum algorithms based on amplitude estimation offer a quadratic speedup, theoretically requiring only Θ(1/ε) coherent simulations of the process to achieve the same precision. However, realizing this advantage in practice hinges critically on the efficiency of the underlying quantum simulation subroutine.

Quantum state representation plays a pivotal role in determining the feasibility of such simulations. Prior art has largely focused on *digital representations*, wherein an entire trajectory (v₁, v₂, ..., v_T) is stored in the computational basis of a quantum register, with amplitudes encoding the probability of each trajectory. While straightforward to construct by quantizing classical sampling algorithms—replacing classical randomness with superposition states and compiling logic into reversible gates—this approach often results in prohibitively deep circuits, especially for complex processes. The gate count typically scales linearly or worse with the number of timesteps T, undermining potential quantum advantages when compounded over the Θ(1/ε) repetitions required by amplitude estimation.

Limitations of current simulation methods are further exacerbated by hardware constraints. Even with future fault-tolerant quantum computers, the overhead from quantum error correction may outweigh the theoretical quadratic speedup if the underlying simulation circuit is too deep. Consequently, there exists a pressing need for more gate-efficient quantum simulation techniques that leverage intrinsic mathematical structures of specific stochastic processes.

One promising application domain is quantitative finance, where stochastic processes model asset price dynamics. For instance, options on realized variance depend on time-averaged squared returns, which are naturally expressed as functionals of the underlying volatility process. If volatility follows fractional Brownian motion—a process exhibiting long-range dependence characterized by a Hurst parameter H—classical simulation requires O(T log T) operations per sample using fast Fourier transforms. Translating this directly into a quantum circuit yields a digital simulation with comparable depth, negating much of the potential benefit from amplitude estimation. Thus, the development of specialized, structure-exploiting quantum simulation methods is essential to unlock practical quantum advantages in such high-value applications.

## SUMMARY

The present invention introduces a new method for simulating stochastic processes on quantum computers, termed *analog simulation*, which fundamentally differs from prior digital approaches by encoding trajectory values in the amplitudes of quantum states rather than in computational basis labels. This analog representation leverages the exponential capacity of Hilbert space, enabling the description of T-timestep trajectories using only O(log T) qubits. The core innovation lies in exploiting the spectral decomposition of certain stochastic processes—particularly Brownian motion and fractional Brownian motion—whose Fourier coefficients are independent Gaussian random variables with known variance decay profiles.

The key points of the simulation method include: first, recognizing that the Wiener series representation of Brownian motion expresses trajectories as inverse Fourier transforms of sequences of independent Gaussians; second, utilizing the quantum Fourier transform (QFT), which can implement the discrete Fourier transform in polylogarithmic depth, to map efficiently preparable frequency-domain states to time-domain trajectory encodings; third, developing a novel "data loading the data loader" technique to coherently prepare superpositions over ensembles of such trajectories, which is essential for compatibility with quantum amplitude estimation; and fourth, generalizing these ideas to fractional Brownian motion by adjusting the power-law decay of variances in the spectral domain according to the Hurst parameter H.

The application of this simulation method enables end-to-end quantum algorithms for estimating properties of stochastic processes with improved asymptotic scaling. For example, in pricing variance swaps under models where volatility follows fractional Brownian motion, the invention achieves a runtime of O(polylog(T) ε⁻ᶜ) with 3/2 < c < 2, outperforming classical Monte Carlo’s O(T ε⁻²) and offering advantages over standard quantum mean estimation in regimes of large T. Similarly, for statistical tests distinguishing diffusive regimes in single-particle tracking, the method provides efficient estimation of time-averaged mean-square displacements, facilitating hypothesis testing with reduced computational overhead.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The invention begins by introducing a quantum state representation of stochastic processes that departs from conventional digital encodings. Instead of storing full trajectories in basis states, the analog representation encodes the value of the process at time t in the amplitude associated with the basis state |t⟩. Specifically, for a trajectory (v₁, v₂, ..., v_T), the analog encoding is the quantum state ∑ₜ₌₁ᵀ vₜ |t⟩, normalized appropriately. This representation inherently requires only ⌈log₂ T⌉ qubits to index time steps, dramatically reducing spatial resources compared to digital methods.

The basis state of the quantum computing system is defined over a register of n = ⌈log₂ T⌉ qubits, where each computational basis state |t⟩ for t ∈ {1, ..., T} corresponds to a discrete time point. The amplitude function of a price trajectory—or more generally, any stochastic process realization—is then the mapping t ↦ vₜ, embedded as the complex amplitudes of the quantum state. This formulation is particularly well-suited for processes whose trajectories exhibit smoothness or spectral sparsity, as such properties translate into compressibility in the amplitude domain.

The motivation for using quantum algorithms for financial instruments stems from the prevalence of path-dependent derivatives whose payoffs are functionals of stochastic trajectories. Traditional Monte Carlo pricing is computationally intensive, and quantum amplitude estimation promises quadratic speedups—if efficient coherent simulation oracles exist. The analog representation facilitates this by enabling direct access to time averages and inner products via simple measurements, bypassing the need to read out individual trajectory points.

To handle the probabilistic nature of stochastic processes, the invention defines a mixed state ρ that represents the ensemble of all possible trajectories. Formally, ρ = 𝔼[|ψ⟩⟨ψ|], where the expectation is over trajectories sampled from the process distribution, and |ψ⟩ is the analog encoding of a single trajectory. Preparing copies of ρ alone is insufficient for amplitude estimation, which requires a *coherent* superposition over trajectories entangled with ancillary "garbage" registers encoding the underlying randomness.

A critical insight is that for Brownian motion and fractional Brownian motion, the mixed state ρ can be efficiently approximated using the discrete cosine transform (DCT). The DCT arises naturally because the Wiener series for Brownian motion on [0,1] uses sine functions, and the discretized version maps to the DCT-II under appropriate boundary conditions. The DCT is noted as the real part of the quantum Fourier transform (QFT) when applied to real-valued, symmetrically extended signals. This connection allows leveraging the QFT’s efficient quantum implementation.

An efficient algorithm for implementing the DCT on a quantum computing system is outlined by adapting known QFT circuits. Since the DCT can be embedded within a larger QFT of doubled size with appropriate symmetrization, existing logarithmic-depth QFT constructions yield a DCT circuit with O(log T) depth and O(log T) qubits. Controlled-phase gates and Hadamard operations are arranged in a recursive pattern to achieve this transformation.

The preparation of the target mixed state σ—which approximates ρ—proceeds by first preparing a state in the frequency domain whose amplitudes are independent Gaussian random variables with variances decaying as k⁻²ᴴ⁻¹ for fractional Brownian motion with Hurst parameter H. The linearity of the DCT ensures that applying it to such a state yields a superposition whose reduced density matrix matches the covariance structure of the desired process.

For standard Brownian motion (H = 1/2), the Fourier coefficients have variances proportional to 1/k. Truncating the infinite Wiener series to L terms introduces an ℓ² approximation error that scales as O(1/L), allowing L = O(1/ε) to achieve ε-accuracy. For H > 1/2, the decay is faster, requiring fewer terms: L = O(ε⁻¹/²ᴴ).

A probability distribution over the DCT coefficients is induced by the Gaussianity of the underlying noise. Specifically, the vector of coefficients (a₁, ..., a_L) consists of i.i.d. N(0,1) variables scaled by k⁻ᴴ⁻⁰·⁵. The goal shifts to preparing a quantum state σ′ that is the analog encoding of a trajectory drawn from this truncated process.

To achieve this, the invention introduces a quantum data loader algorithm A, which prepares amplitude-encoded states from classical vectors using a binary tree of rotation angles. For a fixed vector x, A takes as input a set of angles derived from x and outputs |x⟩. Crucially, for Haar-random unit vectors (equivalent to normalized Gaussian vectors), the data loader angles are statistically independent and follow known beta and gamma distributions due to rotational symmetry.

The "data loading the data loader" algorithm operates by first preparing a quantum state |D′⟩ whose amplitudes encode the probability distribution over the data loader angles θ required to produce Gaussian-distributed output states. Because the angle distributions are independent for Haar-random inputs, |D′⟩ factorizes and can be prepared efficiently by loading each angle register separately using inverse transform sampling or related techniques.

Applying the data loader algorithm A to |D′⟩—that is, using the angle registers as controls for the rotations in A—and subsequently tracing out the angle registers yields the desired mixed state σ′. This procedure effectively integrates over the angle distribution, producing a state whose ensemble matches the truncated stochastic process.

For Brownian motion, explicit formulas for the data loader angles are derived from the gamma distribution of subtree norm squares. The inverse map from angles back to vector components is implicit in the data loader’s recursive structure but need not be computed explicitly during state preparation.

Normalization considerations are addressed by including flag qubits or post-selection to handle sub-normalized states, ensuring physical validity of the prepared density matrices. The overall method for simulating a stochastic process thus comprises: (1) truncating the spectral representation to L terms; (2) preparing the angle distribution state |D′⟩; (3) applying the data loader A controlled on |D′⟩; (4) tracing out ancillas to obtain σ′; and (5) optionally applying the DCT to move to the time domain.

### Example Method

In an exemplary embodiment, the method receives as input a description of a stochastic process—such as fractional Brownian motion specified by drift, variance, and Hurst parameter H—and a time horizon discretized into T steps. The system determines a first quantum circuit configured to prepare a mixed quantum state ρ′ that approximates the analog encoding of the process. This involves computing the discrete cosine transform (DCT) series of the stochastic process, which for fBM yields coefficients with variances proportional to k⁻²ᴴ⁻¹.

From this spectral representation, a probability distribution for the coefficients in the DCT series is determined, namely independent Gaussians with the prescribed variances. This induces a probability distribution over the rotation angles D′ required by the data loader algorithm to produce such coefficient vectors. The first quantum circuit is then executed on a quantum computing device to generate the mixed quantum state ρ′, which serves as input to downstream quantum algorithms such as amplitude estimation for expectation value computation.

The classical computing system 410 handles high-level orchestration, parameter calculation, and result interpretation, while the quantum computing system 420 executes the core state preparation and transformation routines. Classical computing system 410 and quantum computing system 420 are communicatively coupled via a control interface.

Quantum computing system 420 includes a plurality of qubits 450 arranged in one or more qubit registers, each manipulated by dedicated qubit controllers 440. Each qubit 450 may be realized using superconducting circuits, trapped ions, or other physical qubit technologies, with qubit controller 440 providing precise microwave or laser pulses for gate operations. In some embodiments, a separate qubit controller 440 is allocated per qubit 450 to enable parallel gate execution.

Execution of a quantum routine on computing system 400 begins with the classical generation of a quantum program specifying gate sequences and measurement protocols. This program is compiled into low-level instructions for qubit controllers 440, which execute the quantum program by applying time-synchronized control signals to qubits 450. Upon completion, measurement outcomes are recorded and processed classically to compute final results, which may include estimated expectation values or statistical test outcomes.

Classical computing system 410 includes a processor 502 for general-purpose computation, coupled to a chipset 504 that manages data flow between components. Memory 506 provides volatile storage for active programs and data, while storage device 508 offers non-volatile persistence for software and datasets. User interaction is facilitated via keyboard 510 and pointing device 514, with visual feedback rendered through graphics adapter 512 to display 518.
