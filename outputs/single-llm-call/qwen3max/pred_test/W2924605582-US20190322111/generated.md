## FIELD

The present invention relates generally to the field of functional printing and magnetic nanomaterials, and more specifically to the magnetic field patterning of nickel nanofibers. The invention provides a method for producing aligned nickel nanowires through the thermal reduction of a metal-organic decomposition (MOD) precursor ink under the influence of a magnetic field, enabling the fabrication of printed, geometrically patterned, magnetically aligned metallic nanowire structures with enhanced electrical and magnetic anisotropy. The technology is particularly applicable to the fields of printed electronics, magnetic sensors, data storage, and flexible electronic devices.

## BACKGROUND

The development of printable metallic inks has enabled significant advances in the field of printed electronics, where conductive features are deposited onto substrates using additive manufacturing techniques. Metallic inks are typically formulated as either nanoparticle dispersions or molecular precursor solutions. Nanoparticle inks, while offering high metal loading, suffer from inherent stability issues such as particle agglomeration and solvent evaporation, which can lead to nozzle clogging and inconsistent deposition during printing processes such as aerosol jet or inkjet printing. These challenges are particularly pronounced in aerosol-based systems, where solvent loss during atomization can alter the ink’s rheological properties mid-process, compromising print fidelity.

In contrast, metal-organic decomposition (MOD) precursor inks—also known as molecular or solution-based inks—offer improved homogeneity and long-term stability because they consist of dissolved metal complexes rather than suspended particles. However, MOD inks historically have lower metal content by weight compared to nanoparticle inks, which can limit the conductivity of the final printed feature. Despite this limitation, MOD inks provide a cleaner processing route, as their thermal decomposition yields volatile organic byproducts that evaporate completely, leaving behind high-purity metallic films without the need for post-processing purification.

Nickel, as a ferromagnetic transition metal, possesses a unique combination of properties including good electrical conductivity, high saturation magnetization, excellent corrosion resistance, and a high thermal coefficient of resistance, making it highly desirable for magnetic and electronic applications. However, the formulation of nickel-based precursor inks has been challenging due to the tendency of nickel to oxidize upon exposure to air during or after reduction. Prior art methods for fabricating nickel nanowires have relied on multi-step, template-assisted syntheses—such as hydrothermal growth, electrochemical deposition within porous membranes, or chemical reduction in autoclaves—followed by careful handling under inert atmospheres to prevent oxidation. These approaches are not compatible with direct, scalable, and substrate-integrated printing processes.

Digital printing techniques—including inkjet, aerosol jet, screen printing, and microextrusion—have emerged as powerful tools for depositing functional materials with high spatial resolution. Among these, aerosol jet printing stands out for its ability to print on both planar and non-planar surfaces with a large standoff distance, enabling deposition on complex geometries. Yet, despite the widespread use of silver and copper inks in printed electronics, there remain very few examples of successfully printed ferromagnetic metals, and even fewer that achieve controlled nanostructure alignment during the printing process itself.

The ability to align nanowires during fabrication is critical for applications that exploit shape anisotropy, such as giant magnetoresistive sensors, magnetic memory elements, and optical polarizers. Conventional alignment methods require external fields applied post-deposition or rely on mechanical stretching or electric fields, which add complexity and cost. Thus, there exists a long-felt but unmet need for a simple, one-step, printable method to produce aligned nickel nanowires directly on a target substrate, without templates, under ambient conditions, and with tunable electrical and magnetic properties.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides a method for producing aligned nickel nanowires by printing a nickel metal-organic decomposition (MOD) precursor ink onto a substrate, followed by thermal curing in the presence of a magnetic field. This process enables the direct, template-free formation of geometrically patterned, magnetically aligned metallic nanowires with enhanced anisotropic electrical and magnetic properties. The invention further encompasses the sequential printing, curing, and magnetic alignment of two or more distinct geometric patterns, allowing for the creation of interlayered nanofiber architectures—such as orthogonal nanowire grids—that exhibit isotropic or directionally tailored functionalities.

In one embodiment, a homogeneous nickel MOD ink is formulated from nickel formate and ethylenediamine, yielding a stable, water-based solution suitable for aerosol jet printing. Upon deposition, the ink is thermally reduced at approximately 240°C, a temperature sufficient to decompose the organic ligands and reduce nickel to its metallic state. When this reduction is performed under a weak homogeneous magnetic field (e.g., 250 Oe), the nascent nickel nanoparticles self-assemble along the field lines before sintering into continuous, aligned nanowires. In the absence of a magnetic field, the same process yields a porous, randomly oriented nickel film.

The resulting aligned nanowires exhibit significantly lower electrical resistivity along the alignment axis compared to the perpendicular direction, and demonstrate enhanced magnetic remanence parallel to the nanowire orientation due to shape anisotropy. By printing multiple layers with alternating magnetic field directions during curing, a nanowire grid structure is formed that exhibits near-isotropic electrical conductivity and elevated magnetic remanence in multiple directions. This capability enables the design of multifunctional printed devices with spatially programmable electromagnetic responses.

The method is compatible with a wide range of substrates, including rigid glass and flexible polymers, and can be adapted to various digital printing platforms beyond aerosol jet, such as inkjet or flexography, by adjusting the ink’s rheological properties. The invention thus represents a scalable, ambient-condition, single-step route to functional magnetic nanomaterials with applications in sensing, data storage, flexible electronics, and metamaterials.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The invention is practiced by first preparing a printable ink comprising a nickel-containing metal-organic complex dissolved in a suitable solvent. In a preferred embodiment, nickel formate is complexed with ethylenediamine in aqueous solution to form a homogeneous MOD ink. Additives such as polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP) and surfactants like BYK 333 may be included to stabilize the solution and tune surface tension and viscosity for compatibility with specific printing techniques. The ink is characterized to ensure appropriate rheological properties: for aerosol jet printing, a surface tension of approximately 44 mN/m and a viscosity of about 3.2 cP are optimal.

Prior to printing, the substrate—such as a glass microscope slide or a flexible polymer—is cleaned and optionally treated with atmospheric plasma to enhance wettability and adhesion. The ink is then deposited using a digital printing method; in the exemplary case, aerosol jet printing is employed, wherein the ink is ultrasonically atomized, focused by a sheath gas, and directed onto the substrate through a nozzle at a controlled standoff distance and translational speed. A solid square pattern of 1 cm × 1 cm is printed with a wet film thickness of approximately 4 µm per layer.

Following deposition, the printed film is thermally cured. Thermogravimetric and differential thermal analysis reveal that complete reduction of nickel occurs at 235–240°C, where the organic components decompose into volatile gases (e.g., CO, CO₂, H₂), creating a self-reducing atmosphere that prevents oxidation. Curing is performed at 240°C for 15 minutes. Critically, when this curing step is conducted in the presence of a homogeneous magnetic field—applied via permanent magnets or electromagnets oriented in a desired direction—the nickel nanoparticles formed during decomposition align along the field lines before sintering into continuous nanowires. Each nanowire appears as a “string of pearls” under scanning electron microscopy, composed of fused 100–250 nm nanoparticles oriented parallel to the magnetic field.

In the absence of a magnetic field, the same thermal treatment yields a porous, granular nickel film with no preferential orientation. The degree of alignment in magnetically cured samples is quantified using 2D fast Fourier transform (FFT) analysis of SEM images, which reveals sharp peaks at angles corresponding to the field direction, whereas non-magnetic samples show isotropic FFT patterns.

Electrical characterization via four-point probe demonstrates that aligned nanowire films exhibit lower resistivity along the alignment axis (e.g., ~560 µΩ·cm for a nanowire grid structure, approximately 80 times that of bulk nickel). Magnetic hysteresis measurements confirm enhanced remanent magnetization parallel to the nanowire axis (20 emu/g) compared to the perpendicular direction (7 emu/g), evidencing shape-induced magnetic anisotropy. Grid structures formed by alternating field directions during multilayer printing show balanced properties in orthogonal directions, enabling isotropic conduction and multidirectional magnetic response.

The process is demonstrated on flexible substrates, confirming compatibility with bendable electronics. The technology is not limited to nickel; other ferromagnetic metals such as cobalt or iron may be used with analogous MOD precursors. The method enables the fabrication of aligned interlayered nanofibers with enhanced isotropic electrical properties when configured as grids, and offers a pathway to custom electromagnetic metamaterials through geometric patterning and field-directed assembly.

### EXAMPLE 1

In a specific implementation, analytical-grade nickel formate (0.8 g, 5.38 mmol) was dissolved in a mixture of ethylenediamine (0.72 mL, 10.77 mmol) and distilled water (4 mL) under stirring for 15 minutes at room temperature. The solution was filtered through a 0.2 µm syringe filter, and additives were introduced: 1% w/w polyvinylpyrrolidone (MW 3500) and 20 µL of a 10% v/v aqueous solution of BYK 333 surfactant. The resulting ink exhibited a surface tension of 44.01 mN/m and a viscosity of 3.2 cP, as measured by pendant drop tensiometry and microviscometry, respectively.

Glass microscope slides were cleaned with isopropyl alcohol and treated with atmospheric plasma using a Surfx Atomflo system to improve ink spreading. Aerosol jet printing was performed using a NanoJet printer with ultrasonic atomization at 1–2 MHz, air as carrier gas, and a sheath gas flow to focus the aerosol stream. A 1 cm × 1 cm solid square was printed using a 25-gauge tip at a 5 mm standoff distance and 2 mm/s stage speed, yielding a wet film thickness of ~4 µm.

Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) and differential thermal analysis (DTA) showed two-stage decomposition: solvent evaporation below 100°C and nickel reduction at 235°C. Based on this, curing was conducted at 240°C for 15 minutes. Samples were processed in two conditions: (1) without a magnetic field, and (2) under a 250 Oe homogeneous magnetic field aligned in a fixed direction.

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) revealed that magnetically cured samples formed aligned nanowires, while non-magnetic samples yielded random particulate films. Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) confirmed the presence of elemental nickel with minor carbon and oxygen residues. Film thickness was measured via optical and stylus profilometry, showing shrinkage due to solvent and ligand loss.

Electrical resistivity was measured using a four-point probe. Aligned nanowire films showed directional conductivity, with lowest resistivity along the alignment axis. A two-layer nanowire grid—printed with orthogonal magnetic fields during each cure—achieved a resistivity of 560 µΩ·cm, representing a significant improvement over prior art.

Magnetic characterization via vibrating sample magnetometry (VSM) showed that aligned films exhibited a remanent magnetization of 20 emu/g parallel to the nanowires and 7 emu/g perpendicular, compared to 10 emu/g in both directions for unaligned films. The saturation magnetization was consistent at ~30 emu/g across all samples, matching bulk nickel values.

The process was successfully replicated on flexible substrates, demonstrating feasibility for wearable and conformal electronics. The Ni MOD ink was shown to be stable, printable, and capable of producing pure, aligned nanowires without templates or post-processing, establishing a novel route to functional magnetic nanomaterials.
