# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD OF INVENTION

The present invention relates to methods and systems for predicting the in vivo toxicity of oligonucleotides, particularly single-stranded antisense oligonucleotides (ASOs), prior to their administration in animal models or humans. More specifically, the invention provides an in vitro assay platform that utilizes primary mammalian hepatocytes to assess the hepatotoxic potential of oligonucleotides by measuring a defined set of molecular and cellular biomarkers that correlate with in vivo liver injury. This predictive capability enables the early identification and selection of oligonucleotide candidates with reduced risk of hepatotoxicity, thereby streamlining drug development, reducing reliance on animal testing, and enhancing the safety profile of therapeutic oligonucleotides intended for clinical use.

## BACKGROUND

Antisense oligonucleotides (ASOs) represent a promising class of therapeutics capable of modulating gene expression through sequence-specific binding to target RNA molecules, leading to degradation via RNase H1 recruitment or steric blockade of translation or splicing. Over the past three decades, significant advances have been made in oligonucleotide chemistry, including the incorporation of modified nucleosides such as locked nucleic acids (LNAs) and phosphorothioate internucleoside linkages, which enhance nuclease resistance, binding affinity, and pharmacokinetic properties. Despite these innovations, the clinical translation of ASOs has been hindered by dose-limiting toxicities, particularly hepatotoxicity and immunotoxicity, which often emerge during preclinical or clinical development. These adverse effects are not always predictable from sequence or chemical composition alone, as structurally similar oligonucleotides can exhibit vastly different toxicity profiles in vivo.

The development of oligonucleotide therapeutics is further complicated by the lack of reliable in vitro models that accurately recapitulate in vivo toxicity. Traditional cell lines such as HepG2 have proven inadequate due to their limited capacity for gymnotic uptake—the unassisted internalization of oligonucleotides without transfection reagents—which is essential for modeling the physiological exposure conditions observed in vivo. Consequently, preclinical safety assessment has relied heavily on rodent studies, which are resource-intensive, ethically challenging, and often fail to translate to human outcomes. Although certain sequence motifs have been associated with increased hepatotoxicity risk, no comprehensive in vitro assay has previously demonstrated robust correlation with in vivo liver injury across a diverse panel of oligonucleotides.

Recent efforts have explored bioinformatics approaches to predict toxicity based on sequence features, but these methods remain limited by incomplete mechanistic understanding and insufficient validation against empirical data. There exists a critical need for a physiologically relevant, high-throughput in vitro assay that can reliably predict the hepatotoxic potential of oligonucleotides early in the drug discovery pipeline. Such an assay would enable the rational design and selection of safer candidates, reduce attrition in later-stage development, and support regulatory decision-making by providing human-relevant safety data.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides an in vitro method for predicting the in vivo hepatotoxicity of oligonucleotides, comprising administering an oligonucleotide to primary mammalian hepatocytes under gymnotic conditions—without the use of transfection reagents or conjugates—and measuring one or more biomarkers of cellular stress, damage, or dysfunction after a defined culture period. The method enables the identification of oligonucleotides with low hepatotoxic potential by comparing biomarker levels to reference control data derived from known toxic and non-toxic oligonucleotides. In a preferred embodiment, the biomarkers include lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) release, intracellular adenosine triphosphate (ATP) levels, microRNA-122 (miR-122) secretion, glutathione (GSH) content, and cytokine production, particularly interleukin-1 alpha (IL-1α) and macrophage inflammatory protein-1 alpha (MIP-1α).

The invention further encompasses a screening method for selecting oligonucleotides suitable for in vivo administration. This method involves obtaining a library of oligonucleotides—such as variants of a parent antisense oligonucleotide differing in nucleobase sequence, sugar modifications, or stereochemistry of phosphorothioate linkages—administering each member of the library to primary hepatocytes in vitro, culturing the cells for a short duration (e.g., 48–72 hours), and measuring the aforementioned biomarkers. Oligonucleotides that induce minimal changes in these biomarkers relative to negative controls are selected as candidates with reduced hepatotoxic risk. Optionally, the selected oligonucleotides may be administered in vivo for further evaluation.

A key feature of the invention is the use of gymnotic delivery, which mimics the natural uptake mechanism of oligonucleotides in the liver and ensures that observed toxicities reflect biologically relevant interactions rather than artifacts of forced transfection. The assay is validated using both rodent and human primary hepatocytes, demonstrating cross-species relevance and enabling human-specific risk assessment. By correlating in vitro biomarker responses with historical in vivo toxicity data—including plasma alanine aminotransferase (ALT) elevations and histopathological findings—the method provides a robust predictive tool for early-stage drug development.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

### Prediction of In Vivo Toxicity

The invention enables the prediction of in vivo hepatotoxicity by assessing the comparative toxicity profile of oligonucleotides in a controlled in vitro setting. By exposing primary hepatocytes to candidate oligonucleotides and quantifying specific biomarkers, one can identify compounds that are unlikely to cause significant liver injury upon systemic administration. This approach facilitates the selection of oligonucleotides with a wide safety margin—defined as the ratio between the toxic dose and the efficacious dose—and supports the calculation of a therapeutic index. Non-toxic oligonucleotides identified through this assay are prioritized for further development, thereby reducing the likelihood of late-stage clinical failure due to hepatotoxicity.

### Primary Mammalian Hepatocyte Cells

The assay relies on primary mammalian hepatocytes, which are isolated from liver tissue via perfusion techniques, such as retrograde collagenase perfusion of the portal vein. These cells may be used fresh or cryopreserved and retain key metabolic and transport functions absent in immortalized cell lines. Alternatively, hepatocyte-like cells differentiated from induced pluripotent stem (iPS) cells may be employed, provided they demonstrate competent gymnotic uptake and biomarker responsiveness. Cultures may be maintained as monocultures or co-cultured with non-parenchymal cells (NPCs)—including Kupffer cells, stellate cells, and endothelial cells—to model cell-cell interactions that influence toxicity. Predictive biomarkers include ATP depletion, LDH leakage, miR-122 release, GSH reduction, and cytokine secretion (e.g., IL-1α, MIP-1α), with co-cultures offering enhanced sensitivity for immunotoxic signals.

### Administering the Oligonucleotide—Gymnotic Delivery (Gymnosis)

Oligonucleotides are administered directly to hepatocyte cultures in serum-free medium at concentrations ranging from 1 to 30 micromolar, without transfection agents—a process termed gymnosis. Cells are cultured under standard conditions (37°C, 5% CO₂) for 48 to 72 hours to allow sufficient time for uptake, intracellular trafficking, and manifestation of toxic effects. Biomarkers such as LDH and ATP are measured at the endpoint and compared to reference values. Caspase-3/7 activity may also be assessed to detect early apoptotic signaling. Gymnotic delivery is advantageous because it replicates the natural pharmacokinetic exposure of oligonucleotides in the liver, avoiding non-physiological stress induced by lipid-based transfection reagents.

### Control Data/Reference Values

Control data are essential for interpreting biomarker measurements. Negative controls include vehicle-treated cells or cells exposed to known non-toxic oligonucleotides, while positive controls involve treatment with established hepatotoxic oligonucleotides or compounds like acetaminophen. A toxic response is indicated by a statistically significant decrease in cellular ATP, elevation of LDH in supernatant, increase in miR-122 or cytokines, or depletion of intracellular GSH. Reference datasets may be compiled from libraries of oligonucleotides with well-characterized in vivo toxicity profiles, enabling quantitative comparison and threshold-based classification of new candidates.

### (Determination of) Hepatotoxicity

Hepatotoxicity is defined as chemical-driven liver damage manifesting as necrosis, apoptosis, steatosis, or inflammation. In the context of oligonucleotide therapeutics, it is commonly assessed in vivo by elevated plasma transaminases (ALT/AST) and histopathology. The present invention provides an in vitro surrogate by screening libraries of oligonucleotide variants—derived from a parent sequence—for reduced toxicity. Libraries may consist of gapmer designs with varying LNA placements, stereodefined phosphorothioate linkages, or nucleobase substitutions. Each variant is tested in primary hepatocytes, and those showing minimal biomarker perturbation are selected for in vivo evaluation. The method is applicable to antisense oligonucleotides, including LNA-modified gapmers, and supports the development of stereodefined phosphorothioate oligonucleotides with improved safety.

### Parent Oligonucleotide

The parent oligonucleotide is typically a non-stereodefined phosphorothioate-modified antisense oligonucleotide, often in a gapmer configuration with high-affinity wings (e.g., LNA) and a central DNA gap. It may exhibit known hepatotoxicity in vivo, serving as a starting point for generating safer derivatives through stereochemical or sequence optimization.

### Library of Variants

A library of variants is created by introducing stereodefined phosphorothioate internucleoside linkages at specific positions within the parent oligonucleotide. Each variant contains at least two, and preferably all, phosphorothioate linkages in a defined stereochemical configuration (Rp or Sp). Modifications may be confined to the gap region, wing regions, or distributed throughout. The nucleobase sequence and pattern of sugar modifications (e.g., LNA, 2′-O-methoxyethyl) are retained to preserve target affinity and potency. Libraries may include child oligonucleotides with 1–20 independently stereodefined linkages, enabling structure-toxicity relationship analysis.

### Stereodefined Oligonucleotide Embodiments

Stereodefined oligonucleotides are synthesized using chiral auxiliaries or enzymatic methods to control the stereochemistry of each phosphorothioate linkage. The invention includes methods for creating libraries of such variants from a parent ASO, screening them in the hepatocyte assay, and identifying those with reduced in vitro toxicity while maintaining in vivo or in vitro potency (e.g., target mRNA knockdown). Retention of the original modification pattern ensures that efficacy is not compromised during toxicity optimization.

### Primary Hepatocytes Assay Example

In a representative protocol, primary mouse hepatocytes are seeded in collagen-coated 96-well plates at 25,000 cells/well in William’s Medium E supplemented with 10% fetal calf serum. After 3–4 hours of attachment, medium is replaced with serum-free WME, and oligonucleotides are added at 1–30 μM. Cells are cultured for 72 hours, after which supernatants are collected for LDH, miR-122, and cytokine assays, and cell lysates are analyzed for ATP and GSH. Co-cultures include NPCs seeded first, followed by hepatocytes after 30 minutes. All assays are performed in triplicate to ensure reproducibility.

## Definitions

### Oligonucleotide

An oligonucleotide is a single-stranded polymer of nucleotide monomers, typically 8 to 50 nucleotides in length, composed of naturally occurring or chemically modified nucleosides linked by phosphodiester or modified internucleoside linkages. Oligonucleotides may be synthesized by solid-phase phosphoramidite chemistry and purified by HPLC or PAGE. They are used therapeutically to modulate gene expression via hybridization-dependent mechanisms.

### Antisense Oligonucleotides (ASOs)

Antisense oligonucleotides are oligonucleotides designed to bind complementary RNA sequences through Watson-Crick base pairing, leading to RNase H-mediated degradation, translational arrest, or splicing modulation. ASOs include gapmers, mixmers, and splice-switching oligonucleotides, and are used to treat genetic, oncologic, and metabolic diseases.

### Modified Internucleoside Linkages

Modified internucleoside linkages include phosphorothioate, phosphorodithioate, methylphosphonate, and boranophosphate groups, which confer nuclease resistance. Phosphorothioate linkages, where a sulfur atom replaces a non-bridging oxygen, are most common in therapeutic ASOs. Neutral linkages such as phosphotriester or amide-based backbones may also be used.

### Modulation of Expression

Modulation of expression refers to the upregulation or downregulation of target gene expression at the RNA or protein level. Inhibition includes degradation or translational blockade, while restoration may involve exon inclusion or readthrough of premature stop codons. Modulation is confirmed by qPCR, Western blot, or functional assays.

### Modified Oligonucleotides

Modified oligonucleotides contain chemically altered nucleosides to enhance stability, affinity, or pharmacokinetics. Phosphorothioate linkages improve protein binding and half-life. High-affinity modifications include 2′-O-methoxyethyl (2′-MOE), 2′-fluoro, and locked nucleic acid (LNA) nucleosides, which increase melting temperature (Tm). LNA features a methylene bridge connecting the 2′-oxygen and 4′-carbon of the ribose, forming a bicyclic structure. General formulae define substituents R1*, R2, R3, R5, R5* on the sugar ring, with nucleobase B being adenine, guanine, cytosine, thymine, or uracil. Stereoisomers of LNA, including thio-LNA, amino-LNA, and oxy-LNA, are encompassed. Gapmers consist of a central DNA region flanked by modified wings (e.g., LNA), enabling RNase H recruitment in the gap. Mixed-wing designs and gapbreaker oligonucleotides—with non-DNA residues in the gap—are also included.

### RNase H Activity and Recruitment

RNase H activity refers to the endonucleolytic cleavage of RNA in an RNA:DNA heteroduplex. ASOs with a central DNA gap recruit RNase H1 upon hybridization, leading to target RNA degradation.

### Efficacy

Efficacy is determined by measuring target knockdown (e.g., EC50 for mRNA reduction) in vitro or in vivo. Therapeutic index is calculated as the ratio of toxic dose (e.g., TD50) to efficacious dose (ED50). Potency retention is confirmed when stereodefined variants maintain similar EC50 values to the parent oligonucleotide.

### Conjugate Moieties

Conjugate moieties include carbohydrates (e.g., GalNAc), lipids (e.g., cholesterol), proteins (e.g., antibodies), small molecules (e.g., folate), and polymers (e.g., PEG), attached to enhance delivery, targeting, or pharmacokinetics.

### Stereodefined Oligonucleotides

Stereodefined oligonucleotides possess phosphorothioate linkages with specified stereochemistry (Rp or Sp) at one or more positions. Stereoselective synthesis ensures uniform diastereomers, which may exhibit distinct toxicity and efficacy profiles compared to stereorandom counterparts.

## Immunotoxicity Biomarker Assay

The invention further includes a method for predicting immunotoxicity by measuring cytokine release (e.g., IL-1α, MIP-1α, TNF-α) from hepatocyte-NPC co-cultures treated with oligonucleotides. Elevated cytokine levels after 24–72 hours indicate pro-inflammatory potential, enabling early de-risking of immunotoxic candidates.

## EXAMPLES

### Example 1

Primary mouse hepatocytes were isolated via two-step collagenase perfusion, seeded in collagen-coated plates, and treated with LNA gapmers for 72 hours under gymnotic conditions. Co-cultures included NPCs isolated by Percoll gradient. RNA was extracted using RNeasy kits, and qRT-PCR was performed for target mRNA. LDH, albumin, GSH, and ATP were quantified using commercial kits. Apoptosis was assessed via caspase-3/7 luminescence. Cytokines were measured by multiplex ELISA, and miR-122 by TaqMan assay with ath-miR-159 normalization. Human hepatocytes from BioreclamationIVT were used similarly.

### Example 2

The in vitro assay was validated using 7 myd88-targeting ASOs with known mouse in vivo hepatotoxicity. ATP and LDH after 72 hours accurately discriminated toxic from non-toxic compounds.

### Example 3

Toxic ASOs induced LDH release, ATP depletion, and GSH reduction in mouse hepatocytes, while albumin secretion remained unchanged, indicating specificity of cytotoxicity markers.

### Example 4

Caspase-3/7 activation was detected at 48 hours with toxic ASOs, confirming apoptosis as a mechanism of cell death.

### Example 5

Hepatocyte-NPC co-cultures showed similar sensitivity to monocultures for LDH, ATP, and GSH, but enhanced cytokine responses.

### Example 6

miR-122 and cytokines (IL-1α, MIP-1α) were elevated in supernatants of co-cultures treated with toxic ASOs.

### Example 7

miR-122 release occurred in both mono- and co-cultures, indicating direct hepatocyte damage.

### Example 8

Testing 34 ASOs showed 94% concordance between in vitro (ATP/LDH) and in vivo (plasma ALT) hepatotoxicity.

### Example 9

Human primary hepatocytes correctly identified hepatotoxicity for 5/7 myd88 ASOs and two clinical-stage ASOs (anti-Survivin, anti-Bcl2), demonstrating human relevance.
