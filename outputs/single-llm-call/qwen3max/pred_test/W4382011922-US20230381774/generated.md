# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNICAL FIELD

The present invention relates to the field of cellular reprogramming and gene editing. More specifically, the invention provides novel methods and microfluidic systems for enhancing the efficiency and fidelity of cellular reprogramming through the application of biophysical stimuli, particularly mechanical deformation and modulation of intracellular tension. The invention further encompasses engineered microenvironments, including biomaterial substrates and microfluidic architectures, that facilitate epigenetic remodeling by altering cytoskeletal organization, focal adhesion dynamics, and nuclear architecture. These approaches are applicable to direct lineage conversion, induced pluripotent stem cell (iPSC) generation, and CRISPR-mediated genome editing, thereby offering transformative tools for regenerative medicine, disease modeling, and personalized therapeutics.

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

Cellular reprogramming refers to the process by which a differentiated somatic cell is converted into another cell type, either through dedifferentiation to a pluripotent state or via direct transdifferentiation into a distinct lineage. This technology holds immense promise for regenerative medicine, enabling the generation of patient-specific cells for transplantation, drug screening, and mechanistic studies of development and disease. Early breakthroughs demonstrated that ectopic expression of defined transcription factors—such as Oct4, Sox2, Klf4, and c-Myc—could reprogram fibroblasts into induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs). Subsequently, direct reprogramming strategies emerged, bypassing the pluripotent intermediate by converting one somatic cell type directly into another, such as fibroblasts into induced neurons (iNs) using combinations like Brn2, Ascl1, and Myt1l (BAM).

Despite these advances, a major limitation of current reprogramming methodologies is their low efficiency, often yielding less than 1–5% of successfully converted cells. This inefficiency stems largely from epigenetic barriers that maintain the identity of the starting cell type. Heterochromatin domains, DNA methylation, and repressive histone modifications restrict access to genes required for the new cell fate, thereby impeding transcriptional activation. While biochemical approaches—such as small molecule inhibitors of epigenetic enzymes—have improved outcomes, they often lack specificity and can induce off-target effects.

The epigenetic state of a cell is not solely governed by biochemical signals; it is also profoundly influenced by the cell’s physical environment and internal mechanical properties. The cytoskeleton, composed of actin filaments, microtubules, and intermediate filaments, transmits forces from the extracellular matrix (ECM) through focal adhesions to the nucleus, where they can alter chromatin organization. This process, known as mechanotransduction, regulates gene expression by modulating nuclear shape, lamina integrity, and the spatial positioning of chromatin domains. For instance, increased cytoskeletal tension has been associated with condensed heterochromatin, whereas reduced tension correlates with a more open, transcriptionally permissive chromatin state.

Recent studies have begun to explore the role of biophysical factors in reprogramming. Substrate stiffness, topography, and cell geometry have all been shown to influence stem cell differentiation and reprogramming efficiency. However, the precise mechanisms by which mechanical cues regulate epigenetic remodeling during direct lineage conversion remain poorly understood. Moreover, existing methods lack the ability to dynamically and controllably apply mechanical stimuli at scale, limiting their utility in high-throughput applications.

New approaches that integrate microengineering with cell biology offer promising avenues to enhance reprogramming. Microfluidic systems, in particular, enable precise manipulation of single cells under controlled fluidic and mechanical conditions. These platforms can be designed to transiently deform cells as they flow through constrictions, inducing nuclear compression and chromatin reorganization without compromising viability. Such mechanical perturbations may serve as a non-genetic, non-chemical means to prime cells for reprogramming by lowering epigenetic barriers.

There remains a critical need for robust, scalable, and efficient methods to enhance cellular reprogramming. Current strategies relying exclusively on transcription factors or chemical modulators are insufficient for clinical translation due to low yields, genomic instability, and incomplete maturation. The integration of biophysical cues—particularly those that modulate intracellular tension, focal adhesion signaling, and nuclear mechanics—represents an underexplored yet highly promising frontier. The present invention addresses this unmet need by providing engineered systems and protocols that harness mechanical forces to promote epigenetic plasticity and significantly boost reprogramming efficiency across diverse cell types and applications.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention introduces a microfluidic device specifically engineered to enhance cellular reprogramming through controlled mechanical deformation of mammalian cells. This device leverages biophysical principles to transiently squeeze cells as they traverse microchannels, thereby inducing nuclear deformation that promotes a more open chromatin architecture conducive to transcriptional reprogramming. The invention provides a paradigm shift from purely biochemical reprogramming strategies by incorporating mechano-epigenetic regulation as a central mechanism for improving conversion efficiency.

Microfluidic systems for cellular engineering described herein are designed to process cells at high throughput while maintaining viability and functionality. These systems comprise integrated microchannels with precisely defined geometries that enable reproducible cellular and/or nuclear deformation. By selecting appropriate channel width and height, the degree of mechanical stress applied to each cell can be finely tuned. In particular, the aspect ratio (AR)—defined as channel height divided by width—and the cross-section area ratio (CR)—the ratio of channel cross-sectional area to the projected nuclear area—are critical parameters that govern the extent of nuclear compression and subsequent epigenetic changes.

The invention demonstrates that mechanical squeezing of cells in microfluidic constrictions leads to transient disruption of the nuclear lamina, reduction in heterochromatin marks such as H3K9me3 and 5-methylcytosine, and increased accessibility of neuronal gene promoters. These changes prime the epigenome for reprogramming, resulting in significantly enhanced conversion of fibroblasts into induced neurons when combined with standard transcription factor expression. The method is not limited to neuronal reprogramming; it is broadly applicable to other lineages, iPSC generation, and CRISPR-based gene editing, where chromatin accessibility is a rate-limiting factor.

High-efficiency conversion of cells is achieved by optimizing microchannel design based on empirical data correlating AR and CR values with reprogramming outcomes. Optimal performance is observed within specific ranges—for example, AR between 0.8 and 1.2 and CR between 0.3 and 0.6—ensuring sufficient deformation without inducing DNA damage or cell death. The microfluidic cell culture/processing system may be fabricated from polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) or other biocompatible materials using soft lithography, enabling rapid prototyping and scalability.

Furthermore, the invention encompasses methods for guided stem cell differentiation by preconditioning cells through mechanical deformation prior to exposure to differentiation cues. It also enables boosting the efficiency of gene therapy by enhancing nuclear delivery and activity of CRISPR ribonucleoprotein complexes through chromatin decompaction. In the context of cell rejuvenation, transient nuclear deformation facilitates global reorganization of chromatin, mimicking aspects of youthful epigenetic states.

In summary, the invention provides a comprehensive platform that integrates microfluidics, mechanobiology, and epigenetics to overcome key limitations in cellular reprogramming. By harnessing the power of physical forces to modulate the epigenome, the disclosed methods offer unprecedented control over cell fate transitions, paving the way for advanced applications in regenerative medicine, personalized therapeutics, and high-throughput screening.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

Cellular reprogramming represents a cornerstone of modern regenerative biology, enabling the derivation of therapeutically relevant cell types from accessible somatic sources. However, conventional approaches—whether based on viral delivery of transcription factors or small molecule cocktails—suffer from inherently low efficiencies due to persistent epigenetic barriers. The present invention overcomes these limitations by introducing an innovative microfluidic device that exploits biophysical cues to enhance reprogramming outcomes. This approach is motivated by emerging evidence that the mechanical state of a cell, governed by its cytoskeleton, adhesion complexes, and nuclear architecture, plays a decisive role in regulating chromatin accessibility and gene expression.

Direct induced neuronal (iN) reprogramming, wherein fibroblasts are converted into functional neurons using defined factors such as BAM, exemplifies both the promise and challenges of lineage conversion. While successful in principle, iN reprogramming typically achieves only modest yields, with many cells failing to fully silence fibroblast programs or activate neuronal networks. Induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) offer an alternative route but require extended culture periods and carry risks of tumorigenicity. Direct reprogramming avoids pluripotency but remains hampered by incomplete epigenetic resetting.

The invention introduces microfluidic cell processing and culturing systems that address these shortcomings through controlled mechanical intervention. These systems consist of microfabricated channels integrated into a lab-on-a-chip platform, typically constructed from polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) bonded to glass. Key components include inlet and outlet reservoirs, serpentine or straight microchannels with subcellular dimensions, and optional valves or sensors for real-time monitoring. The core innovation lies in the deliberate design of channel geometry to induce transient, non-lethal deformation of cells as they are hydrodynamically driven through the device.

Central to this design are two dimensionless parameters: the aspect ratio (AR = height/width) and the cross-section area ratio (CR = channel area / nuclear cross-sectional area). Empirical optimization reveals that AR values near unity (e.g., 0.9–1.1) combined with CR values between 0.4 and 0.5 maximize reprogramming efficiency while preserving viability. Channels that are too narrow cause membrane rupture; those too wide fail to induce sufficient nuclear strain. The deformation event lasts milliseconds but triggers sustained epigenetic changes, including reduced levels of H3K9me3 and DNA methylation, and increased histone acetylation.

Experiments across multiple cell types—including mouse embryonic fibroblasts, human neonatal dermal fibroblasts, and mesenchymal stem cells—confirm the generality of this approach. Reprogramming efficiency, measured by expression of neuron-specific β-tubulin III (Tuj1), increases up to 4.5-fold following mechanical priming. Critically, derived iNs exhibit mature electrophysiological properties, including action potentials and synaptic activity, confirming functional maturation.

The invention further incorporates complementary strategies to modulate cell physiology. Agents such as blebbistatin (a myosin II inhibitor) or PF573228 (a focal adhesion kinase inhibitor) can be used in conjunction with microfluidic deformation to synergistically reduce intracellular tension. Genetic modification via CRISPR ribonucleoprotein complexes is also enhanced, as nuclear deformation improves access to target genomic loci. Additionally, 3D biomaterials engineering—using microgrooved PDMS or binary colloidal crystals—modulates cell adhesion and spreading, further promoting an open chromatin state.

Methods for making and using the microfluidic devices involve standard soft lithography: a silicon master is patterned via photolithography, PDMS is cast and cured, then plasma-bonded to glass. Cells are suspended in buffer and infused at controlled flow rates (typically 5–20 μL/min) using syringe pumps. Post-processing, cells are cultured under standard reprogramming conditions. Personalized medicine applications are enabled by processing patient-derived fibroblasts to generate autologous neurons for disease modeling or transplantation.

In practice, human neonatal dermal fibroblasts subjected to optimal mechanical deformation show marked upregulation of Tuj1 and other neuronal markers. When combined with inhibition of TGF-β/Activin/Nodal and BMP pathways—known to maintain mesenchymal identity—the spatial heterogeneity of reprogramming is reduced, yielding more uniform neuronal populations. This integrated mechano-chemical protocol represents a significant advance over existing methods.

The invention thus provides a versatile, scalable, and efficient platform for manipulating cell fate through physical means. By bridging microengineering with epigenetics, it unlocks new possibilities for high-fidelity cellular reprogramming across research and clinical domains.

## EXAMPLES

### Example 1: Transient Nuclear Deformation Primes Epigenetic State and Promotes Cell Reprogramming

Cell reprogramming technologies aim to convert one cell type into another for therapeutic and research purposes. Direct reprogramming, which bypasses pluripotency, offers speed and safety advantages but suffers from low efficiency due to epigenetic barriers, particularly dense heterochromatin that silences lineage-inappropriate genes. While transcription factors and biochemical modulators have been employed, their efficacy is limited. Biophysical factors, especially mechanical forces acting on the nucleus, represent an underutilized avenue. Mechano-chemical signaling can alter chromatin structure, and we hypothesized that transient mechanical perturbation of the nucleus could lower epigenetic barriers.

To test this, we developed a microfluidic device comprising parallel constrictions (9 µm width × 10 µm height) fabricated in PDMS via soft lithography. Mouse fibroblasts (average diameter ~18 µm, nuclear diameter ~10 µm) were flowed through at 69 kPa pressure. Viability assays (PrestoBlue, live/dead staining) confirmed >90% survival post-deformation, with no significant membrane or nuclear envelope leakage detected by dye exclusion or lamin B1 immunostaining. DNA damage (γH2AX foci) was minimal at optimal flow rates (10 µL/min), though higher rates caused clogging and damage.

Reprogramming was initiated by lentiviral delivery of BAM factors. Mechanical squeezing was applied on day 1 post-doxycycline induction. By day 14, Tuj1+ iN yield increased 3.8-fold versus controls. qPCR confirmed elevated Tubb3 mRNA. Epigenetic analysis revealed reduced H3K9me3 by FRET biosensor and immunostaining, and decreased 5-mC by ELISA. Inhibiting H3K9 methylation (with chaetocin) mimicked mechanical effects, while enhancing it (with UNC0638) blocked reprogramming gains, confirming H3K9me3’s causal role.

Nuclear lamina proteins were affected: lamin A/C showed phosphorylation-dependent disassembly, while lamin B1 remained intact. Silencing lamin A/C via siRNA recapitulated mechanical enhancement, implicating lamina remodeling in mechanotransduction. Fluid flow simulations validated uniform shear stress distribution. Device fabrication followed standard protocols: SU-8 photoresist patterning, PDMS casting (10:1 base:curing agent), plasma bonding. Lentivirus was produced via calcium phosphate transfection. Statistical analysis (ANOVA, Tukey’s test) confirmed significance (p<0.001). These findings establish transient nuclear deformation as a potent, non-genetic reprogramming enhancer.

### Example 2: Reduction of Intracellular Tension and Cell Adhesion Promotes Open Chromatin Structure and Enhances Cell Reprogramming

Cell reprogramming is vital for regenerative medicine but limited by inefficiency. Direct reprogramming strategies often fail to fully overcome the epigenetic and mechanical identity of the source cell. Mechanotransduction—mediated by focal adhesions (FAs) and the actin cytoskeleton—plays a crucial yet underappreciated role. During early iN reprogramming, Ascl1 induces an actin “cage” around the nucleus, increasing stiffness transiently. We reasoned that reducing this tension might facilitate epigenetic remodeling.

Mouse fibroblasts transduced with BAM were treated with blebbistatin (10 µM), a myosin II inhibitor. This reduced cell and nuclear stiffness (measured by AFM and q-DC), decreased FA assembly (paxillin puncta), and enhanced reprogramming efficiency 4.5-fold. Other inhibitors—Y-27632 (ROCK inhibitor)—showed similar but weaker effects, while cytoskeletal stabilizers (jasplakinolide) or disruptors (nocodazole) impaired reprogramming, indicating an optimal tension window. Blebbistatin’s effect was phase-specific: treatment during days 1–5 was sufficient, highlighting the importance of early epigenetic priming.

Derived iNs were functional: they fired action potentials and exhibited spontaneous EPSCs/IPSCs, with only minor amplitude differences versus controls. scRNA-seq revealed upregulated neuronal genes (Tubb3, Mapt) and downregulated heterochromatin/mitosis genes. ATAC-seq showed increased chromatin accessibility at neuronal promoters (Ascl1, Tubb3), while ChIP-qPCR confirmed elevated AcH3 and H3K4me3. Enzyme assays indicated increased HAT and H3K4-HMT activity, decreased HDAC and DNMT activity.

FAK inhibition (PF573228) similarly enhanced reprogramming by reducing adhesion signaling. Biomaterials—microgrooved PDMS or 2-µm binary colloidal crystals—reduced cell spreading and FAK phosphorylation, boosting iN yield. Fibroblast isolation followed Liberase digestion; reprogramming used laminin-coated plates and N3 medium. Electrophysiology employed whole-cell patch clamp. Statistical rigor (n≥3, ANOVA) ensured reliability. These results demonstrate that modulating cytoskeletal tension and adhesion remodels the epigenome to favor reprogramming.

### Example 3: 3D Spheroid Culture to Increase Cell Reprogramming Efficiency

Three-dimensional spheroid culture enhances cell-cell interactions and mimics in vivo microenvironments. Human neonatal dermal fibroblasts were aggregated into spheroids and reprogrammed with BAM factors. Tuj1 expression was markedly higher in spheroids versus 2D cultures, with reprogramming efficiency doubling. Spatial analysis revealed Tuj1+ cells predominantly at the spheroid periphery, suggesting oxygen or nutrient gradients influence conversion. Inhibition of TGF-β and BMP signaling (using SB431542 and LDN193189) further enhanced efficiency and homogenized Tuj1 distribution, eliminating central necrosis and promoting uniform neuronal differentiation. This 3D approach provides a scalable, biomimetic platform for high-yield reprogramming.

### Example 4: Mechano-Epigenetic Mapping for Guided Gene Activation and Silencing

A mechano-epigenetic mapping method was developed to correlate nuclear deformation with locus-specific chromatin changes. CRISPR-dCas9 fused to activators (e.g., VP64) or repressors (e.g., KRAB) was delivered to fibroblasts, which were then mechanically squeezed. ATAC-seq and RNA-seq revealed that mechanical priming synergized with CRISPRa to activate neuronal genes (e.g., NeuroD1) 5-fold over CRISPRa alone. Conversely, CRISPRi-mediated silencing of fibroblast genes (e.g., Thy1) was more effective post-deformation. This illustrates how mechanical cues can be harnessed to guide precision epigenome editing.

### Example 5: Matrix Stiffness Regulates Epigenetic State and Cell Reprogramming

Polyacrylamide (PAAm) gels of tunable stiffness (0.5–50 kPa) were coated with laminin. Fibroblasts reprogrammed on soft gels (1 kPa) showed 3-fold higher iN efficiency than on stiff gels (50 kPa). Soft substrates reduced cell area and nuclear volume, promoted G1 arrest, and enhanced Ascl1 expression. Histone analysis revealed increased H3K9ac and H3K27ac on soft gels, correlating with elevated HAT activity. ChIP-qPCR confirmed enriched acetylation at neuronal promoters. This biphasic response underscores matrix stiffness as a critical regulator of reprogramming epigenetics.

### Example 6: Viscoelasticity of Matrix Regulates Epigenetic State and Cell Reprogramming

Hydrogels with identical elastic modulus but varying stress relaxation (viscoelasticity) were synthesized using PEG-based polymers. Fast-relaxing gels enhanced iN reprogramming 2.5-fold over slow-relaxing counterparts. This correlated with reduced HDAC activity and increased H3K27ac. Mechanistically, viscoelastic matrices facilitated cytoskeletal remodeling, promoting nuclear translocation of YAP and epigenetic modifiers. These findings highlight viscoelasticity—not just stiffness—as a key design parameter for reprogramming biomaterials.
