# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

Ductal carcinoma in situ (DCIS) represents a non-invasive form of breast cancer wherein malignant epithelial cells are confined within the basement membrane of the mammary ducts without stromal invasion. DCIS accounts for approximately 20% of all screen-detected breast cancers and is clinically significant due to its potential to progress to invasive breast cancer if left untreated. Epidemiological studies indicate that between 20% and 53% of women with untreated DCIS will develop invasive disease over a period of ten years or more. Consequently, current clinical management typically involves local control through surgical excision—either lumpectomy or mastectomy—with or without adjuvant radiotherapy, and occasionally endocrine therapy. Despite these interventions, a substantial proportion of patients—ranging from 10% to 35%—experience local recurrence following breast-conserving surgery, with roughly half of these recurrences manifesting as invasive breast cancer. This clinical reality underscores the urgent need for reliable prognostic biomarkers capable of stratifying DCIS patients according to their individual risk of recurrence, thereby enabling personalized treatment strategies that avoid both under-treatment and overtreatment.

Existing risk prediction models for DCIS, such as the Van Nuys Prognostic Index (VNPI) and the Memorial Sloan Kettering DCIS nomogram, rely primarily on conventional clinicopathological parameters including tumor size, nuclear grade, margin status, patient age, and presence of comedo necrosis. However, these tools suffer from significant limitations in terms of reproducibility, consistency, and predictive accuracy across diverse patient populations. Moreover, they fail to incorporate molecular or cellular-level features that may offer deeper insights into the biological aggressiveness of individual DCIS lesions. The commercially available Oncotype Dx DCIS Score, which is based on a gene expression signature, has demonstrated only modest prognostic utility and is restricted in applicability to specific subsets of patients, particularly those with low- or intermediate-grade tumors and adequate surgical margins. These constraints highlight a critical gap in the current diagnostic and prognostic armamentarium for DCIS.

Centrosome amplification (CA)—defined as an abnormal increase in either the number (numerical amplification) or volume (structural amplification) of centrosomes—has emerged as a hallmark of genomic instability in cancer. In normal diploid cells, centrosome duplication is tightly regulated, resulting in exactly two centrosomes during mitosis. In contrast, cancer cells frequently exhibit supernumerary or enlarged centrosomes, which can drive chromosomal missegregation, aneuploidy, and intratumoral heterogeneity. Importantly, CA has been documented not only in invasive carcinomas but also in pre-invasive lesions such as DCIS, suggesting that it arises early in tumorigenesis and may serve as a harbinger of aggressive disease behavior. Previous semi-quantitative studies have linked CA to higher tumor grade, larger tumor size, and increased risk of recurrence or metastasis in various malignancies, including breast cancer. However, the absence of a standardized, quantitative methodology for assessing CA in clinical specimens has precluded its translation into routine clinical practice.

Given the limitations of current predictors and the compelling biological rationale linking centrosome dysfunction to cancer progression, there exists a clear and unmet need for a robust, objective, and quantifiable metric that captures the extent and nature of centrosome abnormalities in DCIS. Such a metric could provide clinically actionable information beyond what is offered by traditional histopathological criteria, thereby improving risk stratification and guiding therapeutic decisions. The invention described herein addresses this need by introducing a novel, semi-automated analytical framework for centrosomal phenotyping that enables precise quantification of both numerical and structural centrosome amplification in formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissue samples. This approach culminates in the generation of a continuous Centrosome Amplification Score (CAS), which demonstrates superior prognostic performance compared to existing models and holds significant promise for enhancing the precision of DCIS management.

## SUMMARY

The present invention encompasses multiple embodiments directed to methods and systems for predicting the risk of local recurrence in patients diagnosed with ductal carcinoma in situ (DCIS) of the breast through the quantitative assessment of centrosome amplification. In one embodiment, the invention provides a method for determining a Centrosome Amplification Score (CAS) based on the frequency and severity of both numerical and structural centrosome abnormalities observed in tumor tissue samples. This CAS serves as a continuous, objective biomarker that stratifies DCIS patients into high-risk and low-risk categories for local recurrence within ten years of diagnosis, thereby informing clinical decisions regarding the necessity of adjuvant radiotherapy following surgical resection.

The method begins with the preparation of a tissue sample obtained from a patient diagnosed with DCIS. The sample may be derived from formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) full-face sections, tissue microarrays, core biopsies, fresh frozen sections, fine needle aspirates, circulating tumor cells, or patient-derived xenografts, among other sources. The sample is then processed for visualization using immunohistochemical or immunofluorescent staining techniques. Specifically, centrosomes are labeled using an antibody against γ-tubulin, while nuclei are counterstained with a DNA-binding dye such as Hoechst. Alternatively, transmission electron microscopy may be employed for ultrastructural analysis, though immunofluorescence combined with high-resolution confocal microscopy is preferred for three-dimensional volumetric assessment.

Following staining, three-dimensional image stacks are acquired using a confocal microscope equipped with appropriate optics and detectors. These images are then subjected to computational analysis using specialized software capable of rendering volumetric reconstructions of individual centrosomes associated with each nucleus. A critical step in the analytical procedure involves the determination of the normal three-dimensional volume range for centrosomes in healthy breast epithelium. This reference range is established by analyzing centrosome volumes in normal breast tissue obtained from reduction mammoplasties or adjacent uninvolved tissue from cancer patients, yielding a defined upper limit—typically 0.74 µm³—for what constitutes a structurally normal centrosome.

Centrosomes identified in the DCIS sample are categorized into two distinct types: individually distinguishable centrosomes (iCTRs) and megacentrosomes (mCTRs). iCTRs are defined as γ-tubulin-positive structures whose volumes fall within the established normal range, whereas mCTRs are those with volumes exceeding this threshold and thus represent structurally amplified organelles. For each nucleus in the field of view, the number and volume of associated iCTRs and mCTRs are recorded.

The invention further includes a defined algorithm for calculating a composite Centrosome Amplification Score (CAS). This score comprises two subcomponents: a numerical CAS (CASi) reflecting the degree of numerical centrosome amplification, and a structural CAS (CASm) reflecting structural abnormalities. CASi is computed based on the percentage of cells exhibiting more than two iCTRs (frequency component) and the average excess number of iCTRs per such cell beyond the normal diploid complement of two (severity component). Similarly, CASm incorporates both the frequency of cells containing mCTRs and a severity metric derived from the z-scores of mCTR volumes relative to the normal upper limit, adjusted by the standard deviation of mCTR volumes in the sample. Both subcomponents are scaled using empirically determined factors to ensure equal weighting in the final total CAS (CAStotal = CASi + CASm).

Once CAStotal is calculated for a given patient’s sample, it is interpreted using a predefined cutoff value—such as 1.436, determined through statistical optimization in a discovery cohort—to classify the patient as being at high or low risk of local recurrence within ten years. This risk profile directly informs treatment recommendations: patients classified as low-risk may be candidates for surgery alone without adjuvant radiotherapy, while those deemed high-risk would benefit from the addition of radiotherapy to reduce recurrence risk.

In another embodiment, the invention provides a computer program product comprising non-transitory computer-readable storage media encoded with program instructions executable by a processor to perform the aforementioned analytical steps. The program includes routines for three-dimensional image processing, volume determination, centrosome counting, classification into iCTR and mCTR categories, and execution of the CAS calculation algorithm. The system may be implemented on a multi-processor computing platform capable of multitasking and multithreading, interfaced with input/output circuitry and network adapters for data transfer and integration into clinical laboratory workflows. By automating the complex image analysis and scoring process, the invention ensures reproducibility, objectivity, and scalability, making centrosomal phenotyping feasible for routine clinical use.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

Ductal carcinoma in situ (DCIS) is a heterogeneous pre-invasive breast lesion characterized by the proliferation of malignant epithelial cells confined within the ductal lumen. Despite its non-invasive nature, DCIS carries a significant risk of progression to invasive breast cancer, necessitating accurate risk stratification to guide therapeutic intervention. Centrosome amplification (CA)—encompassing both numerical increases in centrosome number and structural enlargement of centrosomal volume—has been increasingly recognized as a key driver of chromosomal instability and tumor evolution. The present invention introduces a comprehensive methodology for quantifying CA in DCIS tissues, yielding a Centrosome Amplification Score (CAS) that robustly predicts ten-year local recurrence risk.

The motivation for focusing on CA stems from its high prevalence in breast cancers, including pre-invasive stages, and its association with aggressive clinicopathological features such as high nuclear grade and comedo necrosis. Unlike subjective histological assessments, CA provides an organellar-level metric that reflects underlying genomic dysregulation. The CAS methodology was developed and validated using two independent cohorts: a discovery cohort (DC, n=133) and a validation cohort (VC, n=119), both comprising pure DCIS cases with long-term follow-up and complete clinicopathological data. Results demonstrated that recurrent DCIS cases exhibited significantly higher levels of both numerical and structural CA compared to non-recurrent cases, irrespective of tumor grade or treatment modality.

The CAS calculation integrates four key dimensions: frequency and severity of numerical CA (quantified as CASi), and frequency and severity of structural CA (quantified as CASm). Numerical CA is assessed by identifying cells with more than two iCTRs—γ-tubulin-positive centrosomes within the normal volume range (0.2–0.74 µm³). The frequency component of CASi is the proportion of such cells, while the severity component reflects the average excess number of iCTRs beyond two. Structural CA is evaluated by detecting mCTRs—centrosomes exceeding 0.74 µm³—and computing a severity score based on z-transformed volume deviations from the normal upper limit, normalized by the standard deviation of mCTR volumes in the sample. Both CASi and CASm are scaled (using βi = 0.1 and βm = 0.148 for breast tissue) to ensure balanced contribution to CAStotal.

Statistical analyses confirmed the strong prognostic power of CAS. In the DC, high CAStotal was associated with significantly poorer recurrence-free survival (RFS; p<0.001, HR=6.3), outperforming traditional variables like grade and comedo necrosis, which lost significance in multivariate models. Similar results were replicated in the VC (p<0.0001), demonstrating generalizability. Bootstrap analyses on the combined cohort (n=252) yielded consistent hazard ratios (HR=5.22 univariate; HR=6.58 multivariate), confirming robustness. Kaplan-Meier curves clearly stratified patients into high- and low-risk groups, with 85.7% of recurrences occurring in the high-CAS group in the DC and 75% in the VC.

Importantly, CAS provided incremental prognostic value beyond the Van Nuys Prognostic Index (VNPI). While VNPI failed to significantly stratify recurrence risk in this cohort (HR=0.70, p>0.05), CAStotal achieved superior discrimination (HR=5.6, p<0.001). Multivariate Cox models incorporating both VNPI and CAS confirmed that only CAS remained independently predictive of RFS (HR=6.86). Furthermore, CAS enabled deeper stratification within high-risk clinicopathological subgroups: for example, among high-grade DCIS patients with comedo necrosis, those with low CAS had a recurrence rate of only 10%, compared to 83% in the high-CAS subgroup.

The methodology is compatible with diverse sample types, including FFPE full-face sections, tissue microarrays, core biopsies, fresh frozen tissue, fine needle aspirates, circulating tumor cells, and patient-derived xenografts. Staining protocols employ γ-tubulin immunofluorescence with nuclear counterstain, followed by 3D confocal imaging and semi-automated analysis using software such as IMARIS. Centrosomes are classified per nucleus, and volumes are measured in three dimensions to avoid underestimation inherent in 2D methods. The algorithm computes CAS components through defined mathematical formulations, ensuring reproducibility.

Performance metrics further validate CAS as a clinical tool. Using Harrell’s concordance index, CAS achieved 72.6% predictive accuracy. Confusion matrix analysis in the DC showed high sensitivity (85%) and negative predictive value (93%), indicating excellent ability to identify patients unlikely to recur—critical for sparing low-risk individuals unnecessary radiotherapy. Notably, CAS retained prognostic significance even in patients treated with breast-conserving surgery alone, where it identified a high-risk subgroup with HR=17.05 (p=0.0005), suggesting its utility in guiding adjuvant therapy decisions.

The invention also encompasses a computer program product comprising machine-readable instructions for executing the entire analytical pipeline—from image acquisition and segmentation to CAS computation and risk classification. This software can be deployed on standard computing hardware with multi-threading capabilities, integrated into digital pathology workflows via network interfaces, and updated with firmware or configuration data to adapt to new tissue types or staining protocols. The system supports object code, source code, assembler instructions, and microcode, ensuring compatibility across platforms.

In summary, the CAS methodology represents a paradigm shift in DCIS prognostication by leveraging quantitative organellar phenotyping to generate a continuous, biologically grounded risk score. Its superiority over existing models, compatibility with routine clinical samples, and potential to reduce overtreatment underscore its transformative potential in precision oncology.
