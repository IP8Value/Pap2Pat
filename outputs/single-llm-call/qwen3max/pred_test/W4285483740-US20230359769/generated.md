# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD

The present invention relates to anonymizing datasets, and more specifically to methods and systems for producing k-anonymous or smooth-k-anonymous versions of binary datasets while preserving data utility. The invention is particularly applicable to the anonymization of sparse binary matrices representing user-feature relationships, such as bipartite graphs, user lists, or points in a binary space, where each row corresponds to a user and each column to a feature, and an entry indicates the presence or absence of a particular feature for a given user. The techniques disclosed herein are designed to protect the privacy of individuals represented in such datasets when the datasets are shared or released for public or third-party use, while maintaining sufficient fidelity to the original data to support downstream analytical tasks, including machine learning applications.

## BACKGROUND

In the era of big data, the collection, storage, and analysis of personal information have become ubiquitous across numerous domains, including healthcare, finance, social networking, and e-commerce. While such data-driven approaches offer significant societal and commercial benefits, they also pose substantial privacy risks to individuals whose data is being processed. To mitigate these risks, various formal privacy models have been developed to govern the release of sensitive datasets. Among these, k-anonymity has emerged as a widely adopted pre-processing technique for tabular data anonymization. The concept of k-anonymity, first introduced by Sweeney, requires that each record in a released dataset be indistinguishable from at least k-1 other records with respect to a set of quasi-identifying attributes—those combinations of attributes that could potentially be used to re-identify individuals when linked with external information. By ensuring that every individual is hidden within a group of at least k individuals sharing the same quasi-identifier values, k-anonymity aims to prevent identity disclosure through linkage attacks. However, traditional k-anonymity implementations typically rely on generalization or suppression of attribute values, which can significantly degrade data utility, especially when the optimal k-anonymous version of the dataset requires the removal of a large fraction of the original data. Moreover, the problem of finding the optimal k-anonymous dataset that best preserves the original information is known to be NP-hard, and existing approximation algorithms often provide weak guarantees in practical scenarios where substantial data modification is unavoidable. These limitations motivate the need for improved anonymization techniques that offer stronger utility preservation while maintaining meaningful privacy protections.

## SUMMARY

The present invention introduces a novel method for k-anonymizing a dataset, particularly suited for binary matrices representing user-feature relationships. The method comprises clustering entities—such as users—into groups of at least k members, determining a majority condition for each feature within each cluster, and assigning a uniform data item to all entities in the cluster based on this majority condition. Specifically, for each cluster and each feature, if the majority of entities in the cluster originally possessed the feature, then all entities in the cluster are assigned the feature in the anonymized dataset; otherwise, the feature is removed from all entities in the cluster. This approach, referred to herein as smooth-k-anonymity, extends traditional k-anonymity by allowing the addition of features (edges) under the constraint that any added feature must be supported by a majority of the original cluster members, thereby enhancing data utility without compromising the core anonymity guarantee. The invention further encompasses a computing system configured to execute the aforementioned method, comprising one or more processors and memory storing instructions that, when executed, cause the system to perform the steps of obtaining a dataset, clustering entities, determining majority conditions, and generating an anonymized output. Additional aspects of the invention include variations of the clustering procedure, such as employing lower-bounded r-median or metric facility location formulations to optimize cluster formation, as well as mechanisms for merging or splitting clusters to satisfy size constraints and improve overall data fidelity.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

k-Anonymity is a foundational concept in data privacy that ensures each individual in a released dataset cannot be distinguished from at least k-1 other individuals based on a specified set of attributes known as quasi-identifiers. The motivation for k-anonymity stems from the recognition that even when direct identifiers like names or social security numbers are removed, combinations of seemingly innocuous attributes—such as zip code, date of birth, and gender—can uniquely identify individuals when cross-referenced with publicly available databases. By requiring that every combination of quasi-identifier values appears at least k times in the dataset, k-anonymity mitigates the risk of identity disclosure through linkage attacks. An alternative privacy framework, differential privacy, provides a stronger, mathematically rigorous guarantee by ensuring that the inclusion or exclusion of any single individual’s data has a negligible impact on the output of a query or algorithm. Differential privacy achieves this by injecting calibrated noise into the computation, but this randomization can severely degrade data utility, especially when releasing entire datasets rather than aggregate statistics. In fact, for sparse binary matrices—a common representation of user-feature data—differential privacy mechanisms either provide weak privacy guarantees or require so much noise that the underlying signal is destroyed, rendering the data useless for most analytical purposes. Quasi-identifiers, in the context of k-anonymity, are those attributes that, when combined, could potentially identify an individual. In the present invention, we assume that all features may serve as quasi-identifiers, reflecting a conservative privacy stance. Formally, a dataset is k-anonymous if, for every record, there exist at least k-1 other records with identical values on all quasi-identifying attributes. When applied to binary matrices, k-anonymity implies that each row (representing a user) must be identical to at least k-1 other rows. The present disclosure introduces several example aspects that improve upon conventional k-anonymity. Notably, the invention provides enhanced privacy guarantees by enabling the construction of anonymized datasets that retain significantly more of the original information compared to suppression-based k-anonymity, especially in structured data such as those generated by stochastic block models. The computing system implementing the invention includes standard components such as processors, memory, storage, and input/output interfaces, configured to execute the anonymization operations described herein. The operations for k-anonymizing a dataset begin with obtaining a dataset, which may be represented as a binary matrix, a bipartite graph, or a collection of user lists, where rows correspond to users and columns to features, and entries indicate feature possession. Clustering entities—i.e., grouping users—is performed by mapping each entity to a point in a high-dimensional binary space, where each dimension corresponds to a feature, and the coordinate value indicates presence or absence of that feature. Centers are then established in this space, and entities are distributed among these centers to form clusters, each containing at least k entities. This clustering problem is formulated as a lower-bounded r-median problem, which seeks to minimize the total distance between entities and their assigned cluster centers while respecting the lower bound on cluster size. Alternatively, a metric facility location approach may be employed, where each potential center is treated as a facility with an associated opening cost, and the objective is to minimize the sum of facility opening costs and assignment distances. The opening cost for each facility is determined based on the proximity of the facility to its nearest neighbors, encouraging the selection of centers that represent dense regions of the data. Points are assigned to facilities using approximation algorithms that balance cost and distance. To refine the clustering, entity clusters may be merged if they are too small, or split if they exceed a certain size threshold, ensuring that all final clusters meet the k-size requirement while preserving data structure. The majority condition is then determined for each cluster and each feature: if more than half of the entities in a cluster originally had a given feature, that feature is assigned to all entities in the cluster in the anonymized dataset; otherwise, it is removed. This step ensures that the anonymized dataset satisfies the smooth-k-anonymity condition, which permits the addition of features only when justified by a majority of the original cluster members. The resulting anonymized dataset is then distributed for use, providing strong privacy protection against re-identification while maintaining high utility for downstream tasks. The technical effects and benefits of the invention include significantly improved data utility compared to traditional k-anonymity and differential privacy, as demonstrated by higher Jaccard similarity between original and anonymized datasets. The improvements in computing technology are manifested in the efficient polynomial-time algorithms that enable scalable anonymization of large, sparse datasets, overcoming the limitations of NP-hard exact solutions. Producing anonymized datasets according to the invention facilitates safe data sharing in domains such as healthcare analytics, social science research, and commercial data collaboration. Applications of the example aspects extend to any scenario involving the release of user-feature data, including recommendation systems, market basket analysis, and network analysis. The computing systems may be implemented as a single device or as multiple interconnected devices, operating in centralized or distributed architectures. Data anonymization may also be applied to graph structures, yielding k-anonymized or k-smooth-anonymized graphs that preserve structural properties while protecting node identities. The flexibility of computer-based systems allows implementation on a single component or across multiple devices, with databases and applications integrated as needed.
