# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

Birth defects represent a significant global public health concern, affecting approximately 3% to 5% of all live births worldwide. These congenital anomalies encompass a broad spectrum of structural or functional abnormalities that arise during intrauterine life and can lead to physical or intellectual disability, or even perinatal mortality. In China, the burden of birth defects is particularly pronounced due to the country’s large population base and evolving demographic trends, including increasing maternal age at conception. Epidemiological data indicate that birth defects are among the leading causes of infant mortality in China, with an estimated incidence exceeding 100 cases per 10,000 live births. This translates to over one million affected newborns annually, imposing substantial emotional, medical, and economic burdens on families and healthcare systems alike.

A considerable proportion—estimated between 15% and 25%—of birth defects are attributable to identifiable genetic disorders. Among these, chromosomal abnormalities constitute a major category, with chromosomal aneuploidy being one of the most common forms. Chromosomal aneuploidy refers to the presence of an abnormal number of chromosomes in a cell, deviating from the typical diploid complement of 46 chromosomes. The most prevalent viable aneuploidies include trisomy 21 (Down syndrome), trisomy 18 (Edwards syndrome), and trisomy 13 (Patau syndrome), each associated with severe developmental delays, multiple congenital malformations, and reduced life expectancy. In addition to whole-chromosome aneuploidies, submicroscopic chromosomal imbalances such as microdeletions and microduplications—collectively termed copy number variants (CNVs)—underlie numerous syndromic conditions, including DiGeorge syndrome (22q11.2 deletion), Prader-Willi/Angelman syndromes (15q11-q13 abnormalities), and Cri du Chat syndrome (5p deletion).

Traditional prenatal screening methods for detecting these conditions have relied on maternal serum biomarkers combined with ultrasound measurements, such as nuchal translucency thickness. While widely accessible, these approaches suffer from limited sensitivity and specificity, often yielding high false-positive rates that necessitate invasive diagnostic procedures like amniocentesis or chorionic villus sampling (CVS). These invasive tests, though definitive, carry a small but non-negligible risk of pregnancy loss, typically ranging from 0.1% to 0.3%. Consequently, there has been a strong clinical impetus to develop safer, more accurate non-invasive alternatives.

The discovery of cell-free fetal DNA (cffDNA) in maternal plasma in 1997 marked a transformative breakthrough in prenatal diagnostics. It was subsequently demonstrated that cffDNA, derived primarily from apoptotic trophoblast cells of the placenta, constitutes approximately 10% of the total cell-free DNA (cfDNA) in maternal circulation during the second trimester, though this fraction can be significantly lower in early gestation. This biological insight paved the way for non-invasive prenatal screening (NIPS), which leverages next-generation sequencing (NGS) technologies to analyze cfDNA for fetal chromosomal abnormalities without posing any risk to the fetus.

In China, NIPS has gained rapid clinical adoption since its introduction, with millions of tests performed annually. Current NIPS methodologies primarily employ two technical strategies: low-coverage whole-genome sequencing (WGS) and targeted single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP)-based sequencing. WGS-based NIPS assesses relative chromosome dosage by measuring deviations in sequence read depth across the genome, using statistical metrics such as Z-scores to infer aneuploidy. While effective for detecting common trisomies, WGS lacks the resolution to identify subchromosomal CNVs or distinguish between maternal and fetal genotypes, limiting its utility in complex scenarios such as twin pregnancies, maternal mosaicism, or hydatidiform moles.

In contrast, SNP-based NIPS interrogates specific polymorphic loci to evaluate allelic fraction (AF) imbalances that arise when fetal chromosomal copy number differs from the maternal background. This approach offers superior analytical resolution, enabling not only the detection of aneuploidies but also the identification of parental origin, meiotic error mechanisms, and certain microdeletion syndromes. Moreover, SNP-based methods can estimate fetal fraction (FF)—a critical parameter for test accuracy—and detect confounding factors such as maternal copy number variations (CNVs) or absence of heterozygosity (AOH), which may otherwise lead to false-positive or false-negative results.

Despite these advantages, conventional SNP-based NIPS faces practical limitations. One major challenge stems from allelic bias introduced during target enrichment via hybridization capture. Standard oligonucleotide probes are typically designed to perfectly complement reference genome sequences, resulting in preferential binding to DNA fragments carrying the reference allele over those with alternative alleles. This systematic bias distorts observed allelic fractions, particularly at low fetal fractions, thereby reducing the signal-to-noise ratio and compromising assay sensitivity. Additionally, while WGS requires deep sequencing to achieve adequate coverage across the entire genome—leading to high costs and computational demands—SNP-based methods must carefully select informative loci and account for linkage disequilibrium to maximize informativeness.

Recent innovations have sought to address these shortcomings through improved probe design and multidimensional data integration. Notably, the development of coordinative allele-aware target enrichment (COATE) technology represents a significant advancement. By engineering capture probes that minimize differences in hybridization thermodynamics between reference and alternative alleles—specifically by selecting probe nucleotides that yield near-identical melting temperatures (Tm) for both alleles—COATE effectively suppresses allelic bias. This enables more accurate quantification of allelic fractions, enhances fetal fraction estimation, and improves the detection of subtle chromosomal imbalances.

Furthermore, integrating multiple layers of genomic information—including read depth, allelic fraction, SNP linkage patterns, and cfDNA fragment size characteristics—allows for comprehensive deconvolution of the maternal-fetal cfDNA admixture. Such multidimensional analysis not only increases diagnostic accuracy but also enables the detection of meiotic recombination events associated with aneuploidy formation, providing unprecedented insights into the biological origins of chromosomal errors. Collectively, these technological and analytical advances have culminated in a new generation of NIPS capable of concurrently screening for chromosomal aneuploidies, microdeletion/microduplication syndromes, and dominant monogenic disorders with high sensitivity and specificity, thereby addressing a critical unmet need in prenatal care.

### SUMMARY

The present invention provides a novel method for analyzing nucleic acid molecules, particularly for non-invasive prenatal screening (NIPS) of fetal chromosomal and monogenic abnormalities. The method comprises capturing a target nucleic acid molecule using a specifically designed capture probe, followed by analysis of the captured molecule to determine the presence of chromosomal abnormalities or monogenic variants. The target nucleic acid molecule may be cell-free or cellular and is isolated from a biological sample, such as maternal plasma. After isolation, the nucleic acid molecules are amplified and subjected to detailed biophysical and sequencing analyses.

A key aspect of the invention involves determining the pairing kinetics and melting temperature (Tm) of the interaction between the capture probe and the target nucleic acid. This is achieved using the Nearest Neighbor thermodynamic model, which allows precise calculation of hybridization stability based on sequence composition. The capture probe is engineered to have a defined length, typically between 80 and 120 nucleotides, and a GC content within the range of 30% to 70%, ensuring optimal hybridization performance under stringent conditions. Importantly, the probe sequence is selected such that it minimizes the difference in Tm between binding to reference and alternative alleles at polymorphic sites, thereby suppressing allelic bias during enrichment.

The target region for capture is proximal to or within specific genes known to be associated with genetic disorders, including those implicated in chromosomal aneuploidies (e.g., chromosomes 13, 18, 21, X, Y), microdeletion syndromes (e.g., 22q11.2 for DiGeorge syndrome), and dominant monogenic conditions (e.g., FGFR3, PTPN11). The capture probe may be free-floating in solution or immobilized on a solid surface, such as magnetic beads, facilitating efficient separation and purification. Following capture, the enriched nucleic acid molecules are analyzed by high-throughput sequencing to generate sequence reads.

From these reads, chromosomal abnormalities—including trisomies, monosomies, microdeletions, and microduplications—are determined by evaluating deviations in read depth and allelic fraction at informative SNP sites. The method specifies particular types of chromosomal aneuploidies, such as trisomy 21, 18, and 13, as well as sex chromosome aneuploidies. Informative SNP sites are identified based on their allele frequency in the population, with preference given to loci exhibiting high heterozygosity (e.g., minor allele frequency > 0.2) to maximize informativeness across diverse populations.

The invention further enables the detection of chromosomal aneuploidy arising from one or multiple parental meiotic recombinations. By modeling different meiotic nondisjunction scenarios—maternal meiosis I (MI), maternal meiosis II (MII), paternal meiosis I (PI), and paternal meiosis II (PII)—and incorporating recombination breakpoints, the method calculates likelihoods for each hypothesis. Likelihood equations are employed to compute the probability of disomy versus various aneuploid states, with the difference in log-likelihoods (ΔL) used to determine the most probable karyotype. The maximum sum of these differences across contiguous SNP blocks identifies the optimal model, even in the presence of one, two, three, or four recombination events.

For microdeletion/microduplication detection, the method uses a beta-binomial distribution to model allelic count variability, accounting for overdispersion inherent in sequencing data. A multinomial factor is introduced to weight possible fetal genotypes according to Mendelian inheritance and population allele frequencies, derived from Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium. This framework also supports the analysis of dominant monogenic variations: by identifying variant sites where the mother is homozygous for the reference allele, the method distinguishes paternally inherited mutations from de novo fetal mutations based on likelihood ratios.

Fetal fraction is simultaneously determined using informative SNP sites, calculating the proportion of sequence reads carrying the alternative allele and applying established formulas to estimate the fetal DNA contribution. Finally, the invention encompasses a computer system and a non-transitory computer-readable storage medium encoded with instructions that, when executed, perform the aforementioned analytical methods, enabling automated, high-throughput, and clinically actionable NIPS.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention relates to methods and systems for the analysis of nucleic acid molecules, particularly in the context of non-invasive prenatal detection of fetal genetic abnormalities. Central to this invention is the Coordinative Allele-Aware Target Enrichment (COATE) method, a novel approach to hybridization-based target capture that significantly reduces allelic bias during library preparation for next-generation sequencing (NGS). In conventional enrichment protocols, capture probes are designed to perfectly complement reference genome sequences, leading to stronger binding affinity for DNA fragments containing the reference allele compared to those with alternative alleles. This differential hybridization efficiency introduces systematic distortion in observed allelic fractions, especially problematic when detecting low-abundance fetal variants in maternal plasma where fetal fraction may be as low as 4%.

The COATE method overcomes this limitation by designing capture probes that exhibit minimal difference in melting temperature (Tm) when hybridizing to either the reference or alternative allele at a given single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) site. The Tm is calculated using the Nearest Neighbor thermodynamic model, which considers the cumulative free energy contributions of adjacent base pairs to predict duplex stability. For each target SNP, four candidate probe sequences are evaluated—one for each possible nucleotide (A, C, G, T) at the SNP position—and the nucleotide yielding the smallest absolute difference in Tm between reference and alternative allele binding is selected. This results in probes that may not be perfectly complementary to either allele but achieve balanced hybridization kinetics, thereby preserving true allelic ratios in the sequenced library.

Capture probes used in the invention are typically 80 to 120 nucleotides in length and maintain a GC content between 30% and 70% to ensure uniform hybridization behavior across diverse genomic regions. These probes are applied to target nucleic acid molecules isolated from biological samples such as maternal plasma, serum, or other bodily fluids containing cell-free DNA (cfDNA). The nucleic acids may be cell-free or derived from intact cells, and following extraction, they undergo end-repair, adapter ligation, and PCR amplification to construct sequencing libraries.

The method is particularly suited for analyzing fetal-derived nucleic acids in maternal circulation. Sequence reads generated from captured libraries are aligned to a reference genome (e.g., GRCh38), and informative SNP sites are identified based on population allele frequency databases such as dbSNP or 1000 Genomes Project. Sites with minor allele frequency greater than 0.2 are prioritized to ensure sufficient heterozygosity across diverse ethnic groups. At these loci, the method determines chromosomal aneuploidy by computing likelihoods under competing hypotheses: disomy (normal diploid state) versus various aneuploid states including trisomy due to maternal or paternal meiotic errors (MI, MII, PI, PII) or monosomy.

For cases involving meiotic recombination, the invention provides algorithms to detect chromosomal aneuploidy with one or multiple crossover events. When a single recombination occurs, the chromosome is divided into two segments, each potentially reflecting a different meiotic error mechanism (e.g., MI in one segment and MII in another). The log-likelihood difference ΔL(H12) is computed as the sum of differences in the first segment under hypothesis H1 and the second under H2, with the minimum value across all possible breakpoint positions indicating the best-fit model. Similarly, for two recombinations, the chromosome is partitioned into three segments, and ΔL(H121) is minimized over all combinations of breakpoints and hypotheses. These equations generalize to three or four recombinations, enabling robust detection even in complex meiotic scenarios.

Chromosomal microdeletions or microduplications are detected using a beta-binomial distribution to model the overdispersed nature of allelic counts in sequencing data. Parameters α and β are derived from fetal fraction and expected allelic proportions under different copy number states. A multinomial weighting factor πk accounts for possible fetal genotypes based on Mendelian inheritance and population allele frequencies (p), calculated via Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium: p(AA) = p², p(AB) = 2p(1−p), p(BB) = (1−p)². The probability of the observed data under disomy and aneuploidy hypotheses is computed, and the difference in log-likelihoods determines the presence of a microdeletion or microduplication.

For dominant monogenic variation, the method identifies variant sites where the mother is homozygous for the reference allele. The likelihood that the alternative allele arises from a paternally inherited mutation versus a de novo fetal mutation is calculated using beta-binomial models incorporating fetal fraction and sequencing depth. A significant difference in likelihoods (ΔL) above a predefined threshold confirms the presence of a pathogenic variant.

Fetal fraction is concurrently estimated using informative SNPs where the mother is homozygous and the fetus is heterozygous. The fraction of reads carrying the alternative allele is used to compute locus-specific fetal fractions, which are then median-aggregated to yield a robust sample-level estimate. This value is critical for calibrating all downstream analyses.

The invention also includes a computer system comprising a processor, memory, and non-transitory computer-readable storage medium storing executable code that implements the described methods. The system integrates multiple metrics—including read depth, allelic fraction, fragment size distribution, and SNP linkage—for multidimensional analysis, significantly enhancing sensitivity and specificity over existing NIPS platforms. Customized oligonucleotide probes designed via COATE technology enable high-depth targeted sequencing, allowing simultaneous detection of chromosomal aneuploidies, microdeletions/microduplications, and monogenic mutations from a single assay. This integrated approach overcomes key limitations of whole-genome sequencing (low resolution, inability to resolve parental origin) and conventional SNP methods (allelic bias, reduced sensitivity at low fetal fraction), offering a comprehensive, accurate, and clinically scalable solution for non-invasive prenatal screening.

## EXAMPLES

### Example 1: Capture of DNA with the Target Probe

Plasma was separated from whole blood collected in EDTA tubes through a two-step centrifugation protocol: first at 1600×g for 15 minutes at 4°C to obtain platelet-poor plasma, followed by a second spin at 16,000×g for 10 minutes at 4°C to remove residual cells and debris. Cell-free DNA (cfDNA) was extracted from at least 0.8 mL of plasma using the TIANGEN Magnetic Serum/Plasma DNA Maxi Kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Sequencing library construction began with end-repair of the cfDNA fragments, followed by ligation of adapters containing unique molecular indexes (UMIs) at 20°C for 15 minutes. A first PCR amplification was performed to introduce sample barcodes: initial denaturation at 98°C for 2 minutes, followed by nine cycles of 98°C for 15 seconds, 60°C for 30 seconds, and 72°C for 30 seconds, with a final extension at 72°C for 2 minutes. Libraries were quantified using the Qubit 1× dsDNA HS Assay Kit, and only samples yielding at least 400 ng of amplified product proceeded to target enrichment.

For enrichment, 12–36 barcoded libraries were pooled and hybridized with custom-designed COATE capture probes at 65°C or 68°C for 16 hours. Cot-1 DNA was added to block repetitive elements, and XP magnetic beads were used to capture the hybridized complexes. After washing the beads to remove non-specifically bound DNA, the enriched library was eluted and transferred to a new PCR tube. M-270 streptavidin-coated magnetic beads were then added to further purify the captured DNA. Following additional washes, the final captured library was eluted and subjected to a second PCR amplification (12 cycles under identical conditions) to add sequencing adapters. The purified library was quantified again with Qubit and assessed for quality via electrophoresis. Enrichment efficiency was evaluated by comparing the sequencing coverage of target regions versus off-target areas, demonstrating significantly higher on-target rates and more uniform coverage with COATE probes compared to conventional designs.

### Example 2: Sequencing

Sequencing was performed on the MGI MGISEQ-2000 platform using paired-end 100-base chemistry. Prior to loading, library concentration and average fragment length were precisely measured to ensure optimal cluster density. Libraries were circularized using the MGI-Easy Circularization Kit, and DNA nanoballs (DNBs) were generated via rolling circle amplification. Sequencing runs produced raw data files in BCL format, which were converted to FASTQ files and subjected to demultiplexing based on sample barcodes. Data splitting allowed parallel processing of multiple samples, and quality metrics such as Q30 scores and duplication rates were monitored to ensure high-fidelity data generation suitable for downstream analytical pipelines.

### Example 3: The Coordinative Allele-Aware Target Enrichment Improves Capture Homogeneity of Alleles in Target Region

Capture probes were designed using the COATE method for SNPs across chromosomes 13, 18, 21, 22, and X. These probes were synthesized and used to enrich cfDNA libraries from 20 pregnant women. High-throughput sequencing yielded deep coverage (>500×) at target loci. Analysis revealed that the central allelic fraction (CAF)—the median allelic fraction at maternal heterozygous sites—was significantly closer to the expected 0.5 with COATE probes (mean CAF = 0.498) compared to conventional probes (mean CAF = 0.472), indicating reduced allelic bias. The coefficient of variation (CV) of allelic fractions across heterozygous sites was also lower with COATE (CV = 0.12) versus conventional (CV = 0.18). Fetal fraction estimates derived from SNP allelic fractions showed excellent correlation (R² = 0.97) with Y-chromosome-based methods in male pregnancies, confirming the accuracy of COATE-enabled quantification.

### Example 4: Determination of the Negative Threshold of Trisomy 21 Syndrome

To establish a negative threshold for trisomy 21 detection, 200 euploid maternal plasma samples were analyzed. Fetal fraction was calculated using informative SNPs on chromosome 21. Target regions were captured and sequenced, and at each SNP, the probability of disomy versus trisomy was computed using beta-binomial likelihood models. The sum of log-likelihood differences (ΔL) across all informative SNPs was calculated. The 99th percentile of ΔL values in euploid samples was set as the negative threshold (ΔL = −15.2), corresponding to a false positive rate of less than 1%.

### Example 5: Determination of the Positive Threshold of Trisomy 21 Syndrome

Positive reference samples were created by mixing genomic DNA from a trisomy 21 cell line with normal female DNA to simulate fetal fractions of 4%, 6%, 8%, and 10%. These mixtures were processed identically to clinical samples. The ΔL values consistently fell below −30 across all simulated fetal fractions, establishing a positive threshold of ΔL = −25 to ensure high sensitivity while maintaining specificity.

### Example 6: Detection of Trisomy 21 Syndrome in Maternal Plasma

Maternal plasma samples from 38 confirmed trisomy 21 pregnancies were analyzed. All samples yielded ΔL values below the positive threshold, with a mean ΔL of −42.3. Fetal fractions ranged from 4.1% to 19.3%, and all cases were correctly classified, demonstrating 100% sensitivity in this cohort.

### Example 7: Detection of Trisomy in which Homologous Chromosome Recombination has Occurred

A trisomy 21 sample exhibiting both MI and MII SNP patterns was analyzed. The algorithm scanned all possible single-breakpoint models and identified a recombination event at position chr21:28,500,000. The ΔL for the combined MI/MII model was −38.7, significantly lower than models assuming no recombination (ΔL = −22.1), confirming the presence of a meiotic crossover. The detection threshold was met, and the result was validated by amniocentesis.

### Example 8: Detection of Chromosome Microdeletion (Example of DiGeorge)

Genomic DNA from a DiGeorge syndrome cell line (22q11.2 deletion) was mixed with normal maternal DNA to simulate a 6% fetal fraction. After COATE-based enrichment and sequencing, the beta-binomial likelihood ratio for deletion versus disomy yielded ΔL = −28.4, exceeding the positive threshold (−20). The result was confirmed by chromosomal microarray.

### Example 9: Detection of Dominant Monogenic Variation (FGFR3: p.G380R)

cfDNA from a pregnancy with a known FGFR3 c.1138G>A (p.G380R) mutation was analyzed. The mother was homozygous reference (GG), and the variant allele fraction was 7.0%. Using the ACD and FMID filters, the beta-binomial likelihood strongly favored a de novo fetal mutation (ΔL = −18.9). Fragment length analysis showed alternative allele reads were significantly shorter (median 142 bp) than reference reads (median 152 bp; P < 0.001). The system error rate was estimated at 0.1%, and the variant was confidently reported as pathogenic.
