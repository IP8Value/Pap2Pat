# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

Intracellular pathogens represent a significant challenge in clinical diagnostics due to their ability to reside and replicate within host cells, particularly immune cells such as macrophages and dendritic cells. This intracellular lifestyle shields the microorganisms from detection by conventional serological immunoassays, which typically rely on the presence of pathogen-derived antigens or antibodies in biological fluids such as blood or lymphatic fluid. Because the pathogen remains sequestered within host cellular compartments, its biomarkers are not readily accessible in circulation, thereby limiting the utility of standard immunoassay platforms. To overcome this limitation, current diagnostic strategies often require the isolation of infected cells followed by extraction of intracellular nucleic acids for molecular detection via Polymerase Chain Reaction (PCR). However, serological immunoassays remain ineffective for diagnosing active infections caused by intracellular pathogens, especially during early or latent stages when antigen shedding is minimal or absent.

Despite the high sensitivity and specificity of PCR-based methods, these techniques are inherently limited by several practical constraints. PCR requires high-quality, homogeneous DNA samples that are typically obtained through laborious, multistep purification protocols. These procedures are time-consuming, often taking several hours to complete, and can significantly reduce the final yield of target DNA, particularly when the pathogen is present in low abundance. Moreover, the presence of biological matrix components—such as proteins, lipids, and nucleases—in complex clinical samples like blood, tissue biopsies, or stool can interfere with enzymatic amplification, leading to false-negative or inconclusive results. Consequently, the necessity of DNA extraction and purification represents a critical bottleneck in the rapid and reliable diagnosis of intracellular infections.

An alternative approach to enhance detection sensitivity involves the culture of viable intracellular pathogens from clinical specimens prior to PCR analysis. This method, while effective in theory, is severely hampered by the slow growth kinetics of many intracellular bacteria, such as Mycobacterium avium spp. paratuberculosis (MAP), which may require weeks to months of incubation to achieve detectable colony formation. Furthermore, not all clinical isolates are amenable to in vitro culture; some pathogens may be non-viable due to prior antibiotic exposure, host immune activity, or suboptimal transport conditions, rendering them undetectable by culture-dependent methods. Additionally, certain fastidious organisms fail to grow under standard laboratory conditions, further limiting the applicability of culturing as a universal diagnostic tool. These limitations underscore the urgent need for novel diagnostic technologies capable of directly detecting intracellular pathogens in minimally processed clinical samples without reliance on culture or extensive nucleic acid purification.

Recent advances in nanotechnology have opened new avenues for the development of rapid, sensitive, and robust biosensing platforms. Among these innovations, magnetic relaxation nanosensors (MRS) have emerged as a promising class of diagnostic tools that leverage the unique magnetic properties of iron oxide nanoparticles to detect molecular targets through changes in the proton spin-spin relaxation time (T2) of surrounding water molecules. Unlike conventional assays that depend on enzymatic amplification or optical readouts, MRS operate on a physical principle wherein the binding of a target analyte to ligand-functionalized nanoparticles induces measurable alterations in the local magnetic field, which in turn modulate the T2 relaxation signal of bulk water. This mechanism provides inherent signal amplification, as a single binding event influences the relaxation behavior of thousands to millions of water protons, enabling high sensitivity even in turbid or complex media.

A particularly advantageous feature of MRS is their compatibility with crude or minimally processed biological samples, eliminating the need for DNA purification or pathogen culture. Moreover, MRS assays are typically single-step, reagent-efficient, and compatible with portable instrumentation, making them suitable for point-of-care applications. Recent developments have revealed a unique mode of MRS-target interaction wherein binding—not nanoparticle clustering—drives a concentration-dependent increase in T2 relaxation time. This “binding-induced T2 increase” mechanism, especially when implemented with low-valency nanoparticle probes, offers enhanced sensitivity at low target concentrations, which is ideal for detecting rare genomic markers of intracellular pathogens. The convergence of these attributes—speed, sensitivity, simplicity, and robustness in complex matrices—positions MRS as a transformative technology for the diagnosis of intracellular infections, addressing critical gaps left by existing serological, PCR, and culture-based methods.

## SUMMARY

The present invention discloses a hybridizing magnetic relaxation nanosensor (hMRS) specifically engineered for the rapid, sensitive, and specific detection of Mycobacterium avium spp. paratuberculosis (MAP), an intracellular pathogen implicated in Johne’s disease in cattle and Crohn’s disease in humans. The hMRS comprises a polyacrylic acid-coated iron oxide nanoparticle functionalized with an oligonucleotide probe complementary to a highly conserved genomic element of MAP known as IS900. This unique design enables the hMRS to directly bind to IS900 DNA sequences in minimally processed clinical samples, inducing a measurable increase in the transverse relaxation time (T2) of surrounding water protons, which serves as a quantitative indicator of MAP presence.

The hMRS is configured to operate under low-valency conditions, with an average of approximately 55 oligonucleotide probes per nanoparticle, which optimizes the sensor’s response for high signal output at low target concentrations—a critical feature for detecting sparse genomic markers of slow-growing intracellular pathogens. Unlike conventional magnetic relaxation sensors that rely on nanoparticle clustering to generate a T2 decrease, the hMRS of the present invention produces a T2 increase upon target binding, enabling faster kinetics, improved sensitivity, and simplified assay interpretation. The invention further encompasses a streamlined protocol involving brief thermal treatment of crude biological samples to release intracellular DNA, followed by direct incubation with hMRS and measurement of T2 changes within 60 minutes.

Performance evaluation of the hMRS demonstrates superior capabilities compared to nested PCR (nPCR), the current gold standard for MAP detection. The hMRS achieves single-genome-copy sensitivity in both pure and crude DNA preparations, outperforms nPCR in complex matrices such as homogenized tissues and peripheral blood, and provides more accurate correlation with clinical disease status in patients with Crohn’s disease. Importantly, the hMRS eliminates the need for DNA purification, enzymatic amplification, and prolonged culture steps, thereby reducing assay time from weeks to under one hour while maintaining or exceeding the diagnostic accuracy of existing methods. The invention thus provides a robust, cost-effective, and clinically translatable platform for the detection of intracellular pathogens, with broad applicability to human and veterinary diagnostics, epidemiological surveillance, and therapeutic monitoring.

### Design and Synthesis of hMRS

DNA isolation for PCR involves phenol-chloroform extraction, yielding pure but low-yield DNA. hMRS bypass this by using crude DNA from heat-lysed cells. The hMRS probe is a 15-mer (5′-ATGTGGTTGCTGTGT-3′) complementary to IS900. Composed of polyacrylic acid-coated iron oxide nanoparticles, hMRS bind MAP DNA via hybridization.

Heating at 95°C for 3 minutes (twice) denatures DNA, reducing torsional strain and enabling probe access. Cooling permits reannealing with hMRS bound. This induces a T2 increase, monitored by a compact NMR relaxometer (0.47 T). Specificity testing against various mycobacteria and Gram-positive/negative bacteria confirmed no cross-reactivity; only MAP DNA produced a significant ΔT2+.

Sensitivity testing with pure and crude MAP DNA showed concentration-dependent T2 increases, with single-genome detection. Low valency (55 oligos/nanoparticle) ensures high signal at low target levels. Compared to nPCR—which failed with crude DNA and required 6+ hours—hMRS delivered results in 30 minutes with superior sensitivity.

In clinical isolates from Crohn’s patients, hMRS matched or exceeded nPCR, identifying a blood culture positive missed by nPCR in tissue. In cattle tissues with Johne’s disease, hMRS detected MAP in homogenates without purification. In blood samples, hMRS outperformed both direct and culture-based nPCR in predicting disease status, with ROC analysis confirming superior diagnostic accuracy.

### Discussion

The hMRS represents a breakthrough in intracellular pathogen detection. Its sensitivity (single genome copy), speed (<60 min), and compatibility with crude samples address key limitations of PCR and culture. The T2 increase mechanism, driven by low-valency binding, provides amplified signal without clustering, enabling robust performance in complex media.

Portable relaxometers allow point-of-care use. hMRS detects intracellular pathogens directly in blood and tissue, circumventing culture delays. This enhances clinical diagnosis of Crohn’s disease and Johne’s disease, and supports research into MAP’s role in chronic inflammation. By providing rapid, quantitative pathogen load data, hMRS also aids in understanding pathogenesis and monitoring therapy.

### Methods

IS900-specific hMRS were prepared by incorporating propargyl groups on polyacrylic-acid-coated iron oxide nanoparticles via carbodiimide chemistry. Azide-modified oligonucleotides were conjugated via copper-catalyzed click chemistry. Epitope-sensing MRS were formulated by conjugating nanoparticles to Protein G, then to anti-MAP antibodies.

Valency was assessed by protein quantification. Size (78±3 nm) and zeta potential (–35 mV) were measured by DLS and zetasizer. Relaxivities (R1=45, R2=60 mM⁻¹s⁻¹) were determined at 0.47 T. Oligonucleotide concentration was quantified by UV absorbance. hMRS were stored at 4°C.

MAP lab strains and clinical isolates were grown in BACTEC bottles and quantified by BACTEC 460. Heat-inactivation was by autoclaving. Pure DNA was extracted via phenol-chloroform; crude DNA by boiling. hMRS and nPCR experiments used 6 µg Fe/mL nanoparticles and standard nPCR protocols.

Blood samples from Crohn’s patients and healthy controls were collected under IRB approval. White blood cells were isolated by centrifugation; DNA extracted crudely (boiling) or purely (phenol-chloroform). Culturing involved 12-week MGIT incubation.

T2 times were measured on a Minispec relaxometer using CPMG pulse sequences. Statistical analysis included two-proportion z-tests and ROC curves, with clinical status as the classifier variable.
