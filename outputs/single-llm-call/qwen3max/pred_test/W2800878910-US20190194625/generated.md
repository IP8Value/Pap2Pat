# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

The blood-brain barrier (BBB) is a highly specialized and dynamic interface that separates the circulating blood from the brain parenchyma, thereby maintaining central nervous system homeostasis and protecting neural tissue from potentially harmful substances. Comprising primarily brain microvascular endothelial cells interconnected by tight and adherens junctions, the BBB functions in concert with pericytes, astrocytes, microglia, oligodendrocytes, and neurons—collectively forming the human neurovascular unit (NVU). These cellular components engage in intricate bidirectional signaling that regulates barrier integrity, transport mechanisms, immune surveillance, and metabolic support. Despite its critical physiological role, the BBB presents a formidable obstacle to drug delivery for neurological disorders, contributing significantly to the high attrition rate of central nervous system (CNS)-targeted therapeutics during clinical development.

Current in vitro models of the BBB have been instrumental in advancing basic understanding of barrier function but remain limited in their physiological relevance. Traditional two-dimensional (2D) monocultures or co-cultures often rely on immortalized cell lines that fail to recapitulate the native cellular composition, spatial organization, and functional complexity of the human NVU. While more advanced three-dimensional (3D) models incorporating primary human brain microvascular endothelial cells, pericytes, and astrocytes have demonstrated improved expression of junctional proteins and transporters such as P-glycoprotein (P-gp) and glucose transporter 1 (GLUT1), they typically exclude key parenchymal cell types—including microglia, oligodendrocytes, and electrically active neurons—that are integral to neurovascular coupling and disease pathophysiology. Moreover, existing models frequently lack the structural architecture necessary to mimic the tubular geometry of cerebral microvessels or the dynamic perfusion conditions present in vivo. Consequently, these systems exhibit limited predictive power for assessing compound permeability, neurotoxicity, or the effects of pathological insults such as hypoxia.

There exists a pressing need for an improved in vitro BBB model that faithfully recapitulates the multicellular complexity, spatial organization, and functional attributes of the adult human neurovascular unit. Such a model must incorporate all six major cortical cell types—endothelial cells, pericytes, astrocytes, microglia, oligodendrocytes, and neurons—in physiologically relevant proportions and spatial arrangements. It should support long-term culture viability, express critical BBB markers including tight junction proteins (e.g., ZO-1, claudin-5), adherens junction proteins (e.g., VE-cadherin, β-catenin), and efflux/transport proteins (e.g., P-gp, GLUT1), and demonstrate functional responses to pharmacological and pathological stimuli. Furthermore, the model should be amenable to integration into microfluidic platforms for perfusion-based studies and scalable for high-throughput screening applications in drug discovery and neurotoxicology.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides an in vitro blood-brain barrier model that overcomes the limitations of prior art by incorporating a complete complement of human neurovascular unit cell types arranged in a spatially organized, three-dimensional architecture that mimics native brain tissue. The model comprises an endothelial cell layer that envelops an inner brain tissue layer containing neurons, astrocytes, microglia, and oligodendrocytes, thereby establishing a physiologically relevant barrier between vascular and parenchymal compartments. In certain embodiments, a porous membrane is interposed between the endothelial and brain tissue layers to facilitate controlled molecular exchange while maintaining structural separation. The invention further encompasses a microfluidic device that houses the in vitro BBB model, enabling perfusion of the endothelial lumen and real-time monitoring of barrier function under dynamic flow conditions.

This integrated system replicates key features of the human BBB, including the expression of tight and adherens junction proteins, polarized localization of efflux and nutrient transporters, charge- and size-selective permeability, and responsiveness to physiological and pathological stimuli such as histamine-induced barrier opening and hypoxia-mediated junctional disruption. The model supports long-term culture stability, maintains high cell viability for at least 21 days in vitro, and permits personalized applications through the use of patient-derived induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs). By accurately modeling the multicellular interactions and functional dynamics of the human NVU, the invention provides a robust platform for compound screening, toxicity assessment, mechanistic studies of neurological diseases, and the development of BBB-penetrating therapeutics.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF EMBODIMENTS

### Terminology and Definitions

As used herein, the term “blood-brain barrier (BBB) model” refers to an in vitro construct that recapitulates the structural and functional properties of the human neurovascular unit, including but not limited to the presence of brain microvascular endothelial cells forming a continuous layer with tight and adherens junctions, supported by pericytes and astrocytes, and adjacent to parenchymal cells such as neurons, microglia, and oligodendrocytes. The term “neurovascular unit (NVU)” denotes the functional ensemble of endothelial cells, pericytes, astrocytes, microglia, oligodendrocytes, and neurons that collectively regulate BBB integrity and cerebral homeostasis. “Porous membrane” refers to a semi-permeable substrate, optionally coated with extracellular matrix proteins such as collagen or laminin, that allows diffusion of molecules while providing mechanical support for cell attachment and stratification. “Bioprinting” encompasses additive manufacturing techniques wherein cells suspended in a hydrogel carrier are deposited in predefined spatial patterns to generate 3D tissue constructs. “Sacrificial hydrogel” denotes a temporary scaffold material that can be removed post-printing to create hollow luminal structures mimicking blood vessels.

### In Vitro Blood Brain Barrier Model with Endothelial Cell Layer

The invention provides an in vitro BBB model comprising a confluent layer of human brain microvascular endothelial cells (HBMECs) that forms a continuous barrier enclosing an inner core of brain parenchymal cells. This endothelial layer expresses hallmark BBB proteins including zona occludens-1 (ZO-1), claudin-5, VE-cadherin, β-catenin, P-glycoprotein (P-gp), and glucose transporter 1 (GLUT1), as confirmed by immunohistochemical staining and functional assays. The endothelial cells are derived from primary human sources or differentiated from induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs), ensuring human relevance and enabling patient-specific modeling. The model exhibits low paracellular permeability to large molecules such as IgG under baseline conditions and demonstrates reversible barrier opening upon exposure to histamine, validating its dynamic responsiveness.

### Model with Neuronal Cell Layer and Porous Membrane

In alternative embodiments, the BBB model includes a neuronal cell layer positioned adjacent to the endothelial layer, with a porous membrane interposed therebetween. The neuronal layer comprises mature, electrically active human neurons, which may include dopaminergic subtypes identified by tyrosine hydroxylase (TH) expression. The porous membrane, fabricated from materials such as polycarbonate or polyethylene terephthalate, possesses pore sizes ranging from 0.4 to 8.0 micrometers and is optionally coated with basement membrane components like collagen IV or laminin to enhance endothelial adhesion and polarization. This configuration enables compartmentalized analysis of compound penetration across the endothelial barrier and subsequent effects on neuronal viability and function.

### Making the Model by Depositing Cells on a Porous Membrane

The BBB model is fabricated by sequentially seeding cells onto opposite surfaces of a porous membrane housed within a transwell insert or microfluidic chamber. Initially, pericytes and astrocytes are seeded on the basolateral side to establish a supportive stromal layer. Subsequently, HBMECs are seeded on the apical surface and cultured under conditions promoting junctional maturation, including the use of endothelial growth medium supplemented with hydrocortisone and cyclic AMP. After barrier formation is confirmed by transendothelial electrical resistance (TEER) measurements or tracer flux assays, neuronal cells—either as dissociated cultures or pre-formed spheroids—are introduced into the basolateral compartment to complete the NVU assembly.

### Making the Model by Adding Cells to Organoids

In another embodiment, the BBB model is constructed through a staged self-assembly process involving organoid formation. First, a core organoid comprising astrocytes, microglia, oligodendrocytes, and neurons is generated using hanging drop or low-adhesion culture methods. After 48 hours of aggregation, HBMECs and pericytes are added to the culture, where they migrate to the organoid periphery and form an enveloping endothelial layer. This staged approach enhances endothelial coverage and barrier continuity compared to simultaneous co-aggregation of all six cell types, as evidenced by uniform CD31 and ZO-1 staining across the organoid surface.

### Model in a Tubular Shape to Mimic a Blood Vessel

To better emulate the cylindrical geometry of cerebral microvessels, the BBB model is engineered in a tubular configuration. This is achieved by bioprinting a sacrificial hydrogel filament—composed of Pluronic F127 or gelatin—around which endothelial cells and supporting mural cells are deposited in a fibrin or collagen hydrogel matrix. Following crosslinking, the sacrificial core is removed by cooling or enzymatic digestion, leaving a patent lumen that can be perfused with culture medium or test compounds. Neuronal and glial cells are either embedded in the surrounding matrix or cultured in an adjacent chamber separated by a porous membrane, enabling vascular-parenchymal crosstalk under flow conditions.

### Cells May Be Obtained from Donors or Stem Cells

All cellular components of the BBB model may be sourced from primary human donors or differentiated from human iPSCs. Primary cells—including HBMECs, pericytes, and astrocytes—are isolated from surgical specimens or commercial vendors and expanded under defined culture conditions. iPSC-derived neurons, microglia, and oligodendrocytes are generated through established differentiation protocols involving sequential exposure to morphogens and growth factors. The use of iPSCs enables the creation of disease-specific models harboring known genetic mutations associated with neurological disorders such as Alzheimer’s disease, Parkinson’s disease, or amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, thereby facilitating personalized drug screening and mechanistic studies.

### 1. Blood, Brain Barrier Models and Methods of Making the Same.

The invention provides an in vitro blood-brain barrier model comprising an endothelial cell layer and a neuronal cell layer, with a porous membrane interposed between them to enable controlled molecular exchange while maintaining structural segregation. The endothelial layer consists of human brain microvascular endothelial cells that form a continuous monolayer expressing tight junction proteins ZO-1 and claudin-5, adherens junction proteins VE-cadherin and β-catenin, and functional transporters P-gp and GLUT1. The neuronal layer contains electrically active human neurons, including dopaminergic subtypes, along with supporting glial cells—astrocytes, pericytes, microglia, and oligodendrocytes—that collectively constitute a complete neurovascular unit. The porous membrane, optionally coated with collagen or laminin to enhance cell adhesion, facilitates nutrient diffusion and permits assessment of compound permeability from the vascular to parenchymal compartment.

The model is fabricated by depositing cells onto a porous membrane using either manual seeding or automated bioprinting techniques. In one method, a suspension of endothelial cells, pericytes, and astrocytes is printed onto the apical surface of the membrane, followed by neuronal and glial cells on the basolateral side. Alternatively, pre-formed organoids containing parenchymal cells are placed in contact with the membrane, and endothelial cells are subsequently seeded to form an overlying barrier. In a tubular configuration, the model mimics a blood vessel by embedding endothelial cells around a sacrificial hydrogel core that is later removed to create a perfusable lumen. Cells may be suspended in a hydrogel carrier—such as fibrin, collagen, or Matrigel—for bioprinting, ensuring high viability and spatial precision. The hydrogel may also serve as a sacrificial material to define hollow spaces when patterned and subsequently dissolved. All cell types may be derived from human donors or differentiated from iPSCs, enabling both generic and patient-specific applications. The resulting construct is integrated into a microfluidic device that allows continuous perfusion of the endothelial lumen, simulating physiological shear stress and enabling real-time monitoring of barrier integrity and compound transport.

### 2. Methods of Use.

The BBB model of the invention is employed in a variety of applications related to drug discovery, toxicology, and disease modeling. In compound screening, the model is used to assess the efficacy, toxicity, or metabolic activity of candidate therapeutics by measuring their ability to cross the endothelial barrier and exert effects on parenchymal cells. Permeability is quantified by tracking the penetration of fluorescently labeled compounds—such as FITC-IgG or small molecule tracers—into the neuronal compartment using confocal microscopy or plate reader assays. The model also enables evaluation of immunological activity by introducing immune cells or cytokines into the vascular compartment and monitoring inflammatory responses in microglia and astrocytes. Furthermore, it serves as a platform for testing novel strategies to enhance BBB penetration, including receptor-mediated transcytosis, transient barrier disruption, or nanoparticle-based delivery systems.

Personalized medicine applications are facilitated by constructing models using cells derived from a specific subject, allowing prediction of individual responses to CNS-targeted drugs. Disease modeling is achieved by incorporating cells harboring known genetic mutations—such as those in APP, PSEN1, LRRK2, or SOD1—to study pathogenic mechanisms and screen corrective therapies. The model is particularly valuable for investigating conditions involving BBB dysfunction, including ischemic stroke, traumatic brain injury, multiple sclerosis, and neurodegenerative diseases. Under hypoxic conditions, the model recapitulates the downregulation and disorganization of junctional proteins observed in vivo, providing a physiologically relevant system for studying neurovascular injury and testing neuroprotective agents.

## EXAMPLES

### Example 1

A vessel-like BBB model was fabricated using 3D bioprinting technology. Human neurons differentiated from iPSCs were suspended in a fibrin hydrogel bioink and printed into a cylindrical structure surrounding a central sacrificial Pluronic F127 filament. Endothelial cells, pericytes, and astrocytes were co-printed in a concentric layer adjacent to the sacrificial core. Following thermal removal of the Pluronic, the lumen was perfused with endothelial growth medium, promoting the formation of a confluent endothelial lining. The neuronal construct was maintained in neural maintenance medium, and after 7 days of culture, immunostaining confirmed the expression of MAP2 and βIII-tubulin in neurons, GFAP in astrocytes, and CD31 in endothelial cells. Confocal imaging revealed ZO-1 localization at endothelial cell borders, indicating functional tight junction assembly. Viability assays demonstrated >90% live cells throughout the construct, confirming the biocompatibility of the printing process and culture conditions.

### Example 2. Human Cortex Model with Spheroid Culture System

A 3D spheroid model of the BBB was created using the hanging drop method. Primary human astrocytes, pericytes, and brain microvascular endothelial cells were mixed in a 5:1:1 ratio and combined with iPSC-derived neurons, microglia, and oligodendrocyte progenitors to achieve a final composition of 15% astrocytes, 15% pericytes, 30% endothelial cells, 20% neurons, 15% oligodendrocytes, and 5% microglia. Spheroids formed within 96 hours and exhibited high viability (>85%) for 21 days in vitro, as assessed by calcein AM/ethidium homodimer staining. Immunohistochemistry confirmed the peripheral localization of CD31+ endothelial cells and PDGFRβ+ pericytes, with GFAP+ astrocytes and Iba1+ microglia distributed in the intermediate zone and MAP2+/TH+ neurons in the core. Claudin-5 and ZO-1 were expressed at endothelial junctions, and P-gp and GLUT1 showed polarized membrane localization. The model was used to evaluate neurotoxicity: mercury(II) chloride caused significant ATP depletion in BBB-deficient neuronal spheroids but not in intact BBB+ spheroids, demonstrating charge-selective barrier function.

### Example 3: Bioprint Functional Cortical Tissue

Functional cortical tissue was bioprinted by suspending human iPSC-derived neurons in a fibrin hydrogel containing thrombin and aprotinin. The bioink was extruded through a nozzle into a crosslinking bath, forming layered constructs that were subsequently cultured in neural differentiation medium. After 10 days, viability assays showed >90% live cells, and immunostaining revealed robust expression of βIII-tubulin and MAP2, confirming neuronal maturation. A microvessel was simultaneously printed by co-depositing endothelial cells, pericytes, and astrocytes around a sacrificial hydrogel core. Upon core removal, the vessel was perfused, and CD31 staining confirmed endothelial confluence. The integrated neurovascular unit was used to test MPTP toxicity: MPTP reduced ATP levels in BBB+ constructs due to its lipophilicity and conversion to MPP+ in glial cells, whereas MPP+ alone had no effect, validating the model’s ability to discriminate BBB-permeable versus impermeable compounds.

### Example 4. Three-Dimensional Bioprinting of the Human Neurovascular Unit

The human neurovascular unit (NVU) was bioprinted using primary human cells or iPSC-derived counterparts. Cells were expanded in 2D culture, then formulated into bioinks: endothelial cells and pericytes in fibrin, and neurons, astrocytes, microglia, and oligodendrocytes in hyaluronic acid-based hydrogel. Cortical tissue containing mature MAP2+ neurons was printed adjacent to capillary-scale and micro-arteriole-scale NVU constructs. After 14 days of culture, immunohistochemistry confirmed the presence of all six cell types with appropriate markers: CD31 (endothelium), PDGFRβ (pericytes), GFAP (astrocytes), Iba1 (microglia), O4/MBP (oligodendrocytes), and TH (dopaminergic neurons). Junctional proteins ZO-1, claudin-5, VE-cadherin, and β-catenin were localized at endothelial borders, and transporters P-gp and GLUT1 exhibited polarized expression. The constructs maintained structural integrity and high viability, demonstrating utility in high-throughput drug screening and neurotoxicity testing for CNS therapeutics.
