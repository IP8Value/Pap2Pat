## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates to a microviscometer, specifically a microviscometer based on asynchronous magnetic bead rotation (AMBR) for detecting and quantifying analytes in solution through measurement of viscosity changes. The invention provides a label-free, real-time method for monitoring biochemical reactions involving macromolecules such as nucleic acids, proteins, carbohydrates, and lipids, by detecting physical or chemical changes that alter the viscosity of the reaction medium.

## BACKGROUND

The detection of deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) is fundamental to numerous scientific, clinical, and industrial applications, including infectious disease diagnostics, genetic screening, forensic identification, and biopharmaceutical development. Current DNA detection technologies predominantly rely on fluorescence-based methods integrated into platforms such as quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR), restriction fragment length polymorphism (RFLP), and ligation detection reaction (LDR). While these methods offer high sensitivity and specificity, they require fluorescent labeling of probes or primers, specialized optical instrumentation, and often complex data interpretation algorithms. These requirements increase cost, limit portability, and hinder deployment in resource-limited settings.

Alternative approaches to DNA detection have been explored to circumvent the need for labeling and optical detection. Among these, viscosity-based detection presents a promising label-free strategy. The viscosity of a double-stranded DNA (dsDNA) solution is directly influenced by both the concentration and the average molecular length of the DNA strands. In enzymatic reactions such as restriction digestion, where long DNA molecules are cleaved into shorter fragments, the solution viscosity decreases. Conversely, in polymerase chain reaction (PCR), where specific DNA sequences are amplified and elongated, the viscosity increases proportionally with product formation. Critically, these viscosity changes occur without any chemical modification of the DNA, enabling intrinsic, label-free monitoring of reaction progress.

Despite the theoretical appeal of viscosity-based DNA detection, practical implementation has been limited by the lack of sensitive, miniaturized, and rapid viscometry techniques compatible with small sample volumes typical of diagnostic assays. Traditional viscometers, such as capillary or rotational devices, require milliliter-scale volumes and are ill-suited for integration into microfluidic or point-of-care platforms. Moreover, they often lack the temporal resolution necessary for real-time reaction monitoring. Thus, there exists a critical need for a microscale viscometer capable of detecting minute viscosity changes in microliter or sub-microliter volumes with high sensitivity and speed.

The present invention addresses this need through the application of asynchronous magnetic bead rotation (AMBR), a technique that leverages the rotational dynamics of paramagnetic microbeads in a rotating magnetic field to infer the viscosity of the surrounding fluid. This approach enables real-time, label-free, and highly sensitive detection of DNA and other macromolecular analytes in minimal sample volumes.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention introduces a novel method and apparatus for detecting analytes based on Asynchronous Magnetic Bead Rotation (AMBR). The AMBR principle relies on the observation that a paramagnetic bead suspended in a fluid and subjected to a rotating magnetic field will rotate synchronously with the field at low driving frequencies but will transition to asynchronous rotation when the driving frequency exceeds a critical threshold determined by the fluid’s viscosity. In the asynchronous regime, the bead’s rotation period becomes linearly proportional to the viscosity of the surrounding medium.

The method of detecting an analyte change according to the invention involves monitoring a physical or chemical change in the state of the analyte that results in a measurable alteration of the solution’s viscosity. A physical change in state may include changes in molecular conformation, aggregation, or phase separation, while a chemical change may involve bond formation or cleavage, such as polymerization, hydrolysis, or ligation.

The invention encompasses various embodiments wherein the analyte is selected from the group consisting of nucleic acids, proteins, carbohydrates, lipids, cells, and synthetic polymers. The method enables real-time measurement of reaction kinetics, allowing continuous monitoring of processes such as DNA amplification, enzymatic digestion, protein folding, or cellular responses without the need for labels or external reporters.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides methods based on Asynchronous Magnetic Bead Rotation (AMBR) for detecting and quantifying analytes through viscosity measurement. The method employs commercially available paramagnetic microbeads that are introduced into a sample solution containing the analyte of interest. Upon exposure to a controlled rotating magnetic field, the rotational behavior of the beads is monitored optically or via alternative detection means. Changes in the rotation period of the beads directly correlate with changes in solution viscosity, which in turn reflect alterations in the analyte’s concentration, length, conformation, or interaction state.

The use of magnetic particles offers significant advantages, including ease of handling, compatibility with aqueous biological samples, and the absence of photobleaching or background interference common in optical methods. The motivation for viscosity change detection stems from the intrinsic relationship between macromolecular properties and fluid rheology—specifically, that the presence, concentration, size, or structural state of biomolecules modulates the resistance to flow in their solvent environment.

Application examples include monitoring PCR amplification, restriction enzyme digestion, protein synthesis, and cellular secretion. The advantages of AMBR include label-free operation, minimal sample consumption, rapid measurement (typically under one minute), real-time capability, and potential for integration into portable or microfluidic diagnostic devices. For clarity, the term “analyte” as used herein refers to any molecule, complex, or cell whose presence, concentration, or state change induces a detectable viscosity shift in the solution.

### Asynchronous Magnetic Bead Rotation (AMBR)

The AMBR principle is founded on the nonlinear rotational dynamics of paramagnetic beads in a rotating magnetic field. At low driving frequencies, the magnetic torque exerted on the bead overcomes viscous drag, resulting in synchronous rotation—i.e., the bead rotates at the same angular velocity as the applied field. However, as the driving frequency increases beyond a critical value dependent on fluid viscosity, the bead can no longer maintain synchrony and enters an asynchronous rotation regime, where its rotation period becomes longer than the field period.

This asynchronous rotation is distinct from synchronous rotation and is characterized by a stable, albeit slower, rotational motion that is highly sensitive to the surrounding fluid’s viscosity. When multiple beads are present, they may form transient clusters due to magnetic dipole interactions; however, under appropriate field strengths and bead concentrations, individual bead rotation dominates, and cluster effects are minimized. Viscosity is measured by determining the rotation period of the bead(s) and applying a pre-established calibration curve relating period to viscosity.

The AMBR approach represents a significant advancement over prior art, which either assumed synchronous rotation only or failed to exploit the linear relationship between rotation period and viscosity in the asynchronous regime. The invention operates effectively with extremely small sample volumes, including volumes less than 10 microliters, less than 1 microliter, less than 100 nanoliters, less than 10 nanoliters, less than 1 nanoliter, and even down to picoliter ranges when integrated into microfluidic channels.

AMBR exhibits broad viscosity detection capabilities, accurately measuring viscosities ranging from approximately 0.89 centipoise (cP) to 2.8 cP, and potentially extending to higher or lower ranges with optimized bead and field parameters. The method enables detection of analyte concentrations through their contribution to solution viscosity, with applications in quantifying DNA during amplification, monitoring protein expression, assessing carbohydrate polymerization, and evaluating lipid assembly.

Specific monitoring applications include real-time observation of nucleic acid synthesis (e.g., PCR), protein synthesis (e.g., cell-free translation), nucleic acid hydrolysis (e.g., nuclease activity), nucleic acid ligation (e.g., DNA repair assays), protein hydrolysis (e.g., protease activity), and other biochemical transformations that alter macromolecular size or concentration. In summary, AMBR provides a versatile, sensitive, and cost-effective platform for label-free biochemical analysis. Its advantages over prior art—including elimination of labeling, reduced instrumentation complexity, and compatibility with miniaturization—make it particularly suited for point-of-care diagnostics and high-throughput screening. The method overcomes limitations of fluorescence-based systems and traditional viscometers, offering a robust alternative for real-time reaction monitoring.

### Analytes

Analytes in the context of the present invention refer to any substance whose presence, concentration, or state change induces a measurable viscosity shift in a solution. A “change in state” encompasses physical transformations such as conformational shifts, aggregation, dissociation, or phase transitions, as well as chemical transformations including bond formation (polymerization, ligation) or bond cleavage (hydrolysis, digestion).

Cells serve as analytes when their presence, density, activation state, or secretory activity alters medium viscosity. Exemplary cell types include mammalian cells, bacterial cells, yeast, and blood cells. Nucleic acids, including DNA and RNA, are key analytes; nucleic acid oligomers are defined as short sequences typically ranging from 10 to 10,000 base pairs in length, with common examples including primers (15–30 nt), amplicons (100–5000 bp), and restriction fragments (50–25,000 bp). Nucleobases include adenine, thymine, guanine, cytosine, and uracil, as well as universal bases such as inosine or nitroindole derivatives that pair promiscuously. Oligomer lengths may vary from 10, 20, 50, 100, 500, 1000, to 5000 nucleotides or base pairs.

Proteins are defined as polypeptides composed of amino acid chains, including enzymes, antibodies, receptors, and structural proteins. Amino acids are classified as standard (20 canonical types), non-standard, or modified residues. Protein synthesis detection includes monitoring translation in vitro or in vivo. Non-naturally occurring proteins encompass engineered variants, fusion proteins, and synthetic polypeptides. Fragments, analogs, and variants include truncated forms, sequence mutants, and post-translationally modified species.

Carbohydrates include monosaccharides, disaccharides, and oligosaccharides (short sugar chains of 2–20 units). Lipids as analytes include fatty acids, phospholipids, and lipid-derived hormones such as prostaglandins, leukotrienes, and steroid hormones like cortisol and testosterone.

## EXAMPLES

### Example 1

Reagents used in viscosity testing included glycerol/water mixtures, lambda DNA EcoRI digests (3530–21,226 bp), and pUC18 HaeIII digests (80–587 bp), all obtained from commercial suppliers. Paramagnetic microbeads of 7.6, 16, and 45 μm diameter were employed. Viscosity measurement was performed by introducing beads into sample solutions containing 0.2% bovine serum albumin to prevent surface adhesion. Sample preparation involved mixing bead suspension with analyte solution and loading between glass slides separated by double-sided tape and nickel spacers to maintain a 210 μm gap, sealed with silicone to prevent evaporation. Image analysis was conducted by recording bead rotation at 10 frames per second, generating intensity-time plots via ImageJ, and determining rotation periods using fast Fourier transform in MATLAB. Viscosities were independently verified using a Ubbelohde viscometer by measuring flow time through calibrated marks in a temperature-controlled water bath.

### Calibration of AMBR Viscometer

A calibration experiment was conducted using glycerol/water solutions of known viscosity. A linear relationship was observed between solution viscosity and bead rotation period across 0.89–2.8 cP. A correlation curve was constructed by plotting rotation period against viscosity, yielding high uniformity. The theoretical framework is based on nonlinear magnetic oscillation: below a critical frequency, beads rotate synchronously; above it, asynchronous rotation occurs. Magnetic torque on a paramagnetic bead is given by τ_mag = (π/2) χ'' V_m B² / μ₀, where χ'' is the imaginary susceptibility, V_m magnetic volume, B field strength, and μ₀ permeability of free space. Viscous drag torque is τ_visc = κ η V (dθ/dt), with κ = 6 for spheres, η viscosity, V bead volume. Equating torques yields T ∝ η, confirming linearity. Experimental data validated this relationship across bead sizes and field frequencies, with optimal performance at higher driving frequencies away from instability thresholds.

### Example 3

The influence of bead properties on rotation periods was investigated by measuring 20 beads in identical solutions. A relative standard deviation of ~10% was observed, with no clear size dependency, indicating that magnetic content variation—not size—is the primary source of inter-bead variability. Single-bead measurements over time showed only ~1% deviation, confirming that bead heterogeneity, not measurement noise, dominates error. This supports averaging across multiple beads for accurate viscosity determination.

### Preparation of Digestion Reaction Samples

Digestion reactions employed lambda DNA (48,502 bp) cleaved by EcoRI into fragments of 3530–21,226 bp. Reactions contained enzyme, buffer, DNA, and nuclease-free water, incubated at 37°C for 1 hour, then equilibrated to 25°C before AMBR measurement. PCR samples were prepared with primers targeting a 4500 bp region, master mix, and template DNA, thermocycled for up to 41 cycles with aliquots removed at intervals. Gel electrophoresis on 0.8% agarose gels confirmed product formation, with fluorescent intensities of 4500 bp bands quantified via ImageJ. A linear relationship between viscosity and DNA concentration was established, consistent with η = η₀(1 + C[η]), where [η] is intrinsic viscosity. DNA solutions exhibited Newtonian behavior in dilute regimes. EcoRI digestion reduced viscosity proportionally to fragment number. AMBR detected digestion completion and PCR progression in real time, with viscosity increases correlating to amplicon accumulation. Sequence variations altering restriction sites were detectable via viscosity differences. PCR product formation was monitored cycle-by-cycle, demonstrating the method’s utility for real-time, label-free nucleic acid quantification.
