## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

Histological analysis of biological tissues remains a cornerstone of diagnostic pathology and biomedical research, enabling visualization of cellular morphology and molecular markers through techniques such as immunohistochemistry and immunofluorescence. A critical prerequisite for accurate protein detection in fixed tissue sections is the preservation of antigenicity—the ability of an epitope to be recognized by a specific antibody. Aldehyde-based cross-linking agents, particularly formaldehyde and paraformaldehyde, are widely employed as fixatives due to their capacity to preserve tissue architecture by forming methylene bridges between amino groups of adjacent proteins. However, this very mechanism compromises antigenicity by masking or altering epitopes, thereby impeding antibody binding and reducing detection sensitivity.

Current methods for reversing aldehyde-induced epitope masking rely primarily on heat-induced antigen retrieval (HIAR), which typically involves heating tissue sections in acidic or basic buffers such as citrate or EDTA. While effective for some antigens, these approaches are inconsistent across different tissue types and target proteins, often failing to restore detectable signal for structurally sensitive or highly cross-linked epitopes. Moreover, conventional HIAR protocols can cause tissue damage, including detachment from slides or morphological distortion, especially when aggressive pH conditions or prolonged heating are required. Alternative enzymatic retrieval methods, such as pepsin digestion, introduce additional variability and may degrade the very antigens they aim to unmask.

The limitations of existing antigen retrieval strategies underscore a persistent need for more reliable, broadly applicable, and gentle methods that effectively reverse aldehyde-mediated epitope masking without compromising tissue integrity. Prior art has not adequately addressed the root chemical cause of antigen masking—namely, the covalent modification of lysine and other nucleophilic residues by formaldehyde-derived methylene adducts. Consequently, there remains a significant deficiency in the field: a lack of reagents that chemically reverse or prevent the formation of these cross-links at the molecular level, thereby restoring native protein conformation and antibody accessibility in a controlled and reproducible manner.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a novel antigen retrieval method based on the use of aldehyde scavenging agents that chemically react with residual or cross-linked aldehydes in fixed tissue, thereby reversing epitope masking and enhancing protein detectability. Specifically, the method employs a solution comprising one or more aldehyde scavenging agents capable of reacting with formaldehyde or formaldehyde-derived adducts to liberate masked epitopes. In certain embodiments, the method includes an optional heating step to accelerate the reaction kinetics and improve penetration of the scavenging agent into the tissue matrix.

The aldehyde scavenging agent solution is formulated to operate under mild acidic to neutral pH conditions (typically pH 3–7), minimizing tissue damage while maximizing antigen recovery. Representative aldehyde scavenging agents include, but are not limited to, ascorbic acid, 2-imidazolidinone, tris(hydroxymethyl)aminomethane (Tris), hydrazides, amino alcohols, and other nucleophilic compounds that form stable adducts with aldehydes. The invention further encompasses kits containing pre-formulated solutions of these scavenging agents, optionally combined with nonionic surfactants and stabilizing agents to enhance performance and shelf life.

In addition to direct application in immunohistochemical workflows, the invention includes methods for identifying new antigen retrieval agents by testing candidate compounds in model systems involving formaldehyde-fixed peptides or proteins, followed by detection via mass spectrometry or immunoassay. This platform enables rapid screening and optimization of novel scavengers for specific diagnostic or research applications.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

As used herein, the terms “a” and “an” are intended to mean “at least one” unless context clearly indicates otherwise. The term “comprise” and its grammatical variants are inclusive and open-ended, allowing for additional elements or steps not explicitly recited. The word “or” is used in the inclusive sense, meaning “and/or,” unless the context dictates exclusivity. The term “about” refers to a range of ±10% of the stated value, unless otherwise specified. “Unmasking” refers to the chemical or physical process by which an epitope previously rendered inaccessible due to fixation becomes available for antibody binding.

The invention provides a method for retrieving antigens and improving detection of proteins in aldehyde-fixed tissue sections. This method comprises contacting the fixed tissue with a solution containing an aldehyde scavenging agent, optionally followed by heating. The heating step may involve temperatures ranging from 80°C to 98°C for durations of 30 to 60 minutes, typically performed in a pressurized decloaking chamber to prevent evaporation. Following antigen retrieval, the tissue is stained using standard immunohistochemical or immunofluorescent protocols to detect the retrieved protein or antigen.

The concentration of the aldehyde scavenging agent in the solution is typically between 0.1% and 10% (w/v), with 5% (w/v) being particularly effective for ascorbic acid and 2-imidazolidinone. The pH of the solution is maintained between 3 and 7, with optimal performance observed in the range of pH 3–4 for many scavengers. Representative aldehyde-based cross-linking agents include formaldehyde, paraformaldehyde, glutaraldehyde, and acetaldehyde. Suitable aldehyde scavenging agents encompass ascorbic acid, Tris, 2-imidazolidinone, hydroxylamine, semicarbazide, and cysteine.

An optional nonionic surfactant may be included to improve tissue penetration and reduce background staining. Representative nonionic surfactants include Triton X-100, Tween-20, and NP-40, typically used at concentrations of 0.1% to 1%. A stabilizing agent such as sodium azide or glycerol may also be added to prolong reagent shelf life. A preferred formulation comprises 5% ascorbic acid, 0.5% Triton X-100, and 0.02% sodium azide in aqueous solution at pH 3.5.

The invention further provides a kit for retrieving a protein of interest in fixed tissue, comprising a container holding the aldehyde scavenging agent solution and instructions for use. Additionally, a method for identifying antigen retrieval agents is disclosed, involving lyophilizing a formaldehyde-fixed protein, adding it to a test solution containing a candidate agent, heating the mixture, and detecting the protein via mass spectrometry or immunoassay.

Compounds useful for unmasking and detecting proteins include amino ethanol derivatives (e.g., ethanolamine), amino acids and derivatives (e.g., lysine, cysteine), amine and aniline compounds (e.g., aniline, benzylamine), hydrazine and hydrazide derivatives (e.g., adipic acid dihydrazide), urea derivatives (e.g., thiourea), thiol derivatives (e.g., dithiothreitol), hydroxyl compounds (e.g., glycerol), and keto derivatives (e.g., pyruvate).

### Compounds

Representative amino ethanol derivatives include 2-aminoethanol, 3-aminopropanol, and N-(2-hydroxyethyl)ethylenediamine. Amino acids and derivatives encompass lysine, arginine, histidine, cysteine, homocysteine, and N-acetylcysteine. Amine and aniline-containing compounds include methylamine, ethylamine, aniline, p-toluidine, and benzylamine. Hydrazine and hydrazide derivatives include hydrazine hydrate, isonicotinic acid hydrazide, and succinic acid dihydrazide. Urea derivatives include urea, thiourea, and 1,3-dimethylurea. Thiol derivatives include mercaptoethanol, dithiothreitol, and glutathione. Hydroxyl compounds include glycerol, sorbitol, and mannitol. Keto derivatives include pyruvic acid, acetone, and diacetyl.

### EXAMPLE 2

Antigen retrieval was successfully demonstrated in formaldehyde-fixed mouse brain tissue using 5% ascorbic acid at pH 3.5. Sections treated with this formulation showed markedly enhanced immunofluorescent signal for Collagen IV compared to untreated controls, confirming effective reversal of formaldehyde-induced epitope masking.

### EXAMPLE 3

The antigen retrieval method proved cross-compatible with multiple immunohistochemical detection systems, including avidin-biotin complex (ABC), tyramide signal amplification (TSA), and direct fluorophore-conjugated antibodies, indicating broad utility across diverse assay platforms.

### EXAMPLE 4

Visualization of blood vessels in formaldehyde-fixed brain sections was significantly improved following treatment with 5% ascorbic acid. Collagen IV staining revealed intricate vascular networks with high contrast and minimal background, demonstrating practical applicability in neurovascular research.

### EXAMPLE 5

Vascular antigen retrieval was achieved in adult mouse brain tissue fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde. After deparaffinization and rehydration, sections were heated in 5% ascorbic acid (pH 3.5) at 95°C for 45 minutes. Subsequent immunostaining for Claudin-5 and Collagen IV revealed robust signal intensity, confirming unmasking of vascular-associated antigens.

### EXAMPLE 6

Recovery of detectable protein from formaldehyde-fixed tissue was quantified by measuring free amino acid content before and after antigen retrieval. Treatment with ascorbic acid resulted in a 3.2-fold increase in available free amino groups, as assessed by fluorescamine assay, indicating reversal of formaldehyde adducts.

### EXAMPLE 7

Antigen retrieval was successfully applied to adult human brain vasculature preserved in formalin. Sections processed with 5% ascorbic acid showed clear delineation of microvasculature using anti-Collagen IV, whereas conventional citrate retrieval yielded weak or undetectable signal.

### EXAMPLE 8

Fluorescent enhancement was observed after antigen retrieval using a solution of 5% ascorbic acid supplemented with 0.5% Triton X-100. Tissue sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and heated in the retrieval solution at 95°C for 45 minutes. Staining for Collagen IV using a 488 nm excitation wavelength revealed uniform, intense labeling of blood vessels. Control sections heated in water alone exhibited elevated autofluorescence and poor specific signal. Addition of 5% ascorbic acid not only enhanced specific staining but also reduced tissue autofluorescence, resulting in superior signal-to-noise ratios. Unheated controls showed negligible staining, confirming the necessity of both chemical and thermal components for effective antigen retrieval.
