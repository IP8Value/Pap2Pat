# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNICAL FIELD

The present invention relates to quantum information processing. More specifically, the invention pertains to devices, systems, and methods for performing fault-tolerant quantum computation using spin-based qubits implemented in semiconductor quantum dots, with particular emphasis on architectures that mitigate leakage errors—especially charge leakage errors—that arise during quantum operations. The invention further relates to surface code implementations in two-dimensional arrays of quantum dots, wherein data and ancillary qubits are coupled via mediator regions to enable high-fidelity two-qubit gates while preserving robustness against both computational and non-computational errors.

## BACKGROUND

Quantum computers hold the promise of exponential speedups for certain computational problems, including quantum simulation, optimization, and cryptanalysis. Unlike classical bits, which exist in definite states of 0 or 1, quantum bits—or qubits—can exist in superpositions of these states, enabling parallel computation across exponentially large state spaces. This capability is harnessed through quantum algorithms such as Shor’s factoring algorithm and Grover’s search algorithm, which rely on coherent manipulation of quantum states. However, quantum states are fragile and susceptible to environmental noise, leading to decoherence and operational errors. To overcome these limitations, quantum error correction (QEC) codes have been developed to encode logical qubits redundantly across multiple physical qubits, allowing errors to be detected and corrected without collapsing the quantum state.

Among QEC codes, the surface code has emerged as a leading candidate for scalable, fault-tolerant quantum computing due to its high error threshold (approximately 1%), local parity-check operations, and compatibility with two-dimensional hardware layouts. The surface code operates by measuring stabilizer generators—products of Pauli operators over plaquettes of data qubits—to detect bit-flip (X) and phase-flip (Z) errors. These measurements are performed using ancillary qubits that interact with neighboring data qubits via controlled quantum gates, typically controlled-Z (CZ) or controlled-NOT (CNOT) operations.

Qubits can be physically realized in various platforms, including superconducting circuits, trapped ions, and semiconductor spin systems. In semiconductor quantum dots, qubits are often encoded in the spin states of individual electrons confined electrostatically in nanoscale potentials. The spin-up and spin-down states of an electron form a natural two-level system, and quantum operations are performed using magnetic or electric fields. The Pauli operators—X, Y, and Z—provide a complete basis for describing single-qubit operations and measurements. Measurements project the qubit state onto eigenstates of a chosen Pauli operator, but imperfect control or environmental coupling can introduce errors, including bit flips, phase flips, and more insidious leakage errors.

Decoherence—the loss of quantum coherence due to interaction with the environment—is a major obstacle to maintaining long-lived quantum states. Quantum error correction mitigates decoherence by continuously monitoring and correcting errors, but it assumes that all errors remain within the computational subspace spanned by the logical |0⟩ and |1⟩ states. Leakage errors occur when the system transitions into states outside this subspace, such as higher orbital or charge states in quantum dots. Unlike standard errors, leakage errors are not corrected by conventional QEC protocols and can accumulate, eventually corrupting the entire computation.

To address general errors, stabilizer codes—including the surface code—are constructed from commuting sets of multi-qubit Pauli operators called stabilizers. Surface codes arrange data qubits on a two-dimensional lattice, with ancilla qubits placed at the centers of plaquettes to measure X- and Z-type stabilizers. Each plaquette corresponds to a group of four data qubits whose joint parity is checked periodically. The architecture requires nearest-neighbor interactions and local measurements, making it amenable to planar fabrication technologies.

In surface code implementations using quantum dots, two-qubit gates are typically mediated by exchange interactions between adjacent electron spins. However, direct exchange necessitates extremely close spacing (tens of nanometers), which complicates integration of control lines, readout sensors, and charge reservoirs. Moreover, scaling such dense arrays introduces challenges in crosstalk, heat dissipation, and individual qubit addressability. While crossbar and modular architectures have been proposed to alleviate routing constraints, they often sacrifice parallelism or introduce new error sources such as electron shuttling losses.

A critical limitation of existing quantum dot surface code proposals is their vulnerability to leakage errors, particularly charge leakage—where an electron escapes from its designated quantum dot. Such events disrupt the charge configuration essential for spin qubit operation and cannot be corrected by standard surface code decoding. Worse, leaked charges may propagate through the array during gate operations, creating spatially correlated errors that overwhelm the error correction capacity. Thus, there exists a need for a quantum dot architecture that inherently suppresses or rapidly corrects leakage errors without introducing significant overhead in qubit count, circuit depth, or fabrication complexity.

## SUMMARY

The present invention introduces a novel device for quantum information processing that is specifically engineered to mitigate leakage errors in surface code architectures based on semiconductor spin qubits. The device comprises a two-dimensional array of confinement regions configured to host qudits—generalized quantum units that include qubits as a special case—arranged according to a surface code layout. The device includes a first plurality of confinement regions serving as data regions for storing quantum information, and a second plurality of confinement regions functioning as ancillary regions for error detection. Critically, the invention incorporates mediator regions situated between data and ancillary regions to facilitate controlled interactions while increasing physical separation between active qubit sites.

Each confinement region is an electrostatically defined quantum dot capable of confining one or more electrons. Data regions contain single electrons whose spin states encode data qudits (typically qubits), while ancillary regions consist of double quantum dots initialized in a spin-singlet state to serve as ancillary qudits. Mediator regions are elongated quantum dots occupied by an even number of electrons (e.g., two or four), which do not store quantum information but enable tunable exchange coupling between neighboring data and ancilla spins via Ruderman-Kittel-Kasuya-Yosida (RKKY)-like interactions. Charge reservoirs are integrated adjacent to mediator regions to supply or absorb electrons during initialization and reset operations.

The device is robust against leakage errors—particularly charge leakage—through a dual mechanism: (1) rapid relaxation of charge deficits from data/ancilla dots to mediator dots via tunneling, and (2) periodic resetting of mediator dots to the charge reservoirs during idle phases of the error correction cycle. This design ensures that charge leakage from computational qubits is transient and localized, preventing propagation across the array. Spin leakage in ancilla qubits is inherently suppressed by reinitialization in every stabilizer cycle and symmetry properties of the singlet-triplet readout.

Confinement regions are defined by gate electrodes on a semiconductor heterostructure, typically silicon metal-oxide-semiconductor (SiMOS) or silicon/silicon-germanium (Si/SiGe). Data dots and ancilla dots are comparable in size (~30 nm diameter), while mediator dots are elongated (~30 nm × 300 nm) to provide sufficient separation for integrating charge reservoirs and measurement apparatus. Interactions between confinement regions are mediated by tuning the on-site energy of mediator dots relative to data/ancilla dots, thereby controlling the strength of the effective exchange interaction.

The invention further incorporates micromagnets proximate to data dots to generate local magnetic field gradients, enabling fast electric-dipole spin resonance (EDSR) for single-qubit gates and establishing the Zeeman splitting difference (Ω) necessary for implementing controlled-phase gates via dipole-dipole-like interactions in the Ω ≫ J regime. Ancilla dots are not equipped with micromagnets, simplifying fabrication and reducing crosstalk.

A method for controlling charge stability is provided, comprising: attaching confinement regions to charge reservoirs during initialization; tuning relative energy levels to load electrons into desired dots; detaching from reservoirs to isolate the quantum system; applying static and oscillating magnetic fields for spin manipulation; and intermittently reattaching mediator regions to reservoirs during idle periods to reset charge states. This method facilitates coherent spin interactions, implements quantum circuits (including surface code stabilizer checks), and enables high-fidelity measurement of ancillary qudit states via Pauli spin blockade.

The controller apparatus includes a processor, memory, user interfaces, communication modules, and power sources, configured to execute sequences of voltage pulses to gate electrodes and magnetic field generators. A computer-readable medium stores instructions for performing the above methods, including partitioned stabilizer checks, virtual Z rotations, and active reset protocols. The invention encompasses modifications such as alternative mediator occupancies, different semiconductor materials, and hybrid control schemes. Reference numerals in the accompanying figures correspond to specific components as described herein. The scope of the invention is defined by the claims and includes equivalents thereof.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The quantum information processing device of the present invention is designed for fault-tolerant operation using spin qubits in a silicon-based quantum dot array. The core components include data dots, ancilla dots, mediator dots, and charge reservoirs, all arranged in a two-dimensional lattice compatible with surface code error correction. Each data dot confines a single electron, with its spin state (|↑⟩ or |↓⟩) encoding a data qubit. Similarly, each ancilla dot pair forms a double quantum dot initialized in the (0,2) charge configuration, where the ground state is a spin singlet, serving as an ancilla qubit for parity measurements.

Data dots and ancilla dots exhibit similar physical dimensions, typically with diameters of approximately 30 nanometers, defined by overlapping gate electrodes on a SiMOS heterostructure. The electron spin states in these dots benefit from long coherence times, especially when fabricated on isotopically purified ²⁸Si substrates, which minimize magnetic noise from nuclear spins. Single-qubit logic gates are implemented via electron spin resonance (ESR) or electric-dipole spin resonance (EDSR). ESR uses global oscillating magnetic fields, while EDSR exploits local magnetic field gradients—generated by integrated micromagnets near data dots—to drive spin rotations with electric fields, achieving gate speeds exceeding 10 MHz with fidelities up to 99.9%.

Ancilla dots are measured using Pauli spin blockade (PSB), a technique that links spin state to charge configuration. When the two electrons in the ancilla double dot occupy a triplet state, interdot tunneling is blocked, resulting in a measurable charge shift detectable via nearby charge sensors or dispersive gate-based readout. This allows single-shot determination of whether a stabilizer check succeeded (singlet) or failed (triplet). Unlike prior architectures that use one ancilla dot for interaction and another as a passive reference, the present invention treats both ancilla dots symmetrically, enabling parallel interaction with two data qubits and halving the stabilizer cycle time.

Mediator dots are elongated quantum dots (~30 nm × 300 nm) positioned between each data-ancilla pair. They are loaded with an even number of electrons (preferably two) and serve solely to mediate exchange interactions. By tuning the mediator’s on-site energy via gate voltages, the exchange coupling strength J between distant spins can be switched between an “on” state (~1 MHz) and an “off” state (~100 Hz residual), enabling high-fidelity two-qubit gates. The three-dot system (data–mediator–ancilla) supports a total of four electrons, and the exchange interaction arises from virtual electron hopping processes, described by a fourth-order perturbation formula involving tunnel couplings and energy detunings.

The two-dimensional array follows the surface code layout, with data qubits at lattice vertices and ancilla qubits at plaquette centers. Each X-stabilizer plaquette involves four data qubits interacting with one half of a double-dot ancilla, while Z-stabilizers use the other half, enabling simultaneous X and Z checks in alternating cycles. Global ESR pulses apply √Y rotations to all data qubits to switch between X and Z basis measurements, while ancilla qubits remain unaffected due to their singlet symmetry under global spin rotations.

Two-qubit gates are implemented via mediated exchange. In the Ω ≫ J regime (large Zeeman splitting difference between dots), the interaction approximates a dipole-dipole coupling, yielding an S = diag(1,1,1,i) gate after evolution time π/J. Combined with virtual Z rotations, this constructs a CZ gate. In the Ω ≪ J regime, √SWAP gates are generated, which—along with an explicit Z_π rotation—also yield CZ. Virtual Z gates are implemented by phase-shifting subsequent microwave pulses, avoiding physical execution and associated errors. Only one explicit Z gate per CZ is needed, minimizing circuit depth.

Charge reservoirs are ohmically contacted regions adjacent to mediator dots, used for initial electron loading and periodic reset. During device initialization, all confinement regions are attached to reservoirs by lowering tunnel barriers, allowing electrons to populate dots according to electrochemical potentials. Energy levels are then tuned to isolate the system, and magnetic fields are applied for spin manipulation. Crucially, mediator dots are reconnected to reservoirs during idle periods of the stabilizer cycle to reset any charge imbalances caused by leakage, without interrupting active computations elsewhere in the array.

Leakage errors—particularly charge leakage from data/ancilla dots—are mitigated by rapid charge relaxation: if an electron escapes a qubit dot, an electron from a neighboring mediator dot tunnels in within ~10 ns, restoring the charge configuration. The resulting charge deficit in the mediator is later corrected by reservoir reset. This prevents leakage propagation and reduces leakage to effective depolarizing errors within each stabilizer half-plaquette. Spin leakage in ancillas is removed by reinitialization every cycle and does not spread to data qubits.

The device architecture supports CMOS-compatible fabrication, with a multi-layered gate stack defining quantum dots, barriers, and reservoir connections. A unit cell includes data dots, ancilla dots, mediators, and implanted ohmic regions beneath a silicon oxide layer, with conductive gate layers patterned above. Side profiles show vertical confinement via heterostructure band engineering and lateral confinement via gate-induced potentials.

Operation proceeds via a partitioned stabilizer cycle: the surface code is divided into four color classes, each activated sequentially. Within each partition, stabilizer checks are performed in parallel, while mediators in inactive partitions undergo reset. This eliminates spatial and temporal error correlations, ensuring Markovian noise suitable for efficient decoding. Active reset operations—intermittent reconnection of mediators to reservoirs—prevent leakage accumulation without adding latency.

The controller apparatus includes a processor executing pulse sequences, memory storing calibration data, user interfaces for monitoring, communication ports for external coordination, and power management. Magnetic field generators produce static fields for Zeeman splitting and oscillating fields for ESR/EDSR. The method for controlling charge stability involves precise timing of attachment/detachment to reservoirs, energy level tuning, and magnetic field application to implement quantum circuits reliably.

Simulations show surface code thresholds of 0.76–0.86% for gate errors and 0.23–0.27% for charge leakage errors under realistic noise models, demonstrating robustness. Decoherence is suppressed via isotopic purification and dynamical decoupling, with estimated phase-flip probabilities per cycle well below threshold. The architecture thus provides a scalable, leakage-resilient platform for fault-tolerant quantum computing in silicon.
