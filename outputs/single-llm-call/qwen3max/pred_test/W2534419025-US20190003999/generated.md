## BACKGROUND

Single particle cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM) has emerged as a powerful technique for determining the three-dimensional structures of biological macromolecules at near-atomic resolution. Central to this methodology is the preparation of samples on electron microscopy grids that must support the target molecules in a thin layer of vitrified ice while minimizing background noise and non-specific interactions. Traditional sample preparation methods often involve adsorbing proteins onto amorphous carbon films supported on copper grids. However, these conventional substrates suffer from several limitations, including poor electron conductivity, high background scattering, and non-specific binding of biomolecules, which collectively degrade image contrast and complicate data interpretation.

Efforts to improve grid performance have focused on enhancing both mechanical stability and biochemical specificity. Rigid grids with improved thermal and electrical conductivity have been developed to reduce beam-induced motion during imaging. Additionally, on-grid purification strategies using affinity ligands such as nitrilotriacetic acid (NTA) conjugated to lipid monolayers have enabled selective capture of histidine-tagged proteins directly from complex mixtures like cell lysates. Despite these advances, lipid-based affinity grids remain mechanically fragile, prone to denaturation under electron beam exposure, and limited in their capacity to dissipate charge, thereby compromising image quality.

Low-dose imaging techniques are essential in cryo-EM to minimize radiation damage to biological specimens. These techniques rely heavily on high signal-to-noise ratios, which are difficult to achieve when using traditional carbon supports due to their inherent thickness and heterogeneity. Single particle analysis further demands uniform particle distribution and minimal preferred orientation—conditions rarely met with standard grid coatings. Consequently, there exists a critical need for novel grid materials that combine superior physical properties with tailored biochemical functionality.

Lipid monolayer affinity grids represent one approach to addressing these challenges; however, they exhibit significant drawbacks. Their fluid nature leads to structural instability during cryofixation and imaging, and their organic composition contributes to high background noise. Moreover, the hydrophobic core of lipid bilayers can induce conformational changes or denaturation in captured proteins, limiting their utility for high-resolution structural studies.

These limitations motivate the development of new grid technologies that integrate the mechanical robustness and electronic properties of advanced nanomaterials with precise molecular recognition capabilities. In particular, two-dimensional materials such as graphene and its derivatives offer unique advantages, including atomic-scale thickness, exceptional thermal and electrical conductivity, and chemical tunability. By functionalizing such materials with affinity ligands and incorporating effective antifouling strategies, it becomes possible to create next-generation EM grids capable of supporting high-fidelity structural analysis of biomolecules directly from crude biological samples.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides an electron microscopy grid comprising a support film composed of graphene or graphene oxide sheets modified with capture agents and deactivating agents. The capture agents, such as nitrilotriacetic acid (NTA), are covalently attached to the graphenic surface to enable specific, reversible binding of target molecules bearing complementary affinity tags, such as polyhistidine sequences. Concurrently, deactivating agents—including small molecule blockers like 4-aminobenzoic acid (PABA) and proteinaceous antifouling layers such as bovine serum albumin (BSA)—are employed to suppress non-specific adsorption of contaminants present in complex biological matrices. This dual-functionalized architecture enables on-grid purification and structural analysis of target proteins directly from clarified cell lysates, significantly streamlining cryo-EM workflows while enhancing image contrast and resolution.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The invention encompasses novel electron microscopy grids engineered with optimized elasticity and rigidity to withstand the mechanical and thermal stresses associated with cryo-EM sample preparation and imaging. These grids feature ultra-thin support films derived from graphene or graphene oxide (GO), which provide superior electron transparency and charge dissipation compared to conventional amorphous carbon films. The operation of these grids hinges on the synergistic integration of molecular capture functionality and surface passivation chemistry.

Coatings applied to the grid substrate are chemically modified to present capture agents that selectively bind target biomolecules. In a preferred embodiment, the coating consists of GO sheets functionalized with NTA groups via lysine linkers. These NTA moieties serve as chelators for divalent metal ions such as Ni²⁺, which in turn coordinate with polyhistidine tags genetically fused to proteins of interest. This well-established affinity interaction enables highly specific immobilization of His-tagged targets from complex mixtures.

Deactivating agents are incorporated to neutralize reactive functional groups on the GO surface—such as epoxides, aldehydes, and carboxylic acids—that would otherwise mediate non-specific adsorption of cellular components. Examples of deactivating agents include aromatic amines like PABA, which react with electrophilic sites on GO, and inert proteins like BSA, which form a steric barrier against fouling. The use of multiple deactivating agents in sequence—first PABA to covalently cap reactive residues, followed by BSA to provide a dense proteinaceous shield—has been shown to dramatically reduce background binding while preserving specific capture efficiency.

Graphene and GO possess distinct physicochemical properties that make them ideal for EM grid applications. Graphene offers unparalleled electrical conductivity and mechanical strength but is challenging to functionalize uniformly. GO, though less conductive, contains abundant oxygenated functional groups that facilitate covalent modification with capture and deactivating agents. Both materials can be processed into monolayer or few-layer films suitable for high-resolution imaging.

Capture agents beyond NTA may also be employed, including antibodies, streptavidin, or other ligands tailored to specific epitopes or tags. Modification of GO coatings with capture agents typically involves activation of carboxyl groups using reagents like thionyl chloride (SOCl₂), followed by coupling to amine-containing ligands under controlled conditions. For instance, tris(O-t-butyl)-Nα,Nα-dicarboxymethyllysine is reacted with activated GO, then deprotected with trifluoroacetic acid (TFA) to yield GO-NTA.

Deactivating agents function through either covalent conjugation or physical adsorption. PABA reacts with residual epoxides and aldehydes on GO via nucleophilic addition, whereas BSA adsorbs non-covalently to form a hydrophilic, non-adhesive layer. The sequential application of both agents ensures comprehensive surface passivation.

Targets of the disclosure include any biomolecule amenable to affinity tagging, particularly recombinant proteins expressed with His₆, FLAG, or Strep-tags. The grids find primary application in structural biology, enabling single-particle cryo-EM analysis without prior chromatographic purification.

Proteins for capture are prepared by standard recombinant expression in bacterial, insect, or mammalian systems, followed by lysis and clarification to remove cellular debris. Affinity tags, especially polyhistidine sequences, allow direct engagement with NTA-functionalized grids upon addition of Ni²⁺.

Imaging of captured proteins proceeds via negative-stain TEM or cryo-EM. Resolutions achieved using the disclosed grids exceed those attainable with lipid-based or carbon-only supports, with demonstrated reconstructions reaching 8.1 Å for GroEL.

Structural analysis benefits from the minimal background and uniform particle distribution afforded by the antifouling design. Graphene or GO sheets are used either as free-standing films or supported on lacey carbon or Formvar-coated grids. Lipid monolayers may be optionally deposited atop the GO layer to mimic native membrane environments, though this is not required for soluble protein studies.

Single-wall carbon nanotubes are not central to the preferred embodiment but may be incorporated as reinforcing elements in composite grids. GO-modified grids are prepared by Langmuir-Schaefer (L-S) transfer of compressed monolayers at the air-water interface, often using isopropanol (IPA)/water subphases to enhance film uniformity.

Metal ions, particularly Ni²⁺, are essential for activating NTA groups. Incubation with NiSO₄ solutions charges the chelator, enabling His-tag coordination. Non-specific reaction sites are blocked post-functionalization using PABA and BSA, as described.

In practice, clarified lysate is incubated directly with the grid for 2–5 minutes, allowing target capture while contaminants are washed away. Subsequent cryo-fixation by plunge-freezing yields vitrified samples ready for high-resolution imaging.

## EXAMPLES

### Example 1

Graphene oxide (GO) sheets were functionalized with nitrilotriacetic acid (NTA) by first activating carboxylic acid groups on GO using thionyl chloride (SOCl₂) in dimethylformamide (DMF), followed by reaction with tris(O-t-butyl)-Nα,Nα-dicarboxymethyllysine. The resulting intermediate, GO-NTA-(O-t-Bu)₃, was deprotected using trifluoroacetic acid (TFA) to yield GO-NTA. Successful functionalization was confirmed by Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy, which showed loss of t-butyl C–H stretches (~2933 cm⁻¹) after deprotection, and by UV-Vis spectroscopy, which revealed a characteristic absorption peak at ~280 nm consistent with n→π* transitions in oxidized graphenic systems.

### Example 2

GO was synthesized from graphite powder using an improved Hummers’ method involving oxidation with KMnO₄ in concentrated H₂SO₄/H₃PO₄, followed by quenching and purification. The resulting material exhibited a UV-Vis absorption maximum at ~240 nm in solid state, shifting to ~270–280 nm in aqueous dispersion, confirming successful exfoliation and oxidation.

### Example 3

GO-NTA was synthesized by reacting SOCl₂-activated GO with tris(O-t-butyl)-Nα,Nα-dicarboxymethyllysine in acetonitrile at 100°C for 72 hours. After extensive washing, the protected product was treated with TFA/THF (1:3 v/v) at 60°C for 5 hours to remove t-butyl groups, yielding water-dispersible GO-NTA.

### Example 4

GO-NTA sheets were exfoliated by probe sonication (150 W, 45 s on/off cycles) in 5:1 methanol/water, followed by centrifugation at 1200 g to remove aggregates. The supernatant contained predominantly monolayer sheets suitable for Langmuir film formation.

### Example 5

Exfoliated GO-NTA was deposited at the air-water interface of a Kibron μTrough using a syringe pump at 100 μL/min until a surface pressure of 15 mN/m was reached, facilitating monolayer assembly.

### Example 6

Compression of the GO-NTA monolayer yielded a surface pressure-area isotherm indicative of progressive sheet alignment and edge-to-edge packing, with collapse occurring above 15 mN/m. Films transferred at this pressure onto silicon wafers exhibited uniform ~1.3 nm thickness by AFM, confirming monolayer formation.

### Example 7

GO-NTA was partially deactivated by incubating with 4-aminobenzoic acid (PABA) under sonication and rotary mixing, forming PABA-GO-NTA via Schiff base or nucleophilic addition reactions with aldehyde/epoxide groups.

### Example 8

PABA-GO-NTA was further labeled with aminofluorescein under dark conditions to produce F-PABA-GO-NTA, enabling fluorescence-based characterization of film coverage and thickness on EM grids.

### Example 9

Bovine serum albumin (BSA) was adsorbed onto PABA-GO-NTA grids by incubating with 0.1 mg/mL BSA for 5 minutes, followed by water washes. Fluorescence microscopy of F-PABA-GO-NTA grids confirmed uniform coating. GO concentration was determined via UV-Vis at 280 nm using experimentally derived extinction coefficients from standard curves. Grids treated with purified His₆-T7 phage showed Ni²⁺-dependent binding, reversible by imidazole. His₆-T7 lysate was prepared by infecting BL21 cells, followed by centrifugation to obtain supernatant. His₆-GroEL was expressed from ASKA library, induced with IPTG, lysed, and clarified. Cryo-EM grids were prepared by blotting lysate-exposed BSA-PABA-GO-NTA grids and plunge-freezing. Single-particle analysis yielded an 8.1 Å map, revealing additional density in the GroEL pore due to His-tag extensions. Lipid mixtures were not used in final grids, but L-S transfer employed IPA/water subphases. Incubation times were 2 min for lysate binding. Materials included GO, SOCl₂, lysine-NTA ester, PABA, BSA, NiSO₄, and fluorescein. Syntheses of various PEGylated lipids (e.g., NTA-PEG2000-DSPE) were performed but not used in the final optimized protocol; instead, direct GO-NTA functionalization proved superior. Nuclear magnetic resonance confirmed intermediate structures during multi-step organic syntheses of custom NTA-linkers, though the preferred route utilized commercially available tris-t-butyl lysine-NTA.
