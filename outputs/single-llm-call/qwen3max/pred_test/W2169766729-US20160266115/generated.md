# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

The immune system is a complex and highly coordinated network of cells, tissues, and molecular mediators that collectively defend the body against pathogens, malignancies, and other threats to homeostasis. Central to its function is the ability to distinguish between self and non-self, mount targeted responses to foreign or aberrant entities, and subsequently resolve inflammation to restore physiological balance. This dynamic system operates through a series of interdependent cellular interactions, where the relative abundance, activation state, and spatial distribution of immune cell subsets critically influence the nature, magnitude, and duration of an immune response. Traditional approaches to assessing immune status have often focused on isolated cell populations—such as quantifying regulatory T cells (Tregs) as a percentage of CD4+ T cells or lymphocytes—without accounting for systemic variables such as overall leukocyte counts or lymphopenia. Such methods fail to capture the integrated nature of immune regulation, wherein every leukocyte subset, regardless of lineage, can potentially interact with others within a defined volume of blood. Consequently, the absolute frequency of immune cells per unit volume emerges as a more physiologically relevant metric for predicting immune behavior, particularly in clinical contexts such as cancer immunotherapy or critical illness.

The immune system comprises both innate and adaptive components, each populated by distinct yet interconnected cell types. The innate immune system includes granulocytes (neutrophils, eosinophils, basophils), monocytes, macrophages, dendritic cells, and natural killer (NK) cells, which provide rapid, non-specific defense mechanisms. The adaptive immune system, mediated primarily by T and B lymphocytes, offers antigen-specific responses with memory capabilities. Within these broad categories, numerous functional subsets exist—such as CD4+ helper T cells, CD8+ cytotoxic T cells, regulatory T cells (CD4+CD25+CD127lo), CD19+ B cells, and CD14+HLA-DRlo/neg immunosuppressive monocytes—each playing specialized roles in immune surveillance, tolerance, and suppression. Critically, the functional output of the immune system arises not from individual cell types in isolation but from the collective interactions among all circulating leukocytes. Therefore, a comprehensive understanding of immune status requires simultaneous quantification of multiple immune markers in absolute terms (e.g., cells per microliter of blood), enabling the reconstruction of the complete immune landscape within an individual. This systems-level perspective forms the foundation of the present invention, which leverages quantitative flow cytometry and bioinformatic clustering to define reproducible immune profiles that correlate with disease states, therapeutic response, and clinical outcomes.

## SUMMARY

The present invention introduces a novel methodology for characterizing human immune system profiles based on the absolute quantification of multiple leukocyte subsets in peripheral blood. Immune system profiles, as defined herein, represent stable, reproducible patterns of immune cell composition that reflect the systemic immune status of an individual. These profiles are derived by performing multiparameter flow cytometry on whole blood samples to determine the absolute counts (cells/μl) of key immune cell populations—including granulocytes, lymphocytes, monocytes, CD3+ T cells, CD4+ T cells, CD8+ T cells, CD19+ B cells, NK cells, regulatory T cells, and CD14+HLA-DRlo/neg monocytes—and comparing these values to a reference database of healthy volunteers. The method involves generating a comprehensive database of immune phenotypes from both healthy individuals and patients with diverse pathologies, including glioblastoma, renal cell carcinoma, non-Hodgkin lymphoma, ovarian cancer, and acute lung injury. This database serves as the foundation for similarity analysis, wherein unsupervised hierarchical clustering algorithms group individuals with comparable immune phenotypes into discrete immune profiles.

The invention further provides a method for determining an individual’s immune system profile by normalizing their absolute cell counts relative to the mean values observed in healthy controls, followed by comparison to the established database using bioinformatic tools such as principal component analysis and Euclidean distance-based clustering. Once classified, the immune profile can be used to assess the individual’s immune status, predict likelihood of response to immune-modulating therapies, and stratify risk for adverse medical outcomes. Notably, certain immune profiles—particularly those resembling the healthy volunteer phenotype—are associated with significantly improved overall survival across multiple cancer types, independent of specific diagnosis. The invention also enables the identification of pathological subtypes based on immune characteristics rather than tissue origin, facilitating more precise diagnostic classification. For example, the method includes specific therapeutic applications for glioblastoma, renal cell carcinoma, and non-Hodgkin lymphoma, wherein treatment decisions are guided by the patient’s immune profile. In one embodiment, patients exhibiting a low CD4+/CD14+HLA-DRlo/neg monocyte ratio—a biomarker derived from the immune profile—are identified as high-risk and may be prioritized for aggressive or alternative immunotherapeutic regimens. Additionally, the invention provides a platform for accelerating drug development by enabling the testing of immune-modulating agents in cohorts stratified by immune profile rather than disease type, thereby increasing the likelihood of detecting meaningful therapeutic effects.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The methods and materials disclosed herein enable the systematic assessment of human immune system profiles through quantitative immunophenotyping of peripheral blood. The process begins with the collection of whole blood samples from a subject, which are then subjected to direct staining with fluorochrome-conjugated antibodies targeting lineage-defining surface markers without prior density gradient separation or cell enrichment, thereby preserving the native cellular composition. Flow cytometry is performed using calibrated instruments, and absolute cell counts per microliter are determined using standardized reagents such as BD TruCount™ tubes for lymphocyte subsets, while monocyte and granulocyte counts are derived from forward and side scatter characteristics combined with subset percentages. The immune markers analyzed include, but are not limited to, total granulocytes, lymphocytes, monocytes, CD3+ T cells, CD19+ B cells, (CD56+CD16+) NK cells, CD4+ T cells, regulatory T cells (CD4+CD25+CD127lo), CD14+HLA-DR+ monocytes, and CD14+HLA-DRlo/neg immunosuppressive monocytes. These data are compiled into a centralized database containing immune phenotypes from healthy volunteers and patients with various diseases.

The database is normalized by dividing each individual’s absolute cell count for a given marker by the mean value of that marker in the healthy volunteer cohort, yielding a dimensionless ratio that reflects deviation from health. Clustering algorithms—specifically unsupervised agglomerative hierarchical clustering with Euclidean average linkage—are applied to these normalized ratios to identify groups of individuals with similar immune phenotypes, termed immune profiles. Each immune profile is defined by a minimum of seven members and minimal dendrogram branch separation, ensuring statistical robustness. A healthy immune system profile is established based on the aggregate data from 40 healthy volunteers, with specified reference ranges for each cell type: for example, lymphocytes range from 1,000–4,000 cells/μl, CD4+ T cells from 400–1,500 cells/μl, and CD14+HLA-DRlo/neg monocytes typically below 50 cells/μl. Immune System Profile 1 is characterized by near-normal lymphocyte counts but elevated CD14+HLA-DRlo/neg monocytes (50–150 cells/μl). Profile 2 exhibits reduced total monocytes (<200 cells/μl) and very low CD14+HLA-DRlo/neg monocytes (<20 cells/μl). Profile 3 shows marked leukocytosis, with granulocytes >8,000 cells/μl, lymphocytes >4,000 cells/μl, and CD14+HLA-DRlo/neg monocytes >200 cells/μl. Profile 4 features granulocytosis (>7,000 cells/μl) coupled with lymphopenia (<800 cells/μl) and high CD14+HLA-DRlo/neg monocytes (>150 cells/μl). Profile 5 is defined by profound leukopenia, with total leukocytes <3,000 cells/μl, lymphocytes <500 cells/μl, and monocytes <100 cells/μl.

An individual’s immune profile is determined by calculating the ratio of their absolute cell counts to the healthy control means and applying the same clustering algorithm used to define the reference profiles. Alternative methods for cell quantification, including PCR-based enumeration or microarray technologies, may be employed, though flow cytometry remains the preferred modality due to its multiparametric capacity and direct cell counting capability. These immune profiles are strongly associated with clinical outcomes: Profiles 1 and 2 correlate with favorable prognosis, while Profiles 3, 4, and 5 are linked to poor survival and resistance to therapy. In therapeutic applications, glioblastoma patients classified into Profile 4 may receive checkpoint inhibitors combined with myeloid-targeting agents, whereas those in Profile 2 may benefit from vaccine-based approaches. Similarly, renal cell carcinoma patients in Profile 3 may be treated with IL-2 or adoptive T cell therapy, while non-Hodgkin lymphoma patients in Profile 5 may require hematopoietic support prior to immunotherapy. The invention encompasses all such treatment strategies guided by immune profile classification. Examples demonstrate the method’s utility across diverse diseases, confirming the uniqueness of each profile and its prognostic power. The scope of the invention includes all embodiments wherein immune profiling informs diagnosis, prognosis, or treatment selection, and concludes with the recognition that this approach represents a paradigm shift in personalized immunology.

### EXAMPLES

The invention was validated through the analysis of peripheral blood samples from 40 healthy volunteers and patients diagnosed with glioblastoma (GBM), non-Hodgkin lymphoma (NHL), renal cell carcinoma (RCC), ovarian cancer (OVA), and acute lung injury (ALI). Samples were collected under institutional review board approval, with cancer patients being either newly diagnosed or recently relapsed and off chemotherapy for at least eight weeks; ALI patients met American-European Consensus Conference criteria. Whole blood was stained directly with antibody panels targeting key immune markers, including CD3, CD4, CD8, CD19, CD56, CD16, CD25, CD127, CD14, HLA-DR, and CD86. BD TruCount™ tubes enabled absolute quantification of T, B, and NK cells, while other subsets were calculated from percentages multiplied by total lineage counts. Data acquisition was performed on a BD FACSCalibur™ flow cytometer, and analysis utilized CellQuest, Multiset, and FlowJo software with standardized gating strategies.

Multiparameter analysis involved converting all values to cells/μl, normalizing to healthy volunteer means, log-transforming, and applying hierarchical clustering in Partek Genomics Suite. Principal component analysis confirmed segregation by immune profile rather than disease alone. In GBM, three profiles emerged: Profile 1 (mixed healthy and untreated patients), Profile 2 (mostly healthy), and Profile 3 (dexamethasone-treated patients). NHL and RCC similarly showed disease-specific clustering, while OVA patients distributed across profiles. ALI patients segregated into three profiles, with Profile 2 exhibiting 71% mortality versus 19% in others. Cross-disease analysis revealed five major immune profiles shared across pathologies. Profile 1 resembled health but with elevated CD14+HLA-DRlo/neg monocytes; Profile 2 had low monocytes; Profile 3 showed leukocytosis and immunosuppressive markers; Profile 4 featured granulocytosis with lymphopenia; and Profile 5 displayed profound leukopenia. Survival analysis demonstrated that patients in Profiles 1 and 2 had median overall survival of 915 days versus 379 days in other profiles (p=0.009). Correlation studies revealed that CD14+HLA-DRlo/neg monocytes inversely correlated with CD4+ T cells (Spearman r = -0.3244, p<0.001), leading to the development of the CD4+/CD14+HLA-DRlo/neg ratio as a prognostic biomarker. Using a cut-point of 2.0, patients with a high ratio had 30-month median survival versus 9 months for low ratio (p=0.006). Hierarchical clustering also identified novel relationships, such as granulocytes co-clustering with CD14+HLA-DRlo/neg monocytes and myeloid-derived suppressor cells clustering independently. These findings confirm that immune profiles transcend individual diseases, provide actionable clinical insights, and enable the discovery of new immunological principles. The technical approach—combining whole blood flow cytometry, absolute quantification, and bioinformatic clustering—offers a scalable platform for immune monitoring, drug development, and precision medicine.
