## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates generally to autonomous sensor devices for measuring physical stresses experienced by aquatic organisms during passage through hydraulic structures, and more particularly to an improved sensor fish device configured to simulate the size, shape, density, and buoyancy of juvenile migratory fish while collecting high-resolution data on acceleration, rotation, pressure, temperature, and orientation during turbine or spillway passage. Fish passing through hydropower turbines or other hydraulic infrastructure may suffer injury or mortality due to exposure to extreme hydraulic conditions, including rapid pressure changes, high shear stress, turbulence, mechanical strike from rotating components such as runner blades, and cavitation effects. These injury mechanisms are especially relevant in large river systems such as the Columbia and Snake Rivers, where aging turbine infrastructure is being rehabilitated or replaced with designs intended to improve fish survival rates while maintaining power generation efficiency. To guide these engineering efforts, it is essential to characterize the precise hydraulic and mechanical stresses that act upon fish during downstream passage through complex flow environments.

Traditional methods for evaluating turbine biological performance rely on field studies using live fish, which provide valuable data on injury and mortality outcomes but offer limited insight into the specific physical conditions that cause those biological responses. Live fish studies cannot identify the spatial locations within a turbine where deleterious stresses occur, nor can they quantify the magnitude or duration of those stresses with sufficient precision to inform targeted design modifications. To address this gap, earlier generations of autonomous sensor devices—collectively referred to as “Sensor Fish”—were developed to mimic the physical characteristics of juvenile salmonids and record in situ measurements of key environmental parameters during turbine passage. However, these prior art devices suffered from several limitations that restricted their broader applicability. For example, the Gen 1 Sensor Fish was specifically engineered for large Kaplan turbines in the Columbia River basin and lacked the pressure range, robustness, and data acquisition capabilities needed for use in high-head dams equipped with Francis turbines or in pump-storage facilities, which often subject passing objects to higher pressures, greater mechanical impacts, and more complex flow regimes.

Prior art sensor devices also exhibited constraints in terms of deployment logistics, recovery reliability, battery life, data storage capacity, and cost-effectiveness. Their relatively large size and non-neutral buoyancy limited their ability to accurately replicate the trajectory and behavior of real fish, while their limited sensor suite and sampling rates hindered comprehensive characterization of transient hydraulic events. Moreover, the recovery mechanisms employed in earlier designs were often unreliable under turbulent or deep-water conditions, leading to loss of valuable data and hardware. These shortcomings motivated the development of a next-generation sensor platform capable of operating across a wider range of hydraulic environments, with enhanced durability, higher-fidelity sensing, improved recovery functionality, and reduced manufacturing cost. The resulting Gen 2 Sensor Fish addresses these needs through a compact, neutrally buoyant form factor; a multi-sensor array sampling at 2048 Hz; a robust pressure sensor rated for up to 12 bars absolute; and a dual-stage recovery system incorporating both positive buoyancy activation and active location signaling via RF beacon and high-intensity LEDs. This new device enables more accurate correlation between physical stressors and biological outcomes, thereby supporting the design of ecologically sustainable hydropower infrastructure worldwide.

## SUMMARY

The present disclosure introduces the Gen 2 Sensor Fish, an advanced autonomous sensor platform engineered to replicate the physical properties of a yearling salmon smolt while measuring critical hydraulic and mechanical parameters during passage through hydroelectric turbines, spillways, and other water conveyance structures. The Gen 2 Sensor Fish features a cylindrical housing 24.5 mm in diameter and 89.9 mm in length, weighing approximately 42.1 grams, and is neutrally buoyant in fresh water at deployment. It incorporates a suite of high-performance sensors including a primary three-axis analog accelerometer (±200 g full-scale), a digital three-axis gyroscope (±2000°/s), a pressure sensor (0–12 bar absolute), a primary temperature sensor (−40°C to +125°C), and an eCompass module integrating a secondary 3-axis accelerometer and magnetometer for orientation tracking. Data are acquired at a sampling frequency of 2048 Hz and stored on internal non-volatile flash memory for up to five minutes of continuous recording. Power is supplied by a rechargeable battery managed by a low-quiescent-current LDO regulator and controlled by a 16-bit PIC microcontroller operating from an internal low-frequency oscillator to minimize energy consumption.

Operation begins when a user activates the device by holding a magnet near an embedded magnetic switch, triggering a self-check sequence that verifies battery voltage and memory availability. Upon successful validation, the device enters a configurable delay period before commencing data acquisition. Following completion of data collection, a second programmable interval elapses before the recovery mechanism is activated. This mechanism employs nichrome wires that, when briefly energized by the microcontroller, sever fishing lines tethering internal spring-loaded weights, thereby rendering the device positively buoyant. Once at the surface, the Gen 2 Sensor Fish periodically activates four high-intensity orange LEDs and a 146 MHz RF beacon to facilitate visual and radio-based recovery. After retrieval, the device is docked into a specialized station that recharges the battery and downloads stored data via a two-pin serial interface using TTL-to-USB conversion. Accompanying software converts raw binary outputs into calibrated physical units and generates comma-separated value (CSV) files for analysis and visualization. ## DESCRIPTION

The purpose of this patent is to protect the novel design, architecture, and operational methodology of the Gen 2 Sensor Fish, an autonomous, fish-mimicking sensor platform for characterizing physical stresses in hydraulic environments. The scope of the invention encompasses the integrated hardware configuration, sensor arrangement, power management system, data acquisition protocol, recovery mechanism, and associated firmware that collectively enable high-fidelity, in situ measurement of acceleration, rotation, pressure, temperature, and orientation during passage through hydroelectric infrastructure. Embodiments of the invention include the specific mechanical layout, electronic components, material selections, and control logic described herein, as well as functionally equivalent alternatives that achieve the same technical result.

The sensor fish housing is constructed from corrosion-resistant materials suitable for prolonged freshwater immersion and is hermetically sealed to protect internal electronics. Its external dimensions—24.5 mm in diameter and 89.9 mm in length—are carefully chosen to match the morphometric profile of a yearling salmon smolt, ensuring hydrodynamic similarity during passage. The total mass of approximately 42.1 grams yields neutral buoyancy in fresh water at typical river temperatures, allowing the device to follow trajectories comparable to those of live fish. Internally, components are arranged symmetrically about the geometric center to align the center of gravity with the center of mass, minimizing rotational artifacts during free motion.

At the core of the system is a low-power 16-bit PIC microcontroller that orchestrates all operations, including sensor polling, data logging, recovery actuation, and status indication. This microcontroller operates primarily from an internal low-frequency oscillator to conserve energy, though it may utilize an external crystal for timing-critical functions when necessary. Power is drawn from a rechargeable lithium-polymer battery regulated by a low-dropout (LDO) linear regulator with ultra-low quiescent current, ensuring extended standby life. A bi-color LED provides visual feedback: yellow indicates activation or data transfer, green signifies full charge or successful acquisition, and red denotes fault conditions.

Data acquisition is performed by a coordinated array of sensors mounted on flexible and rigid printed circuit boards. The primary accelerometer, an Analog Devices ADXL377, offers a full-scale range of ±200 g per axis with bandwidths up to 1300 Hz (x/y) and 1000 Hz (z), and is positioned precisely at the device’s center of mass on the flex circuit board to maximize measurement fidelity during impulsive events. Oversampling and anti-aliasing filters are implemented in firmware to enhance signal integrity. Complementing this is a secondary 3-axis digital accelerometer within the LSM303DLHC eCompass module, which, while limited to ±16 g, improves orientation estimation during lower-stress phases of passage.

The ITG-3200 gyroscope provides 3-axis rotational velocity measurements with a full-scale range of ±2000°/s and an initial zero-rate output tolerance of ±40°/s. Its linear acceleration sensitivity of 0.1°/s/g ensures minimal cross-axis interference. The gyroscope supports user-selectable low-pass filtering and communicates via a digital I²C interface. Similarly, the eCompass module integrates a 3-axis magnetometer with selectable ranges up to ±8.1 gauss, enabling heading determination with ±4° accuracy after calibration. Both the gyroscope and eCompass include on-board temperature sensors that supplement the primary TC1046 temperature sensor—a linear analog device with 6.25 mV/°C slope—mounted adjacent to the pressure sensor and thermally coupled via heat-conductive epoxy to ensure rapid thermal equilibration with ambient water.

The MS5412-BM pressure sensor, mounted on the flex board, delivers a 0–12 bar absolute measurement range with a 30-bar overpressure rating. Its Wheatstone bridge output is amplified by an instrumentation amplifier with gain 13.0 and offset 40 mV to match the analog input range of the microcontroller’s 12-bit successive approximation ADC. All analog sensors share a common, filtered supply voltage and ground plane to minimize noise coupling.

The recovery mechanism comprises two nichrome wire actuators—one at each end—each connected to a transistor-switched capacitor bank that delivers a high-current pulse to rapidly heat the wire and sever a restraining fishing line. Bleeder resistors safely discharge the capacitors post-actuation. Upon release of the first weight, the device becomes positively buoyant and ascends. Firmware then activates four outward-facing, high-intensity orange LEDs and a 146 MHz RF beacon in a periodic duty cycle to aid recovery; alternative LED colors may be used for specific visibility requirements.

Activation occurs via a magnetic reed switch triggered by an external magnet. Post-recovery, the device docks into a station that interfaces with the download board via two pins, enabling simultaneous battery recharging and high-speed (921.6 kbps) serial data transfer through a TTL-to-USB converter. The accompanying Sensor Fish Communicator software handles command parsing, data extraction, unit conversion using pre-stored calibration coefficients, and CSV export. Firmware implements a state machine governing activation, delay, acquisition, resurface timing, recovery actuation, and beacon operation, ensuring reliable autonomous function in demanding field conditions.
