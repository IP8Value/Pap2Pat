# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

### Technical Field

The present invention pertains to the field of metallurgy and, more specifically, to the production of low-carbon steel materials exhibiting enhanced surface hardness without requiring post-solidification processing such as alloying, rolling, heat treatment, or surface modification. The invention further relates to methods for controlling the microstructure of low-carbon steels during solidification through precise manipulation of free oxygen content and cooling rate to achieve a gradient in mechanical properties, particularly ultra-high surface hardness combined with a tough, ductile interior core. The resulting steel materials are suitable for applications demanding wear resistance at the surface while maintaining impact toughness and formability in the bulk, such as in automotive components, structural elements, tools, and machinery parts.

### Description of the Related Art

Low-carbon steels, typically defined as steels containing less than 0.25 weight percent carbon, are widely employed in industrial applications due to their excellent ductility, weldability, and toughness. These attributes stem from their predominantly ferritic microstructure, which facilitates plastic deformation under stress. However, a significant limitation of conventional low-carbon steels is their relatively low strength and hardness, especially at the surface, which restricts their use in applications where resistance to abrasion, indentation, or fatigue is critical. To overcome this deficiency, numerous surface hardening techniques have been developed and implemented in industry. These include carburizing, nitriding, induction hardening, and laser surface melting—processes that either introduce interstitial elements (e.g., carbon or nitrogen) into the surface layer or induce rapid phase transformations through localized heating and quenching. While effective, these methods are inherently multi-step, energy-intensive, and costly, often requiring specialized equipment, extended processing times, and careful control of atmosphere or electromagnetic parameters.

Conventional approaches to enhancing the mechanical properties of low-carbon steels have largely focused on post-solidification interventions. For instance, thermomechanical processing such as controlled rolling followed by accelerated cooling can refine grain size and promote the formation of bainite or acicular ferrite, thereby improving strength. Alternatively, microalloying with elements like niobium, vanadium, or titanium can inhibit grain growth and provide precipitation strengthening. However, these strategies still necessitate additional manufacturing steps beyond casting and do not intrinsically generate a hardened surface layer during solidification. Moreover, even with such enhancements, achieving martensitic-level surface hardness in low-carbon steels without subsequent quenching or carburization has remained elusive. There exists a long-felt but unmet need in the art for a method of producing low-carbon steel that inherently possesses an ultra-hard surface region directly from the casting process, eliminating the need for secondary treatments while preserving the desirable bulk properties of low-carbon steel. The present invention addresses this need by leveraging controlled solidification dynamics and oxide inclusion engineering to create a functionally graded microstructure with exceptional surface hardness.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides novel low-carbon steel materials and methods for their production that yield an ultra-hard surface layer without requiring post-casting heat treatment, alloying, or surface modification. In one embodiment, the invention involves the controlled introduction of free oxygen into molten low-carbon steel, followed by rapid solidification against high-conductivity substrates, to generate a microstructure comprising lath martensite, upper bainite, and acicular ferrite in the surface region. This microstructural gradient results in a surface Vickers hardness exceeding 400 HV—comparable to that of high-carbon martensite—while maintaining a ductile, tough interior typical of conventional low-carbon steels. A key aspect of the invention is the deliberate manipulation of free oxygen content (typically between 35 and 50 ppm) in conjunction with sub-rapid cooling rates (ranging from approximately 400 to 1000 K/s during solidification) to promote the formation of fine, Ti-containing oxide inclusions that act as potent nucleation sites for acicular ferrite and influence the partitioning of carbon during phase transformation. The technique, referred to herein as single-step Direct-Cast Hardening (DiCH), enables the direct production of steel sheets or components with a hardened surface layer formed inherently during casting. The resulting steel materials exhibit a unique combination of ultra-high surface hardness, high yield strength in the surface microconstituents (up to 2378 MPa in lath martensite regions), and retained bulk toughness, making them highly suitable for structural and wear-resistant applications without additional processing.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention introduces improved low-carbon steel materials and associated production methods that achieve unprecedented surface hardness through intrinsic solidification control rather than post-processing. Central to this innovation is the strategic management of oxide inclusions within the steel matrix, which serve as critical microstructural modifiers during phase transformation. Specifically, the invention exploits the relationship between free oxygen content in the molten steel and the cooling rate during solidification to tailor the size, distribution, and composition of oxide inclusions, thereby directing the formation of desired ferritic and martensitic phases near the surface. Free oxygen, introduced via controlled addition of FeO or other oxygen-bearing agents, reacts with deoxidizing elements such as titanium to form fine oxide particles. When the free oxygen content is maintained within a specific range—preferably between 35 and 50 parts per million (ppm)—and the molten steel is rapidly cooled against high-thermal-conductivity surfaces (e.g., copper plates), these oxides precipitate as submicron-scale inclusions that are uniformly dispersed in the near-surface region.

The benefits of fine oxide inclusions are manifold. First, they act as heterogeneous nucleation sites for acicular ferrite (AF), a microstructure characterized by randomly oriented, high-aspect-ratio ferrite needles with high-angle grain boundaries. Acicular ferrite is known to enhance toughness by impeding crack propagation. Second, the presence of Ti-containing oxides—such as TiO₂, Ti₂O₃, or complex oxides like MnTiO₃ or SiTiO₃—further promotes AF nucleation due to favorable lattice matching with ferrite. The critical size for effective heterogeneous nuclei of acicular ferrite is typically below 1 micrometer; inclusions larger than this tend to favor polygonal ferrite (PF) or quasi-polygonal ferrite (Q-PF), which offer lower strength and hardness. In the context of this invention, abbreviations are defined as follows: AF denotes acicular ferrite, PF denotes polygonal ferrite, Q-PF denotes quasi-polygonal ferrite, UB denotes upper bainite, and M denotes lath martensite. The role of fine inclusions in promoting AF formation is pivotal, as AF not only improves toughness but also refines the overall microstructure by subdividing prior austenite grains.

Acicular ferrite exhibits superior mechanical properties compared to polygonal ferrite. While PF forms at austenite grain boundaries and grows with low misorientation angles, resulting in coarse, equiaxed grains with limited strengthening effect, AF nucleates intragranularly on inclusions and develops a chaotic, interlocking network of fine needles. This morphology significantly increases dislocation density and grain boundary area, thereby enhancing both strength and fracture resistance. The variation in oxide inclusion composition—dictated by the concentrations of Ti, Mn, Si, and O in the melt—further modulates nucleation efficiency. Ti-containing inclusions, in particular, demonstrate a strong potency for AF nucleation due to their crystallographic compatibility with ferrite. The invention establishes that a critical inclusion size threshold exists—approximately 0.5 to 1.0 micrometers—below which inclusions effectively catalyze AF formation; above this size, nucleation favors softer PF variants.

The production technique according to the invention, termed single-step Direct-Cast Hardening (DiCH), involves melting a low-carbon steel precursor (containing ≤0.03 wt% C, along with controlled amounts of Mn, Si, and Ti), adjusting free oxygen content via FeO addition, and then rapidly solidifying the melt between chilled copper plates. This method eliminates all post-solidification hardening steps. The benefits of precisely controlling free oxygen content and cooling rate are profound: they jointly determine the density and fineness of oxide inclusions, which in turn govern the phase distribution in the solidified structure. The resulting ultra-hard, low-carbon steel exhibits a pronounced property gradient: surface hardness reaches 420 HV or higher, while the interior retains the ductility of conventional low-carbon steel. This gradient arises because surface regions experience the highest cooling rates (900–1000 K/s in the first 50 µm), promoting martensite and fine bainite, whereas deeper regions (50–200 µm) cool more slowly (400–900 K/s), favoring acicular ferrite and coarser bainite.

In practicing the invention, a precursor material is heated and melted in an induction furnace to approximately 1600°C. FeO is added to achieve a target free oxygen content of 35–50 ppm. Subsequently, Fe-Ti, Fe-Mn, and Fe-Si are introduced to adjust titanium (0.01–0.02 wt%), manganese, and silicon levels, respectively. The molten steel is then contacted with two parallel copper plates separated by a narrow gap (e.g., 2.5 mm), causing instantaneous solidification into a flat sheet. Heat extraction occurs primarily through the copper plates, establishing a steep thermal gradient. Solidification completes within milliseconds, followed by brief air cooling (e.g., 30 seconds) and optional water quenching to suppress undesirable phase transformations. The resulting steel structure features an ultra-hard surface region (0–200 µm depth) composed of lath martensite, upper bainite, and acicular ferrite, with nanohardness values up to 9.87 GPa in martensitic zones.

Alternatively, the technique may involve direct addition of FeO and other alloying components to the molten steel to simultaneously control oxygen potential and chemical composition. This approach ensures in-situ formation of oxide inclusions during solidification, leading to a steel structure with a self-generated ultra-hard surface region. No external carburizing, nitriding, or laser treatment is required. The method is scalable, cost-effective, and compatible with continuous casting processes.

### EXAMPLES

To demonstrate the efficacy of the invention, ultra-hard low-carbon steel samples were produced using the single-step DiCH method and compared to ordinary low-carbon steel of similar bulk composition but lacking controlled oxygen and rapid solidification. Four experimental samples (S1–S4) were prepared with varying initial free oxygen contents: S1 and S2 at ~35 ppm, S3 and S4 at ~50 ppm. All samples contained 0.023 wt% C, 0.8 wt% Mn, 0.2 wt% Si, and 0.015 wt% Ti. Two key parameters were controlled: free oxygen content and cooling rate during solidification. The DiCH method was applied uniformly: molten steel was cast between copper plates, held for 0.24 s, air-cooled for 30 s, and water-quenched. This yielded property-gradient steel with refined microstructures in the surface layer.

Microstructural analysis revealed significant refinement in all DiCH samples compared to conventionally cast steel. Oxide inclusions were observed exclusively in the DiCH samples, with densities increasing with free oxygen content. High cooling rates (simulated at 900–1000 K/s near the surface) promoted the formation of submicron oxide inclusions (<1 µm), which acted as nucleation sites for acicular ferrite. Ultra-high hardness (up to 420 HV) was achieved in S4, correlating with high inclusion density and fine martensitic laths.

Material preparation involved melting TG30 steel, adjusting chemistry, and casting into 2.5-mm-thick sheets. Four samples (S1–S4) were cut for analysis. Cooling rates were calculated using ProCAST simulation software, yielding average rates of ~950 K/s (0–50 µm) and ~650 K/s (50–200 µm). Stress tests included Vickers hardness measurements across the cross-section and nanoindentation on individual microconstituents.

The single-step DiCH method consistently produced hardened surface layers. Particle density variation (PDV)—defined as the standard deviation of inclusion counts across multiple fields of view—was used to assess inclusion uniformity. S1 (low O₂, moderate cooling) showed low particle density and high PDV, indicating sparse, clustered inclusions. S3 (high O₂, same cooling) exhibited higher density but still elevated PDV. In contrast, S2 and S4—subjected to optimized cooling—displayed high particle density and low PDV, signifying uniform dispersion of fine oxides. SEM imaging confirmed that S2 and S4 contained homogeneous distributions of Ti-rich oxide inclusions, whereas S1 and S3 showed agglomeration.

Cross-sectional microstructural examination revealed distinct zones: 0–50 µm depth dominated by lath martensite and B1 upper bainite (lath aspect ratio >30); 50–200 µm depth featuring acicular ferrite and B2 upper bainite (aspect ratio <10). EDS analysis confirmed Ti, Mn, and O in inclusions, with S4 showing complex (Mn,Ti,Si)-oxides. TEM and selected-area diffraction (SADP) verified the body-centered tetragonal (bct) structure of martensite in S4, consistent with carbon-enriched regions (estimated 0.14 wt% C locally).

Inclusion size distribution in S4 peaked at 200–400 nm, with >80% below 1 µm—ideal for AF nucleation. S4 exhibited nearly double the inclusion density of S3, directly linking oxygen control and cooling to inclusion refinement. Vickers hardness profiles showed surface values of 420 HV for S4 versus 150 HV in the core. Nanoindentation confirmed lath martensite hardness at 9.87 GPa, upper bainite at 4.18 GPa, and acicular ferrite at 2.93 GPa. These differences underscore the microstructural origin of surface hardening and validate the invention’s ability to generate martensite-level hardness in ultra-low-carbon steel without carburization.
