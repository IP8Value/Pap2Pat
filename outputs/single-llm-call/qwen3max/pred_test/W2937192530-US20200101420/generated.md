# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates generally to the field of solar-powered water purification and desalination technologies. More specifically, the invention pertains to an apparatus and method for solar membrane distillation that utilizes a photothermal, superhydrophobic graphitic foam membrane integrated with micro/nanostructured carbon particles and hydrophobic surface chemistry to achieve high-efficiency, low-cost desalination of saline or contaminated water using solar energy as the sole thermal input. The invention is particularly suited for off-grid, remote, or disaster-relief applications where access to conventional energy sources and infrastructure is limited or unavailable, and where reliable production of potable water from seawater, brackish water, or wastewater is critical.

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

Access to clean drinking water remains a global challenge, especially in arid regions and developing communities. Solar desalination has emerged as a sustainable and environmentally benign approach to address this issue by harnessing abundant solar energy to evaporate and subsequently condense pure water from saline sources. However, conventional direct solar evaporation systems—where sunlight directly heats a bulk volume of water—suffer from low thermal efficiency due to significant heat loss to the surrounding environment and the large thermal mass of the water body. These limitations result in slow evaporation rates and poor scalability.

To overcome these inefficiencies, researchers have turned to interfacial solar evaporation using porous carbon nanomaterials, which localize heat at the water–air interface, thereby minimizing bulk heating and maximizing evaporation efficiency. Nanostructured carbon materials, such as graphene, carbon nanotubes, and carbon black, exhibit excellent broadband light absorption and high photothermal conversion efficiency. When engineered into porous architectures, they also provide capillary pathways for water transport and large surface areas for vapor generation. Despite these advantages, standalone photothermal evaporators often face challenges related to salt accumulation, material degradation, and insufficient vapor management, which can lead to performance decay over time.

Membrane distillation (MD) offers a complementary solution by separating water vapor from liquid feed through a hydrophobic microporous membrane, driven by a vapor pressure gradient. In MD, only vapor molecules pass through the membrane pores, while non-volatile contaminants—including salts, heavy metals, and pathogens—are retained. This process inherently produces high-purity permeate and is less sensitive to feed concentration than reverse osmosis. However, conventional MD systems require external thermal energy to maintain the necessary temperature differential across the membrane, rendering them energy-intensive and unsuitable for decentralized applications.

Although solar-driven MD has been explored, existing systems often suffer from inadequate solar absorption, poor thermal localization, membrane wetting due to insufficient hydrophobicity, and inefficient condensation. Moreover, many designs lack integrated pre-heating mechanisms or fail to optimize the geometric configuration of the distillation chamber for maximum solar utilization. These limitations hinder the practical deployment of solar MD at scale. There is therefore a pressing need for an integrated, self-sustaining solar membrane distillation system that combines high photothermal efficiency, robust superhydrophobicity, effective vapor transport, and intelligent thermal management—all within a simple, durable, and manufacturable architecture.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a solar membrane distillation apparatus designed to efficiently produce fresh water from saline or contaminated sources using only solar energy. The apparatus comprises a solar distillation membrane fabricated from a graphitic foam substrate functionalized with a hydrophobic composition to create a superhydrophobic surface. This membrane serves as both a photothermal absorber and a vapor-selective barrier. The apparatus includes a water chamber configured to hold the feed water in contact with one side of the distillation membrane, and a vapor chamber adjacent to the opposite side of the membrane, wherein water vapor generated by solar heating permeates through the membrane into the vapor chamber. A condenser is operatively connected to the vapor chamber to cool and collect the purified water vapor as liquid permeate.

In a preferred embodiment, the distillation membrane exhibits a superhydrophobic surface with a water contact angle exceeding 150 degrees, achieved through the covalent attachment of fluorosilane molecules to the graphitic foam structure. The membrane may be configured in various geometries, including planar, tubular, or vessel-shaped forms, to optimize solar exposure and vapor flow dynamics. A pre-heating chamber is optionally integrated upstream of the water chamber to elevate the feed water temperature using waste heat recovered from the condenser or auxiliary solar heating, thereby enhancing the overall thermal efficiency of the system. The graphitic foam is further enhanced with embedded micro- and/or nanoparticles of carbonaceous materials, which increase light absorption across the solar spectrum and create hierarchical nanotextures that reinforce superhydrophobicity. The hydrophobic composition, typically comprising (heptadecafluoro-1,1,2,2-tetrahydrodecyl) trichlorosilane or similar compounds, is applied via solution immersion to ensure uniform coverage of the internal pore surfaces, preventing membrane wetting during prolonged operation.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

The membrane distillation apparatus of the present invention is constructed around a core component: the solar distillation membrane. This membrane is formed from a graphitic foam (GF) baseline support, which is a rigid, highly porous carbon structure derived from heat-treated mesophase pitch. The GF exhibits excellent thermal stability, mechanical robustness, and intrinsic photothermal properties. The foam is machined into desired shapes—such as tubes, flat panels, or open vessels—using precision tools to form the structural backbone of the distillation unit.

The graphitic foam possesses a three-dimensional interconnected pore network with controlled bulk porosity ranging from 70% to 95% and average pore sizes between 100 and 500 micrometers. Surface porosity is further refined by applying an intermediate porous layer composed of a slurry of graphite particles and phenolic resin at a 2:1 weight ratio. This slurry is coated onto the GF surface using a manual applicator and then thermally cured at 300°C for one hour, creating a micro-roughened skin that enhances capillary action and provides anchoring sites for subsequent hydrophobic functionalization.

Following this, the membrane is rendered superhydrophobic by immersing it in a 0.1 wt% solution of (heptadecafluoro-1,1,2,2-tetrahydrodecyl) trichlorosilane in hexane for 12 hours. The silane molecules covalently bond to hydroxyl groups on the carbon surface, forming a dense, low-surface-energy monolayer. After immersion, the membrane is oven-dried at 100°C for one hour and rinsed thoroughly with ethanol and water to remove physisorbed species. The resulting surface achieves a water contact angle greater than 150°, confirming superhydrophobicity, as verified by optical tensiometry.

The apparatus housing is constructed from transparent or translucent materials—such as borosilicate glass or polycarbonate—to allow solar irradiation to penetrate and strike the distillation membrane. The water chamber is sealed against the membrane’s feed side and contains the saline or contaminated water. The vapor chamber, located on the permeate side, is maintained at a lower temperature and pressure to sustain a vapor pressure gradient. A condenser, typically a cooled metal coil or finned heat exchanger, is positioned within or adjacent to the vapor chamber to convert vapor into liquid permeate, which is then collected via a treated water exhaust conduit.

In operation, solar radiation (simulated or natural, at AM 1.5G intensity of 100 mW/cm²) is absorbed by the graphitic foam, which rapidly converts light to heat due to its high absorption efficiency (A > 95% across 330–2500 nm). The localized heating at the membrane–water interface drives evaporation, and vapor molecules diffuse through the hydrophobic pores without wetting. Air initially present in the vapor chamber is evacuated either passively by vapor displacement or actively via a small vacuum assist; in some embodiments, vapor flow itself drives air evacuation through venturi effects.

A pre-heating chamber may be incorporated, wherein feed water passes through a metal pipe or conduit wrapped around the condenser, recovering latent heat from the condensing vapor. Alternatively, a dedicated solar water heater—comprising a dark-colored absorber plate and insulated enclosure—can pre-heat the feed to 40–60°C before entry into the main water chamber, significantly boosting flux.

Micro/nano carbon particles (e.g., graphite nanoplatelets with surface area 225–275 m²/g) are optionally embedded in the surface layer at concentrations of 5–10 wt%, with particle sizes ranging from 50 nm to 10 µm. These particles enhance light trapping through multiple scattering and increase surface roughness at the nanoscale, synergistically improving both photothermal performance and hydrophobic stability.

Performance testing under solar simulation demonstrates permeate fluxes exceeding 1.2 kg/m²·h under 1-sun illumination, with salt rejection >99.9% and stable operation over 100+ hours without fouling or wetting.
