# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates to methods for identifying patients diagnosed with schizophrenia who are at elevated risk of experiencing a relapse of psychotic symptoms. More specifically, the invention provides a clinically validated approach that combines targeted assessment of a defined subset of symptom domains—derived from the Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale (PANSS)—with longitudinal monitoring to enable timely intervention. The invention further encompasses systems and methods for remote, frequent, and patient-centered symptom tracking using digital health technologies, thereby facilitating early detection of relapse precursors that may not be captured during standard infrequent clinical visits. This approach is particularly suited for integration into telehealth platforms, mobile applications, and wearable-based monitoring systems designed to support ongoing management of chronic psychiatric conditions.

## BACKGROUND

Schizophrenia is a severe and chronic psychiatric disorder characterized by a constellation of positive symptoms (e.g., delusions, hallucinations, disorganized thinking), negative symptoms (e.g., blunted affect, social withdrawal, avolition), and cognitive impairments. The disease course is typically marked by recurrent episodes of acute exacerbation, or relapse, which can lead to progressive functional decline, treatment resistance, increased risk of suicide, and substantial socioeconomic burden. Despite advances in antipsychotic pharmacotherapy, relapse remains a common occurrence, with studies indicating that up to 80% of patients experience at least one relapse within the first two years following initial stabilization. Effective relapse prevention is therefore a cornerstone of long-term management strategies.

The symptoms of schizophrenia are heterogeneous and dynamic, often fluctuating in severity over time. Early warning signs of relapse may include both psychotic manifestations—such as emerging delusions or auditory hallucinations—and non-psychotic indicators like sleep disturbances, heightened anxiety, irritability, or social withdrawal. However, these prodromal changes are frequently subtle, transient, and subjectively experienced, making them difficult to detect without systematic and frequent monitoring. Moreover, cognitive deficits inherent to the disorder can impair patients’ insight into their own symptomatology, further complicating early recognition.

Current clinical practice for managing schizophrenia relies heavily on periodic face-to-face assessments, typically occurring monthly or even less frequently in resource-limited settings. During these brief encounters, clinicians often rely on global impressions or abbreviated rating scales rather than comprehensive symptom inventories due to time constraints. As a result, critical changes in specific symptom dimensions may go unnoticed until a full-blown relapse necessitates hospitalization or emergency intervention. This reactive model fails to capitalize on the window of opportunity for preemptive adjustment of treatment, such as medication titration or psychosocial support, which could mitigate or prevent relapse altogether.

There is a pressing need for a more proactive, data-driven approach to relapse prediction that leverages frequent, structured, and patient-reported symptom assessments. While comprehensive instruments like the PANSS provide detailed quantification of symptom severity across 30 domains, their administration is time-intensive and impractical for routine or high-frequency use. Furthermore, not all PANSS items demonstrate equal sensitivity to impending relapse. Thus, identifying a minimal yet highly predictive subset of symptoms that reliably signal deterioration would significantly enhance the feasibility and efficacy of early intervention protocols.

Recent technological advancements offer promising avenues for continuous mental health monitoring through smartphones, tablets, and wearable devices. These tools can facilitate regular self-reporting and passive data collection (e.g., sleep patterns, mobility, voice characteristics). However, to maximize patient compliance and minimize cognitive burden—particularly in a population prone to fatigue and attentional deficits—it is essential to focus data collection on the most clinically relevant indicators. Without empirical guidance on which symptoms are most predictive of relapse, digital monitoring systems risk collecting excessive or irrelevant data, leading to poor adherence and reduced clinical utility. The present invention addresses this gap by identifying a specific set of PANSS-derived symptoms whose escalation reliably precedes relapse, thereby enabling efficient, targeted, and actionable remote monitoring.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The invention provides a method for identifying a patient diagnosed with schizophrenia who is at increased risk of experiencing a relapse of psychotic symptoms. The method comprises administering a pharmaceutical agent to the patient to maintain symptom stability, and subsequently monitoring the severity of a targeted set of psychiatric symptoms through interview-based or self-reported observations. The targeted symptom set includes delusions (P1), conceptual disorganization (P2), hallucinations (P3), excitement (P4), suspiciousness (P6), anxiety (G2), and tension (G4), as defined by corresponding items of the Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale (PANSS). In certain embodiments, the method further includes monitoring hostility (P7) and unusual thought content (G9).

The method further involves measuring the levels of one or more biomarkers in a biological sample obtained from the patient, wherein the biomarkers are selected from the group consisting of inflammatory markers, neurotrophic factors, and metabolomic profiles associated with psychotic exacerbation. However, in preferred embodiments, the primary predictive signal is derived from the specified symptom domains rather than biomarkers, given the current limitations in validated peripheral biomarkers for schizophrenia relapse.

Upon detecting a clinically significant increase in the severity of one or more of the specified symptoms—particularly when such increases occur over a period of approximately 7 to 10 days prior to anticipated relapse—the method triggers an intervention. This intervention may include adjusting the dose of the administered pharmaceutical agent, initiating adjunctive therapy, or prompting a clinical evaluation. The pharmaceutical agent is typically an antipsychotic medication, such as paliperidone or its extended-release or long-acting injectable formulations, but may also include other second-generation antipsychotics.

The goal of the invention is to decrease the likelihood of full relapse by enabling timely, pre-emptive clinical actions based on early and specific symptom changes. By focusing on a parsimonious set of seven PANSS items that have been empirically shown to exhibit the greatest magnitude of increase immediately prior to relapse, the method achieves high predictive specificity while minimizing patient burden. This approach is especially well-suited for implementation in digital health platforms that support remote, frequent, and automated symptom tracking, thereby overcoming the limitations of infrequent clinic-based assessments.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

The method of the invention is intended for use in a patient population diagnosed with schizophrenia according to established diagnostic criteria, such as those in the DSM-IV-TR or DSM-5, and who are in a clinically stable phase of the illness. Stability is typically defined as maintenance of low symptom severity for at least eight weeks, as evidenced by a PANSS total score below a predefined threshold and absence of recent hospitalization or aggressive behavior. Patients may be receiving maintenance therapy with oral or long-acting injectable antipsychotics, including but not limited to paliperidone, risperidone, or aripiprazole.

Symptom severity is measured using a structured assessment protocol that evaluates the seven key PANSS items: P1 (Delusions), P2 (Conceptual disorganization), P3 (Hallucinations), P4 (Excitement), P6 (Suspiciousness), G2 (Anxiety), and G4 (Tension). In some embodiments, additional items such as P7 (Hostility) and G9 (Unusual thought content) may be included to enhance sensitivity. Assessment may be conducted via clinician-administered interviews, caregiver reports, or, preferably, patient self-report through electronic questionnaires delivered via mobile devices.

The PANSS assessment is a standardized clinical tool widely used in schizophrenia research and practice. It consists of 30 items rated on a 7-point scale (1 = absent to 7 = extreme), grouped into three subscales: Positive (7 items), Negative (7 items), and General Psychopathology (16 items). While the full PANSS is comprehensive, its length limits frequent use. The present invention circumvents this limitation by utilizing only the subset of items most predictive of relapse, as identified through rigorous statistical analysis of longitudinal clinical trial data.

Biomarker identification is contemplated as a supplementary component, though not essential to the core method. Potential biomarkers include serum levels of C-reactive protein, interleukin-6, brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF), or specific metabolites identified through mass spectrometry. However, the primary innovation lies in the symptom-based predictive model, which does not require invasive sampling.

Administration of the pharmaceutical agent follows standard clinical guidelines, with dosage adjustments made based on tolerability and response. The invention does not prescribe a specific drug but rather provides a monitoring framework applicable across antipsychotic regimens.

Remote detection of symptom severity is a key feature of the invention. Patients are prompted at regular intervals—preferably daily or every other day—to complete a brief electronic questionnaire assessing the seven target symptoms. Responses are transmitted securely to a central server or healthcare provider dashboard. The system compares current scores to baseline and prior assessments, flagging significant increases (e.g., a rise of ≥1 point on any of the seven items over a 7-day window). Automated alerts notify clinicians when relapse risk exceeds a predefined threshold, enabling rapid intervention.

### PANSS

The Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale (PANSS) is a validated 30-item rating scale used to assess symptom severity in individuals with schizophrenia. Each item is scored from 1 (absent) to 7 (extreme) based on a semi-structured clinical interview and collateral information. The PANSS comprises three subscales: the Positive Scale (items P1–P7), which measures psychotic symptoms such as delusions and hallucinations; the Negative Scale (items N1–N7), which assesses deficits in emotional expression and motivation; and the General Psychopathology Scale (items G1–G16), which captures a broad range of non-specific symptoms including anxiety, tension, and cognitive disturbances.

The PANSS is administered by trained clinicians and typically takes 30 to 45 minutes to complete. While it is the gold standard in clinical trials, its length and complexity hinder routine use in outpatient settings. Despite its comprehensiveness, not all PANSS items are equally informative for predicting relapse. Empirical evidence indicates that relapse is primarily heralded by exacerbations in specific positive and general psychopathology symptoms, rather than negative or cognitive domains.

The predictive value of the PANSS for relapse has been limited by its infrequent administration in real-world practice. However, when analyzed longitudinally in controlled trials, certain items consistently show marked increases in the days preceding relapse. This observation forms the basis for the present invention’s focused approach.

To address the limitations of the full PANSS, the invention introduces a refined subset of items: P1 (Delusions), P2 (Conceptual disorganization), P3 (Hallucinations), P4 (Excitement), P6 (Suspiciousness), G2 (Anxiety), and G4 (Tension). In alternative embodiments, the subset may include P7 (Hostility) and G9 (Unusual thought content), yielding a nine-item panel. This subset retains high predictive power while drastically reducing assessment time and cognitive load.

The advantages of using this subset are manifold. It enables frequent monitoring without overwhelming the patient, aligns with the natural trajectory of relapse (which predominantly involves positive and affective symptoms), and facilitates integration into digital health tools. Remote detection is achieved through electronic devices such as smartphones or tablets, which deliver interactive questionnaires modeled on the PANSS item definitions. For example, a patient might be asked: “Over the past 24 hours, have you had strong beliefs that others are plotting against you?” (corresponding to P6).

Completed assessments are transmitted in real time to a secure cloud-based platform. The system automatically compares current scores to historical baselines and calculates trends. If the severity of one or more target symptoms increases beyond a clinically significant threshold—such as a sustained 1-point rise over 7 days—an alert is generated and sent to the patient’s care team. This allows for timely clinical review and potential intervention before full relapse occurs.

The increased frequency of assessments afforded by remote monitoring is a critical advantage. Traditional clinic visits occur too infrequently to capture the rapid symptom escalation observed in the 7–10 days before relapse. Daily or bi-daily self-reports, by contrast, provide a high-resolution temporal map of symptom dynamics, enabling earlier and more precise detection of deterioration. This paradigm shift—from episodic to continuous monitoring—represents a significant advancement in the proactive management of schizophrenia.

## EXAMPLES

### PANSS: Methods

Data were pooled from three randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled clinical trials (NCT00086320, NCT00111189, and NCT01529515) evaluating the efficacy of paliperidone formulations in preventing relapse in adults with schizophrenia. All studies followed a similar design: after a screening phase, eligible patients underwent an open-label stabilization phase on active treatment, followed by randomization to either continued active treatment or placebo during a double-blind phase. PANSS assessments were conducted at least every four weeks, with more frequent evaluations in the initial phase of one study. The pooled dataset included 907 patients, of whom 267 experienced relapse during the double-blind phase. Study designs differed slightly in drug formulation (oral extended-release vs. long-acting injectables) and duration, but all used consistent relapse criteria and PANSS administration protocols. Previous findings confirmed that all paliperidone formulations significantly delayed time to relapse compared to placebo.

### PANSS: Definition of Relapse

The primary efficacy variable was time to first relapse during the double-blind phase. Relapse was defined by any of the following: (1) psychiatric hospitalization due to symptom decompensation; (2) clinically significant suicidal/homicidal ideation or aggressive behavior; (3) a ≥25% increase in PANSS total score (for baseline >40) or ≥10-point increase (for baseline ≤40) on two consecutive assessments within 7 days; or (4) a significant increase in any of six pre-specified PANSS items (P1, P2, P3, P6, P7, G8) to predefined thresholds. Patients meeting these criteria were classified as relapsed, regardless of investigator judgment. Ten patients initially misclassified as relapsed were excluded, and 14 correctly meeting criteria but not labeled as relapsed were reclassified, ensuring analytical consistency.

### PANSS: Statistical Analysis

Individual PANSS items were ranked by mean change from randomization to relapse among the 267 relapsed patients. Linear and non-linear mixed-effects models were applied to estimate symptom trajectories relative to relapse time. Observations were realigned by days before relapse. The non-linear model used an exponential function: Y = a^(T−b) + c + δ_i + ε_ij, where T is days relative to relapse, b represents the time (in days) before relapse at which a 1-point increase from baseline occurs, and δ_i is a subject-level random effect. Model selection favored the exponential form for interpretability. Parameters were estimated using SAS 9.4, with subject-level random effects accounting for intra-individual correlation.

### PANSS: Demographics and Characteristics of Patients Experienced a Relapse During Double-Blind Phase

Among 907 randomized patients, 267 (29.4%) relapsed. Relapsed patients had a higher mean PANSS total score at double-blind baseline (54.9 vs. 52.5, p=0.002) but were otherwise comparable in age, sex, race, BMI, and age at diagnosis to non-relapsed patients. No significant demographic differences suggested that relapse was driven by baseline heterogeneity, supporting the generalizability of symptom trajectory findings.

### Example 5

Analysis revealed that seven PANSS items exhibited mean increases >1 point at relapse: P1 (1.53), P6 (1.49), P3 (1.44), G2 (1.32), P4 (1.29), G4 (1.24), and P2 (1.12). These items showed consistent elevation across relapse subtypes (hospitalization, PANSS-defined, total score-defined) and treatment groups (placebo vs. paliperidone). Trajectory modeling indicated that these symptoms began rising 7–10 days before relapse, with a 1-point increase occurring 0.33 to 1.17 days prior. For instance, delusions (P1) showed a b-parameter of −1.17, meaning a 1-point rise occurred 1.17 days before relapse. Similar patterns were seen for suspiciousness (−0.82) and hallucinations (−0.74). In contrast, items like motor retardation (G7) showed no meaningful pre-relapse increase (b = 3.23). These findings confirm that relapse is preceded by a narrow, predictable window of symptom escalation in specific domains. The results support the use of this seven-item subset for early detection, as changes are both large in magnitude and temporally proximate to relapse. Remote monitoring at weekly or higher frequency would capture this critical period, enabling timely intervention to avert full relapse.
