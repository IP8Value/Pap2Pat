# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

The present invention relates generally to the field of quantum computing, and more specifically to systems, methods, and computer program products for estimating quantum resources required for financial derivative pricing. Quantum computing holds significant promise for accelerating computationally intensive tasks across various domains, including finance. One particularly compelling application is in the pricing of financial derivatives—contracts whose value is derived from underlying assets such as stocks, currencies, or commodities. Classical approaches to derivative pricing, especially for path-dependent instruments like autocallable options and target accrual redemption forwards (TARFs), often rely on Monte Carlo simulations that scale poorly with required accuracy, typically converging at a rate of O(1/√M) where M represents the number of simulated paths. Quantum algorithms based on amplitude estimation offer a theoretical quadratic speedup, achieving convergence at O(1/M). However, realizing this advantage in practice demands careful analysis of the full computational pipeline, including state preparation, payoff evaluation, and error management under fault-tolerant constraints.

Despite the theoretical promise, prior art has largely overlooked the resource overhead associated with loading stochastic processes into quantum states—a critical prerequisite for quantum derivative pricing. Existing methods such as the Grover-Rudolph algorithm, while efficient in complexity-theoretic terms, require classical integration steps that negate any quantum advantage when applied to derivative pricing. Other approximate techniques fail to address normalization issues that lead to exponentially growing errors in multi-step stochastic models. Consequently, there remains a pressing need for practical, end-to-end methodologies that not only leverage quantum speedups but also provide realistic estimates of the logical qubit count, T-depth, and clock speed necessary to outperform classical counterparts. The present invention addresses these gaps by introducing novel re-parameterization techniques, optimized path-loading strategies, and comprehensive error analyses tailored to real-world financial instruments, thereby establishing concrete thresholds for quantum advantage in derivative pricing.

## SUMMARY

The present invention introduces multiple embodiments designed to estimate and optimize the quantum resources required for pricing financial derivatives using fault-tolerant quantum computers. These embodiments collectively enable accurate, efficient, and scalable quantum derivative pricing by addressing key challenges in state preparation, arithmetic implementation, and error control.

In one embodiment, the invention provides a system comprising a quantum resource estimation system configured to analyze and predict the computational requirements for executing quantum derivative pricing algorithms. This system includes specialized components for re-parameterizing stochastic models, estimating transition probabilities, and evaluating complex payoff structures under geometric Brownian motion assumptions.

In another embodiment, the invention discloses a computer-implemented method for estimating quantum resources needed to price path-dependent derivatives. The method involves discretizing asset price paths, transforming between price and return spaces to simplify probability loading, applying variational techniques to prepare Gaussian states, and performing rigorous error analysis to bound truncation, discretization, and amplitude estimation errors.

A further embodiment encompasses a computer program product embodied on a non-transitory computer-readable medium, containing executable instructions that, when executed by a processor, cause the performance of operations including loading multivariate stochastic processes into quantum superpositions, computing discounted payoffs in quantum parallel, and applying iterative amplitude estimation to extract expected values with provable accuracy guarantees.

Collectively, these embodiments summarize a framework for quantum resource estimation that accounts for all stages of the derivative pricing pipeline—from initial path distribution loading through final payoff evaluation—while providing concrete metrics such as T-count, T-depth, logical qubit requirements, and clock speed thresholds necessary to achieve quantum advantage over classical Monte Carlo methods.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention introduces a quantum resource estimation system designed to evaluate the feasibility and efficiency of quantum algorithms for pricing financial derivatives. Central to this system is the recognition that derivative contracts derive their value from underlying assets, which are modeled as stochastic processes evolving over time. A derivative asset, in this context, refers to any financial instrument whose payoff depends on the trajectory of one or more underlying assets, such as stocks, bonds, or commodities. The purpose of derivative pricing is to compute the fair value of entering into such a contract today, accounting for future uncertainty in asset prices and the resulting payoff structure.

To model asset price dynamics, the invention employs the geometric Brownian motion framework, a standard assumption in quantitative finance that captures the log-normal behavior of asset returns. Under this model, the vector of asset prices S_t ∈ ℝ^d_+ at time t evolves according to transition probabilities defined by a multivariate log-normal distribution. Equivalently, by working in return space—where log-returns R_t = ln(S_t / S_{t−1}) follow a multivariate normal distribution—the invention simplifies the representation of path probabilities, enabling independent sampling across time steps and assets after appropriate affine transformations.

The payoff of a derivative, denoted f(ω), quantifies the financial gain to the contract holder along a given path ω = (S_0, ..., S_T). For discounted payoffs, this value is adjusted by the risk-free rate r to reflect the opportunity cost of capital, yielding an expectation E[e^{−rT}f(ω)] that constitutes the derivative’s fair price. Pricing thus reduces to computing this expectation under the assumed stochastic model.

Re-parameterization component 108 applies Cholesky decomposition to the covariance matrix Σ, enabling affine transformations R_t = μ_t + L R̃_t that map standard normals to correlated asset returns.

Estimation component 110 implements iterative quantum amplitude estimation (IQAE), which avoids costly quantum Fourier transforms while maintaining O(1/ε) convergence. Variational component 202 trains Ry-CNOT ansätze to prepare standard Gaussian states using energy-based cost functions derived from the quantum harmonic oscillator Hamiltonian, followed by L∞ refinement. Error analysis component 302 computes bounds on truncation (via Chernoff tail inequalities), discretization (via multidimensional midpoint rule), and arithmetic errors (from fixed-point operations).

Discrete-time multivariate stochastic processes model d assets over T timesteps. Auto-callable options and TARFs serve as representative path-dependent derivatives. Resource estimates quantify T-count, T-depth, and qubit requirements. The re-parameterization method shifts computation to return space, avoiding exponential normalization factors inherent in Riemann summation. Hybridization of pre-trained variational circuits enables portable, problem-independent Gaussian loaders.

Fault-tolerant quantum computing requires sufficient logical qubits and low T-depth to meet runtime constraints. Logical clock speeds must support billions of operations within seconds. Correlation between assets is encoded in Σ, with ρ_ij ∈ [−1,1]. Path probabilities derive from joint Gaussian densities. The risk-free rate r adjusts for time value of money.

Classical pricing relies on Monte Carlo; quantum alternatives use amplitude estimation for quadratic speedup. Grover-Rudolph loading is impractical due to embedded classical integration. The core algorithm loads paths, computes payoffs in superposition, encodes expectations in ancilla amplitudes, and extracts values via IQAE.

Return space offers advantages: independent Gaussians, simplified loading, and compatibility with variational methods. Normalized payoffs f̃(ω) = (f(ω) − f_min)/(f_max − f_min) fit within [0,1] for amplitude encoding. Algorithm 2.1 outlines four phases: distribution loading, payoff calculation, amplitude marking, and estimation.

Path loading uses Grover-Rudolph (infeasible), qGANs (unpredictable overhead), or Riemann summation (error-prone). Importance sampling mitigates normalization issues by rescaling with auxiliary distributions h(x). Truncation restricts domains to [B_l, B_u], discarding negligible tails. Discretization error scales inversely with grid resolution.

Arithmetic errors arise from fixed-point approximations. Exponential, square root, and arcsine computations use piecewise polynomial evaluations. Comparator circuits implement logical conditions for barrier and cap features in TARFs. Controlled-Ry rotations encode payoffs into amplitudes.

Resource estimates for basket autocallables (d=3, T=20) and TARFs (d=1, T=26) show re-parameterization reduces T-depth by ~3× versus Riemann summation. Variational training achieves L∞ errors <10⁻⁶ with shallow circuits. Digitization of rotation angles introduces O(1/M_digit) errors, manageable with fine grids.
