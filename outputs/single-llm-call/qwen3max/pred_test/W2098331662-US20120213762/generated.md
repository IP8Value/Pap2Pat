# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

Lysosomal enzymes are a class of hydrolytic proteins essential for the degradation of macromolecules within the lysosome, an acidic intracellular organelle responsible for cellular waste processing and recycling. These enzymes are synthesized in the endoplasmic reticulum as precursor polypeptides bearing N-linked oligosaccharides. During transit through the Golgi apparatus, a subset of these oligosaccharides is modified by the addition of mannose 6-phosphate (M6P) residues, a post-translational modification that serves as a molecular signal for lysosomal targeting. The M6P-tagged enzymes are recognized by cation-dependent and cation-independent M6P receptors (CD-MPR and CI-MPR, respectively), which mediate their transport from the trans-Golgi network to endosomes and ultimately to lysosomes. This canonical M6P-dependent pathway ensures efficient delivery of newly synthesized lysosomal enzymes to their functional destination.

Defects in lysosomal enzyme activity—whether due to genetic mutations that impair enzyme synthesis, stability, or catalytic function—result in a group of inherited metabolic disorders collectively known as lysosomal storage diseases (LSDs). In LSDs, undegraded substrates accumulate within lysosomes, leading to progressive cellular dysfunction, tissue damage, and multi-organ pathology. Pompe disease, for example, is caused by deficiency of acid alpha-glucosidase (GAA), resulting in pathological glycogen accumulation primarily in cardiac and skeletal muscle. Enzyme replacement therapy (ERT) has emerged as a primary therapeutic strategy for several LSDs, including Pompe, Gaucher, and Fabry diseases. In ERT, recombinant forms of the deficient enzyme are administered intravenously with the goal of being taken up by target cells via M6P receptor-mediated endocytosis and delivered to lysosomes to restore catabolic function. However, the efficacy of conventional ERT is often limited by suboptimal receptor binding affinity, inefficient cellular uptake, variable glycosylation patterns affecting M6P content, and poor biodistribution to critical tissues such as skeletal muscle.

To overcome these limitations, Glycosylation Independent Lysosomal Targeting (GILT) technology was developed as an alternative lysosomal delivery platform. GILT leverages high-affinity peptide ligands that bind directly to the CI-MPR independent of M6P modifications. Specifically, the GILT tag is derived from insulin-like growth factor II (IGF-II), a natural ligand of the CI-MPR that binds with nanomolar affinity to domains 10–13 of the receptor. By fusing a truncated, bioactive portion of IGF-II to a lysosomal enzyme, the resulting chimeric protein can be efficiently internalized via CI-MPR-mediated endocytosis without reliance on glycan-based M6P signals. This approach decouples lysosomal targeting from the complexities of glycosylation, offering greater consistency in manufacturing and potentially enhanced tissue delivery, particularly to cell types with low M6P receptor expression or impaired M6P biosynthesis pathways.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides improved compositions and methods for the targeted delivery of lysosomal enzymes to lysosomes via the cation-independent mannose 6-phosphate receptor (CI-MPR), utilizing engineered furin-resistant IGF-II muteins as lysosomal targeting moieties. These IGF-II muteins exhibit enhanced stability, reduced off-target binding to the insulin receptor and IGF-I receptor, and retained high-affinity binding to the CI-MPR, thereby enabling more efficient and specific lysosomal enzyme delivery.

In particular, the invention features furin-resistant lysosomal targeting peptides derived from IGF-II, wherein specific amino acid substitutions abolish recognition and cleavage by the proprotein convertase furin, a ubiquitous protease that can degrade targeting tags during biosynthesis or circulation. Unexpectedly, certain mutations introduced to confer furin resistance also resulted in a significant reduction in binding affinity for the insulin receptor, minimizing potential hypoglycemic or mitogenic side effects associated with IGF-II signaling. Similarly, additional substitutions were identified that diminish binding to the IGF-I receptor, further improving the safety profile of the targeting moiety.

A key embodiment of the invention is a furin-resistant IGF-II mutein that retains high-affinity binding to the CI-MPR while exhibiting reduced affinity for both the insulin receptor and the IGF-I receptor. This mutein comprises specific amino acid substitutions at or near the native furin cleavage site (typically R-X-K/R-R motifs), such as replacement of arginine or lysine residues with alanine, serine, or other non-basic amino acids. In some embodiments, the mutein includes deletions or replacements of amino acid residues in the C-terminal region of IGF-II, which is dispensable for CI-MPR binding but contributes to receptor cross-reactivity.

The invention further encompasses targeted therapeutic fusion proteins comprising a lysosomal enzyme—such as human acid alpha-glucosidase (GAA)—fused to a furin-resistant IGF-II mutein via a flexible peptide spacer. The spacer enhances proper folding, stability, and accessibility of both the enzyme and the targeting domain. Suitable lysosomal enzymes include those deficient in Pompe, Fabry, Gaucher, or other LSDs, with GAA being a preferred example for the treatment of Pompe disease.

Nucleic acids encoding the IGF-II mutein, the fusion protein, or components thereof are also provided, enabling recombinant expression in mammalian, insect, yeast, or other host cells. Pharmaceutical compositions comprising the fusion protein in a therapeutically effective amount, optionally formulated with excipients suitable for intravenous administration, are disclosed for use in treating lysosomal storage diseases. Methods of treatment involve administering the fusion protein to a patient in need thereof, resulting in enhanced lysosomal delivery, substrate clearance, and clinical improvement compared to conventional enzyme replacement therapies.

“Human acid alpha-glucosidase (GAA)” is a lysosomal enzyme that hydrolyzes glycogen to glucose; its deficiency causes Pompe disease.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

### Introduction to GILT Technology for Lysosomal Targeting

The invention builds upon Glycosylation Independent Lysosomal Targeting (GILT) technology, which utilizes high-affinity ligands of the CI-MPR to direct therapeutic proteins to lysosomes without dependence on M6P modifications. The CI-MPR, also known as the IGF-II/M6P receptor, contains multiple binding domains: domains 1–3 and 7–9 bind M6P, while domains 10–13 specifically bind IGF-II. By fusing a bioactive fragment of IGF-II to a lysosomal enzyme, the resulting fusion protein engages the CI-MPR via the IGF-II binding site, triggering endocytosis and lysosomal delivery.

### Lysosomal Enzymes

Lysosomal enzymes are hydrolytic proteins that function optimally at acidic pH and are essential for degrading carbohydrates, lipids, proteins, and nucleic acids. Suitable enzymes for use in the invention include human GAA, alpha-galactosidase A (for Fabry disease), glucocerebrosidase (for Gaucher disease), and others. The enzyme may share at least 85%, 90%, 95%, or 99% sequence identity with the native human enzyme, as determined by global alignment algorithms such as BLAST or ClustalW over the full-length sequence. Pompe disease, caused by GAA deficiency, serves as a primary exemplar due to the critical need for improved muscle-targeted ERT.

### Pompe Disease

Pompe disease is an autosomal recessive LSD resulting from mutations in the GAA gene. It manifests as glycogen accumulation in lysosomes of cardiac and skeletal muscle, leading to progressive weakness, cardiomyopathy, and respiratory failure. Infantile-onset Pompe presents within months of birth with severe hypertrophic cardiomyopathy and death by age 1–2 years if untreated. Juvenile and adult-onset forms progress more slowly but cause debilitating myopathy. Residual GAA activity correlates inversely with disease severity; patients with <1% activity typically develop infantile disease. Life expectancy is significantly reduced without intervention.

### Enzyme Replacement Therapy

ERT involves intravenous infusion of recombinant lysosomal enzymes, which are taken up by cells via receptor-mediated endocytosis. Conventional ERT for Pompe uses alglucosidase alfa, which relies on M6P for CI-MPR binding. However, M6P content varies with production conditions, limiting batch consistency and tissue delivery efficiency, especially to skeletal muscle.

### Glycosylation Independent Lysosomal Targeting

GILT technology replaces M6P dependence with a covalently attached IGF-II-derived peptide that binds CI-MPR domains 10–13 with high affinity (Kd ~1–20 nM). This enables robust, glycosylation-independent uptake, compatible with diverse expression systems including non-mammalian hosts incapable of M6P synthesis.

### Furin-Resistant GILT Tag

Native IGF-II contains a furin cleavage site (e.g., R⁶⁸VRR⁷¹ in mature human IGF-II). During expression or circulation, furin can cleave the GILT tag, generating heterogeneous products and reducing targeting efficiency. To address this, the invention introduces mutations that abolish furin recognition. For example, R68A, R70A, R71A, or combinations thereof prevent cleavage. Lysine or alanine substitutions are preferred due to their neutral or small side chains. Up to 10% of residues in the IGF-II fragment may be altered without compromising CI-MPR binding.

Binding to CI-MPR is maintained with dissociation constants (Kd) of ≤50 nM, preferably ≤20 nM, as measured by surface plasmon resonance. Mutations are strategically placed outside the CI-MPR binding interface (primarily involving residues in helices A and C of IGF-II) but within regions critical for insulin/IGF-I receptor engagement. Deletion of C-terminal residues (e.g., beyond position 60) or truncation after residue 67 further reduces off-target binding while preserving CI-MPR affinity. Minimal functional regions include IGF-II residues 8–67 fused to the enzyme via a spacer (e.g., GGG or similar).

### Binding Affinity for the Insulin Receptor

Reduced insulin receptor binding is confirmed via competitive binding assays using biotinylated insulin. Mutant IGF-II muteins show IC50 values at least 5-fold higher than wild-type IGF-II, indicating diminished affinity. This minimizes activation of insulin signaling pathways, reducing risks of hypoglycemia or aberrant cell proliferation.

### Mutagenesis

Mutations are introduced via site-directed mutagenesis, PCR-based methods, or synthetic gene construction. Conservative (e.g., Leu→Ile) or non-conservative (e.g., Arg→Ala) substitutions, insertions, or deletions are employed. Scanning mutagenesis identifies residues whose alteration enhances stability or specificity.

### Spacer

A peptide spacer (e.g., 3–20 amino acids, such as GGGGS repeats) links the IGF-II mutein to the lysosomal enzyme, ensuring spatial separation and independent folding. Spacers may include protease-resistant sequences or solubility-enhancing motifs.

### Cells

Expression hosts include mammalian cells (HEK293, CHO, BHK, HeLa, HT1080, NS/0, etc.), insect cells (Sf9, Sf21), yeast (Pichia, Saccharomyces), bacteria (E. coli), and plant cells. CHO and HEK293 are preferred for proper folding and secretion of complex fusion proteins.

## EXAMPLES

### Example 1

ZC-701, a GILT-tagged GAA fusion protein, exhibited heterogeneity due to furin cleavage. N-terminal sequencing revealed cleavage after R71 in the IGF-II tag. SDS-PAGE showed multiple bands corresponding to full-length and cleaved species. Quantification indicated ~30% cleavage, compromising batch uniformity and reducing effective dose.

### Example 2

Mutant ZC-701 constructs (e.g., 1459: R70A; 1460: R71A; 1461: R70A/R71A) demonstrated complete resistance to furin cleavage in vitro and in cell culture. Additional mutants (1726: ΔC-terminal 5 aa; 1749: R68A/R70A/R71A) showed enhanced stability. Deletion mutants (1758: Δ60–67) retained CI-MPR binding but lost insulin receptor reactivity.

### Example 3

Stable CHO lines expressing mutant GILT-GAA produced >200 mg/L protein. Purified enzyme exhibited specific activity of ~140,000 units/mg and K_m of ~6 mM, comparable to rhGAA.

### Example 4

Competitive binding assays showed mutant IGF-II muteins (e.g., ZC-1487) had IC50 >100 nM for insulin receptor vs. ~5 nM for wild-type IGF-II, confirming reduced off-target binding. CI-MPR binding remained potent (IC50 ~15 nM).

### Example 5

Uptake assays in L6 myoblasts demonstrated that furin-resistant GILT-GAA achieved 25-fold higher cellular internalization than rhGAA, with K_uptake of 5 nM vs. 140 nM, and superior glycogen clearance in Pompe mice.
