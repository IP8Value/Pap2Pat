# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNICAL FIELD

The present invention pertains to the technical field of food analysis and authentication, specifically to methods for the detection, identification, and differentiation of meat products based on their volatile organic compound (VOC) profiles. More particularly, the invention relates to a rapid, non-destructive analytical method employing gas chromatography–ion mobility spectrometry (GC–IMS) combined with multivariate statistical analysis to characterize and distinguish donkey meat from different genetic strains. The invention further encompasses applications in food quality control, species and strain authentication, fraud detection, and flavor profiling in the meat industry. This method is especially suited for distinguishing between SanFen (SF) and WuTou (WT) donkey meat based on unique VOC biomarkers without requiring extensive sample preparation or destructive processing, thereby enabling high-throughput, accurate, and reliable meat authentication in commercial and regulatory settings.

## BACKGROUND

Meat flavor is a critical determinant of consumer acceptance, market value, and product authenticity. Among emerging meat sources, donkey meat has gained significant attention due to its nutritional profile—being low in calories yet rich in protein and polyunsaturated fatty acids—and its distinctive sensory characteristics. In China, donkey husbandry plays a vital role in rural economic development, with donkey meat contributing over 80% of the sector’s economic output. However, the increasing demand for this premium meat has also heightened the risk of mislabeling, adulteration, and substitution with lower-value meats, necessitating robust analytical tools for verification and quality assurance.

Traditional methods for analyzing meat flavor compounds, such as solid-phase microextraction coupled with gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (SPME–GC–MS) or gas chromatography–olfactometry–mass spectrometry (GC–O–MS), have been widely used to identify volatile organic compounds responsible for characteristic aromas. While effective, these techniques suffer from several limitations: they require complex and time-consuming sample pre-treatment steps—including heating, solvent extraction, or distillation—which may alter the native VOC profile; they are often labor-intensive and not amenable to rapid screening; and they typically involve expensive instrumentation and highly trained personnel. Moreover, such methods are generally unsuitable for real-time or on-site analysis in production or inspection environments.

Gas chromatography–ion mobility spectrometry (GC–IMS) has emerged as a promising alternative, offering high sensitivity, rapid analysis, minimal sample preparation, and the ability to operate under ambient conditions. GC–IMS separates volatile compounds first by gas chromatography and then by ion mobility in a drift tube under an electric field, producing two-dimensional data that can be visualized as topographic plots or spectral fingerprints. When coupled with chemometric tools such as principal component analysis (PCA) or orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA), GC–IMS enables the discrimination of complex biological samples based on subtle differences in VOC composition. Despite its successful application in distinguishing beef, mutton, chicken, and cured pork products, GC–IMS combined with multivariate analysis has not previously been applied to the authentication of donkey meat or the differentiation of donkey strains based on intrinsic flavor chemistry. There remains a clear and unmet need for a rapid, reliable, and non-invasive method capable of identifying strain-specific VOC signatures in raw donkey meat to support traceability, quality control, and consumer protection.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides a novel method for the identification and differentiation of donkey meat from distinct genetic strains, specifically SanFen (SF) and WuTou (WT) donkeys, based on the analysis of volatile organic compounds (VOCs). The method comprises a sample treatment step wherein a raw meat sample is placed in a sealed headspace vial and incubated under controlled temperature and agitation to allow equilibration of volatiles. This is followed by a sample analysis step using gas chromatography–ion mobility spectrometry (GC–IMS), which separates and detects VOCs without the need for chemical derivatization or extraction. The resulting two-dimensional ion mobility spectra are then subjected to a data analysis step involving chemometric modeling, including dynamic principal component analysis (PCA), partial least squares discriminant analysis (PLS-DA), and orthogonal PLS-DA, to extract discriminatory features and identify strain-specific biomarkers.

The method enables the detection of at least 38 identified VOCs belonging to four chemical classes—ketones, alcohols, aldehydes, and heterocyclic compounds—with 17 of these compounds exhibiting statistically significant differences between SF and WT strains. Key differential markers include elevated levels of acetone, ethanol, isopropyl alcohol, benzaldehyde, and 2-pentanone-m in SF donkey meat, and higher concentrations of hexanal isomers, pentanal isomers, 3-octenal, and oct-1-en-3-ol in WT donkey meat. The invention offers several advantages over existing approaches: it eliminates complex sample preparation, reduces analysis time to under 20 minutes per sample, provides high reproducibility, and delivers interpretable spectral fingerprints that can be used for rapid visual comparison. Furthermore, the integration of multivariate statistical validation ensures model robustness and minimizes overfitting. Applications of the invention extend beyond strain differentiation to include meat authentication, fraud detection, quality grading, flavor standardization, and support for geographical indication claims in the global meat trade.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE EMBODIMENTS

In a preferred embodiment of the invention, the sample treatment step involves weighing 1.5 grams of raw donkey meat—preferably from the longissimus dorsi muscle—and placing it into a 20-mL headspace glass vial sealed with a silicone/polytetrafluoroethylene cap. The vial is then incubated at 60°C for 15 minutes while being agitated at 500 rpm to promote the release of volatile compounds into the headspace without thermal degradation. This mild, non-destructive conditioning mimics natural aroma emission and preserves the integrity of labile VOCs.

The sample analysis is performed using a FlavourSpec® GC–IMS instrument equipped with an MXT-5 capillary column (15 m × 0.53 mm × 1.0 μm) and an automated headspace sampler. The GC oven is maintained at a constant 40°C, and ultra-high-purity nitrogen serves as both carrier and drift gas. The carrier gas flow is programmed to increase from 2 mL/min (0–2 min) to 20 mL/min (2–10 min) and finally to 100 mL/min (10–20 min). The IMS drift tube is operated at 60°C with a drift voltage of 5 kV and a drift gas flow of 150 mL/min, while ionization is achieved via tritium (³H) in positive ion mode. A 500-μL aliquot of the equilibrated headspace is automatically injected at 85°C, ensuring precise and repeatable introduction of volatiles.

Following acquisition, the raw GC–IMS data are processed using specialized software plugins (Reporter and Gallery Plot) to generate normalized topographic plots and comparative fingerprint maps. The data analysis embodiment employs MetaboAnalyst 5.0 for multivariate statistical evaluation. Dynamic principal component analysis (PCA) is first applied as an unsupervised method to visualize inherent clustering among samples. Supervised models—PLS-DA and OPLS-DA—are then constructed to maximize separation between SF and WT groups. Model validity is confirmed through permutation testing, where Q² intercept values below zero indicate absence of overfitting. Variable importance in projection (VIP) scores greater than 1, combined with p-values < 0.05 from one-way ANOVA, are used to identify significant differential VOCs.

Fingerprint comparison is conducted by aligning GC–IMS spectra from unknown samples against reference libraries of SF and WT profiles. Differences in peak intensity and position for key markers—such as hexanal-d, ethanol, and acetone—are visually apparent and quantifiable. The dynamic PCA score plot typically shows clear segregation along principal components, with SF samples clustering separately from WT along PC1 or PC2, reflecting underlying biochemical divergence.

In practice, the method successfully identifies donkey meat lines with high accuracy. Example results demonstrate that SF donkey meat exhibits significantly higher levels of ketones and alcohols (e.g., acetone, 2-butanol, ethanol), whereas WT meat is enriched in aldehydes like hexanal and pentanal isomers. Heatmap analysis corroborates these findings, showing distinct expression patterns across the two strains. The entire workflow—from sample loading to final classification—can be completed in less than 30 minutes, making it suitable for routine screening in abattoirs, customs laboratories, or food safety agencies.

The advantages of this embodiment are manifold: it requires no solvents or reagents, avoids thermal artifacts associated with cooking-based analyses, leverages automation for high throughput, and integrates chemometric rigor to ensure scientific defensibility. By linking specific VOC signatures to genetic origin, the invention establishes a new paradigm for meat traceability grounded in intrinsic flavor chemistry rather than external labeling or documentation.
