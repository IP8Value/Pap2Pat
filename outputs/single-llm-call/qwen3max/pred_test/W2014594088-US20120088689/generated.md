# DESCRIPTION

Renal disorders represent a significant global health burden, encompassing a spectrum of acute and chronic conditions that impair kidney structure and function. These disorders can arise from diverse etiologies including systemic diseases, genetic predispositions, infections, toxic insults, and hemodynamic disturbances. The kidneys perform essential physiological roles such as filtration of metabolic waste products, regulation of fluid and electrolyte balance, maintenance of acid-base homeostasis, and endocrine functions including erythropoietin and renin production. Disruption of these critical functions due to renal pathology leads to progressive deterioration in health, increased risk of cardiovascular complications, and ultimately, end-stage renal disease requiring dialysis or transplantation. Early and accurate diagnosis, coupled with reliable prognostic indicators, is therefore crucial for timely intervention and improved patient outcomes.

The clinical features of nephropathies vary widely depending on the underlying etiology, stage of disease, and rate of progression. Common manifestations include proteinuria, hematuria, hypertension, edema, and alterations in serum creatinine and estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR). In many cases, particularly in the early stages of chronic kidney disease (CKD), patients may be asymptomatic, making routine screening and biomarker-based detection essential. Histopathological evaluation of kidney biopsies remains a cornerstone for definitive diagnosis in many glomerular and tubulointerstitial diseases, revealing characteristic patterns of injury such as glomerulosclerosis, tubular atrophy, interstitial fibrosis, and inflammatory infiltrates. However, biopsy is an invasive procedure with associated risks, underscoring the need for non-invasive or minimally invasive biomarkers that can reliably reflect the extent of renal damage and predict future functional decline.

Acute kidney injury (AKI) is defined as a rapid decline in kidney function, typically occurring over hours to days, characterized by an abrupt increase in serum creatinine and/or a reduction in urine output. AKI is a common complication in hospitalized patients, particularly those in intensive care units, and is associated with high morbidity, mortality, and prolonged hospital stays. The causes of AKI are traditionally categorized into three main groups: pre-renal, intrinsic renal, and post-renal etiologies. Pre-renal causes stem from conditions that reduce renal perfusion without direct parenchymal damage, such as hypovolemia, heart failure, sepsis, or renal artery stenosis. Intrinsic renal causes involve direct injury to the kidney structures, including acute tubular necrosis (often due to ischemia or nephrotoxins), glomerulonephritis, interstitial nephritis, and vascular disorders like thrombotic microangiopathies. Post-renal causes result from urinary tract obstruction distal to the kidneys, such as from prostate enlargement, kidney stones, or tumors compressing the ureters.

Diagnosis of AKI relies on consensus criteria established by international guidelines, such as those from the Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) initiative, which define AKI based on absolute or relative increases in serum creatinine within specific timeframes or reductions in urine output. A key diagnostic criterion is a rapid increase in serum creatinine by ≥0.3 mg/dL within 48 hours or a ≥50% increase from baseline within the prior 7 days. Despite its widespread use, serum creatinine has well-recognized limitations as a biomarker for AKI. It is influenced by non-renal factors such as muscle mass, age, sex, and hydration status, and it only rises after significant functional loss has already occurred, thereby delaying diagnosis. Moreover, creatinine does not distinguish between different etiologies of AKI or provide information about the underlying pathological processes.

In response to these limitations, alternative biomarkers for AKI have been investigated, including neutrophil gelatinase-associated lipocalin (NGAL), kidney injury molecule-1 (KIM-1), interleukin-18 (IL-18), and liver fatty acid-binding protein (L-FABP). Current clinical practice for testing renal disease biomarkers often involves a combination of serum and urine analyses, imaging studies, and, when indicated, renal biopsy. High-risk patients for AKI include those undergoing major surgery, receiving nephrotoxic medications (e.g., contrast agents, aminoglycosides), suffering from sepsis, or with pre-existing CKD. Identifying these individuals early allows for preventive strategies and closer monitoring.

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) is defined as abnormalities of kidney structure or function present for more than three months, with implications for health. It is staged based on eGFR and the presence of albuminuria or other markers of kidney damage. The prevalence of CKD is substantial worldwide, affecting approximately 10–15% of the adult population, and is rising due to aging demographics and increasing rates of diabetes and hypertension. There is a critical need for reliable histological and serological markers that can not only diagnose CKD but also predict its progression to end-stage renal disease (ESRD). Established biomarkers such as proteinuria are strong predictors of adverse renal outcomes, but they lack specificity for underlying pathological mechanisms and may not capture early molecular changes preceding functional decline.

Biomarkers that predict the progression of CKD are essential for risk stratification and personalized management. ESRD represents the terminal stage of CKD, requiring renal replacement therapy, and identifying patients at high risk for this outcome enables timely referral to nephrology care and preparation for dialysis or transplantation. Proteinuria, particularly nephrotic-range proteinuria, is a well-established risk marker for CKD progression, but alternative biomarkers are needed to improve predictive accuracy, especially in patients with non-nephrotic proteinuria or preserved eGFR. Cardiovascular disease (CVD) is the leading cause of death in CKD patients, and biomarker candidates that reflect both renal and cardiovascular risk are of particular interest. Molecular biomarker candidates for kidney damage include genes and proteins involved in inflammation, fibrosis, oxidative stress, and cellular repair pathways.

The clinical significance of biomarkers lies in their ability to provide actionable information beyond conventional parameters. Gene expression analysis of human kidney cell lines has revealed dysregulated pathways in disease states. Specifically, gene expression profiles of human proximal tubular epithelial cells have identified novel candidates associated with AKI and CKD. Among these, certain biomarkers are indicative of progressive disease, offering potential for early intervention. Versican, a large chondroitin sulfate proteoglycan of the extracellular matrix, has emerged as an AKI risk factor and is implicated in various renal pathologies. Originally studied in vascular tumors, versican plays a pathogenic role in crescentic glomerulonephritis and other inflammatory kidney diseases. Inhibition of versican or therapeutic antibodies against versican represent potential treatment strategies.

Molecular mapping has shown distinct distributions of PG-M/versican isoforms, particularly V0 and V1, which are upregulated in tumor cell growth and renal injury. Antibodies targeting the NH2-terminal domain or the glycosaminoglycan attachment domain of versican can be used for detection and potential neutralization. The goal of the present invention is to provide a method for assessing the risk of renal disorders, particularly progressive CKD, through the specific determination of versican isoforms V0 and V1. The method according to the invention involves detecting the expression levels of VCAN isoforms—specifically VCAN V0 and VCAN V1—in biological samples from patients. The protein sequence of VCAN V0 includes exons encoding the GAG-α and GAG-β domains, while VCAN V1 lacks the GAG-α domain. Their respective mRNA sequences correspond to splice variants generated by alternative splicing of exons 7 and 8 of the VCAN gene.

The term “VCAN0 and/or VCAN1” as used herein refers to the detection of either or both of the V0 and V1 isoforms of the versican protein or their encoding mRNAs. Expression of these isoforms is associated with disease progression in renal disorders. VCAN isoforms are expressed in kidney cells, particularly in proximal tubular epithelial cells and fibroblasts, and can be detected in kidney biopsies via immunohistochemistry or molecular techniques. The inventive method aids in the classification of chronic kidney disease severity and enables early diagnosis before significant functional decline occurs. The term “patients” encompasses individuals suspected of having or diagnosed with a renal disorder, including those with proteinuric nephropathies, diabetic nephropathy, glomerulonephritis, or other CKD etiologies.

The method of assessing risk of renal disorder comprises detecting VCAN isoforms in a biological sample (e.g., kidney tissue, urine sediment, or blood) and comparing the detected levels to reference values derived from healthy controls or stable disease cohorts. Microarray hybridization can be employed to assess gene expression profiles simultaneously, while PCR-based detection methods, including real-time quantitative PCR, offer sensitive and specific quantification of VCAN mRNA isoforms. Oligonucleotides designed to hybridize specifically to VCAN V0 or V1 transcripts enable selective detection. Polynucleotides corresponding to these isoforms can be amplified and detected using labeled probes. Antibodies specific to VCAN V0 and/or V1 protein isoforms facilitate immunoassays, including competitive and non-competitive formats, using labeled ligands for signal detection.

Antibody microarrays can be constructed to allow high-throughput screening of multiple biomarkers, including versican isoforms. Kits comprising reagents for carrying out the methods—such as primers, probes, antibodies, buffers, and controls—are provided for clinical or research use. Additional markers may be used in combination with VCAN isoforms to enhance predictive power. Immunoassays may utilize solid supports, cross-linkers, and sandwich enzyme immunoassay formats for enhanced sensitivity. Marker-specific moieties, such as antibodies or aptamers, are conjugated to labeled moieties (e.g., enzymes, fluorophores) for detection. The prognosis method involves predicting the likelihood of acute kidney injury or CKD progression based on VCAN isoform levels. Comparison with a cut-off concentration, determined statistically from cohort studies, allows binary risk classification.

The method is non-invasive or minimally invasive when applied to urine or blood samples. It can also be used to assess the potential of test compounds to modulate VCAN expression or to determine the effect of environmental factors on renal versican levels. A set of reagents for determining VCAN variants includes isoform-specific detection tools. Detection of versican protein expression reveals localization in the glomerular compartment (weakly), tubulointerstitial compartment (strongly, especially in fibrosis), and renal cortical blood vessels (media and adventitia). The figures and examples described herein demonstrate the utility of the inventive method for diagnosis, monitoring, prediction, and prognosis of renal disorders, establishing versican isoforms V0 and V1 as robust biomarkers of progressive kidney disease.

## EXAMPLES

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) is a progressive condition characterized by gradual loss of kidney function over time, leading to accumulation of waste products, fluid imbalances, and systemic complications. Its causes are multifactorial, including diabetes, hypertension, glomerulonephritis, and genetic disorders, and its consequences extend beyond renal failure to include heightened cardiovascular risk, anemia, bone disease, and reduced quality of life. Given the often silent progression of CKD, there is an urgent need for biomarkers that can identify patients at risk for rapid decline before irreversible damage occurs. Versican, a large extracellular matrix proteoglycan, has emerged as a potential biomarker due to its upregulation in injured renal tissue and association with fibrosis and inflammation. The following examples outline experimental validation of versican isoforms V0 and V1 as predictive biomarkers in human and animal models of kidney disease.

### Example 1

A cohort of 74 patients with various proteinuric kidney diseases underwent kidney biopsy as part of routine clinical care. RNA was isolated from frozen biopsy tissue using the RNeasy® Micro Kit, and cDNA was synthesized using standard reverse transcription protocols. Real-time PCR was performed using TaqMan® assays specific for VCAN V0 (Hs01007944_m1) and VCAN V1 (Hs01007937_m1), with cyclophilin A as a reference gene. Cell culture experiments were conducted using human proximal tubule HK2 cells and kidney fibroblast TK-173 cells under basal and stimulated conditions. Results showed that both V0 and V1 isoforms were significantly upregulated in patients who later developed progressive disease, defined as doubling of serum creatinine or progression to ESRD during follow-up. Expression levels of V0 and V1 correlated positively with serum creatinine at follow-up (V0: R=0.337, p=0.003; V1: R=0.387, p=0.001) but not strongly with baseline eGFR or proteinuria. When patients were classified as stable or progressive, V0 and V1 expression was 1.56-fold and 1.67-fold higher, respectively, in the progressive group (p=0.034 and p=0.011). Linear regression analysis demonstrated that inclusion of V1 in a model with baseline creatinine and glomerulosclerosis significantly improved the prediction of future renal function (adjusted R²=0.2438, p=0.0001). These findings indicate that versican isoforms V0 and V1 are independent predictors of CKD progression, outperforming traditional risk factors alone.

### Example 2

An extended analysis of the same 74-patient cohort confirmed the initial findings. RNA isolation and real-time PCR methods were identical to Example 1. After excluding five patients with acute renal failure at biopsy (defined by >50% creatinine reduction during follow-up), the association between versican isoforms and progressive disease remained significant (V1: p=0.018; V0: p=0.051). V0 expression correlated significantly with glomerulosclerosis (R=0.312, p=0.012), while V1 correlated with tubular atrophy/interstitial fibrosis (R=0.289, p=0.021) and interstitial inflammation (R=0.254, p=0.048). Cell-specific expression studies revealed that V0 and V1 were predominantly expressed in HK2 proximal tubule cells and TK-173 fibroblasts, with minimal expression in endothelial or immune cells. Immunohistochemistry localized versican protein to the tubulointerstitial compartment, particularly in areas of fibrosis. These results reinforce that versican isoforms V0 and V1 are biomarkers of progressive renal disease, reflecting underlying histopathological damage and predicting functional decline.

### Discussion

The potential of biomarkers for CKD lies in enabling early, targeted interventions to slow or halt disease progression. Versican, as a marker of histopathological damage, bridges molecular pathology with clinical outcomes. Examples 1 and 2 consistently demonstrate that V0 and V1 isoforms are elevated in progressive CKD and add predictive value beyond conventional parameters. This could justify intensified therapeutic options, such as stricter blood pressure control or novel antifibrotic agents, in high-risk patients. Structurally, versican consists of an N-terminal globular domain, central glycosaminoglycan attachment regions (present in V0 and V1), and a C-terminal domain. Its isoforms exhibit tissue-specific expression, with V0 and V1 predominant in stromal and injured tissues. The data support versican’s role as a predictive biomarker, offering a window into active fibrogenic and inflammatory processes before functional decline is evident. In summary, versican is a promising biomarker for CKD progression.

### Example 3

In a mouse model of accelerated nephrotoxic serum nephritis—a model of proliferative glomerulonephritis—renal versican mRNA expression was analyzed at 14 days post-induction. Real-time PCR using a pan-versican TaqMan assay (not isoform-specific) revealed a 3.5-fold upregulation compared to untreated controls (p=0.02). This confirms that versican is induced in experimental renal injury, supporting its relevance as a conserved marker across species and disease models.

### Example 4

Immunohistochemistry was performed on frozen kidney sections from patients with IgA nephropathy and lupus nephritis using a rabbit anti-human versican antibody (sc-25831). Staining revealed pronounced versican expression in the tubulointerstitial compartment, with weaker glomerular signal and strong vascular media/adventitia staining. In areas of interstitial fibrosis, versican exhibited a fibrillar deposition pattern. Healthy control tissue showed minimal expression. This spatial localization qualifies versican as a marker of active tubulointerstitial injury and remodeling, further validating its utility in clinical assessment.
