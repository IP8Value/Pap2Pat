# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

RNA binding proteins (RBPs) constitute a fundamental class of regulatory molecules that govern nearly every aspect of RNA metabolism, including splicing, transport, localization, stability, and translation. These proteins interact with specific RNA sequences or structural motifs to modulate gene expression in a spatiotemporally controlled manner. Dysregulation of RBP activity has been causally linked to a broad spectrum of human diseases, ranging from neurodegenerative disorders such as amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) and frontotemporal dementia to various forms of cancer. Consequently, the precise identification of RNA targets bound by RBPs is critical not only for understanding basic biological mechanisms but also for uncovering novel diagnostic markers and therapeutic targets. Over the past two decades, several methodologies have been developed to map RBP–RNA interactions on a transcriptome-wide scale. Among these, crosslinking and immunoprecipitation (CLIP) and its enhanced variant, eCLIP, have emerged as gold-standard techniques due to their ability to capture direct, in vivo protein–RNA contacts with nucleotide resolution. However, despite their utility, conventional CLIP-based approaches suffer from significant technical limitations that hinder scalability, reproducibility, and applicability to rare clinical specimens. Chief among these limitations is the reliance on sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS–PAGE) followed by nitrocellulose membrane transfer to isolate protein–RNA complexes of defined molecular weight—a labor-intensive, time-consuming step that introduces substantial inter-operator variability and requires large input material. Furthermore, each RBP must be interrogated in a separate immunoprecipitation reaction, which multiplies reagent consumption, sample requirements, and experimental time when profiling multiple RBPs. These constraints have impeded comprehensive annotation of the human RNA-binding proteome, which is now estimated to encompass over 1,500 proteins—far exceeding the number currently characterized by high-confidence experimental data. Thus, there exists a pressing need for an improved methodology that eliminates gel-based size selection, enables multiplexed interrogation of numerous RBPs from a single biological sample, and maintains or exceeds the sensitivity and specificity of existing protocols.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides a novel method for identifying RNA molecules bound by RNA binding proteins (RBPs), termed antibody-barcode eCLIP (ABC). This method comprises contacting an RNA sample with one or more RNA binding proteins under conditions that permit formation of RNA–protein complexes. In a key innovation, the method employs oligonucleotide-conjugated entities—such as antibodies, recombinant Fabs, nanobodies, aptamers, or solid-phase beads—that are covalently linked to unique DNA barcodes. These barcoded entities facilitate the specific capture of RBP–RNA complexes and enable subsequent proximity-based ligation of the bound RNA to the attached oligonucleotide barcode directly on the solid support, thereby generating chimeric RNA–DNA molecules. Following ligation, the chimeric molecules are reverse transcribed into cDNA, amplified by polymerase chain reaction (PCR) using primers complementary to the barcode and adapter sequences, and subjected to high-throughput sequencing. The resulting sequencing data are then computationally processed to demultiplex reads based on their unique barcodes, allowing simultaneous identification of RNA targets for multiple RBPs within a single reaction. Critically, the method obviates the need for SDS–PAGE and membrane transfer by performing all enzymatic steps—including RNA end repair, ligation, and protein digestion—on-bead, significantly streamlining the workflow. Prior to complex isolation, cells or tissues are typically lysed under denaturing conditions to disrupt non-covalent interactions while preserving UV-crosslinked RBP–RNA adducts. Immunoprecipitation is achieved using the oligo-conjugated antibodies, which may be pre-immobilized on magnetic beads for ease of handling. Crosslinking of RNA–protein complexes is preferably accomplished via ultraviolet (UV) irradiation at 254 nm, which induces covalent bonds between proximal RNA and protein moieties. Enrichment of chimeric molecules is inherent in the design, as only RNA molecules in direct contact with the barcoded antibody undergo ligation and thus enter the sequencing library. The invention further encompasses kits containing all necessary components for practicing the method, including barcoded antibodies, ligation enzymes, reverse transcriptase, PCR reagents, sequencing adapters, and buffers optimized for each step of the protocol.

### Methods

The invention provides a robust and scalable method for identifying RNA molecules bound by specific RNA binding proteins. The method begins by contacting an RNA-containing sample—derived from lysed cells or tissues—with one or more RNA binding proteins, either endogenously present or exogenously added, under physiological or near-physiological conditions to allow complex formation. To stabilize transient interactions, the sample is typically subjected to UV crosslinking prior to lysis, creating covalent linkages between RBPs and their cognate RNA targets. Cells are then lysed using a buffer containing detergents and chaotropic agents to solubilize macromolecules while preserving crosslinked complexes. The RNA–protein complexes are isolated via immunoprecipitation using oligonucleotide-conjugated entities that specifically recognize the RBP of interest. These entities may include full-length antibodies, antigen-binding fragments (Fabs), single-domain nanobodies, nucleic acid aptamers, or even inert beads functionalized with affinity ligands, provided they can be covalently linked to a DNA oligonucleotide bearing a unique barcode sequence. Conjugation is achieved through chemoselective reactions, such as amine-reactive NHS ester chemistry targeting lysine residues, thiol-reactive maleimide chemistry targeting cysteine residues, or bioorthogonal click chemistry (e.g., azide–DBCO cycloaddition) for site-specific labeling. Unreacted probes are removed by desalting columns or dialysis to prevent interference in downstream steps. The immunoprecipitated complexes remain immobilized on magnetic beads coated with secondary antibodies or protein A/G, facilitating rapid washing and buffer exchange. A critical step involves proximity-based intermolecular ligation: the 3′ end of the RBP-bound RNA is enzymatically repaired and ligated directly to the 5′ end of the oligonucleotide barcode tethered to the same antibody molecule. This ligation event occurs only when both moieties are in close spatial proximity—i.e., when the RNA is genuinely bound by the target RBP—thereby ensuring high specificity. The resulting chimeric RNA–barcode molecules are then released from the beads by proteolytic digestion of the protein components, reverse transcribed into cDNA, and subjected to adapter ligation and PCR amplification using primers that anneal to universal sequences flanking the barcode. Amplified libraries are purified, quantified, and sequenced on a next-generation platform such as Illumina. Computational analysis involves trimming of sequencing adapters and unique molecular identifiers (UMIs), alignment of reads to the reference genome, demultiplexing by barcode, and peak calling to identify enriched binding sites. Randomized UMI sequences are used to collapse PCR duplicates and accurately quantify original RNA molecules. In multiplexed implementations, multiple barcoded antibodies—each targeting a distinct RBP—are combined in a single immunoprecipitation reaction, enabling parallel profiling of dozens of RBPs from one sample. The complement of all other RBPs in the pool serves as an internal control (“complement control” or CC) for statistical enrichment analysis, eliminating the need for separate input controls.

### Kits

The invention further provides integrated kits designed to facilitate the implementation of the disclosed methods. Such kits contain a curated set of reagents sufficient to perform the entire workflow from cell lysis to sequencing library preparation. Core components include a panel of pre-conjugated, barcoded antibodies against well-characterized RBPs, along with unconjugated oligonucleotides for custom conjugation. Additional kit elements comprise T4 polynucleotide kinase and T4 RNA ligase for RNA end repair and ligation, reverse transcriptase with associated buffers, high-fidelity DNA polymerase for PCR amplification, sequencing adapters compatible with Illumina platforms, and dual-index primers for sample multiplexing. The kits also include specialized buffers optimized for each step: lysis buffer containing Igepal CA-630 and sodium deoxycholate, high-salt and low-salt wash buffers for stringent immunoprecipitation stringency, ligation master mix with PEG8000 to enhance ligation efficiency, and proteinase K solution for complete digestion of proteinaceous material. Magnetic beads pre-coated with anti-rabbit or anti-mouse IgG are provided for rapid antibody immobilization. Optional components may include RNase inhibitors, DNase for genomic DNA removal, and RNA cleanup columns. All reagents are quality-controlled and validated for performance in the ABC protocol, ensuring reproducibility across users and laboratories.

## EXAMPLES

### Example 1

This example details a singleplex implementation of the ABC method to identify RNA targets of RBFOX2 in HEK293XT cells. First, an oligonucleotide barcode containing a 5′ azide group and a 3′ PCR handle was synthesized and purified. A primary antibody against RBFOX2 was modified with dibenzocyclooctyne (DBCO)-NHS ester via lysine conjugation, followed by purification to remove excess reagent. The azide-functionalized barcode was then conjugated to the DBCO-labeled antibody via strain-promoted alkyne–azide cycloaddition (SPAAC), yielding a stable triazole linkage. HEK293XT cells were cultured, harvested, and UV-crosslinked at 400 mJ/cm². Cell pellets were lysed in buffer containing 1% Igepal, 0.1% SDS, and protease/RNase inhibitors, followed by sonication and limited RNase I digestion to fragment RNA. The lysate was incubated overnight with magnetic beads pre-coupled to the barcoded RBFOX2 antibody. After extensive washing with high-salt and low-salt buffers, RNA 3′ ends were repaired using T4 polynucleotide kinase. Proximity ligation was performed by adding T4 RNA ligase and incubating at room temperature, joining the RNA to the antibody-tethered barcode. Proteinase K digestion released chimeric RNA–barcode molecules, which were purified using an RNA clean-up kit. Reverse transcription was carried out using a primer complementary to the barcode, followed by second-strand synthesis and adapter ligation. Libraries were PCR-amplified with dual-index primers, purified with AMPure XP beads, and analyzed on an Agilent TapeStation to confirm size distribution. Sequencing on an Illumina NextSeq 2000 yielded approximately 25 million reads per sample. Bioinformatic processing involved UMI extraction, adapter trimming, reverse complementation (due to library strand orientation), and alignment to the human genome (GRCh38) using STAR. Reads were demultiplexed by barcode, and peaks were called using CLIPper. Analysis revealed strong enrichment at known RBFOX2 binding sites, such as the intronic GCAUG motif in NDEL1, with library complexity and motif recovery comparable to eCLIP. Figures 6–9 illustrate genome browser tracks, metagene profiles, and quantitative comparisons demonstrating equivalence between ABC and eCLIP.

### Example 3

This example demonstrates a ten-plex ABC experiment profiling ten RBPs—RBFOX2, SLBP, DDX3, EIF3G, IGF2BP2, FAM120A, PUM2, ZC3H11A, LIN28B, SF3B4, and PRPF8—in K562 cells. Each primary antibody was individually conjugated to a unique DNA barcode via click chemistry and immobilized on magnetic beads. K562 cell pellets were crosslinked, lysed, and fragmented as in Example 1. The lysate was split and incubated with a mixture of all ten barcoded antibody–bead conjugates simultaneously. After immunoprecipitation and washing, on-bead RNA end repair and barcode ligation were performed. Following proteinase digestion and RNA cleanup, reverse transcription and library construction proceeded as before. qPCR was used to determine optimal PCR cycles for each library. Sequencing generated ~250 million reads total, which were computationally demultiplexed into ten individual datasets. Peak calling using the complement control (CC) strategy—where each RBP’s enrichment was assessed against the aggregate signal from the other nine RBPs—yielded binding profiles highly concordant with ENCODE eCLIP data. For instance, DDX3 and EIF3G showed 5′ UTR enrichment, while PUM2 and IGF2BP2 localized to 3′ UTRs. Splicing factors PRPF8 and SF3B4 exhibited sharp peaks at 5′ and 3′ splice sites, respectively. Tables 16 and 17 summarize peak counts and genomic distributions. Schematic diagrams (not shown) illustrate the on-bead ligation mechanism, and bar graphs confirm expected binding preferences for each RBP.

### Example 4

This example outlines a protocol for amplifying and quantifying individual barcoded libraries post-ABC. Reagents include Q5 High-Fidelity DNA Polymerase, dual-index Illumina primers, and AMPure XP beads. PCR amplification is performed with cycling conditions of 98°C for 30 sec, followed by 10–18 cycles of 98°C for 10 sec, 60°C for 30 sec, and 72°C for 30 sec, concluding with a 72°C extension. Post-PCR cleanup uses a 0.8× bead-to-sample ratio to remove primers and dimers. Libraries are quantified by qPCR and assessed for size distribution on a TapeStation. Sequencing on Illumina platforms yields reads that are mapped to the human genome; barcodes are used to assign reads to specific RBPs. Figures 11 and 12 display correlation plots and overlap coefficients between ABC and eCLIP datasets, confirming high reproducibility and specificity. This streamlined workflow enables rapid, cost-effective profiling of RBP–RNA interactomes in input-limited samples such as patient biopsies.
