# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

Metamaterials are artificially engineered materials designed to exhibit electromagnetic properties not found in naturally occurring substances. These materials derive their unique characteristics from subwavelength structural features rather than from their chemical composition alone. By arranging constituent elements—often referred to as meta-atoms—in periodic or aperiodic configurations at scales smaller than the operating wavelength of incident electromagnetic radiation, metamaterials can manipulate the phase, amplitude, polarization, and propagation direction of electromagnetic waves in ways that conventional materials cannot. Historically, metamaterials have enabled phenomena such as negative refraction, cloaking, superlensing, and anomalous reflection or transmission. The development of metamaterials has been particularly transformative in optics and electromagnetics, where they offer a pathway to miniaturize and enhance the performance of traditional optical components. However, early implementations often relied on metallic resonators that introduced significant ohmic losses, limiting efficiency and practical utility. Subsequent advances shifted focus toward all-dielectric metamaterials, which leverage Mie resonances in high-refractive-index materials to achieve low-loss operation, albeit typically within narrow bandwidths due to the resonant nature of the underlying physics. Despite these improvements, conventional metasurface design methodologies remain constrained by limited geometric degrees of freedom, hindering simultaneous optimization of efficiency, bandwidth, and polarization control.

## SUMMARY

The present invention outlines a method for creating metadevices through an inverse electromagnetic design approach that begins with a desired optical functionality and computationally derives a physical structure capable of achieving it. This method enables the realization of high-efficiency, broadband, and polarization-sensitive electromagnetic devices that overcome limitations inherent in conventional metasurface designs. The invention further describes a system for generating such metadevices, integrating computational design algorithms with additive manufacturing technologies to produce functional prototypes rapidly and cost-effectively. Other principal features of the invention include the use of low-loss dielectric polymers such as high impact polystyrene (HIPS), the formulation of boundary conditions tailored to specific device functionalities—including polarization splitting, beam bending, and focusing—and the experimental validation of fabricated devices across millimeter-wave frequencies. The disclosed platform is scalable across the electromagnetic spectrum and supports the creation of complex, non-intuitive geometries that would be impractical or impossible to conceive using traditional design paradigms.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

Dielectric metadevices represent a class of electromagnetic structures composed entirely of non-metallic, low-loss dielectric materials arranged in subwavelength patterns to control wavefronts with high precision. Unlike conventional optical elements—such as lenses, mirrors, and polarizers—which rely on gradual phase accumulation over macroscopic path lengths, dielectric metadevices achieve abrupt phase shifts within ultra-thin layers, enabling compact integration into modern photonic systems. However, conventional optical elements suffer from several drawbacks: they are bulky, difficult to miniaturize, and often incapable of performing multiple functions simultaneously without cascading multiple components. These limitations motivate the use of metamaterials and metasurfaces, which promise unprecedented control over light-matter interactions at deeply subwavelength scales. Yet existing metasurface designs frequently depend on predefined geometric motifs like nanopillars, split-ring resonators, or V-antennas, whose limited parameter space restricts the achievable efficiency, operational bandwidth, and polarization versatility. To address these shortcomings, the present invention introduces an inverse electromagnetic design method that treats the entire spatial domain as a continuous optimization variable, thereby exploring a vastly expanded design landscape unconstrained by human intuition or symmetry assumptions.

This inverse design methodology employs an objective-first algorithm that formulates the electromagnetic wave equation as a non-convex optimization problem. Specifically, the algorithm seeks a spatial distribution of permittivity ε(r) that minimizes the deviation between simulated and desired electromagnetic field distributions at predefined input and output boundaries. The optimization alternates between solving for the electric field E given a fixed ε and updating ε given a fixed E, iteratively converging toward a feasible solution. Electromagnetic simulations—typically based on finite-difference time-domain (FDTD) or finite-element methods—are embedded within the optimization loop to evaluate candidate designs accurately. A key advantage of this approach is its ability to discover non-resonant, broadband structures that avoid the narrowband limitations of Mie-resonance-based metasurfaces. Furthermore, the invention leverages additive manufacturing—specifically fused deposition modeling (FDM) 3D printing—to fabricate the resulting complex geometries with high fidelity. Additive manufacturing offers exceptional scalability, supporting feature resolutions from nanometers to millimeters, and thus enables fabrication across microwave, millimeter-wave, terahertz, and even infrared regimes when appropriate materials are used.

In the demonstrated embodiments, high impact polystyrene (HIPS) serves as the dielectric medium due to its low cost, ease of processing, and exceptionally low loss tangent (tan δ < 0.003) in the 26–38 GHz band. With a relative permittivity ε′ ≈ 2.3 (refractive index n ≈ 1.52), HIPS allows sufficient phase modulation when structured appropriately. To achieve a full 2π phase shift between regions filled with HIPS and those filled with air, the device thickness t must satisfy Δφ = 2π(n − 1)t/λ ≥ 2π, yielding a minimum thickness of approximately 2λ. This constraint informs the physical dimensions of all fabricated metadevices. Testing of electromagnetic properties is performed using a vector network analyzer (VNA) coupled to horn antennas, with far-field angular transmission measured over ±40° in 2° increments and frequency sweeps from 26 to 38 GHz. Radar-absorbing materials surround the setup to suppress spurious reflections.

The design, fabrication, and characterization workflow begins by specifying the desired functionality—e.g., polarization splitting or focusing—and translating it into boundary conditions for the inverse design algorithm. For meta-gratings, periodic boundary conditions enforce diffraction into specific orders; for metalenses, perfectly matched layers (PMLs) absorb outgoing waves while the target output mimics a cylindrical wave converging at a focal point. The two-dimensional inverse design approach assumes infinite extent along the out-of-plane direction (z-axis), though fabricated devices are approximately 10λ tall to approximate this condition. One exemplary embodiment is a free-space polarization splitter (FIG. 2A) that deflects y-polarized (parallel) and z-polarized (perpendicular) plane waves into +1 and −1 diffraction orders, respectively, at ±30° for 33 GHz operation (λ = 9.1 mm). The grating period L = 1.8 cm satisfies the grating equation L sin θ = mλ. The algorithm outputs a binary permittivity map (ε = 1 or 2.3), which is converted into a 3D-printable STL file and fabricated as a 2 cm × 7.2 cm × 8 cm structure. Far-field measurements confirm high transmission efficiency (76% parallel, 54% perpendicular) and rejection ratios exceeding 5 dB, with broad bandwidth (Δλ/λ ≈ 33%) due to non-resonant operation.

Full-field simulations reveal Hz and Ez field amplitudes that illustrate how phase gradients arise from propagation through varying dielectric paths rather than localized resonances. Additional devices include a 15° polarization splitter exhibiting asymmetric bandwidth (18% for perpendicular vs. >50% for parallel polarization) due to guided-mode coupling, and a polarization-independent 30° beam bender that outperforms conventional blazed gratings by reducing power in undesired diffraction orders by factors of 2.0–2.8. Metalenses with focal lengths of 2λ and 15λ demonstrate tight focusing (FWHM = 0.5 cm and 1.1 cm) and numerical apertures approaching theoretical limits, with broadband operation from 28–40 GHz.

The complete process for creating a metadevice involves: determining the device type (e.g., splitter, bender, lens); defining boundary conditions reflecting desired input-output behavior; providing these constraints to the inverse-design algorithm; receiving a permittivity map; converting it to a 3D-printer-compatible file; and printing the structure using an additive manufacturing device. This workflow is implemented on a computing system comprising a processor, operating system, memory, storage, I/O interfaces, and network capabilities. The design algorithm executes on this system, processes boundary conditions, generates the metadevice structure, exports a fabrication file, and communicates directly or via network to the 3D printer. The platform supports rapid prototyping, scalability across frequencies, and incorporation of the printer into the computational environment. Illustrative embodiments demonstrate feasibility, but modifications—such as alternative materials, multi-layer designs, or three-dimensional optimization—are within the invention’s scope. The purpose is to enable efficient, broadband, compact electromagnetic components for communications, sensing, imaging, and radar. Advantages include design freedom, low loss, cost-effective fabrication, and performance surpassing conventional optics. The invention concludes as a versatile, scalable framework for next-generation metadevices.
