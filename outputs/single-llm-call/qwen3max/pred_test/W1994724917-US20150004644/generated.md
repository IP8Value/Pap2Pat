# DESCRIPTION

Staphylococcus aureus is a Gram-positive, facultative anaerobic bacterium that commonly colonizes the human anterior nares and skin, with an estimated carriage rate of approximately thirty percent among healthy individuals in the general population. While often asymptomatic in carriers, S. aureus is a leading opportunistic pathogen responsible for a broad spectrum of clinical manifestations ranging from minor cutaneous infections to life-threatening systemic diseases. The organism’s pathogenic potential stems from its extensive arsenal of virulence determinants, which are tightly regulated in response to environmental cues and host conditions. These virulence factors enable S. aureus to adhere to host tissues, evade immune defenses, acquire nutrients, and cause direct cellular damage through toxin production. Understanding the molecular mechanisms governing the expression of these virulence traits is critical not only for elucidating disease pathogenesis but also for developing diagnostic tools capable of predicting clinical outcomes and therapeutic responses.

S. aureus infections are broadly categorized into two major types: those mediated primarily by adhesion factors and those driven by toxigenic activity. Adhesion-mediated infections typically involve colonization of host surfaces or implanted medical devices, where the bacterium forms biofilms that resist both immune clearance and antimicrobial therapy. In contrast, toxigenic infections result from the secretion of exotoxins that disrupt host cell membranes, interfere with immune signaling, or induce toxic shock syndromes. The balance between these two infection strategies is dynamically regulated by global transcriptional networks that respond to bacterial density, nutrient availability, and host stressors.

Central to this regulatory network are adhesion factors, which include microbial surface components recognizing adhesive matrix molecules (MSCRAMMs) such as fibronectin-binding proteins (FnBPs), clumping factors (ClfA and ClfB), and protein A. These surface-expressed proteins facilitate initial attachment to host extracellular matrix components, endothelial cells, and abiotic surfaces like catheters or prosthetic joints. Their expression is typically highest during the early stages of infection when bacterial colonization is paramount.

Conversely, toxigenic infections are characterized by the production of secreted toxins including hemolysins, leukocidins, enterotoxins, and toxic shock syndrome toxin-1 (TSST-1). Among these, hemolysin δ—also known as delta-toxin—is a small, amphipathic peptide encoded within the RNAIII transcript of the accessory gene regulator (agr) locus. This toxin exhibits potent lytic activity against erythrocytes, neutrophils, and other eukaryotic cells, contributing significantly to tissue destruction and immune evasion during acute infection.

The expression of both adhesion factors and exotoxins is orchestrated by a suite of regulatory elements, with the agr quorum-sensing system serving as the master switch. The agr system comprises four operons (agrBDCA) that collectively sense bacterial population density and modulate gene expression accordingly. At low cell densities, surface protein genes are upregulated to promote colonization; as the population grows and autoinducing peptides accumulate, the agr system activates RNAIII transcription, which in turn represses surface adhesins and induces exotoxin production.

The agr system itself consists of several key components: AgrD, the precursor of the autoinducing peptide (AIP); AgrB, which processes and exports AgrD; AgrC, a membrane-bound histidine kinase that detects extracellular AIP; and AgrA, a response regulator that, upon phosphorylation by AgrC, activates transcription of the P2 and P3 promoters. The P3 promoter drives expression of RNAIII, the primary effector molecule of the agr system. RNAIII functions both as a regulatory RNA that post-transcriptionally modulates target mRNAs and as the messenger RNA encoding hemolysin δ.

Hemolysin δ is synthesized as a 26-amino acid peptide that undergoes N-terminal formylation during translation—a modification typical of bacterial proteins initiated with fMet. Following synthesis, the formyl group is often removed by peptide deformylase, although in many clinical isolates, the formylated form predominates and is detectable by mass spectrometry. The accumulation of hemolysin δ in culture supernatants or whole-cell lysates serves as a reliable phenotypic marker of agr functionality, as its expression is strictly dependent on RNAIII transcription.

Polymorphisms in the hld gene, particularly the glycine-to-serine substitution at position 10 (G10S), alter the molecular mass of hemolysin δ and can be distinguished by high-resolution mass spectrometry. Such variants retain hemolytic activity but exhibit distinct spectral signatures, necessitating careful calibration and interpretation in diagnostic assays.

Deficient strains of S. aureus—those lacking functional agr systems due to mutations in agrA, agrC, RNAIII, or other regulatory loci—are increasingly recognized in clinical settings. These strains fail to produce hemolysin δ and display altered virulence profiles characterized by enhanced biofilm formation, reduced toxin production, and increased persistence in chronic infections. The frequency of agr-deficient isolates varies across patient populations but has been reported in up to 20–40% of bloodstream infections associated with persistent bacteremia or indwelling devices.

Clinically, agr deficiency correlates with adverse outcomes, including prolonged hospitalization, treatment failure, and increased mortality in certain cohorts. Notably, agr dysfunction is strongly associated with persistent bacteremia, where defective quorum sensing impairs the transition from colonization to dissemination, paradoxically promoting long-term survival within the host. Additionally, agr-deficient strains exhibit heightened rates of bacterial autolysis under stress conditions, possibly due to dysregulation of cell wall metabolism.

Biofilm formation is markedly enhanced in agr-deficient strains, as the repression of proteases and other dispersal factors allows for stable matrix accumulation on biotic and abiotic surfaces. This phenotype is particularly relevant in cystic fibrosis (CF) patients, where chronic lung colonization by agr-impaired S. aureus contributes to progressive airway damage and antibiotic tolerance. Similarly, agr dysfunction is linked to the glycopeptide-intermediate S. aureus (GISA) or heterogeneous GISA (hGISA) phenotype, characterized by reduced susceptibility to vancomycin and teicoplanin. The mechanism involves thickened cell walls that sequester glycopeptides, a trait favored under agr-deficient conditions.

Multiple analytical methods exist for assessing hemolysin δ production. Traditional approaches include hemolysis tests on blood agar, which detect synergistic lysis when co-cultured with beta-toxin-producing staphylococci. Animal models, such as murine abscess or sepsis models, provide in vivo validation of virulence attenuation in agr mutants. Molecular techniques like RT-PCR and Northern blotting directly measure RNAIII levels, offering genetic confirmation of agr status. Sequencing of the agr locus identifies loss-of-function mutations, while HPLC enables purification and quantification of hemolysin δ from culture supernatants.

However, the present invention introduces a novel, rapid, and clinically applicable method for studying S. aureus colonies based on matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF MS). This technique involves positioning a bacterial colony directly onto a target plate, applying a chemical matrix, and irradiating the sample with a pulsed laser to ionize molecules. The resulting ions are accelerated through an electric field into a flight tube maintained under vacuum, where their time-of-flight is measured by a detector. From this, the mass-to-charge (m/z) ratio is calculated, generating a mass spectrum that reflects the proteomic profile of the organism.

By focusing on the m/z range of 2800–3200 Th, the presence or absence of hemolysin δ can be determined with high specificity. A peak at 3005±5 Th corresponds to wild-type formylated hemolysin δ, while a peak at 3035±5 Th indicates the G10S variant. The absence of both peaks strongly suggests agr system dysfunction. This result informs clinical decision-making: agr deficiency correlates with chronic infection, reduced glycopeptide sensitivity, and poor response to standard therapies. Thus, MALDI-TOF MS not only identifies the species but simultaneously provides actionable virulence data.

Sample preparation for this method follows routine microbiological protocols. Colonies grown on standard media (e.g., blood agar) are smeared directly onto MALDI targets and overlaid with α-cyano-4-hydroxycinnamic acid (CHCA) or sinapinic acid matrix. After drying, spectra are acquired using calibrated instruments. The MALDI-TOF/TOF variant further enhances specificity by selecting the ion of interest, fragmenting it via collision-induced dissociation, and analyzing the resulting b- and y-type fragment ions. For wild-type hemolysin δ, characteristic fragments include y7 (887.5 Th), y10 (1298.7 Th), and b15 (1621.8 Th); for G10S, shifts in these masses confirm the serine substitution.

In conclusion, this MALDI-TOF MS-based method represents a significant advancement in clinical microbiology, enabling real-time assessment of S. aureus virulence regulation without additional cost or delay beyond standard identification workflows.

## EXAMPLES

### 1. Equipment

The method employs a standard MALDI-TOF mass spectrometer equipped with a nitrogen or solid-state laser operating at 337 nm or 355 nm, respectively. The instrument includes a stainless steel target plate for sample deposition, a vacuum flight tube, an ion reflectron for improved resolution, and a microchannel plate detector. Internal calibration is performed using Escherichia coli ATCC 8739 or polyethylene glycol (PEG3000) standards in the 2800–3200 Th range. The matrix used is either α-cyano-4-hydroxycinnamic acid (CHCA) dissolved in 50% acetonitrile, 2.5% trifluoroacetic acid, or sinapinic acid in similar solvent systems. Control strains include RN6390 (agr-positive, hemolysin δ-producing) and its isogenic agr-knockout derivative RN6911 (hemolysin δ-negative).

### 2. Methodology

Preanalytical parameters require incubation of S. aureus isolates on trypticase soy agar with 5% horse blood for 18–24 hours at 35±2°C. Colonies are harvested using a sterile loop or pipette tip and directly smeared onto the MALDI target in duplicate. One microliter of matrix solution is added, allowed to air-dry, and analyzed within 2 hours. Analytical parameters include laser fluence set just above threshold for ion detection, 500–1000 laser shots accumulated per spot, and mass spectra acquired in positive linear or reflectron mode over m/z 2000–20,000 Th, with specific attention to the 2800–3200 Th window.

### 3. Results

Validation studies confirmed that purified hemolysin δ yields a dominant peak at 3005±1 Th, consistent with the theoretical mass of monoprotonated, formylated peptide (3005.3 Da). Isogenic agr-knockout strains lacked this peak, confirming specificity. Repeatability testing across 18, 24, and 48-hour cultures showed consistent detection, though signal intensity slightly declined at 48 hours. Culture media variations (COS, PVX, CHROM ID SA®, GP agar) did not affect peak presence or absence.

In a prospective collection of 168 clinical isolates, 139 (82.7%) displayed the 3005±5 Th peak, 12 (7.1%) showed 3035±5 Th (G10S variant), and 17 (10.1%) lacked both. MALDI-TOF/TOF analysis of representative strains yielded Mascot scores of 86.3 and 157.4 for wild-type and G10S, respectively, with matching b/y ion series. Northern blot confirmed absent RNAIII in all 17 deficient strains. Genotyping revealed polyclonal origins, ruling out epidemic spread.

Clinically, hemolysin δ deficiency correlated significantly with chronic infection (p=0.005), including CF lung disease and diabetic foot osteomyelitis. No association was found with colonization vs. infection (p=0.541) or biomaterial involvement (p=0.470). Methicillin resistance was more frequent in δ-negative strains (p=0.035), but multivariate analysis showed this was confounded by chronicity. Critically, among 28 GISA/hGISA strains, 9 (32.1%) were δ-negative versus only 9/126 (7.1%) in non-GISA (p=0.001). Multivariate modeling confirmed independent associations between δ-deficiency and both chronic infection (OR=3.21, CI 95%: 1.01–10.24, p=0.048) and glycopeptide resistance (OR=3.91, CI 95%: 1.20–12.76, p=0.023). In seven patients, sequential isolates demonstrated in vivo selection of agr-deficient variants from initially δ-positive populations, underscoring adaptive evolution during therapy. These findings validate hemolysin δ detection by MALDI-TOF MS as a robust, clinically informative surrogate for agr functionality.
