## TECHNICAL FIELD

The present invention relates generally to the field of digital imaging, and more particularly to high-throughput, multi-modal imaging systems and methods for simultaneously capturing bright-field, phase, and fluorescence images from multi-well plates used in biological and pharmaceutical screening applications. The invention specifically pertains to an imaging system that integrates Fourier ptychographic microscopy (FPM) with parallelized optical architectures to achieve high-resolution, aberration-corrected, and extended-depth-of-field imaging across all wells of a standard 96-well plate in a single acquisition sequence.

## BACKGROUND

Multi-well plate readers are ubiquitous tools in modern life science laboratories, enabling rapid absorbance or fluorescence measurements across dozens to hundreds of samples in parallel. Commercially available plate readers can process a full 96-well plate in as little as 10 seconds, providing high-throughput biochemical readouts. However, these instruments offer only bulk, population-averaged data and lack spatial resolution at the cellular or subcellular level. To address this limitation, imaging-based plate readers—often referred to as high-content screening (HCS) systems—have been developed over the past two decades. These systems typically employ a single scanning microscope column that moves sequentially across each well, capturing high-magnification images with cellular detail. While such systems provide rich morphological and functional information, their throughput is severely constrained by mechanical scanning and camera readout speeds. A state-of-the-art HCS imager operating at 1.2 μm resolution may require up to 8 minutes to image a single 96-well plate—approximately 50 times slower than a conventional non-imaging plate reader.

Conventional approaches to accelerating imaging throughput include using higher-speed cameras, faster stage actuators, or reduced fields of view. However, these strategies involve fundamental trade-offs between speed, resolution, signal-to-noise ratio, and coverage. Attempts to implement true parallel imaging—such as deploying 96 independent microscope objectives, one per well—face significant engineering challenges. First, commercial multi-well plates exhibit substantial geometric warping, with well-bottom depths varying by up to 170 μm across a single plate. This variation exceeds the native depth of field (typically <10 μm for moderate-NA objectives), rendering many wells out of focus simultaneously. Second, the physical packing density required for 96 objectives (each confined to a ~9 mm × 9 mm footprint) necessitates miniaturized optics that are difficult to manufacture with consistent optical quality. Plastic-molded lenses, while cost-effective, introduce lens-to-lens variations in aberrations due to surface defects and alignment errors. Third, the liquid meniscus formed in each well during standard cell culture protocols acts as a dynamic optical element, distorting illumination angles and introducing position-dependent aberrations that compromise image fidelity.

Traditional imaging techniques, including widefield, confocal, and light-sheet microscopy, are ill-suited to overcome these combined challenges in a parallelized format. Computational imaging methods such as deconvolution can partially mitigate blur but require accurate point-spread-function models and cannot recover lost high-frequency information. Adaptive optics can correct aberrations but typically require wavefront sensors and complex hardware integration, which are impractical in a massively parallel system. Thus, there remains a critical unmet need for an imaging platform that combines the throughput of plate readers with the resolution and contrast of microscopy, while robustly accommodating real-world variations in plate geometry, lens quality, and sample conditions.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides a high-throughput, multi-modal imaging system configured for simultaneous acquisition of bright-field, phase, and fluorescence images from all wells of a multi-well plate. The system comprises an illumination system, an optical system, an imaging system, a plate receiver system, and a controller configured to execute image reconstruction processes.

The illumination system includes a programmable array of light-emitting diodes (LEDs) positioned above the multi-well plate. The LEDs are arranged in a grid with a pitch that is a rational fraction of the well-to-well spacing, enabling structured, variable-angle illumination across the entire plate. In one embodiment, the LED pitch is one-quarter of the well pitch, allowing each well to be illuminated by multiple LEDs in a controlled sequence while minimizing crosstalk.

The optical system consists of an array of miniature microscope objectives, each aligned to a corresponding well of the multi-well plate. Each objective is designed with a finite conjugate configuration, providing a magnification of approximately 4×, a numerical aperture (NA) of 0.23, and a working distance of 4 mm. The objectives are fabricated via plastic injection molding to reduce cost and enable mass production, despite inherent manufacturing variations.

The imaging system includes a corresponding array of image sensors—preferably CMOS sensors—each optically coupled to one of the miniature objectives. The sensors are mounted on a custom printed circuit board that integrates 96 sensor units in a dense 9 mm × 9 mm grid per well location. The system supports both transmission-mode imaging for Fourier ptychography and epi-fluorescence imaging using external excitation sources and emission filters.

The plate receiver system includes a precision mechanical stage configured to hold and position the multi-well plate relative to the optical and illumination systems. In some embodiments, the stage includes a piezoelectric actuator capable of fine z-axis translation to accommodate gross plate warping or to acquire z-stacks for fluorescence imaging.

The controller is operatively connected to the illumination system, imaging system, and plate receiver. It is configured to orchestrate the acquisition of raw image data under varying illumination conditions and to execute a Fourier ptychographic reconstruction process. This process computationally synthesizes a high-resolution, phase-retrieved image by iteratively combining low-resolution intensity measurements captured under different illumination angles. The controller also implements embedded pupil function recovery to estimate and correct for lens-specific aberrations, as well as computational refocusing to extend the effective depth of field beyond the native limits of the optics. Additionally, the controller manages fluorescence image acquisition, including z-scanning, frame averaging, and co-registration with the phase images.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

### I. Introduction

Imaging systems and methods described herein are directed to high-resolution, high-throughput characterization of biological samples contained in multi-well plates, particularly 96-well and 384-well formats commonly used in drug discovery, toxicology, and cell biology. The primary motivation is to bridge the throughput gap between conventional plate readers and scanning microscopes by enabling simultaneous, parallel imaging of all wells without sacrificing resolution or contrast.

Traditional imaging systems based on single-objective scanning suffer from inherent speed limitations due to mechanical inertia and sequential data acquisition. Even with advanced automation, these systems cannot match the per-plate processing rates of non-imaging readers. Moreover, they are highly sensitive to plate warping and require frequent autofocus routines, further slowing operation. The present invention overcomes these limitations through a paradigm shift: instead of moving a single high-performance microscope across the plate, the system deploys an array of low-cost, miniaturized microscopes that operate in parallel.

A key enabler of this approach is Fourier ptychographic (FP) imaging, a computational microscopy technique that reconstructs high-resolution complex-valued images (amplitude and phase) from a series of low-resolution intensity images captured under varying illumination angles. FP effectively synthesizes a larger numerical aperture by coherently combining spatial frequency information from multiple acquisitions. Critically, FP also enables in situ estimation and correction of optical aberrations through embedded pupil recovery algorithms, making it uniquely suited to handle the lens-to-lens variations inherent in mass-produced plastic optics.

Prior art in FP microscopy has demonstrated aberration correction, extended depth of field, and super-resolution in single-objective configurations. However, scaling FP to a massively parallel architecture introduces new challenges in illumination uniformity, data synchronization, thermal management, and computational load. The present invention addresses these through integrated hardware-software co-design, including a shared LED illumination array, a custom 96-in-1 sensor board, GPU-accelerated reconstruction pipelines, and meniscus-aware ray tracing for accurate angle calibration.

The imaging system comprises several core components: a sample loading system for precise plate positioning; an illumination system with programmable LED arrays; an optical system of miniature objectives; an imaging system of synchronized CMOS sensors; and a controller that manages acquisition and reconstruction. During the image acquisition phase, the system captures a sequence of raw intensity frames under different LED activation patterns. These frames are then processed via a Fourier ptychographic reconstruction algorithm that iteratively updates a high-resolution estimate of the sample’s complex transmission function, while simultaneously recovering the system’s pupil function to correct for aberrations.

### II. Imaging System for Fourier Ptychographic (FP) Imaging and Fluorescent Imaging

The imaging system 100 includes an illumination system 102, a sample loading system 104, an optical system 106, an image sensor system 108, and a controller 110. The illumination system 102 comprises a matrix of light sources, such as RGB LEDs, arranged in a grid with a pitch of 3 mm—exactly one-quarter the well-to-well spacing of a standard 96-well plate. This arrangement allows structured illumination patterns to be projected onto the sample plane such that each well receives illumination from a unique subset of LEDs during each acquisition frame, minimizing inter-well crosstalk.

The sample loading system 104 includes a mechanical stage configured to receive and align a multi-well plate, such as a 96-well plate 208 with individual wells 209. The stage may include guides, clamps, or an automatic loading and ejecting mechanism to ensure repeatable positioning. In one embodiment, a sample platform 215 includes an aperture slot 214 to accommodate the plate while maintaining optical access from above and below.

The optical system 106 consists of 96 miniature microscope objectives, each custom-designed with a 4× magnification, 0.23 NA, and 4 mm working distance. These objectives are housed in a compact 9 mm × 9 mm × 81 mm module per well, sharing a common tube length of 34 mm. The use of plastic injection molding enables cost-effective mass production, albeit with tolerable aberration variations that are later corrected computationally.

The image sensor system 108 includes 96 CMOS sensors, each paired with one objective. The sensors are mounted on a single printed circuit board and interfaced with frame grabbers for high-speed data transfer at rates up to 340 MB/s. A liquid cooling system may be integrated to manage thermal noise during prolonged fluorescence imaging.

The controller 110 coordinates the activation of specific LEDs in the illumination system 102 according to a predetermined sequence, triggers synchronized image capture across all sensors in the image sensor system 108, and stores raw image data in memory. It then executes FP image processing operations, including Fourier transforms, low-pass filtering, amplitude replacement, and iterative phase retrieval, to generate a high-resolution complex image. For fluorescence imaging, the controller activates external excitation sources (e.g., 465 nm LEDs), acquires emission images through a bandpass filter (e.g., 535 nm ± 25 nm), and performs z-scanning and frame averaging to enhance signal-to-noise ratio.

Raw and processed image data are output via communication interfaces to an external computing device or stored on an external memory device. Network communication interfaces enable remote monitoring and control. Multiplexing and demultiplexing schemes allow efficient handling of the massive data streams generated by 96 parallel sensors.

In an alternative embodiment, imaging system 200 includes a housing or enclosure 202 with a rigid frame structure 204 to maintain alignment. Frame alignment rods 206 and alignment through-holes 205 ensure precise registration of optical components. Substrates with through-holes support the LED array and sensor board. A sample platform 305 accommodates the plate, while side-mounted light sources and a multi-lens-array arrangement provide uniform illumination. A removable optical filter enables switching between bright-field and fluorescence modes. The system is summarized as a fully integrated, high-throughput platform for multimodal plate imaging.

### III. Variable-Illumination Fourier Ptychographic Imaging Methods

The FP image acquisition process begins by loading a multi-well plate into the imaging system and initializing the illumination and image sensor systems. A calibration operation determines the precise angles of incidence for each LED, accounting for refraction through the liquid medium and meniscus curvature using a ray-tracing algorithm based on Snell’s law. During the s-th scan, a specific subset of LEDs is activated to illuminate the plate at distinct angles. Light transmitted through each well is collected by its corresponding objective and focused onto the image sensor. The resulting intensity image is acquired and stored in memory.

To maximize throughput, a multiplexing approach is employed: multiple LEDs are turned on simultaneously such that no two LEDs illuminate the same well within the acceptance cone of its objective. This ensures that intensity data from different wells can be separated during reconstruction. After n scans, the system checks whether all required illumination patterns have been captured; if not, it increments s and repeats.

The FP reconstruction process 700 initializes a high-resolution image solution in the spatial domain. It applies a Fourier transform to obtain a frequency-domain representation, applies low-pass filtering to simulate the objective’s limited bandwidth, and propagates the result to the in-focus plane. The amplitude component is replaced with the square root of the measured intensity, and the field is back-propagated to update the high-resolution solution. This loop repeats for all captured images until convergence is achieved, at which point the final high-resolution image is transformed back to the spatial domain.

An alternative FP reconstruction process 800 models the connection between the sample’s complex profile and the captured intensity data, then inverts this model to achieve an aberration-free image. Digital wavefront correction is applied by multiplying by phase factors in the Fourier domain that compensate for estimated aberrations. Convergence is assessed by comparing successive high-resolution solutions.

For fluorescence imaging, the multi-well plate is loaded, and the system is initialized. The plate is illuminated with excitation light (e.g., 465 nm), and emitted fluorescence (e.g., 535 nm) is collected, filtered, and imaged. A z-stack is acquired over a range sufficient to cover expected well-depth variations (e.g., ±50 μm). The sharpest fluorescence image is selected and combined with the FP-reconstructed phase image to produce a multimodal output, where cellular morphology and molecular localization are co-registered without additional alignment steps.
