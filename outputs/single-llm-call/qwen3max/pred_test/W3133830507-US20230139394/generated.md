# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNICAL FIELD

The present invention relates generally to the field of speech recognition systems, and more specifically to systems and methods for enhancing the performance of automatic speech recognition (ASR) for individuals with speech disorders such as aphasia, apraxia, and dysarthria. The invention further pertains to the integration of electrophysiological signals—particularly electroencephalography (EEG)—with traditional acoustic inputs to generate robust multimodal features for improved decoding of distorted or incomplete speech. The technical domain encompasses biomedical signal processing, deep learning architectures for multimodal fusion, neural prosthetics, assistive communication technologies, and real-time speech therapy tools. The invention is particularly relevant in clinical settings where patients recovering from neurological impairments—such as those caused by stroke or traumatic brain injury—struggle with conventional voice-based interfaces due to degraded articulatory control or linguistic formulation. By leveraging non-invasive neural correlates of speech production and perception, the disclosed system provides a novel pathway toward accessible, high-accuracy speech prosthetics that can operate effectively even when audio signals are severely compromised.

## BACKGROUND

Speech therapy plays a critical role in the rehabilitation of individuals suffering from acquired speech and language disorders, including aphasia, apraxia, and dysarthria. These conditions often arise from neurological damage, such as stroke or head trauma, and manifest as impaired ability to produce intelligible, fluent, or grammatically structured speech. Traditional speech therapy relies on auditory feedback and clinician-guided repetition, but progress can be slow and outcomes highly variable. Recent advances have explored the use of automatic speech recognition (ASR) systems as interactive feedback tools during therapy sessions, enabling real-time assessment of patient utterances and personalized intervention. However, conventional ASR systems—designed for normative speech—are ill-equipped to handle the acoustic irregularities characteristic of disordered speech, such as prolonged pauses, distorted phonemes, omitted syllables, or inconsistent prosody. Audio-only ASR models trained on healthy speakers exhibit catastrophic performance degradation when applied to pathological speech, with reported word error rates (WER) exceeding 97% in severe aphasia cases and phoneme error rates (PER) ranging from 75% to 89%. This limitation stems from the fundamental assumption in standard ASR pipelines that input speech conforms to expected acoustic and linguistic patterns, an assumption violated in neurogenic speech disorders. Moreover, the low signal-to-noise ratio, high intra- and inter-speaker variability, and limited availability of large-scale labeled datasets for disordered speech further impede model generalization. Consequently, there exists a pressing need for a robust speech recognition framework that transcends the constraints of acoustic fidelity alone by incorporating complementary physiological signals that encode the speaker’s intended linguistic content prior to or concurrent with vocalization.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides a system and method for EEG-based speech prosthetics that significantly enhances the accuracy of automatic speech recognition for individuals with aphasia, apraxia, and dysarthria. In a first embodiment, the system obtains an audio signal from a user via a microphone, simultaneously acquires EEG signals using non-invasive scalp electrodes, and extracts acoustic representations—specifically Mel frequency cepstral coefficients (MFCCs)—from the audio signal. Concurrently, a pre-trained deep learning regression model maps the EEG features to synthetic acoustic representations that approximate the MFCCs corresponding to the user’s intended speech. These EEG-derived acoustic representations are then concatenated with the actual MFCCs extracted from the audio signal to form a multimodal feature vector. This enriched feature vector is provided as input to an automatic speech recognition (ASR) model, which generates a text-based output representing the decoded utterance. In a second embodiment, the system further incorporates electromyography (EMG) signals to detect and remove articulatory artifacts from the EEG data prior to feature extraction, thereby improving the neural signal quality. Both embodiments are designed to operate in real-time or near real-time, enabling deployment in clinical speech therapy environments or as wearable assistive communication devices. The core innovation lies in the synergistic fusion of neural and acoustic modalities, wherein the EEG component compensates for the degraded or missing information in the audio stream, thereby restoring intelligibility and reducing recognition errors.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The invention is implemented in a device designated as device 100, which serves as a multimodal speech recognition platform. Device 100 comprises a communication unit 110 responsible for managing data transmission and reception. Within communication unit 110, transmit processing circuitry 115 encodes and modulates outgoing data streams, while receive processing circuitry 125 demodulates and decodes incoming signals. A microphone 120 captures the user’s vocalizations, and a speaker 130 provides auditory feedback. The main processor 140 orchestrates all computational operations, interfacing with an input/output (I/O) interface 145 that connects to various input/output devices 150, such as touchscreens, buttons, or display units. Memory 160 stores program instructions and data, including an operating system program 161 and one or more applications 162 implementing the speech recognition algorithms. Sensors 180 include EEG sensors 182 positioned according to the 10-20 international system, a secondary microphone 184 for redundancy or noise cancellation, and EMG sensors 186 placed on facial or neck muscles to monitor articulatory activity. Communication unit 110 operates by establishing wireless links with external servers or peer devices, with TX processing circuitry 115 handling uplink data (e.g., sensor streams) and RX processing circuitry 125 managing downlink commands or model updates. Main processor 140 executes the core signal processing pipeline: it receives raw EEG, EMG, and audio signals, applies filtering and artifact removal, extracts features, runs the regression and ASR models, and outputs recognized text. I/O interface 145 translates user interactions into system commands and renders results via input/output devices 150.

Device 100 communicates with a server 200, which provides cloud-based model training and storage. Server 200 includes a bus system 205 interconnecting a processing device 210, storage devices 215, a communications unit 220, and an I/O unit 225. Memory 230 holds runtime data, while persistent storage 235 archives datasets and trained models. The overall system architecture, denoted as architecture 300, integrates a deep learning regression stage 301 and a speech recognition stage 351. In operation, the system obtains EEG signals from EEG sensors 182, audio signals from microphone 120, and EMG signals from EMG sensors 186. EMG artifacts are removed from EEG signals using linear regression, after which five EEG features per channel—root mean square, zero-crossing rate, moving window average, kurtosis, and power spectral entropy—are extracted at 100 Hz. These features undergo dimensionality reduction via kernel principal component analysis (KPCA) to yield a 10-dimensional vector. A deep learning model 303, comprising a GRU layer and a dense layer, is trained to map this reduced EEG feature vector to 13-dimensional MFCC-like acoustic representations. Separately, MFCCs are extracted from the audio signal. The EEG-derived acoustic representations and audio MFCCs are concatenated and fed into an ASR model 360.

For isolated speech recognition, ASR model 400 employs a single GRU layer (512 units), followed by dropout regularization (rate 0.2), a dense layer (57 units for 57 sentence classes), and a softmax activation to produce label probabilities. Training uses categorical cross-entropy loss and early stopping. For continuous speech recognition, ASR model 500 uses a GRU encoder (512 units) and a decoder combining a dense layer with a 4-gram language model, optimized via CTC loss and decoded with beam search. Speaker identification is handled by ASR model 600, which follows a similar GRU-dropout-dense-softmax structure. The real-time decoding pipeline processes dry EEG, ear EEG, dry EMG, and audio signals through stream generation modules. After EMG artifact removal and feature extraction, EEG features are reduced in dimension, combined across channels, and concatenated with audio features. The final ASR model receives this fused input and outputs text. The method thus enables accurate, multimodal speech recognition for users with severe speech disorders, facilitating effective communication and therapy.
