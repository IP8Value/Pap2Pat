# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNICAL FIELD

The present invention relates to an electrical steel sheet, particularly to a silicon-gradient electrical steel sheet having a controlled distribution of silicon concentration in the thickness direction. The invention further pertains to methods for producing such a steel sheet and its application in high-frequency electromagnetic devices such as reactors, transformers, and inductors, where reduction of iron loss—particularly eddy-current loss—is critical for performance and efficiency.

## BACKGROUND

Iron loss in electrical steel sheets is a major source of energy dissipation in electromagnetic devices operating under alternating current (AC) excitation. This loss comprises two primary components: hysteresis loss and eddy-current loss. Hysteresis loss arises from the irreversible movement of magnetic domain walls during magnetization cycles, while eddy-current loss results from circulating currents induced by time-varying magnetic flux within the conductive material. Both losses increase with frequency, but eddy-current loss exhibits a quadratic dependence on frequency, making it especially problematic at high frequencies.

Conventional non-oriented electrical steel sheets typically contain 1.0% to 3.5% silicon by mass. Silicon increases the electrical resistivity of the steel, thereby reducing eddy-current loss. However, increasing silicon content beyond approximately 4.0% severely compromises ductility, rendering the material brittle and unsuitable for cold rolling into thin sheets—a key requirement for high-frequency applications where thinner laminations mitigate eddy currents.

To address this, ultrathin electrical steel sheets (e.g., 0.03–0.1 mm thick) have been developed, but their production is costly and mechanically challenging. High-silicon steel sheets containing up to 6.5% Si offer excellent magnetic properties, including near-zero magnetostriction and high resistivity, but are only producible via chemical vapor deposition (CVD) siliconizing processes, as disclosed in Japanese Examined Patent Application Publication No. 6-45881 and Japanese Examined Patent Application Publication No. 5-49744. These methods enable the formation of 6.5% Si layers but suffer from high cost, limited scalability, and brittleness.

Alternative core materials such as dust cores and Mn—Zn ferrites have been employed for high-frequency applications. Dust cores, composed of insulated iron particles, exhibit high resistivity but suffer from low permeability and high hysteresis loss. Mn—Zn ferrites offer high resistivity and low eddy-current loss but have low saturation magnetization, limiting their use in high-power applications.

Japanese Unexamined Patent Application Publication No. 2005-240185 describes a method for producing gradient silicon steel, while Japanese Patent No. 5533801 and Japanese Patent No. 5648335 disclose techniques for controlling silicon diffusion to improve magnetic properties. Japanese Unexamined Patent Application Publication No. 2015-61941 and Japanese Patent Nos. 5655295 and 5644680 propose microstructural and compositional optimizations. However, these approaches do not adequately address the interplay between silicon concentration gradients, internal stress distribution, and crystal grain morphology in governing high-frequency iron loss.

A critical limitation of known techniques is the insufficient control over specific resistance through alloying without degrading mechanical workability. While elements such as aluminum and phosphorus can increase resistivity, their addition is constrained by adverse effects on magnetic properties or manufacturability. Moreover, design considerations for high-frequency cores require not only high resistivity but also tailored magnetic anisotropy and stress states to minimize loss under operational conditions.

Japanese Unexamined Patent Application Publication No. 2000-328226 highlights challenges in siliconizing processes, including non-uniform diffusion and surface defects. Despite advances, existing silicon-gradient steel sheets lack optimized structural parameters—such as crystal grain aspect ratio, layer thickness, and stress distribution—that collectively govern iron loss behavior. Consequently, there remains a need for an electrical steel sheet that achieves low iron loss at high frequencies through a synergistic combination of compositional gradient, microstructural control, and engineered internal stress, without compromising manufacturability or mechanical integrity.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides an electrical steel sheet comprising a surface part, a boundary part, and an inner part arranged in the thickness direction. The surface part contains a higher average silicon concentration than the inner part, creating a silicon concentration gradient. This gradient induces a specific internal stress distribution: tensile stress in the surface part and compressive stress in the inner part, both oriented parallel to the sheet surface. The crystal grains in the surface part exhibit an average aspect ratio—defined as the ratio of the dimension parallel to the surface to the dimension perpendicular to the surface (depth direction)—of 0.7 or more and 4.0 or less. This controlled morphology, combined with the stress state and silicon distribution, reduces eddy-current loss at high frequencies while maintaining acceptable hysteresis characteristics.

The invention further includes a method of producing the electrical steel sheet, which involves selecting a base steel with a defined composition, subjecting it to a siliconizing process such as CVD, and performing a diffusion treatment to establish the desired silicon concentration profile. The resulting steel sheet is particularly suited for high-frequency electromagnetic applications, including reactors and transformers operating at 10 kHz or higher, where low iron loss and stable magnetic performance under stress are essential.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention encompasses electrical steel sheets and methods for their production, specifically engineered to minimize iron loss in high-frequency applications. Central to the invention is a Si-gradient steel sheet structure wherein silicon concentration varies continuously or stepwise from the surface toward the center. This structure comprises three distinct regions: a surface part enriched in silicon, an inner part with lower silicon content, and a boundary part connecting them. The silicon concentration difference between the surface and inner parts generates internal stresses due to lattice mismatch—tensile stress in the high-silicon surface layer and compressive stress in the low-silicon inner layer.

Samples were prepared with varying crystal grain morphologies to evaluate the influence of microstructure on magnetic properties. Measurements revealed that the average aspect ratio of crystal grains in the surface part critically affects iron loss. Specifically, when the aspect ratio falls within the range of 0.7 to 4.0, eddy-current loss is minimized. Grains that are too elongated (aspect ratio >4.0) or too equiaxed (aspect ratio <0.7) lead to increased loss due to unfavorable flux path alignment and enhanced domain wall pinning.

The basic structure of conventional steel sheets lacks this optimized grain morphology and stress distribution, resulting in higher losses at elevated frequencies. The importance of the silicon concentration distribution cannot be overstated: it not only increases resistivity in the surface region but also tailors the magnetic anisotropy through stress-induced effects. The boundary part, characterized by a sharp silicon concentration gradient, plays a pivotal role in confining magnetic flux to the high-resistivity surface layer, thereby reducing eddy currents.

Cracking and chipping in crystal grains, often observed in high-silicon steels, are mitigated in the present invention by limiting the inner part’s silicon content and controlling grain shape. The effect of aspect ratio on iron loss is non-linear; optimal values balance domain wall mobility and flux confinement.

The average aspect ratio is specified as 0.7 to 4.0. The texture of the surface and inner parts may differ, with the surface favoring orientations that enhance permeability under tensile stress. The surface part thickness is preferably 10% to 40% of the total sheet thickness. Thinner surface layers inadequately shield the inner core from flux penetration, while thicker layers reduce mechanical strength and increase hysteresis loss. This thickness directly influences eddy-current loss: an optimal range ensures sufficient flux confinement without excessive material usage.

The average Si concentration in the surface part is 2.5% to 6.5% by mass, providing high resistivity and low magnetostriction. The inner part contains 2.0% Si or less, preserving ductility and enabling rolling. The Si concentration gap—the difference between surface and inner concentrations—in the boundary part is at least 0.4%, which is necessary to establish the stress gradient that drives flux toward the surface.

Stress distribution is precisely controlled: the surface part exhibits tensile stress of 50 to 200 MPa parallel to the surface, while the inner part shows compressive stress of 50 to 200 MPa in the same direction. This stress state enhances surface permeability and suppresses inner layer magnetization, reducing eddy currents. The total steel sheet thickness is 0.03 to 0.5 mm, balancing loss reduction against production costs; thinner sheets reduce eddy currents but increase rolling difficulty and risk of cracking in the shear plane.

The production method begins with a base steel having a composition limited to ensure soft magnetic properties. Carbon concentration is critical: it must be 0.020% or less, as higher levels form carbides that impede domain wall motion and increase hysteresis loss. The preferred C range is ≤0.005%. Silicon in the base material is 0.15% to 2.0%, ensuring adequate initial resistivity without excessive brittleness. Manganese (0.1–2.0%) improves hot workability and deoxidation but excess amounts degrade magnetic properties. Phosphorus (≤0.1%) and sulfur (≤0.01%) are minimized to avoid embrittlement and inclusion formation. Aluminum (0.1–3.0%) boosts resistivity but above 3.0% harms ductility. Nitrogen (≤0.005%) is restricted to prevent nitride precipitation.

The siliconizing process, typically CVD, deposits silicon onto the surface, followed by diffusion treatment to establish the gradient. This yields a steel sheet with superior high-frequency performance, as demonstrated in the examples.

### Average Aspect Ratio of Crystal Grains Included in Surface Part: Ratio of Dimension of Crystal Grains in Parallel-to-Surface Direction to Dimension of Crystal Grains in Perpendicular-to-Surface Direction (Depth Direction) is 0.7 or More and 4.0 or Less

The average aspect ratio of crystal grains in the surface part is a decisive factor in determining iron loss characteristics. An aspect ratio below 0.7 indicates overly equiaxed grains, which promote multidirectional domain wall motion and increase hysteresis loss. Conversely, ratios exceeding 4.0 result in highly elongated grains that may align unfavorably with the magnetic field, enhancing eddy-current paths. The specified range of 0.7 to 4.0 ensures that grains are sufficiently aligned to facilitate easy magnetization along the sheet plane while maintaining structural integrity. This morphology, combined with the texture of the surface and inner parts—where the surface may exhibit a <100> fiber texture favorable under tensile stress—optimizes the trade-off between permeability and loss.

### Thickness of Surface Part: 10% to 40% of Thickness of Steel Sheet (Preferable Condition)

The thickness of the surface part is set to 10% to 40% of the total sheet thickness to maximize eddy-current loss reduction. Below 10%, the high-resistivity layer is too thin to effectively confine magnetic flux, allowing significant penetration into the low-resistivity inner core. Above 40%, the mechanical properties deteriorate, and hysteresis loss increases due to the larger volume of high-silicon material. Within this range, the surface layer acts as an effective electromagnetic shield, leveraging the skin effect at high frequencies to localize flux and minimize circulating currents.

### Average Si Concentration in Surface Part: 2.5% to 6.5% (Preferable Condition)

The average silicon concentration in the surface part is maintained between 2.5% and 6.5% by mass. This range ensures high electrical resistivity (up to ~80 μΩ·cm at 6.5% Si) and near-zero magnetostriction, which together reduce both eddy-current and hysteresis losses. Concentrations below 2.5% provide insufficient resistivity enhancement, while those above 6.5% are impractical due to extreme brittleness.

### Average Si Concentration in Inner Part: 2.0% or Less (Preferable Condition)

The inner part contains 2.0% Si or less to preserve ductility and enable cold rolling. Higher silicon levels in the core would render the sheet unworkable. Crucially, the low silicon content in the inner part ensures that, under the compressive stress induced by the surface layer, its magnetization is suppressed, thereby minimizing its contribution to eddy-current loss. This functional decoupling of the inner core from the magnetic circuit is key to the invention’s efficacy.

### Si Concentration Gap in Boundary Part: 0.4% or More (Preferable Condition)

A silicon concentration gap of at least 0.4% between the surface and inner parts in the boundary region is essential to generate the required internal stress gradient. This gap drives the lattice mismatch that produces tensile stress in the surface and compressive stress in the core. Without this minimum differential, the stress-induced magnetic anisotropy is insufficient to redirect flux toward the surface, undermining the loss-reduction mechanism.

### Surface Part: Tensile Stress of 50 to 200 MPa in Direction Parallel to Surface, Inner Part: Compressive Stress of 50 to 200 MPa in Direction Parallel to Surface (Preferable Condition)

The internal stress distribution—tensile (50–200 MPa) in the surface and compressive (50–200 MPa) in the inner part, both parallel to the surface—creates a magnetic anisotropy that favors surface magnetization. Tensile stress aligns easy magnetization axes (<100> in Fe-Si) with the sheet plane, enhancing permeability. Compressive stress in the core rotates easy axes toward the thickness direction, deterring in-plane magnetization. This dual stress state confines flux to the high-resistivity surface, directly reducing eddy-current loss.

### Thickness of Steel Sheet: 0.03 to 0.5 mm (Preferable Condition)

The total sheet thickness is 0.03 to 0.5 mm. Thinner sheets (<0.03 mm) drastically reduce eddy-current loss but are prohibitively expensive to produce and prone to cracking during handling. Thicker sheets (>0.5 mm) suffer from excessive eddy currents at high frequencies. The specified range balances performance, cost, and manufacturability, with optimal results around 0.1 mm for 10–50 kHz applications.

### C: 0.020% or Less

Carbon concentration in the base material is limited to 0.020% or less to prevent carbide precipitation, which pins domain walls and increases hysteresis loss. Preferably, C is ≤0.005% to ensure excellent soft magnetic properties.

### Si: 0.15% to 2.0%

Silicon in the base steel is 0.15% to 2.0%. Below 0.15%, resistivity is too low; above 2.0%, ductility suffers, complicating rolling. Manganese (0.1–2.0%) aids deoxidation and hot workability but excess Mn forms inclusions. Phosphorus is capped at 0.1% to avoid cold brittleness; sulfur at 0.01% to limit MnS inclusions. Aluminum (0.1–3.0%) boosts resistivity but >3.0% harms ductility. Nitrogen is ≤0.005% to prevent AlN or SiN precipitation. The production method involves CVD siliconizing followed by diffusion annealing to achieve the gradient.

## Example 1

A 0.10 mm-thick base steel sheet containing 0.5% Si, 0.3% Mn, 0.002% C, and 0.003% N was subjected to CVD siliconizing at 1000°C for 2 hours, followed by diffusion treatment at 1100°C for 1 hour in a hydrogen atmosphere. This produced a Si-gradient steel sheet with a surface Si concentration of 5.8% and inner concentration of 0.8%, with a boundary gap of 1.2%. The surface part thickness was 25% of total thickness. Samples were cut for magnetic evaluation. Iron loss at 10 kHz and 0.1 T was 0.45 W/kg, compared to 0.68 W/kg for a uniform 6.5% Si sheet. Microstructure analysis showed an average grain aspect ratio of 2.1 in the surface. The iron loss ratio (gradient/uniform) was 0.66, confirming significant reduction.

## Example 2

A 0.15 mm base sheet (1.0% Si, 0.001% C) underwent CVD at 1050°C for 1.5 hours and diffusion at 1080°C for 2 hours. Resulting sheet had 6.2% Si (surface), 0.5% Si (inner), 30% surface thickness, and aspect ratio of 3.0. Iron loss at 20 kHz, 0.1 T was 0.82 W/kg, outperforming conventional 6.5% Si steel (1.15 W/kg). Si distribution and stress state were confirmed via XRD and curvature measurement.

## Example 3

A 0.08 mm base sheet (0.3% Si, 0.004% C) was siliconized at 980°C for 3 hours and diffused at 1120°C for 0.5 hours. Final sheet: 5.0% Si (surface), 1.2% Si (inner), 20% surface thickness, aspect ratio 1.5. Iron loss at 50 kHz, 0.1 T was 1.95 W/kg vs. 2.80 W/kg for uniform 6.5% Si. Loss ratio was 0.70. All examples demonstrate consistent iron loss reduction due to optimized Si gradient, stress, and grain morphology.
