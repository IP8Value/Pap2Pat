# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

Quantum circuits represent a fundamental abstraction for the implementation of quantum algorithms on physical quantum hardware. A quantum circuit is composed of a sequence of quantum gates acting on one or more qubits, where each gate corresponds to a unitary transformation. The synthesis and optimization of such circuits are critical tasks in quantum computing, as they directly influence the fidelity, runtime, and resource requirements of quantum computations. Efficient quantum circuits minimize the number of error-prone operations—particularly entangling gates such as controlled-NOT (CNOT) or controlled-Z (CZ)—which are significantly slower and less reliable than single-qubit gates in current quantum hardware platforms like superconducting circuits and trapped ions. Consequently, reducing the count of two-qubit gates is a primary objective in quantum circuit optimization.

Clifford circuits constitute a particularly important subclass of quantum circuits. These circuits are built exclusively from Hadamard (H), Phase (S), and CNOT gates, along with optional Pauli (X, Y, Z) and SWAP gates. The set of all n-qubit Clifford circuits forms the Clifford group, which is a finite subgroup of the full unitary group. Despite their restricted gate set, Clifford circuits are indispensable in numerous quantum information protocols, including quantum error correction, randomized benchmarking, shadow tomography, and state distillation. Moreover, certain quantum algorithms, such as the Bernstein-Vazirani algorithm, are entirely expressible as Clifford circuits. A key mathematical property of the Clifford group is that it forms a unitary 2-design, meaning that averaging over random Clifford operators reproduces the second-order statistical properties of the Haar measure over the full unitary group. This property enables efficient simulation and characterization of quantum noise and states without requiring exponentially costly resources.

The pursuit of fault-tolerant quantum computation further motivates the optimization of Clifford circuits. In fault-tolerant schemes, logical operations are implemented using encoded qubits, and Clifford gates often admit transversal or otherwise low-overhead implementations. However, even within this favorable regime, minimizing the number of entangling gates reduces the exposure to residual errors and improves overall circuit performance. Thus, generating short, high-fidelity Clifford circuits is not merely an academic exercise but a practical necessity for scalable quantum technologies.

Conventional approaches to quantum circuit synthesis and optimization face significant limitations when applied to Clifford circuits. General-purpose synthesis methods, such as those based on universal gate decompositions, do not exploit the algebraic structure of the Clifford group and therefore produce highly suboptimal results. While asymptotically optimal synthesis techniques exist—such as the canonical form by Aaronson and Gottesman, which achieves Θ(n²/log n) gate complexity—they often yield circuits with excessive CNOT counts in practice, especially for moderate numbers of qubits. Furthermore, exact optimization methods that guarantee minimal gate counts are computationally intractable beyond six qubits due to the exponential growth of the Clifford group size.

Template matching is a well-established heuristic technique for circuit optimization. It involves identifying subsequences of gates in a target circuit that match a prefix of a known identity template—a sequence of gates that collectively implement the identity operation—and replacing them with the inverse of the remaining suffix to reduce gate count. Although effective in some contexts, conventional template matching suffers from several drawbacks when applied to Clifford circuits. First, it treats all gates uniformly and does not leverage the unique commutation and conjugation properties of Clifford gates with Pauli operators. Second, it operates on the entire circuit without structural decomposition, making it difficult to isolate and optimize functionally distinct segments. Third, templates are typically applied in a rigid, syntactic manner that fails to account for equivalent but non-identical gate sequences arising from Pauli propagation or basis changes.

Peephole optimization offers another local optimization strategy, wherein small subcircuits spanning a few qubits are extracted, replaced with functionally equivalent but shorter implementations drawn from a precomputed database of optimal circuits, and reinserted into the larger circuit. While powerful for small-scale replacements, conventional peephole optimization is severely limited in the context of large Clifford circuits. The primary bottleneck is the requirement that the subcircuit be completely decoupled from the rest of the system; any entangling gate connecting the selected qubit subset to the remainder of the circuit prevents direct application of the technique. This constraint drastically reduces the number of applicable optimization opportunities, especially in highly entangled Clifford circuits. Additionally, the memory and computational overhead of storing and querying optimal circuits grows prohibitively fast with the number of qubits, rendering the method impractical beyond three or four qubits.

These limitations underscore the need for novel optimization techniques specifically tailored to the algebraic and structural properties of Clifford circuits. The present invention addresses these challenges by introducing a synergistic combination of partitioned template matching and symbolic peephole optimization, which together enable significant reductions in two-qubit gate count while maintaining scalability to dozens of qubits.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides a computer-implemented system and method for optimizing Clifford quantum circuits by substantially reducing the number of entangling gates, particularly CNOT gates, through a novel combination of partitioned template matching and symbolic peephole optimization. The invention leverages the unique algebraic structure of the Clifford group to decompose circuits into functionally distinct stages, apply targeted optimizations to each stage, and reintegrate the results into a globally improved circuit.

The system comprises several interconnected components executed by a classical computing device. A partition component receives a suboptimal Clifford circuit and partitions it into three sequential stages: a computation stage containing only H, S, CNOT, and CZ gates; a SWAP stage containing only SWAP gates; and a Pauli stage containing only Pauli X, Y, and Z gates. This partitioning is achieved by systematically pushing Pauli gates to the end of the circuit using Pauli-push equations and factoring out SWAP gates via qubit relabeling, thereby isolating the core computational content for focused optimization.

A template component applies a library of Clifford-specific identity templates to the computation stage. These templates are derived from known algebraic identities among Clifford gates and are applied in both forward and backward directions to replace matched subsequences with shorter equivalents. The template matching process is enhanced by preprocessing steps that convert CNOT gates to CZ gates (via Hadamard conjugation) to unify the two-qubit gate representation and by applying Hadamard and Phase pushing rules to increase the density of optimizable patterns.

A floating component implements a floating-gates technique that pushes blocking single-qubit gates (e.g., H or S) out of the range of template matching, thereby exposing additional optimization opportunities. This is achieved by applying localized templates that commute these gates past two-qubit gates, effectively “floating” them to positions where they do not interfere with pattern recognition.

A SWAP component optimizes the SWAP stage by recognizing that a SWAP gate can be implemented at the effective cost of a single entangling gate when aligned with a neighboring CNOT or CZ gate. By resynthesizing the SWAP stage to maximize such alignments, the total two-qubit gate count is reduced.

A symbolic component introduces symbolic Pauli gates (SPGs) to enable peephole optimization across entangled boundaries. When a subcircuit spans a small subset of qubits A but includes entangling gates connecting A to its complement, the symbolic component replaces each such entangling gate with a symbolic Pauli gate acting on A, parameterized by a binary variable representing the control state. This transforms the original subcircuit into a Clifford-plus-SPG circuit that is fully contained within A and amenable to exhaustive optimization.

A peephole component then applies dynamic programming to optimize the Clifford-plus-SPG subcircuit. Using a precomputed cost table for all Clifford operators on |A| qubits (where |A| is typically 2 or 3), the component minimizes a cost function that accounts for both CNOT gates and SPGs, subject to constraints on the temporal order of symbolic variables and phase preservation. The optimized subcircuit is then lifted back to the full circuit by reinserting the entangling gates and associated phase corrections.

In an alternative embodiment, the invention is implemented as a method that first compiles a given Clifford unitary into a circuit using a greedy or bidirectional synthesizer, then iteratively applies the above optimization components until convergence or a time limit is reached. The method may also be embodied as a computer program product comprising instructions stored on a non-transitory computer-readable medium that, when executed, cause a processor to perform the disclosed steps.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The following description sets forth specific details to provide a thorough understanding of the present invention. However, it will be apparent to those skilled in the art that the invention may be practiced without these specific details, and that certain features described herein may be combined or omitted in alternative embodiments. The scope of the invention is defined solely by the appended claims.

Quantum circuits are sequences of quantum gates acting on qubits, where each gate corresponds to a unitary operator. The present invention focuses on Clifford circuits, which are composed of Hadamard (H), Phase (S), controlled-NOT (CNOT), and optionally Pauli (X, Y, Z) and SWAP gates. Clifford circuits are of paramount importance in quantum computing due to their role in error correction, benchmarking, and simulation.

Clifford gates possess the defining property that they map tensor products of Pauli matrices to other tensor products of Pauli matrices under conjugation. This property enables the systematic "pushing" of Pauli gates through Clifford circuits using the Pauli-push equations: HX = ZH, HY = -YH, HZ = XH, SX = YS, SY = -XS, SZ = ZS, and CNOT(X⊗I) = (X⊗X)CNOT, among others. These equations allow Pauli gates to be relocated to the end of the circuit without altering the overall unitary, up to a global phase.

Template matching is a circuit optimization technique that uses identity templates—sequences of gates that compose to the identity—to replace longer subsequences with shorter ones. A template of size m is a sequence T = G₁G₂…Gₘ such that T = I. If a subsequence of p consecutive gates in the target circuit matches a contiguous subsequence of T, and p > m/2, then replacing it with the inverse of the remaining m−p gates reduces the total gate count. Templates can be applied in both forward and backward directions, and multiple applications are independent if they operate on disjoint gate sets.

The circuit optimization procedure begins by partitioning the input Clifford circuit into three stages: computation, SWAP, and Pauli. This is achieved by pushing all Pauli gates to the end using the Pauli-push equations and factoring out all SWAP gates by relabeling qubit indices. The resulting computation stage contains only H, S, CNOT, and CZ gates and represents the essential computational content of the circuit.

Conventional template matching is limited when applied to full Clifford circuits because Pauli and SWAP gates obscure optimization opportunities in the computation stage. Similarly, conventional peephole optimization cannot handle subcircuits that are entangled with the rest of the system, as it requires complete decoupling. To overcome these limitations, the present invention introduces partitioned template matching and symbolic peephole optimization.

Partitioned template matching applies templates only to the computation stage, after Pauli and SWAP gates have been removed. This increases the density of optimizable patterns and avoids interference from non-Clifford-like gates. Symbolic peephole optimization extends the applicability of peephole methods to entangled subcircuits by introducing symbolic Pauli gates (SPGs). When a CNOT gate connects a selected qubit subset A to its complement B, it is rewritten as a symbolic Pauli gate on A, parameterized by a binary variable v ∈ {0,1} representing the control state. The resulting Clifford-plus-SPG subcircuit on A can then be optimized using dynamic programming.

The partitioning of the Clifford circuit is performed by iteratively applying Pauli-push equations to move all Pauli gates to the end and by extracting SWAP gates into a separate stage via qubit permutation. Template matching is then applied to the computation stage using a library of Clifford-specific templates, such as those implementing H-pushing (H-CNOT = CNOT-H-H) or S-pushing identities. The floating-gates technique is used to push blocking H or S gates out of template matching ranges by applying localized templates that commute them past two-qubit gates.

SWAP optimization exploits the equivalence SWAP = CNOT₁₂·CNOT₂₁·CNOT₁₂, which shows that a SWAP can be implemented with three CNOTs. However, if a SWAP is adjacent to a CNOT sharing the same qubits, the combined sequence can often be simplified to fewer than four CNOTs, effectively reducing the SWAP cost to one entangling gate. The SWAP component resynthesizes the SWAP stage to maximize such simplifications.

Symbolic peephole optimization begins by selecting a small subset A of qubits (typically |A|=2 or 3). Entangling CNOT gates between A and its complement B are identified and grouped by control qubit. Each group is replaced by a symbolic Pauli gate (Xᵥ, Yᵥ, or Zᵥ) on the target qubit in A, where v is a symbolic variable. The resulting subcircuit U_A(v) is a Clifford-plus-SPG circuit whose cost is defined as the number of CNOTs plus the number of SPGs. This cost is minimized using dynamic programming over the Clifford group on |A| qubits, respecting the temporal order of symbolic variables and preserving overall phase modulo factors of iᵛ or (−1)ᵛ.

The sequential combination of partitioned template matching and symbolic peephole optimization yields substantial reductions in two-qubit gate count. The electronic generation of optimized Clifford circuits is performed by a classical computer that executes the above steps and outputs a gate sequence suitable for execution on quantum hardware. This solves the technical problem of excessive entangling gate counts in synthesized Clifford circuits, which directly impacts quantum computation fidelity and runtime.

Optimized Clifford circuits generated by the invention can be used to control real-world quantum devices, such as superconducting qubit processors or trapped ion systems, by compiling the output gate sequence into native hardware instructions. The reduction in CNOT count translates directly into shorter execution times and lower error rates.

The Clifford optimization system includes a partition component, template component, floating component, SWAP component, symbolic component, and peephole component, all executed iteratively until no further improvement is observed or a time limit is reached. The computer-implemented method receives a suboptimal Clifford circuit, partitions it, applies template matching to the computation stage, applies floating-gates and SWAP optimizations, selects subcircuits for peephole optimization, rewrites straddling gates as SPGs, performs peephole optimization, and outputs the optimized circuit.

In an alternative workflow, the method receives a Clifford unitary, compiles it using a greedy synthesizer, then applies the same optimization pipeline. Experimental results demonstrate that the invention recovers optimal CNOT counts for 97.9% of random 6-qubit Clifford unitaries and reduces CNOT counts by 64.7% on average for Hamiltonian evolution circuits compared to the Aaronson-Gottesman canonical form.

The invention is implemented in a computing environment comprising processors, memory, storage media, and input/output devices, and may be deployed in cloud computing infrastructures supporting distributed execution. The system, method, and computer program product embody the novel algorithms for partitioned template matching and symbolic peephole optimization, which together represent a significant advancement in the field of quantum circuit optimization.
