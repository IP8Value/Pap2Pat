## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

Lactic acidemia (LA), defined as an abnormal accumulation of lactate in the blood, is a frequent and ominous complication of septic shock (SS), strongly associated with increased morbidity and mortality. The pathophysiology of LA in SS has long been attributed to tissue hypoperfusion and anaerobic metabolism; however, emerging evidence suggests that multiple mechanisms—including accelerated aerobic glycolysis, impaired hepatic clearance, and mitochondrial dysfunction—contribute to elevated lactate levels even in the presence of adequate oxygen delivery. Lactate is produced primarily in skeletal muscle, red blood cells, and other tissues during glycolysis and is normally cleared by the liver through conversion to pyruvate, followed by oxidation in the mitochondria or gluconeogenesis. This metabolic clearance is critically dependent on functional hepatic mitochondria and intact transport mechanisms.

A key component of lactate handling is the monocarboxylate transporter (MCT), particularly MCT1, which facilitates the bidirectional, pH-dependent movement of lactate across hepatocyte membranes. Disruption of this transport system or downstream mitochondrial metabolism can impair lactate clearance, leading to systemic accumulation. Mitochondrial dysfunction in sepsis has been variably reported in the literature, with some studies suggesting profound impairment in oxidative phosphorylation while others report preserved function. These discrepancies may stem from differences in experimental models, timing of assessment, or methodologies used to evaluate mitochondrial respiration. Traditional assays often focus on coupled respiration states or enzymatic activity of individual electron transport chain complexes, potentially overlooking more integrative measures of mitochondrial capacity.

The recent advent of the Seahorse XF24 extracellular flux analyzer provides a dynamic, real-time platform to assess mitochondrial oxygen consumption rates (OCR) under multiple metabolic conditions, including basal respiration, ADP-stimulated (coupled) respiration, oligomycin-inhibited state, FCCP-induced maximal (uncoupled) respiration, and rotenone/antimycin A–inhibited non-mitochondrial respiration. This technology enables comprehensive evaluation of mitochondrial bioenergetic health, including spare respiratory capacity—the difference between maximal and basal OCR—which reflects the ability of mitochondria to respond to increased energy demands. Prior to the present invention, this advanced methodology had not been applied to investigate hepatic mitochondrial function in the context of septic shock, leaving critical gaps in understanding the relationship between mitochondrial performance and lactic acidemia.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides novel methods for the treatment of septic shock and associated lactic acidemia using gentisic acid or a pharmaceutically acceptable salt thereof, such as gentisic acid sodium salt (GSS). In particular, the invention discloses that administration of gentisic acid effectively increases mean arterial pressure (MAP), improves hepatic mitochondrial function—including basal, coupled, and maximal respiration—and significantly reduces blood lactate concentrations in subjects suffering from septic shock. Unlike conventional vasopressors such as norepinephrine (NE), which restore hemodynamic stability but fail to ameliorate lactic acidemia despite improving mitochondrial maximal respiration, gentisic acid uniquely addresses both cardiovascular collapse and metabolic derangement.

The invention further encompasses a medicament comprising gentisic acid or a pharmaceutically acceptable salt thereof for use in the treatment of septic shock or lactic acidemia. The medicament may be formulated for intravenous administration and is effective in reducing serum lactate levels, elevating serum pH, and enhancing mitochondrial oxidative capacity without inducing myocardial injury, as evidenced by stable troponin levels. Additionally, the invention includes methods of preparing such a medicament by combining gentisic acid with a pharmaceutically acceptable carrier, diluent, or excipient, typically in an aqueous solution such as 5% dextrose in water (D5W), suitable for continuous infusion.

## DESCRIPTION OF THE PREFERRED EMBODIMENTS

As used herein, “septic shock” refers to a subset of sepsis in which underlying circulatory and cellular/metabolic abnormalities are profound enough to substantially increase mortality. “Lactic acidemia” denotes a pathological elevation of blood lactate concentration, typically above 2 mmol/L, often accompanied by metabolic acidosis. “Gentisic acid” refers to 2,5-dihydroxybenzoic acid, and “gentisic acid sodium salt” (GSS) is its sodium salt form, which exhibits enhanced solubility and bioavailability for parenteral administration. An “effective amount” means a dose sufficient to produce a clinically meaningful improvement in hemodynamics, mitochondrial function, or lactate clearance without unacceptable toxicity.

In preferred embodiments, the invention utilizes a canine model of bacteremic septic shock induced by continuous intravenous infusion of *Pseudomonas aeruginosa* (ATCC 27853) at approximately 10¹⁰ colony-forming units per hour. This model reliably reproduces human septic shock features, including progressive hypotension, hyperlactatemia, systemic inflammation, and hepatic mitochondrial dysfunction. Using this model, the inventors demonstrated a significant association between septic shock and hepatic mitochondrial depression, particularly a reduction in maximal OCR and spare respiratory capacity, confirming mitochondrial impairment as a feature of prolonged sepsis.

Administration of norepinephrine (NE) reversed systemic hypotension and restored maximal mitochondrial respiration to near-normal levels, yet failed to reduce blood lactate, indicating that mitochondrial recovery alone is insufficient to correct lactic acidemia. In contrast, treatment with GSS not only normalized MAP but also significantly enhanced coupled and basal respiration, reduced serum lactate, and improved systemic pH. Notably, MCT1 protein expression in the liver did not differ among experimental groups, suggesting that altered lactate transport is not the primary mechanism of LA in this model.

Gentisic acid sodium salt was administered intravenously at an average rate of 0.55 ± 0.29 mg/kg/min, achieving hemodynamic and metabolic benefits without elevating cardiac troponin, unlike NE-treated animals which showed evidence of myocardial stress. The effective amount of gentisic acid is determined based on clinical parameters including MAP stabilization, lactate reduction, and absence of organ toxicity. Individuals at risk of developing sepsis—such as those with severe infection, immunosuppression, or recent surgery—may be prophylactically or therapeutically treated with gentisic acid upon diagnosis of septic shock or lactic acidemia.

The invention provides a method of treating an individual with septic shock comprising administering an effective amount of gentisic acid or a pharmaceutically acceptable salt thereof, resulting in increased MAP, improved mitochondrial oxygen consumption, and decreased blood lactate. Similarly, a method for treating lactic acidemia comprises administering gentisic acid to reduce lactate levels and elevate serum pH. The medicament is preferably prepared by dissolving GSS in D5W for continuous infusion, ensuring stability and compatibility.

Mechanistically, GSS likely exerts its effects through potent antioxidant activity conferred by its two hydroxyl groups on the benzoic acid ring. This may protect mitochondrial components from oxidative damage, enhance adenine nucleotide translocase activity, reduce ATP-mediated feedback inhibition of respiration, and thereby promote efficient lactate clearance—potentially via enhanced gluconeogenesis or reduced glycolytic flux. Unlike NE, which acts primarily as an α-adrenergic agonist to increase vascular tone, GSS appears to target both hemodynamic and metabolic pathways, offering a dual therapeutic advantage. Thus, gentisic acid represents a novel, multifunctional therapy for the integrated management of cardiovascular and metabolic failure in septic shock.

### RESULTS

In the canine model, the time to reach septic shock—defined as MAP falling to 60–65 mmHg—was consistent across septic groups, averaging 4.1 to 4.9 hours, with no significant differences in baseline body weights (20.3–23.6 kg). Total fluid infused was higher in septic groups (~6 L) versus non-septic controls (~3.5 L), attributable to third-spacing and ascites formation. All animals survived to the final measurement point. At baseline, MAP was ~100 mmHg in all groups. During septic shock, MAP dropped to ~60 mmHg in septic groups but remained stable in non-septic controls. Both NE (0.51 μg/kg/min) and GSS (0.55 mg/kg/min) rapidly restored MAP to baseline, with no effect in non-septic GSS animals.

Systemic vascular resistance (SVR) declined sharply during shock due to vasodilation; both treatments trended toward SVR recovery, though not statistically significant. Cardiac output (CO) remained largely stable, with a transient increase in the GSS septic group during shock. Heart rate rose to ~120 bpm in septic groups; GSS significantly lowered HR post-treatment compared to NE and control septic groups. Troponin increased only in the NE group, suggesting myocardial strain. Pulmonary wedge pressure (Pwp) varied slightly but remained within physiological ranges, with no major impact on ventricular filling.

Blood lactate progressively declined in non-septic groups but rose significantly in septic control and NE groups by 5 hours post-shock. In contrast, the GSS septic group showed no lactate increase and a trend toward reduction. Oxygen delivery (DO₂) was lowest in septic controls; NE increased DO₂ primarily via elevated hemoglobin, not CO. Serum pH was higher in the GSS group, indicating mitigation of acidosis.

Hepatic MCT1 protein levels were unchanged across all groups, ruling out transporter dysregulation as a cause of LA. Mitochondrial OCR analysis revealed that septic shock reduced maximal respiration and spare capacity. NE restored maximal OCR but not coupled respiration. GSS uniquely enhanced basal, coupled, and maximal respiration, with coupled OCR nearly matching maximal levels—indicating highly efficient oxidative phosphorylation. Complex IV activity was reduced in septic controls but not significantly altered by treatments, underscoring the superior sensitivity of functional OCR assays over isolated enzyme measurements.

A supplemental study with gallic acid—a structural analog—showed no beneficial effects on mitochondrial function or lactate, highlighting the specificity of GSS’s action, likely due to its distinct pKa and redox properties. Collectively, these results demonstrate that GSS corrects both hemodynamic instability and metabolic dysfunction in septic shock, whereas NE corrects only the former.

## METHODS

### Animal Preparation

Experiments utilized a well-established canine bacteremic model of septic shock. Adult dogs (18–26 kg) were anesthetized with sufentanil citrate and midazolam, mechanically ventilated with 100% oxygen to maintain PaO₂ ~400 mmHg, and monitored per NIH guidelines. Vascular catheters were inserted into the right femoral artery for MAP measurement and right jugular vein for Swan-Ganz catheter placement to assess pulmonary pressures, cardiac output (via thermodilution), and mixed venous blood sampling. A left jugular catheter enabled bacterial infusion and blood draws. Hemodynamic parameters—MAP, CO, SVR, Pwp, heart rate—were continuously recorded. Arterial and mixed venous blood gases, lactate, troponin, liver enzymes, and hematocrit were analyzed at defined intervals. Normal saline was infused to maintain Pwp near baseline (~10 mmHg).

### Experimental Protocols

Five groups (n = 5–16 each) were studied: (1) septic control, (2) non-septic control, (3) NE-treated septic, (4) GSS-treated septic, and (5) GSS-treated non-septic. Baseline measurements were taken after 1 hour of stability. Septic groups received continuous *P. aeruginosa* infusion until MAP fell to 60–65 mmHg (shock condition). NE or GSS was then infused to restore MAP to baseline; septic controls received D5W vehicle. Non-septic groups underwent sham procedures with saline infusion and matched timing. Post-treatment measurements occurred at 3 and 5 hours after shock/sham.

### Mitochondrial Preparation

At study end, animals were euthanized, and liver samples were harvested. Mitochondria were isolated via differential centrifugation in MSHE buffer (70 mM sucrose, 210 mM mannitol, 5 mM HEPES, 1 mM EGTA, fatty acid-free BSA). Tissue was homogenized, centrifuged thrice, and the final pellet resuspended in MAS buffer. Protein concentration was quantified, and ~20 μg mitochondrial protein was plated per well in Seahorse XF24 plates.

### Mitochondria Measurement of Oxygen Consumption Rates (OCR)

OCR was measured using the Seahorse XF24 analyzer. Sequential injections assessed: (1) basal OCR with pyruvate/malate; (2) coupled OCR after ADP (4 mM); (3) oligomycin-inhibited state (2 μM); (4) maximal OCR with FCCP (4 μM); and (5) non-mitochondrial respiration with rotenone/antimycin A (2 μM each). Respiratory control ratios and spare capacity (maximal minus basal OCR) were calculated from duplicate readings.

### To Determine Whether MCT1 Protein is Altered in Septic Shock

Liver tissue was homogenized, and MCT1 protein levels were quantified via Western blot or ELISA to assess transporter expression changes.

### Gallic Acid Septic Group

A supplementary cohort received gallic acid instead of GSS under identical septic conditions to evaluate structural specificity.

### Statistics

Data were analyzed by two-way ANOVA (split-plot design) with factors for group and time, followed by Student-Newman-Keuls post-hoc testing. One-way ANOVA was used for single-timepoint comparisons. Significance was set at *P* < 0.05. Results are expressed as mean ± SD.
