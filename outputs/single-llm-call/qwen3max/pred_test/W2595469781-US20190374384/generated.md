# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

Glaucoma is a progressive optic neuropathy characterized by the degeneration of retinal ganglion cells and their axons, ultimately leading to irreversible vision loss and blindness. It represents the leading cause of irreversible blindness worldwide, affecting millions of individuals across diverse populations. The primary modifiable risk factor for glaucoma progression is elevated intraocular pressure (IOP), which results from an imbalance between the production and outflow of aqueous humor (AH) within the anterior chamber of the eye. Consequently, therapeutic strategies for glaucoma have historically focused on lowering IOP through pharmacological, laser-based, or surgical interventions. When medical therapy with topical hypotensive agents and laser trabeculoplasty fail to achieve adequate IOP control, surgical intervention becomes necessary to create alternative pathways for AH drainage and thereby reduce IOP to a level that halts or slows disease progression.

Current surgical treatment options for glaucoma include trabeculectomy, glaucoma drainage implants (GDIs), and minimally invasive glaucoma surgeries (MIGS). Trabeculectomy, which involves creating a full-thickness sclerostomy covered by a partial-thickness scleral flap to allow controlled egress of AH into the subconjunctival space, remains the gold standard for adult primary open-angle glaucoma. However, its efficacy is significantly limited in pediatric populations, neovascular glaucoma, uveitic glaucoma, and other secondary glaucomas due to aggressive wound healing responses that lead to early bleb failure. Glaucoma drainage implants, such as the Ahmed Glaucoma Valve or Baerveldt implant, were developed to address these challenging cases by diverting AH through a tube to an equatorial plate sutured onto the episcleral surface, where fluid is absorbed by surrounding tissues. Despite their widespread use, long-term success of GDIs is frequently compromised by fibrotic encapsulation around the endplate, forming a dense collagenous capsule that impedes fluid diffusion and elevates IOP over time.

Minimally invasive glaucoma surgeries represent a newer class of procedures designed to enhance AH outflow with improved safety profiles compared to traditional filtration surgeries. These include ab interno trabeculotomy, suprachoroidal shunts, and microstents placed in Schlemm’s canal or the trabecular meshwork. While MIGS devices offer reduced complication rates and faster recovery, their IOP-lowering capacity is generally modest and primarily suited for mild-to-moderate glaucoma. A critical limitation shared across all three surgical modalities—trabeculectomy, GDIs, and MIGS—is the body’s natural wound-healing response, which manifests as fibrosis at the site of surgical intervention. In trabeculectomy, this occurs at the episcleral-conjunctival interface; in GDIs, it surrounds the drainage plate; and in MIGS, it may occlude microstent lumens or adjacent outflow pathways. This fibrotic reaction, driven by activation of fibroblasts, deposition of extracellular matrix, and chronic inflammation, remains the principal cause of surgical failure.

The limitations of existing devices underscore a pressing need for innovative implant designs that can sustain long-term aqueous outflow while evading or minimizing the host fibrotic response. Conventional solid-plate GDIs, despite material refinements such as silicone flexibility, still present a significant mechanical and geometric mismatch with native ocular tissues, provoking persistent foreign body reactions. Similarly, current MIGS devices lack mechanisms to prevent cellular ingrowth or scarring around their structures. Therefore, there exists a clear and unmet clinical demand for a next-generation glaucoma drainage device that integrates biocompatible architecture, dynamic fluid handling, and resistance to fibrotic encapsulation—features that collectively address the root causes of surgical failure in glaucoma management.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a novel glaucoma drainage implant device configured to manage intraocular pressure by facilitating controlled outflow of aqueous humor while substantially reducing fibrotic encapsulation and inflammatory responses. Central to the invention is a microfluidic meshwork structure that replaces the conventional solid drainage plate found in traditional glaucoma drainage implants. This meshwork comprises a network of longitudinal microchannels interconnected by lateral microchannels, forming a lattice-like architecture that enables multidirectional fluid transport. The longitudinal microchannels extend along the primary axis of fluid flow from an inlet interface, while the lateral microchannels provide cross-connectivity, enhancing redundancy and uniform distribution of aqueous humor across the implant surface.

Fluid outlets are distributed throughout the meshwork, allowing aqueous humor to diffuse radially into the surrounding periocular tissues. These outlets are strategically positioned to optimize drainage kinetics and minimize localized pressure gradients that could trigger tissue irritation. In certain embodiments, the invention is realized as a three-dimensional (3D) mesh structure, wherein multiple layers of microchannel networks are stacked or interwoven to increase surface area and fluid-handling capacity without compromising flexibility. The 3D mesh embodiment maintains high porosity and compliance, enabling it to conform intimately to the curved and dynamic surface of the sclera.

The implant further includes an interface region designed to couple with a standard glaucoma drainage tube, ensuring seamless integration with existing surgical workflows. This interface transitions fluid from the lumen of the tube into the distributing section of the microfluidic meshwork. In advanced configurations, the device incorporates a pressure-sensitive valve mechanism that regulates outflow based on intraocular pressure, preventing hypotony during periods of low IOP while permitting drainage when pressure exceeds a predetermined threshold.

Critically, the microchannels are both porous and permeable, constructed from biocompatible materials with wall thicknesses and diameters engineered to mimic cellular dimensions. This design minimizes mechanical mismatch with host tissue and preserves local vascular and cellular networks, thereby suppressing chronic foreign body reactions. The method of managing intraocular pressure according to the invention involves surgically implanting the microfluidic meshwork in the episcleral space, connecting it to the anterior chamber via a drainage tube, and leveraging its biomimetic architecture to sustain long-term aqueous outflow with minimal fibrosis.

### Glaucoma Drainage Meshwork

The present invention introduces a new class of glaucoma surgical devices featuring a microfluidic meshwork engineered for superior biocompatibility and sustained aqueous humor drainage. Unlike conventional solid-plate implants, the disclosed mesh implants consist of a highly flexible and porous lattice structure that mimics the mechanical and topological properties of native ocular tissue. Referring to mesh 10, the core architecture comprises longitudinal microchannels that run parallel to the direction of primary fluid flow, interconnected by lateral microchannels oriented perpendicularly or obliquely to provide structural integrity and fluid redistribution. The geometry of these microchannels is precisely controlled, with wall thicknesses typically ranging from 2 to 6 micrometers and outer diameters of approximately 20 micrometers, yielding inner luminal diameters of about 8 micrometers—dimensions selected to support physiological flow rates (e.g., 2 μL/min at 10 mmHg) while remaining compatible with cellular-scale tissue integration.

The microchannels include porous sections formed by nanoscale perforations or inherent material porosity, enabling transmural diffusion of aqueous humor and nutrients, which supports tissue health and reduces ischemia-driven inflammation. The overall flexibility and compliance of mesh 10 are achieved through ultra-thin construction (e.g., 4 μm total thickness) and a periodic lattice design with a grid period of approximately 100 μm, allowing the mesh to bend, stretch, and conform to the scleral curvature without inducing shear stress or mechanical irritation. The lattice structure features branching nodes that terminate in fluid outlets, which serve as efflux points for aqueous humor into the subconjunctival or episcleral space. The density of microchannels and their spatial period can be tuned to modulate drainage characteristics, including flow resistance and surface area exposure.

Both two-dimensional (2D) and three-dimensional (3D) mesh constructions are contemplated. The 2D mesh offers simplicity in fabrication and deployment, ideal for MIGS applications requiring minimal tissue disruption. The 3D mesh, formed by stacking or interlacing multiple 2D layers, provides enhanced fluid capacity and isotropic drainage, beneficial for refractory glaucoma cases. A hybrid 3D mesh embodiment includes an inner region optimized for high-flow conduction and an outer region with increased porosity for tissue integration. The complete glaucoma drainage implant device integrates this meshwork with a proximal interface section that couples to a standard drainage tube, and may include a pressure-regulating mechanism such as a microvalve. In MIGS implementations, the device functions as a stent with integrated microfluidic meshwork, delivered via ab interno approaches using specialized injectors, offering advantages in safety, recovery time, and compatibility with cataract surgery.

### Methods of Making

The glaucoma drainage meshes of the invention are fabricated using precision microfabrication techniques. In one embodiment, photolithography is employed on silicon wafers coated with a nickel-releasing layer. Microchannel walls are patterned using negative photoresist SU-8, while sacrificial photoresists such as LOR 5A and AZ1505 define the luminal spaces. After patterning, the sacrificial layers are dissolved, releasing the freestanding meshwork. Alternatively, a sacrificial template (e.g., polymer fiber) is drawn into the desired lattice shape and coated with biocompatible materials such as parylene-C, silicone, or polyimide via chemical vapor deposition or dip-coating. Surface treatments—including plasma etching, hydrophilic coatings, or immobilization of anti-inflammatory agents—are applied to enhance biocompatibility. Chemical modifications, such as grafting of polyethylene glycol (PEG) or zwitterionic polymers, reduce protein adsorption and macrophage adhesion. Additionally, the mesh surface may be functionalized to absorb or elute proteins and peptides (e.g., TGF-β inhibitors, IL-10) that actively suppress fibrosis and promote regenerative healing.

### Methods of Use

The mesh implants are used in glaucoma surgery to establish a durable outflow pathway for aqueous humor. In a glaucoma drainage implant (GDI) procedure, the device is implanted in the supratemporal episcleral quadrant, with the microfluidic meshwork placed flat against the sclera approximately 6 mm posterior to the limbus. The drainage tube is inserted into the anterior chamber, and the meshwork remains unsutured or lightly anchored to preserve flexibility. In MIGS applications, the microstent-integrated mesh is delivered ab interno through a clear corneal incision using a preloaded injector, with the distal end positioned in Schlemm’s canal or the suprachoroidal space. The target site for MIGS implantation is selected based on the patient’s anatomy and glaucoma subtype. The key advantage of the MIGS delivery method lies in its minimally traumatic insertion, avoidance of conjunctival dissection, and rapid visual recovery, making it suitable for early-stage disease.

## EXPERIMENTAL EXAMPLES

### Example 1: A Novel Flexible Microfluidic Meshwork to Reduce Fibrosis in Glaucoma Surgery

This example demonstrates the feasibility of a modified glaucoma drainage implant concept wherein the conventional solid plate is replaced by a flexible microfluidic meshwork designed to suppress fibrotic encapsulation. The design was motivated by observations from neural implant research indicating that ultra-flexible, cellular-scale architectures minimize chronic foreign body reactions. The hypothesis was that a conformable, porous meshwork would reduce mechanical mismatch with ocular tissue and preserve local microarchitecture, thereby attenuating fibrosis.

Materials and methods involved fabricating 7 mm × 7 mm meshworks via photolithography on silicon wafers with a nickel release layer. Microchannels featured 20 μm outer and 8 μm inner diameters, with a 100 μm grid period and 4 μm total thickness, validated by finite element simulation to support 2 μL/min flow at 10 mmHg. Three New Zealand rabbits underwent bilateral implantation: one eye received an Ahmed Glaucoma Valve (AGV) PF7 silicone plate (control), and the contralateral eye received the microfluidic meshwork, randomized and without tube connection to isolate tissue response. Standard surgical implantation was performed under anesthesia, with conjunctival closure and postoperative care including antibiotics and tapered corticosteroids.

Animals were monitored for three months with no signs of infection, erosion, or inflammation. Histology revealed a thick fibrotic capsule (89 ± 6.6 μm) beneath AGV plates versus near-absent encapsulation (1.3 ± 0.6 μm) around meshworks (p = 0.002). Inflammatory cells were observed only in <10% of mesh areas where folding created multilayer stacks, suggesting monolayer conformation is critical for optimal biocompatibility.

These results confirm that the microfluidic meshwork significantly reduces fibrosis compared to conventional implants. The rabbit model is clinically relevant, as prior studies validate its predictive value for human fibrotic responses. Although the study excluded aqueous flow, the stark contrast in capsule formation—even without AH exposure—highlights the implant’s intrinsic role in driving fibrosis. Future work will integrate fluid dynamics, but these findings establish the foundational benefit of biomimetic mesh architecture in glaucoma surgery, supporting development of a next-generation GDI with improved long-term outcomes.
