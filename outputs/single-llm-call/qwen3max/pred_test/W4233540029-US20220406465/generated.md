# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNICAL FIELD

The present invention resides in the technical field of digital health and predictive analytics, specifically relating to computer-implemented systems and methods for detecting mental health risk in individuals diagnosed with diabetes mellitus. More particularly, the invention pertains to a machine learning–based framework that leverages passively collected behavioral, physiological, and demographic data—derived from routine interactions with diabetes management tools such as glucometers, mobile applications, web portals, and coaching services—to generate real-time predictions of mental health conditions including depression, anxiety, and related psychological distress. The invention further encompasses architectures for integrating such predictive capabilities into existing chronic disease management platforms, enabling scalable, non-intrusive, and context-aware mental health screening without requiring active user input beyond normal engagement with diabetes self-care technologies.

## BACKGROUND

Diabetes mellitus affects over 34 million individuals in the United States alone, representing a significant public health burden. Among this population, approximately 25% exhibit clinically significant depressive symptoms, while up to 40% suffer from generalized anxiety disorder. These comorbid mental health conditions are strongly associated with reduced adherence to diabetes self-management regimens—including medication compliance, dietary control, physical activity, glucose monitoring, and clinical follow-up—thereby exacerbating glycemic dysregulation and increasing the risk of severe complications such as cardiovascular disease, neuropathy, and renal failure. The economic impact is substantial, with healthcare expenditures for individuals managing both diabetes and mental illness estimated to exceed those of diabetic patients without psychiatric comorbidities by $4 to $9 billion annually.

Despite clear clinical guidelines from the American Diabetes Association and the U.S. Preventive Services Task Force recommending routine mental health screening for all diabetic patients, fewer than half of those affected receive formal diagnosis or intervention. This care gap stems from multiple systemic barriers: a national shortage of mental health professionals, limited psychiatric training among primary care providers who predominantly manage diabetic populations, and insufficient integration of validated screening tools within routine clinical workflows. Traditional assessment methods—such as paper-based questionnaires or structured clinical interviews—are often time-consuming, stigmatizing, and reliant on patient self-reporting, which may be inaccurate due to denial, lack of insight, or fluctuating symptom severity.

Emerging digital methodologies, including passive sensing and ecological momentary assessment (EMA), offer promising alternatives by enabling continuous, unobtrusive data collection within naturalistic settings. Passive sensing captures behavioral and physiological signals without explicit user effort, while EMA involves real-time, in-the-moment sampling of subjective states. Although prior research has explored smartphone-derived metrics—such as call logs, GPS mobility patterns, and accelerometer data—for mental health inference in general populations, these approaches have not been adequately adapted to the unique behavioral ecology of individuals with diabetes. Critically, no known system has harnessed data from blood glucose meters—a near-universal tool in diabetes self-management—as a source of mental health indicators, despite evidence linking glycemic variability, testing frequency, and emotional context during glucose checks to psychological well-being. There remains a pressing need for an integrated, scalable, and privacy-conscious solution that leverages existing diabetes care infrastructure to enable early, automated detection of mental health risk.

## SUMMARY

The present invention addresses the aforementioned limitations by providing a computer-implemented method and system for predicting mental health risk in individuals with diabetes through the analysis of multimodal passive sensing signals. The method improves upon current mental health detection practices by eliminating reliance on active patient reporting and instead utilizing data generated during routine engagement with diabetes management technologies. Central to the invention is the recognition that glucometer usage patterns, coaching interactions, digital platform engagement, and demographic attributes collectively encode behavioral signatures predictive of underlying psychological distress.

The invention employs passive sensing and ecological momentary assessment principles by extracting contextual and behavioral signals from devices and services already used daily by diabetic patients. Specifically, glucometer data inputs include not only blood glucose values but also temporal patterns of use (e.g., time of day, day of week), frequency and consistency of testing, responses to embedded emotional state prompts, and statistical features such as mean and standard deviation of glucose readings over defined intervals. Demographic data inputs encompass age, gender, race, ethnicity, body mass index, and smoking status—factors known to correlate with mental health vulnerability. Mental health status data inputs serve as ground truth labels derived from verified sources including filled prescriptions for psychotropic medications, mental health–related insurance claims, documented clinical assessments, and recorded interventions.

A machine learning system is trained using a supervised learning approach on a dataset comprising these inputs paired with labeled mental health outcomes. The system is validated using temporally disjoint test sets to ensure generalizability across different data sources and time periods. The model is periodically updated using new incoming data and refined through feedback loops, employing optimization techniques such as gradient descent to maintain predictive accuracy over time. Coaching data inputs—reflecting the frequency, duration, success rate, and reciprocity of interactions with diabetes coaches—serve as proxies for social engagement and communication behavior, both of which are disrupted in mood and anxiety disorders. Event data inputs capture digital engagement metrics from mobile apps and web portals, including session frequency, duration, interactivity, consistency, and voluntary sharing behaviors.

The system architecture comprises an input device configured to receive and aggregate multimodal data streams, a machine learning system that processes these inputs to compute a mental health risk score, and an output device that delivers actionable insights to clinicians, care coordinators, or the patient themselves. The input device functionality includes secure ingestion, timestamp alignment, and preprocessing of heterogeneous data types. The machine learning system functionality involves feature engineering, model inference, and uncertainty quantification. The output device functionality enables risk stratification, alert generation, and integration with electronic health records or care management platforms. Through this integrated approach, the invention enables proactive, data-driven identification of at-risk individuals, facilitating timely intervention and improved holistic care.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention provides a comprehensive system for mental health risk detection in diabetes patients, designed to operate within existing digital chronic care ecosystems. The system is implemented as a software module integrated into a diabetes management platform, such as the Livongo for Diabetes Program, though it is adaptable to other similar infrastructures. At its core, the system relies on a machine learning architecture trained to recognize subtle behavioral deviations indicative of emerging mental health concerns.

The machine learning system architecture is based on an ensemble of gradient-boosted decision trees, specifically utilizing the LightGBM framework, selected for its efficiency, scalability, and performance on tabular data with mixed feature types. Alternative embodiments may employ deep learning neural networks for capturing complex temporal dependencies or logistic regression models for interpretability, though empirical validation favored gradient boosting in the disclosed implementation. The model is trained using a supervised learning approach, where input features are mapped to binary or probabilistic labels indicating presence or likelihood of mental health conditions.

Glucometer data collection occurs via cellular-enabled or Bluetooth-connected blood glucose meters that automatically transmit readings and contextual metadata to a central server. Each reading is accompanied by optional user responses to brief emotional state queries (e.g., “How are you feeling right now?” with options like “well,” “stressed,” or “unwell”), creating rich ecological momentary assessments embedded within routine care. Coaching data collection involves logging all inbound and outbound communications between patients and certified diabetes coaches, including call attempts, text exchanges, session durations, and resolution outcomes. Demographics data collection draws from enrollment records, capturing static attributes such as age, sex, race, ethnicity, BMI, and tobacco use status. Event data collection aggregates interaction logs from mobile applications and web portals, tracking login frequency, feature usage, reminder acknowledgments, and social sharing activities.

System components include a data ingestion pipeline, a feature engineering engine, a model inference server, and an alerting interface. The machine learning model is trained on a curated dataset wherein each instance represents a participant-period—a four-week aggregation window of behavioral signals aligned with a verified mental health status label. Training set creation involves filtering out inactive periods (e.g., >30 days without any platform interaction) and applying random undersampling to balance case-control ratios, mitigating class imbalance inherent in real-world prevalence rates.

Mental health status data labeling utilizes two complementary sources: pharmacy claims identifying filled prescriptions for antidepressants, anxiolytics, or antipsychotics; and medical claims documenting diagnoses, psychotherapy sessions, or psychiatric hospitalizations. In the absence of claims data, prescription records alone suffice for labeling. Additional validation layers include documented mental health assessments administered by care teams and records of initiated interventions.

Analysis of input signals reveals that mental health risk correlates with specific behavioral markers. In demographics data, female gender and White race show positive associations with mental health conditions, while male gender and Asian race show negative correlations. Glucometer data exhibits risk signals in elevated glucose variability (high standard deviation), infrequent or irregular testing schedules, and self-reports of “unwell” emotional states during checks. Coaching data indicates risk through low contact frequency, high missed call rates, and short interaction durations. Event data suggests risk via declining app engagement, inconsistent portal logins, and reduced social sharing.

Input data undergoes preprocessing to handle missing values, normalize scales, and encode categorical variables. The system computes a mental health risk score ranging from 0 to 1, interpreted as the probability of current or imminent mental health concern. This score is output to clinicians via dashboards, triggering tiered interventions based on risk thresholds. A feedback process enables model refinement: when new ground truth labels become available (e.g., post-intervention diagnosis), the model is retrained using incremental learning or full retraining with updated datasets. Model updates may employ gradient descent or hyperparameter optimization via libraries such as Hyperopt.

The overall process begins with continuous collection of mental health risk input signals and status labels, followed by periodic training set assembly, model validation against holdout test sets, and deployment into production environments. Upon deployment, the system calculates real-time risk scores for active users, enabling early detection without disrupting daily diabetes management routines. The underlying computer system architecture comprises cloud-based servers, secure APIs, encrypted databases, and client-side applications, ensuring compliance with healthcare data privacy regulations such as HIPAA.
