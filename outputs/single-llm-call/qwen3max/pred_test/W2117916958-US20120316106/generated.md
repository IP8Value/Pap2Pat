# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates to novel antibiotic derivatives and synergistic combinations thereof, particularly those derived from or inspired by lankacidin C (LC) and lankamycin (LM), natural products isolated from *Streptomyces rochei*. More specifically, the invention encompasses improved derivatives of LC and LM, as well as combinations of these compounds with other ribosome-targeting antibiotics that bind to either the peptidyl transferase center (PTC) or the nascent peptide exit tunnel (NPET) of the bacterial large ribosomal subunit. The invention further includes chimeric molecules and covalently conjugated constructs comprising two or more antibiotic moieties designed to simultaneously engage distinct but adjacent functional sites on the ribosome, thereby achieving enhanced antibacterial efficacy through synergistic inhibition of protein synthesis.

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

Ribosomal interfering antibiotics represent a major class of antimicrobial agents that exert their effects by binding to functional regions of the bacterial ribosome, thereby inhibiting protein biosynthesis. Over the past decade, high-resolution crystallographic studies have elucidated the precise binding sites of numerous antibiotics within the large ribosomal subunit. These studies have revealed two principal antibiotic-binding regions: the peptidyl transferase center (PTC), which catalyzes peptide bond formation, and the upper segment of the nascent peptide exit tunnel (NPET), through which newly synthesized polypeptides are translocated. Antibiotics targeting the PTC include phenicols (e.g., chloramphenicol), lincosamides (e.g., clindamycin), pleuromutilins (e.g., tiamulin), oxazolidinones (e.g., linezolid), and type A streptogramins (e.g., dalfopristin). In contrast, macrolides (e.g., erythromycin), ketolides, and type B streptogramins (e.g., quinupristin) bind within the NPET, obstructing the progression of the nascent chain.

The clinical need for new antimicrobial therapies has intensified due to the global rise of multidrug-resistant bacterial pathogens. Synergistic antibiotic combinations—wherein two compounds acting on sequential steps of a biochemical pathway produce a greater effect than the sum of their individual activities—offer a promising strategy to enhance potency, reduce required dosages, and mitigate resistance development. Nature itself has exploited this principle, as exemplified by streptogramin antibiotics, which are naturally secreted as pairs of structurally distinct compounds: a type A component that binds the PTC and a type B component that occupies the NPET. The semisynthetic formulation Synercid®, comprising dalfopristin (a type A streptogramin derivative) and quinupristin (a type B derivative), is clinically used against severe Gram-positive infections, demonstrating the therapeutic value of such synergistic pairs.

Lankacidin C (LC) and lankamycin (LM) are co-produced by *Streptomyces rochei* 7434AN4. LC is a 17-membered macrocyclic compound with a bridged lactone ring, exhibiting both antibiotic and antitumor activities, and is used in veterinary medicine. LM is a 14-membered macrolide structurally similar to erythromycin, bearing 4-acetyl-L-arcanose and D-chalcose sugar moieties, and displays weak antibacterial activity against certain Gram-positive bacteria. Despite their co-regulation and co-production, the molecular mechanisms of action of LC and LM remained unknown prior to the present invention. Earlier studies suggested LC competes with chloramphenicol for ribosome binding, implicating the PTC as its target, while LM’s structural resemblance to erythromycin implied NPET binding. However, no evidence existed regarding whether these compounds could bind simultaneously or act synergistically.

Prior art includes international patent application WO 03/026562, which discloses lankacidin derivatives with modified macrocyclic rings; U.S. Patent Application Publication No. US 2009/0042186, describing methods for producing lankamycin analogs via genetic engineering of biosynthetic pathways; and U.S. Pat. Nos. 7,606,670 and 7,169,756, which relate to ribosome-binding assays and structural modeling of antibiotic-ribosome interactions. None of these references disclose or suggest the synergistic pairing of LC and LM, nor do they provide structural insights enabling rational design of improved derivatives based on simultaneous PTC and NPET binding.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides improved antibiotic derivatives and synergistic combinations thereof, based on the discovery that lankacidin C (LC) and lankamycin (LM) bind simultaneously to adjacent but non-overlapping sites in the bacterial large ribosomal subunit—LC at the peptidyl transferase center (PTC) and LM at the nascent peptide exit tunnel (NPET)—and act synergistically to inhibit protein synthesis. The invention encompasses pharmaceutical compositions comprising two isolated antibiotic molecules: one that binds the PTC (e.g., LC or a derivative thereof, or a type A streptogramin such as virginiamycin M) and another that binds the NPET (e.g., LM or a derivative thereof, or a type B streptogramin such as virginiamycin S, or macrolides like erythromycin, pleuromutilin, chloramphenicol, methylmycin, or clindamycin).

Specific embodiments include deoxy-LM derivatives, desoamine-modified LM derivatives, C-15 substituted LM derivatives, and 3-O-arcanosyl LM derivatives, each designed to optimize binding affinity, reduce steric clash with LC, and enhance synergistic interaction. The invention further provides combinations wherein LC or a derivative is paired with a macrolide, ketolide, or type B streptogramin, and wherein LM or a derivative is paired with a type A streptogramin. Additionally, the invention includes chimeric molecules comprising covalently linked portions of two different antibiotic molecules—one targeting the PTC and the other the NPET—as well as conjugated constructs wherein full antibiotic molecules are joined via chemical linkers.

These derivatives and combinations exhibit improved properties relative to parent compounds, including enhanced biological activity, reduced toxicity, improved membrane permeability, increased bioavailability, and decreased susceptibility to bacterial resistance mechanisms. In particular, macrolide derivatives incorporating positively charged moieties are disclosed to strengthen electrostatic interactions with rRNA, thereby enhancing ribosomal binding. The invention also provides methods for producing such derivatives and combinations via fermentation, semi-synthesis, or total synthesis, and their use in the prevention and treatment of bacterial infections, especially those caused by Gram-positive pathogens resistant to conventional therapies.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

The antibiotics lankacidin C (LC) and lankamycin (LM) are naturally co-produced by *Streptomyces rochei*, suggesting evolutionary optimization for cooperative function. Crystallographic and biochemical analyses reveal that LC binds within the peptidyl transferase center (PTC) of the 50S ribosomal subunit, while LM occupies the nascent peptide exit tunnel (NPET) adjacent to the PTC. This spatial arrangement enables simultaneous binding without steric interference, in stark contrast to the competitive binding observed between LC and erythromycin. The synergistic antibacterial effect arises from dual inhibition of successive steps in protein synthesis: LC blocks peptide bond formation at the PTC, while LM impedes elongation by obstructing the NPET. X-ray diffraction data at 3.5 Å resolution confirm the distinct yet neighboring binding sites, and RNA probing experiments demonstrate concurrent protection of PTC residues (U2506, U2585) by LC and NPET residues (U2609, A2058, A2059) by LM, confirming simultaneous occupancy.

### Lankacidin Binding Site

Lankacidin C binds within a shallow depression of the PTC A-site, where its macrocyclic ring engages in van der Waals contacts with U2504, G2505, and U2506. The 2-methyl group of the lactone inserts into a hydrophobic cleft formed by A2451 and C2452—the same pocket that accommodates the aminoacyl moiety of A-site tRNA and is targeted by chloramphenicol, clindamycin, and dalfopristin. Binding induces a unique conformational rearrangement of rRNA nucleotides, establishing a network of interactions among U2506, G2505, G2581, C2610, and G2576. Notably, G2505 and C2610 form a CG•rS* base pair with a hydrogen bond distance of approximately 2.9 Å, stabilizing the drug-bound state. Second-shell nucleotides—including A2062, C2530, U2531, and A2059—further modulate binding through indirect effects. The macrocyclic ring conformation is critical: hydrogenation, which alters ring geometry, diminishes activity. LC’s binding site partially overlaps with chloramphenicol, explaining their competition, yet differs from dalfopristin in that LC does not induce the 180° flip of U2585, instead causing a modest reorientation of A2602, consistent with its mechanism of inhibiting puromycin reaction with an IC₅₀ of 0.32 µM in *S. aureus* ribosomes.

### Lankamycin Binding Site

Lankamycin binds within the NPET in a manner analogous to erythromycin but with key distinctions that enable co-binding with LC. Hydrogen bonds form between LM’s desosamine-like sugar and A2058/A2059, while hydrophobic interactions involve the macrolactone ring and residues such as A2062 and U2609. Binding induces a subtle shift in A2062 and a rearrangement of C2610, though less pronounced than in erythromycin complexes. Unlike erythromycin, LM lacks the C3-cladinose sugar, reducing steric bulk near the PTC interface. This structural simplification explains why LM (IC₅₀ = 275 µM in *E. coli* cell-free systems) is a weaker inhibitor than erythromycin but avoids clash with LC. RNA probing confirms LM-specific protection of U2609 from CMCT and A2058/A2059 from DMS, hallmark signatures of macrolide binding. The weaker affinity of LM is offset by its compatibility with LC, reflecting evolutionary tuning for synergy rather than standalone potency.

### Lankacidin and Lankamycin Can Simultaneously Bind to the Ribosome

Competition assays show LC displaces radiolabeled erythromycin from the ribosome, indicating mutual exclusivity due to steric overlap between erythromycin’s desosamine and LC’s macrocycle. In contrast, RNA probing demonstrates that LC and LM together protect all signature nucleotides—U2506 and U2585 (LC sites) and U2609 (LM site)—confirming simultaneous binding. Erythromycin, when combined with LC, shows partial loss of protection at both sets of residues, indicative of competition. The ability of LC and LM to co-occupy the ribosome stems from LM’s truncated sugar moiety, which creates sufficient clearance from LC. This co-binding results in allosteric communication: LC binding enhances DMS modification of A2058, suggesting conformational propagation from PTC to NPET. Such bidirectional modulation underpins the functional synergy observed in growth inhibition assays.

### LC and LM Act Synergistically Upon Bacterial Cells

Checkerboard assays with *S. aureus* reveal a concave fractional inhibitory concentration (FIC) index curve, diagnostic of synergy. In vitro cell-free translation assays using GFP reporter expression confirm that sub-inhibitory concentrations of LC and LM together suppress protein synthesis far more effectively than either alone. The mechanism mirrors that of streptogramins: dual blockade of initiation (via PTC inhibition) and elongation (via NPET occlusion) creates a compounded inhibitory effect. Structural data indicate that the LC/LM pair exploits the same ribosomal real estate as streptogramins but with smaller molecular footprints, offering a template for engineering higher-affinity analogs. Evolutionary co-regulation of LC and LM biosynthesis in *S. rochei* supports the biological relevance of this synergy, distinguishing it from non-cooperative antibiotic pairs.

## Materials and Methods

### Crystallography

Crystals of the *Deinococcus radiodurans* 50S subunit (D50S) were grown and soaked in 25 µM lankacidin for 20 hours at 20°C, then cryo-cooled. X-ray diffraction data were collected at synchrotron beamlines (APS 19ID, ESRF ID23-2) at 100 K, processed with HKL2000, and refined using CNS and COOT. The D50S-LC structure was solved by difference Fourier analysis, with model building guided by the known LC chemical structure. Antibiotic binding sites were identified via electron density maps and validated with LigPlot and LPC. Coordinates were deposited in the PDB (accession 3JQ4).

### Antibiotic Binding

Binding affinity was assessed by incubating D50S with radiolabeled antibiotics, followed by gel filtration and scintillation counting. Competition assays measured displacement of [¹⁴C]-erythromycin by LC.

### Inhibition of Cell-Free Protein Synthesis by LC and LM

An *E. coli* transcription-translation system (Promega) was used with pBestLuc plasmid. Antibiotics were pre-incubated, reactions initiated with DNA, and luciferase activity quantified via luminescence.

### Inhibition of the Peptidyl Transferase Reaction by LC

Puromycin reactions with *S. aureus* 70S or D50S were performed; LC inhibited with IC₅₀ values of 0.32 µM and 10 µM, respectively.

### RNA Probing

D50S (200 nM) was treated with LC (50 µM), LM (500 µM), or erythromycin (50 µM), then modified with CMCT or DMS. Primer extension revealed protection patterns at specific nucleotides.

### Cell-Free Translation System Used for Detecting Synergism In Vitro

A custom *E. coli* BL21-DE3 extract was supplemented with amino acids, NTPs, and GFP plasmid. Fluorescence after 1-hour incubation with antibiotic combinations was measured (Ex 485 nm, Em 535 nm) and analyzed by regression.

## Pharmacology

Pharmaceutical compositions comprise a therapeutically effective amount of at least two antibiotics—one PTC-binder and one NPET-binder—in physiologically acceptable carriers (e.g., water, saline, ethanol). Excipients include starch, cellulose, and magnesium stearate. Formulations support oral (tablets, capsules), transmucosal (lozenges), injectable (sterile solutions), parenteral (IV, IM), and rectal (suppositories) administration. Dosage is determined by infection severity, patient weight, and pharmacokinetics, typically ranging from 1–100 mg/kg/day. Toxicity and efficacy are evaluated in murine infection models. Packaged kits include labeled instructions for combination use and FDA approval notices. Derivatives are prepared via semi-synthesis and labeled per regulatory guidelines.

### Chemical Derivatives and Conjugates

Derivatives are molecules modified at specific positions (e.g., C-15 of LM, macrocycle of LC) to enhance binding or solubility. X-ray structures guide rational design; desoamine derivatives are synthesized by replacing native sugars with cationic amines to strengthen rRNA interactions.

### Synthetic and Semi-Synthetic Methods for Producing Antibiotics

Fermentation of engineered *Streptomyces* strains yields natural LC/LM. Semi-synthesis modifies isolated compounds via acylation, glycosylation, or reduction. Total synthesis employs multi-step organic routes to generate non-natural analogs with optimized synergy.
