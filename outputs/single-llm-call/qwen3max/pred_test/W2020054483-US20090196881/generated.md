## INTRODUCTION

Spring viremia of carp virus (SVCV) is a highly pathogenic rhabdovirus that poses a significant threat to global aquaculture, particularly affecting common carp (Cyprinus carpio carpio) populations throughout Europe. SVCV has been documented in numerous European countries including Russia, Romania, the Netherlands, Moldavia, Georgia, Germany, France, the United Kingdom, and Denmark, where it has caused widespread and severe outbreaks in farmed carp stocks. The disease manifests through systemic inflammation, hemorrhaging, and swelling of vital organs such as the spleen, kidney, intestines, and air bladder, ultimately leading to necrosis and death. Outbreaks typically occur in spring when water temperatures rise from winter lows, with peak mortality observed between 11°C and 17°C. While common carp are the principal host species, natural infections have also been reported in other cyprinid fish including koi (Cyprinus carpio koi), goldfish (Carassius auratus), crucian carp (Carassius carassius), silver carp (Hypophthalmichthys molitrix), bighead carp (Aristichthys nobilis), grass carp (Ctenopharyngodon idella), orfe (Leuciscus idus), and tench (Tinca tinca). Experimental infections have further extended the known host range to include roach (Rutilus rutilus), zebrafish (Danio rerio), fathead minnow (Pimephales promelas), and even non-cyprinid species from families such as Poeciliidae, Esocidae, Centrarchidae, Siluridae, and Salmonidae.

Given its high infectivity and potential for catastrophic economic impact, SVCV is classified by the World Organisation for Animal Health (Office International des Épizooties, OIE) as one of only nine notifiable piscine diseases globally, requiring mandatory reporting within 48 hours of detection. The first confirmed outbreak in North America occurred in April 2002 at a major koi production facility in the United States, resulting in the death of 15,000 fish and the euthanasia of an additional 135,000. Subsequent detections followed in Wisconsin, Illinois, Washington State, Missouri, and Canada, confirming the virus’s establishment in the continent. In response, the U.S. Department of Agriculture (USDA) imposed strict import restrictions on live fish, fertilized eggs, and gametes from susceptible species in 2006.

Current control strategies rely heavily on eradication of infected stocks and stringent hygiene measures, as no commercially available vaccine exists. Early attempts using inactivated SVCV vaccines derived from European strains showed limited efficacy and were abandoned due to safety concerns regarding incomplete inactivation, regulatory hurdles, high production costs, and lack of robust efficacy data. DNA vaccines represent a promising alternative, offering advantages such as simple design, thermal stability, low manufacturing cost, absence of infectious viral material, and long-term storage capability. Research on DNA vaccines for fish pathogens—particularly against novirhabdoviruses like infectious hematopoietic necrosis virus (IHNV) and viral hemorrhagic septicemia virus (VHSV)—has demonstrated high efficacy and led to commercial licensing in some regions. However, prior efforts to develop an SVCV DNA vaccine using European isolates yielded inconsistent results, with relative percent survival (RPS) values rarely exceeding 48% and often showing no significant protection.

Phylogenetic analyses have revealed that SVCV isolates cluster into four genogroups (Ia–Id), with all North American isolates belonging to the Ia genogroup of Asian origin, distinct from the Ib–Id European strains. The glycoprotein (G) gene, which encodes the primary surface antigen responsible for eliciting protective immune responses, is the standard target for rhabdoviral DNA vaccines. Despite structural similarities among rhabdoviruses, SVCV belongs to the Vesiculovirus genus and lacks the nonvirion (NV) gene found in Novirhabdoviruses like IHNV and VHSV, potentially influencing immunogenicity. Previous DNA vaccine constructs based on the European Fijan strain (Genogroup Id) failed to confer reliable protection in carp. A Russian study reported partial efficacy, but these findings were never independently validated. More recent trials using mixtures of plasmids encoding partial or full G-gene fragments achieved only modest RPS, with no clear identification of the protective component. Thus, there remains a critical unmet need for an effective, reproducible, and safe DNA vaccine tailored to the North American SVCV Ia genotype to prevent further spread and mitigate economic losses in the ornamental and food fish industries.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a novel DNA vaccine for the prevention and control of Spring viremia of carp virus (SVCV) infection in susceptible fish species, particularly koi and other cyprinids. The vaccine comprises a recombinant plasmid designated pSGnc, which contains an isolated nucleic acid sequence encoding the full-length glycoprotein (G) gene of the North Carolina isolate of SVCV (SVCVnc), belonging to the Ia genogroup. This construct was developed in response to the emergence of SVCV in North America and represents the first DNA vaccine specifically designed against a North American strain of the virus.

To evaluate the efficacy of the pSGnc vaccine, a robust and reproducible challenge model was developed using domestic koi as the primary host species. This model incorporates a rapid cold stress protocol immediately prior to viral challenge, which was shown to be as effective as traditional slow-acclimation methods in inducing consistent and high mortality upon exposure to SVCVnc, thereby streamlining experimental procedures without compromising reliability. Four independent vaccine trial studies were conducted under varying conditions—including differences in fish age, challenge dose, and seasonal timing—to assess the breadth and consistency of protection conferred by the pSGnc construct.

In all four trials, fish vaccinated with pSGnc exhibited significantly lower cumulative percent mortality (CPM) compared to both non-vaccinated controls and fish vaccinated with a mock luciferase-expressing plasmid (pLuc). Non-vaccinated fish suffered mortalities ranging from 70% to 100%, while pLuc-vaccinated groups showed similar high mortality (85–100%), confirming the absence of non-specific protective effects from the vector or delivery method. In contrast, pSGnc-vaccinated fish displayed CPM values between 10% and 50%, corresponding to relative percent survival (RPS) values of 50% to 88%. These results demonstrate statistically significant protection (p ≤ 0.05) across all tested conditions.

Virus quantification via plaque assay confirmed that mortality was directly attributable to SVCV infection, with high viral titers (up to 1.8 × 10⁷ PFU/g) detected in nearly all deceased fish. Notably, survivors from the pSGnc-vaccinated groups in the later trials (C and D) showed no detectable virus, indicating complete clearance and recovery, whereas survivors from control groups consistently harbored replicating virus. This suggests that the pSGnc vaccine not only reduces mortality but also limits viral persistence and potential shedding.

The invention further encompasses the isolated nucleic acid molecule encoding the SVCVnc G-gene, the recombinant polypeptide it expresses, and methods for producing and administering the vaccine. Additionally, the invention includes diagnostic compositions and kits utilizing the recombinant G protein or anti-G antibodies for in vitro detection of SVCV infection or immune status. The objects of the present invention include: (1) providing a safe, effective, and stable DNA vaccine against SVCV; (2) enabling prophylactic immunization of susceptible fish populations in aquaculture and ornamental settings; (3) offering a tool for biosecurity and disease containment in regions threatened by SVCV incursion; and (4) facilitating serological monitoring through specific antigen-antibody assays.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

As used herein, the term “epitope” refers to any antigenic determinant capable of specific binding to an antibody or T-cell receptor. An “epitope” may be a linear epitope, comprising a contiguous sequence of amino acids, or a conformational epitope, formed by the three-dimensional folding of non-contiguous amino acid residues. A “solid phase” denotes any substantially non-deformable support to which molecules may be attached, such as microtiter plates, beads, or membranes. A “biological sample” includes any material derived from a living organism, such as tissue, cells, or fluids, and a “biological fluid” specifically refers to liquids such as blood, serum, plasma, mucus, or homogenates of whole organisms. A substance is “immunologically reactive” if it can bind specifically to an antibody or immune receptor, and an “immune complex” is a stable molecular aggregate formed by the binding of an antigen to its specific antibody.

A “purified protein” is a protein that has been separated from other cellular components, and an “isolated G protein” refers to the SVCV glycoprotein that has been removed from its native viral context. “Essentially purified proteins” are preparations containing at least 90% by weight of the target protein. “Recombinantly expressed” indicates that the protein is produced by host cells transformed with exogenous nucleic acid. Host organisms include “prokaryotes” (e.g., bacteria such as *Escherichia coli*), “lower eukaryotes” (e.g., yeast), and “higher eukaryotes” (e.g., fish, mammalian, or insect cells). A “polypeptide” is a polymer of amino acids, and a “recombinant polynucleotide or nucleic acid” is a DNA or RNA molecule engineered by human intervention, typically comprising a coding sequence operably linked to regulatory elements.

A “recombinant host cell” is a cell that has been genetically modified to contain foreign DNA. A “replicon” is a DNA segment capable of autonomous replication, and a “vector” is a replicon used to transfer genetic material into a host cell. A “control sequence” includes promoters, enhancers, terminators, and other elements that regulate gene expression. A “promoter” is a DNA sequence that initiates transcription, and a coding sequence is “operably linked” to a promoter when positioned to allow transcription under the promoter’s control. An “open reading frame” (ORF) is a continuous stretch of codons that can be translated into a polypeptide. A substance is “immunogenic” if it provokes an adaptive immune response, and a “therapeutic” agent treats or prevents disease. An “effective amount” is the quantity sufficient to achieve a desired biological effect.

The present invention provides a DNA vaccine for protecting fish against SVCV, comprising an isolated DNA molecule encoding the SVCVnc G-gene, inserted into a suitable expression vector such as pcDNA3.1. Delivery methods include intramuscular injection, with a typical dose of 10 µg per fish in 50 µL phosphate-buffered saline. The vector is selected for strong constitutive expression in fish cells, and host cells for plasmid propagation include *E. coli*. Transformation is achieved via standard methods such as electroporation or chemical competence. Expression is driven by a cytomegalovirus (CMV) promoter, and the construct is formulated with pharmacologically acceptable carriers, though adjuvants are generally unnecessary for DNA vaccines.

Administration is performed prophylactically, with immunity developing over 28 days (approximately 546 degree-days). Treatment schedules may involve single or booster doses depending on risk assessment. The invention also includes compositions for in vitro detection of SVCV infection, comprising the recombinant G protein immobilized on a solid phase. Immunoassays may be heterogeneous (e.g., ELISA) or homogeneous, competitive or non-competitive, and include formats such as immunoprecipitation. Kits for diagnosis contain the antigen, detection antibodies, buffers, and controls. Assay conditions ensure specific antigen-antibody binding, with detection via enzymatic, fluorescent, or luminescent reporters.

Monoclonal antibodies against the G protein are produced by standard hybridoma technology for use in diagnostics or passive immunization. Therapeutic applications include active immunization via the DNA vaccine, while prophylactic use involves vaccinating at-risk populations before exposure. Vaccine preparation involves cloning the G-gene into pcDNA3.1, transforming *E. coli*, and purifying plasmid DNA via alkaline lysis. Efficacy is validated in koi subjected to rapid cold stress followed by intraperitoneal challenge with 10⁶ PFU of SVCVnc. Survival is monitored for 26–29 days, with statistical analysis by Kaplan-Meier and log-rank tests. Virus quantification uses plaque assays on EPC cells.

Results consistently show significant protection by pSGnc, with RPS of 50–88%, absence of virus in most survivors, and superiority over prior European constructs. The invention offers a critical tool for SVCV management in North America, with potential for cross-protection against other Ia strains and adaptation to other susceptible species.

### Susceptibility Testing of Goldfish and Koi

Comparative susceptibility testing revealed stark differences between goldfish and koi in response to SVCVnc challenge. Goldfish (*Carassius auratus*), averaging 14.6 g in weight, exhibited high resistance, with 96% survival across both immersion and injection challenges at doses up to 10⁵ PFU/mL and 10⁶ PFU/fish, respectively. In contrast, koi (*Cyprinus carpio koi*) from a specific pathogen-free domestic stock demonstrated extreme susceptibility: 100% mortality following intraperitoneal injection and 75% mortality after immersion at 10⁵ PFU/mL. These results, summarized in Table 2 of the research paper, confirm koi as a highly suitable model for SVCV challenge studies and vaccine evaluation, while goldfish are unsuitable due to their innate resistance under the tested conditions.

### Example 2

In a comparative study of cold stress protocols, koi were subjected to either a slow cold acclimation (decreasing 1°C daily over 14 days to 10–12°C) or a rapid cold shock (immediate transfer to 10–12°C on the day of challenge). Both groups were then challenged with SVCVnc via immersion or injection. No statistically significant difference in cumulative percent mortality (CPM) was observed between the two stress regimens: immersion challenges resulted in 75% CPM and injection challenges in 100% CPM under both protocols. Virus titers in dead and surviving fish were uniformly high (>10⁶ PFU/g), confirming successful infection. Given the equivalent outcomes and greater time efficiency, the rapid cold stress method was adopted for all subsequent vaccine challenge trials.

### Example 3

Four independent vaccine trials (A–D) evaluated the efficacy of the pSGnc DNA vaccine under varied conditions. Mock-challenged fish (exposed to culture medium only) exhibited negligible mortality (0–10%) with no clinical signs or detectable virus. Non-vaccinated (NV) control fish suffered 70–100% mortality upon SVCVnc challenge, while pLuc-vaccinated (mock DNA vaccine) groups showed 85–100% mortality, confirming the lack of non-specific protection. In contrast, pSGnc-vaccinated fish had significantly reduced mortality (10–50%), yielding relative percent survival (RPS) values of 50–88% across all trials. Virus was detected in 98% of dead fish (titers: 7.9×10⁵ to 1.8×10⁷ PFU/g), confirming SVCV as the cause of death. Among survivors, NV and pLuc groups universally harbored virus (4.1×10³ to 4.5×10⁵ PFU/g), whereas pSGnc-vaccinated survivors in trials C and D showed no detectable virus, and only 3 of 12 in trials A and B were positive. The pSGnc vaccine thus provided significant, reproducible protection superior to prior European SVCV DNA constructs and comparable to, though slightly lower than, novirhabdovirus DNA vaccines. The absence of virus in most pSGnc survivors indicates effective viral clearance, underscoring the vaccine’s therapeutic potential.
