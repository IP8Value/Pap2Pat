# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

Healthcare technology has undergone a transformative evolution in recent decades, driven by the integration of computational models into clinical decision-making processes. Among the most impactful innovations are clinical risk prediction models, which estimate the probability of adverse health outcomes—such as mortality, stroke, or heart failure—based on a patient’s demographic, clinical, and laboratory data. These models, ranging from simple scoring systems like the Framingham Risk Score to complex machine learning algorithms trained on large electronic medical record (EMR) datasets, aim to guide diagnosis, treatment selection, and resource allocation. Despite their widespread adoption and demonstrated statistical performance, a critical gap remains in the practical utility of these tools: clinicians often lack insight into when a specific prediction for an individual patient is likely to be inaccurate or misleading. While aggregate metrics such as accuracy, area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), and Brier score provide valuable information about overall model performance across a population, they offer little guidance regarding the reliability of predictions for particular patients or subgroups. This limitation is especially concerning in high-stakes clinical contexts where misclassification can lead to inappropriate interventions or missed opportunities for life-saving care. The challenge is compounded by class imbalance—a common feature in clinical datasets where adverse events occur infrequently—rendering many predictive models prone to overconfidence in high-risk predictions that may not be well-supported by training data. Furthermore, existing approaches to assessing prediction reliability either require access to proprietary training datasets (which are typically unavailable due to privacy regulations) or mandate retraining of complex models, making them impractical for real-time clinical use. Consequently, there exists a pressing need for a model-independent, computationally efficient, and clinically interpretable method to evaluate the trustworthiness of individual risk predictions without reliance on original training data or extensive computational resources.

## SUMMARY

The present invention introduces a novel method for clinical risk prediction that incorporates a patient-specific reliability assessment to enhance the safety and utility of predictive analytics in healthcare settings. Unlike conventional risk scores that output only a probability of an adverse outcome, the disclosed system simultaneously computes an unreliability metric that quantifies the degree to which a given prediction may deviate from the true clinical risk due to limitations in the underlying training data or model applicability. This approach enables clinicians to distinguish between predictions that are robust and those that should be interpreted with caution, particularly in cases involving rare outcomes or atypical patient profiles. The method is fundamentally model-independent, meaning it can be applied to any existing or future clinical risk model—whether derived from logistic regression, neural networks, or rule-based systems—without requiring modification of the original model architecture or access to its training dataset. Instead, the invention leverages summary statistics from the original development cohort to construct generative models of patient features conditioned on outcome status, thereby enabling the derivation of an alternative risk estimate that serves as a benchmark for reliability evaluation. The core innovation lies in comparing the primary clinical risk score with this alternate estimate; a large discrepancy between the two indicates that the prediction is unreliable because the available data do not consistently support a single risk assessment for that patient profile. Embodiments of the invention include a clinical decision support system that integrates this unreliability metric into the user interface, alerting clinicians when a prediction falls above a predefined threshold of unreliability and prompting additional diagnostic evaluation or consultation. Variations of the method accommodate different types of generative models—including multivariate normal distributions, Gaussian mixture models, or deep generative networks—and can be adapted to binary, multi-class, or time-to-event prediction tasks. The system is designed for deployment in diverse healthcare environments, including hospital electronic health record platforms, cloud-based analytics services, and point-of-care mobile applications, ensuring broad accessibility while maintaining compliance with data privacy standards.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The invention is embodied in a clinical decision support system that enhances the interpretability and safety of clinical risk prediction models through proactive reliability assessment. Traditional clinical decision support systems provide static risk estimates based on fixed algorithms, offering no indication of whether a particular prediction is well-supported by historical data. This limitation becomes problematic when models are applied to patients whose characteristics differ significantly from those in the training population, leading to potentially erroneous conclusions. The disclosed system addresses this issue by introducing a dynamic reliability layer that evaluates each prediction in real time.

The need for such a reliability measure arises from the inherent heterogeneity of patient populations and the finite nature of clinical training datasets. Even highly validated models like the GRACE score may perform poorly on subgroups that were underrepresented during development, yet standard performance metrics mask these deficiencies by averaging across all patients. Conventional model-dependent methods for estimating prediction confidence—such as Bayesian posterior intervals or dropout-based uncertainty in neural networks—are tightly coupled to specific model architectures and cannot be retrofitted onto legacy risk scores widely used in practice. On the other hand, model-independent approaches that rely on retraining classifiers with augmented datasets or nearest-neighbor comparisons require access to the original training data, which is rarely feasible in real-world clinical workflows due to regulatory and logistical constraints.

To overcome these limitations, the present invention introduces a trust score approach that operates without retraining or raw data access. However, even this improved method has drawbacks: it assumes availability of training set examples for distance computation and may not generalize well under extreme class imbalance. Recognizing these shortcomings, the inventors developed a proactive reliability assessment framework that functions entirely from summary statistics—namely, class-conditional means and covariances—thereby preserving patient privacy while enabling universal applicability.

In one embodiment, the clinical decision support system comprises a reliability assessment module integrated within a hospital’s EMR infrastructure. This module receives patient feature vectors, applies a pre-existing clinical risk model to generate a primary risk score, and concurrently computes an unreliability metric using generative modeling techniques. The system components include a data ingestion interface, a risk prediction engine, a generative modeling unit, a reliability calculator, and a clinician-facing display adapter.

The reliability assessment module defines a clinical risk score as any function \( f(\vec{x}) \) that maps a patient’s feature vector \( \vec{x} \) to a probability-like value between 0 and 1, representing the estimated likelihood of an adverse outcome. The method for determining reliability involves constructing an alternative risk estimate \( P^G(y=1 | \hat{y}) \), where \( \hat{y} = f(\vec{x}) \), using Bayes’ theorem and likelihoods derived from generative models of the positive and negative outcome classes.

Generative models are introduced to approximate the distribution of patient features within each outcome group. In the preferred embodiment, multivariate normal (MVN) distributions are fitted to the positive and negative subsets of the original training data using only the sample mean and covariance—summary statistics that can be published alongside risk models without compromising individual privacy. From these distributions, synthetic patient records are sampled, processed through the original risk model, and used to empirically estimate the likelihood functions \( P^G(\hat{y} | y=1) \) and \( P^G(\hat{y} | y=0) \).

Bayes’ theorem is then applied to compute the posterior probability \( P^G(y=1 | \hat{y}) = \frac{P^G(\hat{y} | y=1) P(y=1)}{P^G(\hat{y} | y=1) P(y=1) + P^G(\hat{y} | y=0) P(y=0)} \), where \( P(y=1) \) is the baseline prevalence of the outcome in the development cohort. An alternative formula for the probability function emerges naturally from this derivation, emphasizing the role of the relative likelihood ratio \( \beta_{\vec{x}} = \frac{P^G(\hat{y} | y=1)}{P^G(\hat{y} | y=0)} \).

The system further includes server and database components that store model parameters, generative model specifications, and historical reliability assessments. A clinician’s prior decisions database logs past interactions with the system, enabling personalized alert thresholds and longitudinal reliability tracking. In a neural network embodiment, the generative models are implemented as variational autoencoders or normalizing flows, trained on summary statistics via moment-matching objectives. Neuromorphic systems may be employed for ultra-low-power edge deployment in resource-constrained settings.

The neural network functionality extends beyond mere prediction to include uncertainty quantification through latent space analysis. The method for assessing reliability begins by segregating EMR data from the original development cohort into positive (outcome occurred) and negative (outcome did not occur) sets. Generative models are trained separately on each set using maximum likelihood estimation. Synthetic data are then sampled from both models—typically millions of examples—to ensure stable density estimation.

These synthetic samples are processed through the original clinical risk classifier to obtain predicted scores, which are binned into histograms to approximate the conditional likelihoods. Convergence of the numerical distributions is verified using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test across successive sampling iterations. Once converged, the relative likelihoods are calculated for each bin, enabling interpolation for arbitrary input scores.

The alternative risk score \( P^G(y=1 | \hat{y}) \) is computed for each patient, and the absolute difference between this value and the original prediction \( \hat{y} \) yields the unreliability metric \( U(\vec{x}) \). Reliability determinations are made by comparing \( U(\vec{x}) \) to empirically derived thresholds (e.g., top 1% of values in validation cohorts). Patient features are matched with these determinations to enable subgroup analysis and alert customization.

The system modifies the clinician’s user interface to display reliability indicators alongside risk scores—using color coding, icons, or textual warnings. When unreliability exceeds a threshold, the system alerts the clinician, prompts review of the patient’s full EMR, identifies the primary care physician, and issues electronic communications recommending caution or additional diagnostics. The unreliability score is integrated into downstream decision-making protocols, such as triggering multidisciplinary review for high-risk, high-unreliability cases.

Validation using real-world GRACE registry data confirmed that patients in the top 50% of unreliability exhibited worse calibration, higher normalized Brier scores, and lower AUCs compared to more reliable predictions. The unreliability metric was particularly effective in identifying miscalibrated high-risk predictions under class imbalance. Performance was further tested on a stroke risk model, demonstrating generalizability across outcomes.
