## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

Apoptosis, or programmed cell death, is a fundamental biological process essential for maintaining tissue homeostasis, eliminating damaged or infected cells, and shaping organ development. Dysregulation of apoptosis contributes to numerous pathological conditions, including cancer, neurodegenerative disorders, autoimmune diseases, and ischemia-reperfusion injury. The intrinsic or mitochondrial pathway of apoptosis is governed by the BCL-2 protein family, which includes both pro-apoptotic and anti-apoptotic members that interact through conserved BCL-2 homology (BH) domains. Central to this pathway is mitochondrial outer membrane permeabilization (MOMP), a decisive event that commits the cell to death by releasing cytochrome c and other apoptogenic factors into the cytosol, thereby triggering caspase activation.

The BCL-2 protein family is structurally and functionally classified into three subgroups: anti-apoptotic proteins (e.g., BCL-2, BCL-XL, MCL-1), multi-domain pro-apoptotic effectors (BAX, BAK), and BH3-only pro-apoptotic activators or sensitizers (e.g., BIM, BID, PUMA, BAD). Among these, BAX plays a pivotal role as a key executioner of MOMP. In healthy cells, BAX resides in the cytosol as an inactive monomer. Upon apoptotic stimuli, BH3-only proteins such as tBID or BIM directly engage BAX, inducing conformational changes that enable its translocation to the mitochondrial outer membrane, insertion, oligomerization, and pore formation. This tightly regulated process is counterbalanced by anti-apoptotic BCL-2 proteins that sequester either activators or activated BAX/BAK.

Regulation of MOMP hinges on the dynamic equilibrium between pro- and anti-apoptotic BCL-2 family interactions. BAX activation is a multi-step process involving exposure of its N-terminal domain, dissociation of its C-terminal helix α9 from a hydrophobic groove, mitochondrial targeting, membrane integration, and homo-oligomerization. Structural studies have revealed that BAX exists in a closed, autoinhibited conformation in the cytosol, wherein helix α9 occupies the canonical BH3-binding groove, and the N-terminal region (helices α1–α2) remains buried. Activation involves displacement of α9 and structural rearrangements at the N-terminal surface, particularly involving helices α1 and α6, which constitute a “trigger site” for BH3-mediated activation.

The BAX activation pathway is initiated when activator BH3-only proteins bind to this N-terminal trigger site, inducing allosteric changes that release α9 and expose membrane-targeting domains. This process is highly dependent on specific conformational transitions, making BAX a challenging but promising therapeutic target. Diseases characterized by excessive cell survival—such as many cancers—often involve impaired BAX activation due to overexpression of anti-apoptotic proteins or mutations in BAX itself. Conversely, conditions involving excessive cell death—such as myocardial infarction or stroke—may benefit from BAX inhibition. Despite its therapeutic relevance, pharmacological modulation of BAX has been hindered by the lack of high-affinity, conformation-specific molecular probes capable of distinguishing between inactive monomeric, activated, and oligomeric states.

Structural insights into BAX have been limited by the difficulty in generating antibodies or small molecules that selectively recognize specific conformations. Traditional immunization approaches often fail to yield antibodies against cryptic or transient epitopes present only in defined conformational states. Consequently, there remains a critical need for tools that can specifically bind and stabilize the inactive monomeric form of BAX, thereby enabling both mechanistic studies and therapeutic intervention. The present invention addresses this unmet need by providing synthetic Fab antibodies that selectively bind monomeric BAX with high affinity and inhibit its activation by blocking the N-terminal trigger site.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention discloses isolated synthetic antigen-binding fragments (Fabs) that specifically bind to monomeric human BAX protein and methods of using such Fabs for inhibiting BAX-mediated apoptosis. In particular, the invention provides recombinant Fab antibodies, including but not limited to Fab 3G11, that bind to the N-terminal surface of BAX encompassing helices α1 and α6 and the intervening loop, thereby preventing conformational activation triggered by BH3-only proteins such as tBID. These Fabs exhibit nanomolar binding affinity, form stable 1:1 complexes with BAX, and effectively inhibit BAX translocation to mitochondria, oligomerization, and cytochrome c release. The invention further encompasses pharmaceutical compositions comprising these Fabs, conjugates thereof with therapeutic agents, and methods for treating diseases associated with dysregulated apoptosis, including cancer, neurodegenerative disorders, and ischemic injuries. Additionally, the Fabs serve as diagnostic and research tools for detecting inactive monomeric BAX, screening small-molecule modulators, and predicting therapeutic response in patients undergoing apoptosis-inducing therapies.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

### Introduction

Synthetic antibody technology enables the generation of high-affinity, conformation-specific binders without reliance on animal immunization, overcoming limitations associated with immune tolerance and epitope inaccessibility. Using phage display, large libraries of human antibody fragments with diversified complementarity-determining regions (CDRs) can be screened against purified target proteins under controlled conditions. This approach is particularly advantageous for targeting intracellular proteins like BAX, which adopt multiple conformations and contain cryptic epitopes not presented during conventional immune responses. The rationale for utilizing in vitro selection lies in the ability to enforce stringent selection criteria—such as binding exclusively to monomeric BAX—and to recover clones that recognize functional sites critical for protein activity. In the present work, a synthetic Fab library (Library F) was employed to isolate antibodies that bind the inactive cytosolic form of BAX, with the goal of identifying inhibitors of BAX activation.

### Materials and Methods

Recombinant full-length human BAX, C-terminally truncated BAX (ΔC26), and point mutants were expressed in *E. coli* as chitin-binding domain fusions and purified via chitin affinity chromatography followed by size-exclusion chromatography to obtain homogeneous monomeric protein. A synthetic phage-displayed Fab library (Library F), engineered with restricted diversity in CDR-H1 and H2 and expanded diversity in CDR-L3 and H3, was subjected to three rounds of biopanning against immobilized monomeric BAX. Selected clones were converted to soluble Fab expression vectors by introducing a stop codon and hexahistidine tag upstream of the phage coat protein gene. Fabs were expressed periplasmically in *E. coli* BL21(DE3) cells grown in low-phosphate media, harvested, and lysed with BugBuster reagent. Lysates were applied to Ni-NTA columns, washed with imidazole-containing buffer, and eluted with high imidazole. Eluates were dialyzed and further purified over Protein A affinity columns, with final elution at low pH and immediate neutralization. Concentrations were determined by absorbance at 280 nm using calculated extinction coefficients. CDR sequences were derived from DNA sequencing of selected clones. Selected Fabs were also converted to single-chain variable fragments (scFvs) and cloned into mammalian expression plasmids with Myc tags for validation in eukaryotic systems. Binding was confirmed via direct and competitive ELISAs using FLAG-tagged Fabs and horseradish peroxidase-conjugated detection antibodies. Kinetic parameters were measured by biolayer interferometry. Functional assays included liposome permeabilization, mitochondrial cytochrome c release, BAX translocation, and Western blotting. Structural analyses employed NMR spectroscopy, hydrogen/deuterium exchange mass spectrometry (HXMS), and computational docking using HADDOCK with CNS refinement.

### Results

Screening of Library F against monomeric BAX yielded 14 unique Fab clones with diverse CDR sequences and no homology among them, indicating multiple binding solutions. All Fabs bound BAX with EC₅₀ values ranging from 2.3 nM to 250 nM in ELISA. Biolayer interferometry confirmed high-affinity interactions, with Fab 3G11 exhibiting a K_D of approximately 1 nM. Competitive ELISAs revealed that most Fabs, except the low-affinity 2A5, competed for overlapping epitopes on BAX. None of the Fabs activated BAX alone, but high-affinity Fabs (3G11, 3E8, 2B1) potently inhibited tBID-induced BAX activation in liposomal and mitochondrial assays. Fab 3G11 dose-dependently blocked BAX mitochondrial translocation and cytochrome c release. Size-exclusion chromatography confirmed a 1:1 stoichiometric complex between 3G11 and BAX. NMR titration showed slow exchange kinetics consistent with tight binding. HXMS demonstrated significant protection from deuterium exchange in helices α1 and α6 and the α1–α2 loop upon 3G11 binding, localizing the epitope to the N-terminal activation site. Mutagenesis of Lys-21 (α1) and Arg-134 (α6) to glutamate reduced or abolished 3G11 binding and inhibitory function, validating the structural model. HADDOCK docking, guided by HXMS restraints, positioned 3G11 over the α1/α6 interface, directly occluding the BH3 trigger site.

### DISCUSSION

The BCL-2 family orchestrates mitochondrial apoptosis through finely tuned protein–protein interactions. BAX, as a central effector, undergoes dramatic conformational changes during activation, presenting distinct surfaces in its inactive, activated, and oligomeric states. The synthetic Fabs described herein represent the first high-affinity, conformation-specific antibodies that selectively recognize monomeric BAX and inhibit its activation by direct blockade of the N-terminal trigger site. Unlike previously reported BAX inhibitors—such as BCL-2 BH4 or vMIA peptides, which act allosterically at distal sites—the 3G11 Fab engages the very site used by activating BH3-only proteins, offering a novel mechanism of competitive inhibition. This direct occlusion prevents the initial conformational changes required for mitochondrial translocation, effectively halting the apoptotic cascade upstream of MOMP. The discovery of 14 diverse Fabs confirms the druggability of the BAX N-terminal surface and provides a toolkit for probing BAX biology. Therapeutically, these Fabs hold promise for conditions of pathological cell death, while their use in competitive assays enables high-throughput screening for small-molecule BAX inhibitors. Moreover, the success of this synthetic approach underscores its utility for targeting other conformationally dynamic intracellular proteins previously considered "undruggable."
