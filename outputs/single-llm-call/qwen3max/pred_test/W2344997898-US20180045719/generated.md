# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNICAL FIELD

The present invention pertains to the field of molecular and cellular biology, specifically to methods and compositions for the identification, isolation, and characterization of protein-protein interactions and protein-small molecule interactions. More particularly, the invention relates to a novel platform based on virus-like particles (VLPs) for the capture and analysis of native protein complexes under physiological conditions without the need for cell lysis. The invention provides a lysis-independent strategy for co-complex analysis by exploiting the natural ability of certain viral structural proteins to self-assemble into extracellular particles that can encapsulate cytosolic protein complexes during their biogenesis. This approach enables the preservation of transient, weak, or context-dependent interactions that are often lost or disrupted in conventional affinity purification coupled with mass spectrometry (AP–MS) workflows due to harsh lysis conditions. The technical field further encompasses recombinant DNA technology, protein engineering, proteomics, and drug target deconvolution, wherein the disclosed VLP-based system serves as a versatile tool for both basic research and pharmaceutical applications.

## BACKGROUND

Molecular interactions, particularly those between proteins, form the foundation of nearly all biological processes, including signal transduction, gene regulation, metabolic pathways, and immune responses. Understanding these interactions is essential for elucidating cellular mechanisms and for identifying therapeutic targets. Protein-protein interactions (PPIs) are dynamic and often governed by specific structural motifs, post-translational modifications, subcellular localization, and binding affinities that can range from picomolar to micromolar. Consequently, the accurate detection and characterization of PPIs require methodologies that preserve the native cellular environment as much as possible.

Historically, the yeast two-hybrid (Y2H) system has been widely used to detect binary PPIs by reconstituting a transcription factor when bait and prey proteins interact in the yeast nucleus. However, this method suffers from several limitations: it occurs in a non-mammalian cellular context, cannot detect interactions requiring post-translational modifications absent in yeast, and is prone to false positives due to auto-activation or non-physiological nuclear localization. To address some of these shortcomings, phage display was developed, wherein libraries of peptide or protein fragments are expressed on the surface of bacteriophages and screened for binding to immobilized targets. While useful for mapping linear epitopes or high-affinity binders, phage display is limited to extracellular or denatured interactions and cannot recapitulate intracellular complex formation.

The Mammalian Protein–Protein Interaction Trap (MAPPIT) system was introduced as a mammalian two-hybrid alternative that operates in the cytoplasm and relies on cytokine receptor signaling to report interactions. Although MAPPIT allows detection of weak interactions in a more physiologically relevant setting, it is restricted to binary readouts and requires engineered signaling components, limiting its scalability for proteome-wide discovery.

Cytoplasmic interaction traps, including split-ubiquitin and split-luciferase systems, offer alternatives but still depend on artificial reconstitution events that may not reflect true stoichiometry or complex architecture. Collectively, genetic interaction systems—while powerful—are inherently indirect, often qualitative, and unsuitable for isolating intact multi-protein complexes for downstream biochemical or proteomic analysis.

To overcome these constraints, biochemical strategies have been employed, notably co-immunoprecipitation (co-IP) and affinity purification followed by mass spectrometry (AP–MS). These approaches involve cell lysis under defined buffer conditions to solubilize protein complexes, which are then captured via an epitope-tagged bait protein. However, the lysis step fundamentally disrupts cellular compartmentalization, alters protein concentrations, and exposes complexes to non-physiological ionic strengths, detergents, and pH levels. As a result, labile or transient interactions are frequently lost, while non-specific associations may be artificially stabilized. Indeed, systematic studies have shown that complex stability varies dramatically across different lysis protocols, necessitating optimization for each target—a labor-intensive and often impractical requirement.

Classical co-purification strategies, such as tandem affinity purification (TAP), improve specificity through sequential purification steps but still rely on lysis and suffer from similar environmental perturbations. Moreover, these methods are poorly suited for membrane-associated or low-abundance complexes.

The classical approach to identify target proteins of small molecules—such as drugs—involves chemical modification (e.g., biotinylation) of the compound to enable pull-down from cell lysates. This strategy is compromised by the need to chemically alter the small molecule, which can abolish or distort its binding properties, and by the same lysis-induced artifacts that plague PPI studies.

Thus, there remains a critical unmet need for a method that captures protein complexes—including those involving small molecules—in their native, intracellular state, without cell disruption, and that enables robust, scalable, and quantitative downstream analysis. The present invention addresses this need through a novel VLP-based trapping platform that physically encapsulates protein complexes during particle assembly, thereby shielding them from external perturbations and enabling faithful recovery under near-physiological conditions.

## BRIEF SUMMARY

There is a pressing need in the art for improved methods to identify and characterize protein-protein and protein-small molecule interactions that occur within living cells under native conditions. Conventional techniques relying on cell lysis introduce significant artifacts and fail to preserve transient or weak interactions, leading to incomplete or misleading interactome maps. To meet this need, the present invention provides a novel method derived from and significantly improving upon the Virotrap concept, wherein protein complexes are encapsulated within secreted virus-like particles (VLPs) during their biogenesis in mammalian cells. This lysis-free approach ensures that interactions are captured in situ, protected from exogenous interference, and amenable to high-confidence identification via mass spectrometry or other analytical techniques.

The new method involves the expression of a fusion protein comprising a VLP-forming polypeptide (such as HIV-1 Gag) linked to a "bait" protein or a recruiting element that binds a small molecule of interest. During VLP assembly at the plasma membrane, cytosolic interaction partners ("prey") of the bait are co-packaged into the nascent particle. The resulting VLPs, which bud from the cell and are released into the culture medium, contain a snapshot of the bait’s interactome at the time of particle formation. These VLPs can be efficiently purified using surface-displayed tags (e.g., FLAG-tagged VSV-G glycoprotein) in a single-step immunocapture protocol, eliminating the need for ultracentrifugation.

A key innovation of the present invention is the use of artificial VLPs—non-infectious, non-replicating particles engineered solely for complex entrapment—that combine the self-assembly properties of viral structural proteins with modular bait-recruiting architectures. This system enables the unbiased discovery of novel interactors, the validation of known interactions, and the deconvolution of small molecule targets, including membrane proteins and weak binders, with high specificity and reproducibility. Advantages include preservation of native complex integrity, compatibility with dynamic stimulation (e.g., cytokine treatment), reduced background through stringent control filtering, and applicability to both binary and proteome-wide interaction screens.

As used herein, the term “virus-like particle” or “VLP” refers to a non-infectious, self-assembling nanostructure that mimics the morphology of a native virus but lacks viral genomic material and therefore cannot replicate. VLPs are typically composed of one or more viral structural proteins, such as capsid or matrix proteins, and may incorporate heterologous proteins or domains on their surface or within their interior. A “VLP-forming polypeptide” is a polypeptide capable of multimerizing and driving the formation of VLPs when expressed in a suitable host cell; exemplary VLP-forming polypeptides include retroviral Gag proteins, such as HIV-1 p55 Gag.

A “fusion protein” is a single polypeptide chain created by joining two or more genes that originally coded for separate proteins, resulting in a chimeric protein that retains functional properties of each constituent domain. A “polypeptide” is a polymer of amino acid residues linked by peptide bonds, which may be naturally occurring or synthetically produced, and may be modified post-translationally. A “fusion construct” is a nucleic acid molecule encoding a fusion protein, typically comprising operably linked sequences encoding a VLP-forming polypeptide and a bait or recruiting element.

A “recruiting element” is a protein domain or molecule that specifically binds a small molecule or another protein, thereby serving as an intermediary to bring a target into proximity with the VLP scaffold. A “bait” is a protein or molecule of interest whose interaction partners are to be identified; in the context of the invention, the bait is either directly fused to a VLP-forming polypeptide or indirectly recruited via a recruiting element. A “small molecule” refers to a low molecular weight organic compound, typically less than 900 Daltons, which may be a drug, metabolite, or synthetic probe.

The phrase “interacts with” means that two molecules associate directly or indirectly through physical contact, whether transient or stable, and includes both binary and multi-component complex formation. “Recruited to” indicates that a molecule is brought into the vicinity of or incorporated into a VLP through specific or avidity-driven binding to a component of the particle.

VLPs according to the invention are derived from retroviral structural proteins, particularly HIV-1 Gag, but may also be based on other viral systems (e.g., hepatitis B core antigen, HPV L1) or engineered artificial scaffolds. Modifications to the VLP-forming polypeptide may include truncations, point mutations, or insertions to modulate particle size, stability, or cargo capacity, provided that self-assembly is retained. Fusion protein embodiments include N-terminal or C-terminal fusions of bait proteins to Gag, with optional linkers to preserve folding and function. VLP-forming polypeptide embodiments encompass wild-type and mutant forms that support particle release and cargo entrapment.

Small molecule embodiments include therapeutic agents such as simvastatin, tamoxifen, and reversine, which are used in conjunction with recruiting elements like eDHFR fused to Gag. Recruiting element embodiments include engineered binding domains (e.g., FKBP, DHFR) that selectively bind bivalent ligands comprising the small molecule linked to a high-affinity anchor (e.g., methotrexate). Bait embodiments include signaling proteins (e.g., MYD88, FADD), enzymes (e.g., HMGCR), and receptors.

VLP composition includes the VLP-forming polypeptide, the bait or recruiting element, entrapped prey proteins, and optionally envelope glycoproteins (e.g., VSV-G) for pseudotyping and purification. Fusion construct composition comprises regulatory elements (e.g., SRα promoter), coding sequences, and epitope tags (e.g., FLAG, MYC, E-tag) for detection and purification.

VLP-forming polypeptides interact via multimerization domains to form the particle shell. Recruiting elements interact with bivalent small molecules to tether drug targets to the VLP interior. Bait proteins interact with endogenous prey partners in the cytosol, which are then co-encapsulated. The resulting “VLP entrapped complex” is a stable assembly of bait, prey, and VLP scaffold, protected from degradation.

VLP formation occurs at the plasma membrane of transfected mammalian cells (e.g., HEK293T), where Gag multimerizes and buds outward, encapsulating cytosolic content. Composition variations include co-expression of untagged Gag to reduce bait overexpression artifacts, or alternative envelope proteins for cell-type-specific targeting. Prey protein embodiments include cytosolic, membrane-associated, and ER-resident proteins, but generally exclude organelle-confined proteins unless the VLP system is adapted.

Detection methods include Western blotting with epitope-specific antibodies. Analysis methods encompass mass spectrometry (e.g., LC-MS/MS), spectral counting, and bioinformatic filtering against control datasets. Purification methods involve antibody-coated beads (e.g., anti-FLAG Dynabeads) capturing VSV-G-tagged particles from culture supernatant. Enrichment and isolation follow standard immunoprecipitation protocols with mild wash buffers to preserve complexes.

Analysis variations include quantitative comparison of spectral counts, use of false discovery rate (FDR) thresholds, and orthogonal validation (e.g., MASPIT, co-IP).

### EXAMPLES

Plasmid generation involved PCR amplification of HIV-1 p55 Gag from pCMV-dR8.74 and In-Fusion cloning into bait expression vectors (e.g., pMET7-GAG-Ras). Gateway-compatible constructs enabled high-throughput cloning of bait and prey ORFs with N-terminal tags. VLP production was carried out in HEK293T cells via calcium phosphate or PEI transfection, with co-expression of Gag-bait, prey, VSV-G, and FLAG-VSV-G. Supernatants were harvested 24–32 hours post-transfection, cleared by low-speed centrifugation, and filtered.

VLP analysis by Western blot confirmed bait incorporation and prey co-precipitation using anti-p24, anti-FLAG, and anti-E-tag antibodies. Mass spectrometry analysis involved FLAG peptide elution, detergent removal, trypsin digestion, and LC-MS/MS on a Q Exactive instrument, with Mascot searches against human, bovine, and viral databases.

In VLP trapping of simvastatin binders, cells expressing Gag-eDHFR were treated with MTX-PEG-simvastatin. Particles were purified and analyzed by MS, revealing specific enrichment of HMGCR and SQLE. Results demonstrated successful capture of known and putative cholesterol pathway targets.

### Example 1

VLP trapping of simvastatin binders was performed by transfecting HEK293T cells with Gag-eDHFR, VSV-G constructs, and treating with 1 µM MTX-PEG-simvastatin. Control experiments used DMSO. After VLP purification and trypsin digestion, LC-MS/MS identified proteins consistently enriched over 19 control Virotrap experiments and 4 DMSO-treated eDHFR controls. Protein identification methods relied on ≥2 unique peptides in ≥2 biological replicates, with FDR <1%. Results confirmed HMGCR as the primary target and revealed SQLE as a novel interactor, suggesting a broader impact of simvastatin on cholesterol biosynthesis.

### Example 2

A viral trapping experiment was performed using Gag-FADD as bait. HEK293T cells were transfected, VLPs purified, and MS analysis identified CASP8 as a high-confidence interactor, consistent with known death-inducing signaling complex (DISC) components. Background proteins (e.g., actin, albumin) were filtered using the 19-experiment control list.

### Example 3

A viral trapping experiment using Gag-A20 as bait under TNFα stimulation (300 IU/mL) resulted in specific enrichment of RIPK1, TRADD, TNFRSF1A, and TNF, confirming dynamic recruitment to the activated NF-κB signaling complex. Unstimulated controls lacked these components.

### Example 4

A MASPIT assay protocol was employed to confirm the interaction between tamoxifen and HSD17B4. HEK293T cells were co-transfected with eDHFR bait, HSD17B4 prey, and a luciferase reporter. Treatment with MTX-tamoxifen induced significant luciferase activity compared to DMSO, validating the Virotrap-identified interaction in an orthogonal, functional assay.
