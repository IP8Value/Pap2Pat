# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention resides in the field of ophthalmology and ocular surface therapeutics, particularly in the prevention and treatment of ocular surface damage associated with oxidative stress and other environmental insults. More specifically, the invention relates to the use of dynamin inhibitors, such as Dynasore and its analogues including Dyngo-4a, for protecting the ocular surface epithelium and its glycocalyx barrier from damage caused by oxidative stress. The invention further encompasses pharmaceutical compositions comprising such compounds for topical administration to the eye, as well as methods for preventing or reducing vital dye uptake—a clinical marker of ocular surface compromise—by maintaining the integrity of the plasma membrane and apical glycocalyx of corneal and conjunctival epithelial cells. The invention also includes the identification of clusterin as a homeostatic protein that may be used in combination with or as an alternative to dynamin inhibitors for ocular surface protection. This field intersects with molecular pharmacology, epithelial biology, and dry eye disease therapeutics, offering novel approaches to preserve ocular surface health in conditions such as dry eye disease, solution-induced corneal staining (SICS), photokeratitis, and other disorders characterized by epithelial barrier disruption.

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

The ocular surface is a complex, highly organized structure composed of the stratified squamous epithelia of the cornea and conjunctiva, overlaid by a tear film that provides lubrication, nourishment, and defense. These epithelial layers are continuously renewed through a process wherein basal progenitor cells divide and their progeny migrate upward while undergoing terminal differentiation. As cells reach the apical layer, they flatten, develop specialized membrane folds known as microplicae, and express membrane-associated mucins such as MUC16. MUC16, in turn, binds oligomers of the lectin LGALS3 (galectin-3) to form a dense, organized glycocalyx that functions as a critical transcellular barrier against pathogen invasion and dye penetration. Concurrently, tight junctions between adjacent cells create a paracellular barrier, collectively ensuring the impermeability of the ocular surface to exogenous substances. This dual barrier system is essential for maintaining ocular surface homeostasis and optical clarity.

Cell renewal on the ocular surface is rapid, with complete turnover occurring every 5–7 days in humans. During this process, terminally differentiated apical cells gradually lose metabolic activity and are shed via desquamation, a non-inflammatory form of cell loss. However, exposure to environmental stressors—including ultraviolet radiation, allergens, microbial agents, and chemical irritants—can disrupt this delicate balance, leading to premature barrier breakdown, increased desquamation, and epithelial damage. Such damage is a hallmark of dry eye disease (keratoconjunctivitis sicca), a prevalent condition affecting over 20% of the population in developed regions, characterized by tear film instability and ocular surface inflammation.

Clinically, ocular surface damage is commonly assessed using vital dyes such as fluorescein and rose bengal. Fluorescein, a hydroxyxanthene dye, was first introduced in 1882 and remains a standard tool for evaluating corneal epithelial integrity. Rose bengal, derived from fluorescein, gained prominence in the 1930s for its ability to reveal “punctate” staining patterns in dry eye patients. Despite decades of use, the mechanism by which these dyes enter damaged epithelial cells remains incompletely understood. Early studies suggested that rose bengal uptake is blocked by intact mucins, implicating glycocalyx disruption as a prerequisite for staining. In contrast, fluorescein penetrates cells independently of mucin presence, indicating that plasma membrane integrity—not the glycocalyx—is the primary barrier against fluorescein uptake.

Fluorescein hyperstaining, observed as intense intracellular fluorescence in individual superficial cells, has been linked to sublethal cellular stress and early apoptosis. Notably, this process is active and energy-dependent, as it is inhibited at low temperatures or in dead cells. Recent evidence indicates that damaging stresses, such as oxidative stress or exposure to multipurpose contact lens solutions, increase the number of hyperfluorescent cells, suggesting that vital dye uptake reflects a state of compromised but viable cellular function. This phenomenon, termed solution-induced corneal staining (SICS) when associated with contact lens care products, underscores the clinical relevance of understanding dye uptake mechanisms.

A prevailing hypothesis posits that vital dye uptake occurs via endocytic vesicles formed during plasma membrane repair processes triggered by sublethal injury. Endocytosis, a fundamental cellular mechanism for internalizing extracellular material, is mediated by clathrin- or caveolin-dependent pathways, both of which require dynamin GTPases for vesicle scission. Inhibitors of these pathways, such as chlorpromazine (clathrin inhibitor), genistein (caveolin inhibitor), and Dynasore (a broad dynamin inhibitor), have been used to probe endocytic involvement in dye uptake. However, the applicability of findings from monolayer cell cultures—where cells are undifferentiated and lack a functional glycocalyx—to the physiologically relevant stratified and differentiated ocular surface epithelium remains questionable.

Moreover, the concentration of fluorescein used in clinical settings influences staining patterns, with higher concentrations potentially masking subtle barrier defects. The relationship between dye concentration, cellular metabolism, and barrier integrity is therefore complex. While initial studies supported an endocytosis-mediated mechanism, emerging data from more physiologically accurate models challenge this view, suggesting instead that dye entry may result from transient plasma membrane permeabilization due to oxidative lipid peroxidation, without requiring active vesicular transport. This alternative hypothesis necessitates reevaluation of therapeutic strategies aimed at modulating ocular surface responses to stress.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a novel method for protecting the ocular surface from damage caused by oxidative stress and other environmental insults by administering a dynamin inhibitor, such as Dynasore or its analogue Dyngo-4a, to the ocular surface. Contrary to prior assumptions that such compounds act solely by inhibiting endocytosis, the invention discloses that Dynasore and related compounds exert a protective effect by preserving the structural and functional integrity of both the plasma membrane and the apical glycocalyx of corneal and conjunctival epithelial cells. This protection prevents the uptake of vital dyes such as fluorescein and rose bengal, which are clinically used markers of ocular surface damage.

The invention further encompasses a pharmaceutical composition comprising an effective amount of a dynamin inhibitor, optionally in combination with a homeostatic protein such as clusterin, formulated for topical ophthalmic administration. The composition may be delivered as eye drops, gels, ointments, or sustained-release formulations, and is designed to maintain ocular surface barrier function under conditions of stress, thereby preventing or mitigating diseases such as dry eye, SICS, and other forms of ocular surface epitheliopathy.

A key advantage of the invention is its ability to intervene prophylactically or therapeutically in ongoing disease. Although Dynasore does not reverse existing damage when applied after insult, it effectively protects newly differentiating epithelial cells as they migrate to the surface during natural turnover. Thus, chronic administration can gradually resolve persistent ocular surface disease by ensuring that emerging cells are shielded from stress-induced damage. Additionally, the invention reveals that the protective mechanism of Dynasore is independent of its classical role in endocytosis inhibition, pointing instead to modulation of mitochondrial dynamics, plasma membrane cholesterol organization, or actin cytoskeleton stability—pathways that warrant further investigation but already support therapeutic utility.

The invention also introduces the use of clusterin, a secreted chaperone protein, as a complementary or alternative protective agent. Clusterin binds to damaged epithelial surfaces and promotes sealing of membrane breaches, thereby reinforcing barrier function. Recombinant human clusterin, or polypeptides substantially identical thereto, may be incorporated into the disclosed compositions to enhance ocular surface resilience.

In summary, the invention transforms the understanding of vital dye uptake from a passive indicator of damage to a dynamic process that can be modulated therapeutically. By targeting the root cause of barrier failure—oxidative stress-induced membrane and glycocalyx disruption—rather than merely suppressing symptoms, the invention offers a mechanistically grounded approach to preserving ocular surface health.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

Vital dyes, as used herein, refer to water-soluble compounds such as fluorescein, rose bengal, and trypan blue that selectively stain compromised or non-viable cells of the ocular surface while being excluded by healthy, intact epithelia. The staining mechanism of these dyes has long been attributed to loss of membrane integrity; however, recent evidence indicates that uptake can occur in living, metabolically active cells undergoing sublethal stress. The invention elucidates that this uptake is not primarily mediated by endocytic vesicles, as previously hypothesized, but rather results from transient increases in plasma membrane permeability due to oxidative damage, coupled with subtle disruption of the glycocalyx barrier.

The oxidative stress model employed in the invention utilizes tert-butyl hydroperoxide (tBHP) to induce sublethal damage in human corneal epithelial cells and ex vivo mouse eyes. This model recapitulates key features of dry eye disease, including increased vital dye uptake, phosphatidylserine externalization (an early apoptotic marker), and shedding of glycocalyx components such as LGALS3. Importantly, the model distinguishes between monolayer cultures—which lack differentiation and glycocalyx—and stratified, differentiated cultures that better mimic the in vivo ocular surface. In monolayer cells, oxidative stress stimulates both endocytosis and vital dye uptake, and both processes are inhibited by clathrin and caveolin inhibitors. However, in stratified and differentiated cultures, oxidative stress does not stimulate endocytosis, yet vital dye uptake still occurs and is only inhibited by Dynasore and its analogue Dyngo-4a, suggesting an alternative mechanism of action.

Dynasore, a cell-permeable inhibitor of dynamin GTPases, was originally developed to block clathrin- and caveolin-mediated endocytosis. Surprisingly, the invention demonstrates that Dynasore’s efficacy in preventing vital dye uptake in physiologically relevant models is not due to endocytosis inhibition. Instead, Dynasore protects against oxidative stress-induced cytotoxicity, as evidenced by reduced trypan blue uptake and preserved metabolic activity in MTT/WST-1 assays. Furthermore, Dynasore prevents the stress-induced shedding of LGALS3 from the glycocalyx, as shown by Western blot analysis of conditioned media, without affecting genomic DNA release, thereby confirming that protection is at the level of the cell surface rather than through inhibition of cell lysis.

The term “treating” or “treatment,” as used herein, includes both prophylactic and therapeutic interventions aimed at preventing, reducing, or resolving ocular surface damage. “Prevent” or “inhibit” refers to the reduction in incidence, severity, or progression of damage when a compound is administered prior to or concurrently with a stressor. An “effective amount” is that quantity of a dynamin inhibitor or clusterin sufficient to produce a measurable protective effect on the ocular surface, typically ranging from 15 to 80 µM for Dynasore in topical formulations.

Pharmaceutical carriers suitable for ophthalmic delivery include buffered saline, hydrogels, liposomes, and mucoadhesive polymers that enhance residence time on the ocular surface. The term “protein” encompasses naturally occurring or recombinant polypeptides, while “isolated polypeptide or protein” refers to a molecule that has been separated from its native cellular environment. “Substantially the same” denotes polypeptides sharing at least 90% amino acid sequence identity with human clusterin and exhibiting equivalent functional activity.

Clusterin, also known as apolipoprotein J, is a secreted heterodimeric glycoprotein encoded by the CLU gene. Elevated levels of CLU are observed in human inflammatory disorders, where it acts as an extracellular chaperone to stabilize stressed membranes. In the ocular context, clusterin binds to damaged epithelial surfaces and promotes resealing, thereby restoring barrier function. The invention utilizes recombinant human clusterin, particularly isoform 2 preproprotein (UniProt Q14108-2), produced in mammalian expression systems to ensure proper folding and glycosylation. Polypeptides substantially identical to human clusterin may also be employed.

Pharmaceutical compositions of the invention may combine Dynasore or Dyngo-4a with clusterin for synergistic protection. Formulations are optimized for topical administration, with pH adjusted to 6.5–7.5 and osmolarity matched to tears. Dosage regimens may involve once- to four-times-daily instillation, with drug concentrations maintained at levels sufficient to protect differentiating epithelial cells during their 5–7 day migration to the surface. Maintenance therapy is envisioned for chronic conditions such as dry eye, where continuous protection supports epithelial renewal and long-term resolution of disease.

### EXAMPLE 1

The HUGO nomenclature system is used throughout to designate genes and their protein products. The experimental model employed human corneal limbal epithelial (HCLE) cells, a telomerase-immortalized line authenticated by STR profiling and marker expression, and wild-type C57Bl/6J mice approved under USC IACUC protocol #11412. The study design compared unstressed controls to cultures treated with tBHP (3–10 mM) for 2–3 hours, with or without endocytic inhibitors. Statistical analysis used Student’s t-test (P < 0.05) on triplicate data.

Oxidative stress was selected as the damaging stimulus due to its established role in dry eye pathogenesis. tBHP was prepared as an aqueous stock and diluted into culture medium. HCLE cells were grown as monolayers or differentiated for 7 days in high-calcium medium. Inhibitors—chlorpromazine (8 µg/mL), genistein (200 µM), Dynasore (40–80 µM), and Dyngo-4a (15 µM)—were added concurrently with tBHP. Vital dye assays used sodium fluorescein or 0.05% rose bengal, with uptake quantified by plate reader.

Differentiation was confirmed by rose bengal exclusion and phase-contrast microscopy. Mouse eyes were enucleated post-CO2 euthanasia and cultured in K-SFM. Apoptosis was assessed via Annexin V and TUNEL staining; endocytosis via Alexa Fluor 568-transferrin uptake; cytotoxicity via MTT/WST-1 and trypan blue exclusion; glycocalyx integrity via jacalin lectin binding and LGALS3 Western blotting; and cell lysis via ACTB PCR.

Results showed that oxidative stress increased fluorescein hyperstaining in monolayers, linked to early apoptosis but not cell death. In stratified cultures, stress reduced glycocalyx barrier function and increased diffuse fluorescein uptake, but without cytoplasmic concentration. Endocytosis was stimulated in monolayers but not in stratified cells. Only Dynasore and Dyngo-4a blocked dye uptake in differentiated cultures, despite lack of endocytosis stimulation. In mouse eyes, Dynasore prevented dye uptake only when co-administered with tBHP, not when added post-stress, refuting an endocytic mechanism.

Dynasore protected against metabolic decline and plasma membrane damage in monolayers and reduced LGALS3 shedding in stratified cultures, confirming direct cytoprotection. The invention concludes that Dynasore preserves ocular surface integrity by mitigating oxidative damage, offering a promising therapeutic strategy for ocular surface diseases. Future studies will dissect whether protection is mediated via dynamin-dependent mitochondrial stabilization or dynamin-independent effects on membrane architecture.
