# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates generally to solar energy systems and, more particularly, to a solar concentrator battery system incorporating an advanced image-based sun position sensing apparatus for high-accuracy dual-axis solar tracking. The invention provides an integrated solution that enhances the efficiency of photovoltaic (PV) and high-concentration photovoltaic (HCPV) systems by precisely aligning solar collectors with the sun’s position throughout the day. Unlike conventional flat-plate PV installations, which remain stationary and capture only diffuse and direct sunlight incident at suboptimal angles, the disclosed system dynamically adjusts the orientation of solar modules using real-time visual feedback derived from captured solar images. This approach enables maximization of energy yield—particularly critical in HCPV applications where even minor angular deviations can drastically reduce power output due to the narrow acceptance angle of concentrated optics. The invention is especially suited for deployment in regions with high solar irradiance, where achieving levelized costs of electricity competitive with fossil fuel-based generation requires not only efficient conversion but also robust and reliable tracking mechanisms capable of operating under variable atmospheric conditions, including partial cloud cover and fluctuating light intensities. By integrating optical, mechanical, and computational components into a cohesive tracking architecture, the present invention addresses longstanding limitations in sensor accuracy, environmental robustness, and system stability, thereby facilitating broader adoption of solar concentrator technologies in both utility-scale and distributed energy applications.

## DESCRIPTION OF THE RELATED ART

Sun tracking systems are essential for optimizing the performance of solar energy harvesting devices, particularly in high-concentration photovoltaic (HCPV) systems that rely exclusively on direct normal irradiance. To maintain peak efficiency, such systems require continuous alignment with the sun within fractions of a degree. Traditional approaches employ either open-loop control based on astronomical algorithms or closed-loop control using physical sun position sensors. While open-loop systems avoid reliance on real-time sensing, they suffer from cumulative errors due to mechanical drift, installation misalignment, and atmospheric refraction effects. Closed-loop systems, therefore, are preferred for high-precision applications. Historically, these have utilized bar-shadow type photosensors comprising a central occluding bar surrounded by four quadrant photodiodes. The differential current outputs from opposing diodes indicate angular deviation from the sun’s center, enabling corrective actuation. However, this design exhibits significant drawbacks: sensitivity degrades under low-light conditions such as cloudy skies, leading to tracking failure; manufacturing tolerances cause characteristic mismatches among photodiodes, introducing systematic bias; and the limited dynamic range restricts operational flexibility. Subsequent improvements introduced four-quadrant light sensors paired with pinhole apertures to mitigate mismatch issues, yet these remain vulnerable to reduced irradiance and lack the ability to distinguish the sun from bright ambient reflections or artificial light sources. More recently, image-based sun sensors using charge-coupled devices (CCDs) or complementary metal-oxide-semiconductor (CMOS) imagers have emerged, offering superior immunity to weather variations by capturing actual solar imagery. Nevertheless, prior art implementations often rely on commercial off-the-shelf webcams without tailored optical conditioning, resulting in insufficient image resolution, poor contrast, or inadequate field-of-view control. Furthermore, existing image-processing algorithms frequently fail to robustly isolate the solar disk under partial occlusion or high-noise conditions, compromising tracking fidelity. These limitations collectively hinder the reliability and accuracy required for next-generation solar concentrators, necessitating a novel integrated sensing and control architecture that overcomes the deficiencies of conventional sun position detection methodologies.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides an apparatus for sensing the sun’s position through real-time image acquisition and processing, enabling high-accuracy solar tracking for concentrator photovoltaic systems. The core of the invention is an image-based sun position sensor that captures full-color digital images of the sky and employs a multi-stage computational pipeline to identify, isolate, and precisely locate the solar disk. The apparatus comprises a full-color image acquiring unit, which includes an optical assembly and an image sensing element such as a high-resolution CMOS or CCD sensor, configured to receive sunlight through a controlled aperture. Coupled to this is a color pattern conversion unit that transforms the acquired RGB image data into a hue-saturation-lightness (HSL) color space, thereby decoupling luminance from chromatic information and enhancing robustness against intensity fluctuations. An object recognition unit then applies adaptive thresholding to the lightness channel to generate a binary representation wherein the sun appears as a distinct bright region against a dark background. This binary image is processed by an object boundary detecting unit, which utilizes edge-detection algorithms—such as the Sobel operator—to delineate the perimeter of the solar disk. A critical innovation lies in the article circle center benchmark unit, which implements a geometric three-point circle method to compute the centroid of the detected solar boundary with sub-pixel accuracy, even when the disk is partially obscured. The azimuth/elevation angle difference calculating unit compares this computed center with the optical axis reference point (typically the image frame center) to determine angular offsets in two orthogonal axes. Finally, an output driving unit translates these offset values into control signals for actuators in a dual-axis tracking mechanism, effecting realignment of the solar collector toward the sun. The entire system operates in a closed-loop fashion, continuously updating position estimates and drive commands to maintain optimal alignment under diverse environmental conditions, including partial cloud cover and varying solar elevation angles.

## DESCRIPTION OF THE PREFERRED EMBODIMENTS

In a preferred embodiment, the apparatus is constructed as a compact, self-contained module designed for integration with dual-axis solar trackers. The schematic architecture comprises an image position sensing mechanism housed within a protective casing, connected via signal lines to a control mechanism that executes the image processing pipeline and generates actuator commands. The image position sensing mechanism begins with a light entrance hole, which serves as the primary aperture limiting stray light ingress. Immediately behind this aperture resides an optical unit featuring a custom-designed reflecting Cassegrain telescope. This telescope incorporates two concave mirrors, one convex mirror, and a right-angle prism arranged to fold the optical path, thereby reducing the overall length to approximately 10 centimeters while providing adjustable magnification between 5× and 15×. An eyepiece is positioned above the prism to focus the enlarged solar image onto the image sensing element, which may be a high-resolution webcam such as the Logitech C920 (2304 × 1536 pixels) or a lower-resolution alternative like the WEICHU WA-306 (640 × 480 pixels), depending on application requirements. To manage intense solar irradiance and enhance image contrast, the optical path includes a light reducing plate—implemented as a neutral density filter—and a filtering plate that blocks infrared wavelengths beyond 760 nm, protecting the sensor and improving solar disk definition. The entire optical assembly is mounted within a rigid casing equipped with a lateral plate for structural support and an access hole facilitating maintenance or component replacement. The signal line transmits raw image data from the sensing element to the control mechanism, which is implemented on an embedded processor or microcontroller. Within the control mechanism, the full-color image acquiring unit digitizes and buffers incoming frames. The color pattern conversion unit executes real-time transformation from RGB to HSL space using normalized intensity values. The object recognition unit applies a tunable lightness threshold—typically between 0.6 and 0.9 on a normalized scale—to binarize the image, effectively isolating the sun. The object boundary detecting unit then computes gradient magnitudes across the binary image to extract edge contours. The article circle center benchmark unit samples non-collinear points along this contour and solves the perpendicular bisector equations to determine the circle center, aggregating multiple estimates to reject outliers. The resulting coordinates feed into the azimuth/elevation angle difference calculating unit, which maps pixel offsets to angular deviations using pre-calibrated resolution factors (e.g., 0.0017° per pixel for the 15×/high-res configuration). The output driving unit converts these angles into pulse-width modulation (PWM) signals or step commands for azimuth and elevation motors, completing the feedback loop that ensures continuous, high-fidelity solar tracking.
