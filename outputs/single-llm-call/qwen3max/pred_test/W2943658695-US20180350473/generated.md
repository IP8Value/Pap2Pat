# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD

The present invention relates to monitoring ionizing radiation systems, and more specifically to systems and methods for real-time, non-invasive monitoring of nuclear reactor operations using neutron detection at stand-off distances. The invention provides a novel approach to nuclear safeguards by enabling the verification of reactor fuel composition, power output, and operational integrity through the measurement of neutrons escaping from the reactor core and detected outside the reactor shielding. This technology is particularly suited for application in research reactors, small modular reactors (SMRs), and other nuclear facilities where traditional safeguards measures are impractical or insufficient due to design constraints, such as sealed cores or continuous refueling capabilities. The invention supports international nuclear non-proliferation objectives by offering a robust, economical, and scalable method for detecting undeclared changes in fissile material inventory, including potential diversion or misuse of nuclear materials.

## BACKGROUND

Nuclear reactor safeguards are essential mechanisms employed by international regulatory bodies, such as the International Atomic Energy Agency (IAEA), to verify that nuclear materials are not diverted from peaceful uses to weapons programs. These safeguards aim to detect three primary threats: the diversion of declared nuclear materials, the misuse of declared facilities for undeclared production of fissile isotopes like plutonium, and the existence of undeclared nuclear facilities or activities. Traditional safeguards at power reactors often rely on physical inspections, tamper-indicating seals, video surveillance, and periodic inventory verification. However, these methods are less effective for non-traditional reactor types, including research reactors and SMRs, which frequently feature highly enriched uranium fuels, online refueling, limited inspector access, and sealed core designs that preclude direct fuel sampling.

Existing safeguards systems face significant limitations in providing real-time, continuous monitoring of reactor core conditions. While anti-neutrino detection has emerged as a promising technique for remote reactor monitoring—leveraging the fact that anti-neutrinos are emitted during beta decay of fission products and escape the reactor unimpeded—this approach suffers from low interaction cross-sections, requiring massive, expensive detectors and long integration times to achieve meaningful signal-to-noise ratios. Moreover, anti-neutrino detectors are sensitive to background noise from cosmic rays and natural radioactivity, and their deployment is often constrained by logistical and cost considerations, especially in resource-limited settings.

Prior art in reactor monitoring includes systems that use neutron detectors placed within or immediately adjacent to reactor shielding to infer power levels or spatial flux distributions. However, these systems are typically integrated into reactor instrumentation and are not designed for external, stand-off deployment. Furthermore, they do not exploit the relationship between neutron leakage and the isotopic composition of the reactor core for safeguards purposes. The scientific community has long recognized that neutrons produced by fission can escape reactor shielding, but the feasibility of using these escaping neutrons—at distances of tens of meters—for quantitative monitoring of fissile inventory has not been demonstrated or implemented in a practical safeguards context until the present invention.

## SUMMARY

The present invention introduces a system for monitoring a nuclear reactor that employs one or more neutron detectors positioned at a stand-off distance from the reactor core, outside the primary radiation shielding. The system includes a controller configured to receive signals from the neutron detectors and from reactor instrumentation, such as power output sensors. The controller monitors the neutron flux per unit of reactor power and establishes a baseline for this ratio under normal operating conditions. The system is capable of detecting aberrant changes in this ratio, which may indicate alterations in the fissile isotope inventory of the core, such as undeclared fuel removal or addition.

In a specific embodiment, the system includes a second neutron detector located at a different position relative to the reactor, enabling coordinated analysis of signals to discriminate against local environmental interferences or deliberate spoofing attempts. The controller is further configured to determine the isotopic concentration of fissile materials in the core by comparing the measured neutron flux per unit power against an expected value derived from reactor simulation models or historical data. When a statistically significant deviation is detected, the system generates an alert to notify safeguards personnel or automated response protocols.

The expected neutron flux per unit reactor power is established through either simulated modeling of the reactor’s neutron transport characteristics or empirical measurement during a known baseline operational period. The neutron detectors may be of various types, including boron-coated straw (BCS) detectors or B10+-lined helium-3 tubes, and are typically surrounded by a moderator—such as high-density polyethylene (HDPE)—to thermalize fast neutrons for efficient detection. The invention also encompasses a method of monitoring a reactor, comprising the steps of positioning at least one neutron detector at a stand-off location, measuring the neutron count rate, correlating it with reactor power, establishing a baseline ratio, and continuously comparing subsequent measurements to this baseline to detect anomalies.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

Reactor safeguards regimes, as mandated by international agreements, require reliable methods to verify that nuclear facilities operate within declared parameters. Illicit use of reactor facilities—such as producing weapons-grade plutonium under the guise of civilian research or medical isotope production—poses a persistent proliferation risk. Current safeguard monitoring systems, while adequate for large power reactors with frequent inspector access, are ill-suited for the growing number of research reactors and SMRs, which often operate with minimal oversight and complex fuel cycles. This creates a critical need for real-time, unattended monitoring technologies that can provide continuous verification without intrusive access.

Anti-neutrino detectors have been proposed as a solution, but their limitations—including low event rates, high cost, and susceptibility to background interference—render them impractical for widespread deployment. In contrast, the present invention introduces neutron detectors as a viable alternative. Neutrons, unlike anti-neutrinos, interact strongly with matter but can still escape reactor shielding in measurable quantities, especially at epithermal energies. By placing neutron detectors at a stand-off distance—defined as a location outside the reactor’s primary biological shielding but within approximately 100 meters of the core—the system captures a representative sample of these escaping neutrons.

The stand-off distance is carefully chosen to balance signal strength against background noise and practical deployment constraints. Potential configurations include single or multiple detectors arranged in an array around the reactor building, each positioned to view different angular sectors of the core. This multiplicity addresses unknowns that existed prior to the development of the present system, namely whether neutron leakage at such distances would be sufficient for meaningful measurement and whether the signal would correlate reliably with core properties. The discovery that neutron detection at stand-off distances is not only feasible but highly informative represents a foundational advance.

Neutron detectors offer several advantages over anti-neutrino detectors: they are smaller, less expensive, and achieve higher event rates due to the greater interaction probability of neutrons with detector materials. For example, at the National Research Universal (NRU) reactor, neutron detectors recorded signal-to-background ratios exceeding 10:1 even at 69 meters from the core. The neutron count rate \( n_{\text{det}} \) is proportional to the neutron population in the core, which in turn relates to the reactor’s thermal power \( P_{\text{tot}} \) through the equation \( P_{\text{tot}} = V \langle \phi \rangle \sum_i N_i \langle \sigma_{f,i} \rangle E_{f,i} \), where \( V \) is core volume, \( \langle \phi \rangle \) is average neutron flux, \( N_i \) is number density of fissile isotope \( i \), \( \sigma_{f,i} \) is its fission cross-section, and \( E_{f,i} \) is energy per fission. Rearranged, this yields \( \langle \phi \rangle / P_{\text{tot}} \propto 1 / \sum_i m_i \langle \sigma_{f,i} \rangle E_{f,i} / w_i \), showing that the neutron flux per unit power is inversely proportional to a weighted sum of fissile isotope masses.

This relationship enables the system to detect changes in isotopic composition. For instance, as U-235 burns up and Pu-239 builds up, the neutron flux per watt changes predictably due to differences in their fission cross-sections and energy release. Multiple detectors enhance reliability by allowing cross-validation; if one detector shows an anomaly while others do not, the event may be attributed to local interference rather than core changes. Smaller neutron detectors are advantageous for covert or distributed deployment, and their higher event rates enable faster anomaly detection compared to anti-neutrino systems.

The monitoring system 100 includes a nuclear reactor 102 with a core 104 containing fuel bundles 106. Radiation shielding 110 surrounds the core, but some neutrons 108a escape. Neutron detectors 120 are placed outside a stand-off perimeter 122, defined as the minimum distance ensuring safety and practicality. The system controller receives input from reactor power sensors and detector outputs, calibrates the system using initial baseline data, and models expected changes in fuel composition over time. It continuously compares measured neutron flux per unit power to the expected value, triggering alerts upon significant deviations.

Detectors such as BCS or B10+ types are used, often with HDPE moderators to convert fast neutrons to thermal energies for efficient capture. Signal processing components, including amplifiers and digital controllers, timestamp individual neutron events. Data acquisition systems bin these events into time-series histograms, which are analyzed alongside reactor power data. Experimental results at the NRU reactor confirmed a linear relationship between detector count rate and reactor power, and demonstrated that changes in fissile inventory—such as those caused by online refueling or target irradiation—produce measurable shifts in the neutron-to-power ratio.

The system operates as a compliance and audit tool, enabling independent verification of reactor operations. Simulations using Monte Carlo methods validate detector responses and predict neutron leakage under various core conditions. Environmental factors, such as building materials and overburden, are accounted for in baseline establishment. The scope of the invention is broad, encompassing various reactor types and detector configurations, and is not limited to the specific embodiments described.
