## FIELD OF THE DISCLOSURE

The present disclosure relates to compositions and methods for increasing crop yield, particularly in monocot plants such as maize. More specifically, the disclosure provides novel polynucleotides and polypeptides derived from the Zea mays ARGOS (ZAR1) gene, including functional allelic variants thereof, and recombinant expression cassettes encoding these sequences. The disclosure further encompasses transgenic plants engineered to express one or more ZAR1 alleles—either individually or in heterozygous allelic combinations—and methods of using such plants to enhance agronomic performance, including grain yield, drought tolerance, plant vigor, and stress resilience under variable environmental conditions. The invention also includes methods for modulating root, shoot, leaf, and reproductive organ development through controlled expression of ZAR1-derived sequences, thereby enabling precision breeding and biotechnological crop improvement strategies that synergize with conventional heterotic hybrid systems.

## BACKGROUND OF THE DISCLOSURE

Domestication of plants has been a cornerstone of human agricultural advancement, enabling the selection of desirable traits over millennia. Modern plant breeding builds upon this legacy by harnessing natural genetic variation to improve yield, stress tolerance, and other agronomic characteristics. Phenotypic variation observed in natural and cultivated plant populations arises from underlying genetic diversity, including single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs), insertions, deletions, and structural variants. The identification of specific genes that contribute to complex traits such as grain yield remains a central challenge, as these traits are typically quantitative—controlled by multiple loci with small individual effects and significantly influenced by environmental factors.

Transgenic manipulation of plant genomes offers a complementary approach to traditional breeding, allowing targeted introduction or modulation of gene function. Early successes in biotechnology focused on simple traits like insect resistance and herbicide tolerance, which are governed by single genes and exhibit consistent efficacy across environments. However, extending this success to complex agronomic traits requires deeper understanding of gene-environment interactions and the functional relevance of naturally occurring allelic variation. In elite breeding germplasm, favorable alleles—those conferring improved performance under target environmental conditions—are enriched through decades of selection. Heterosis, or hybrid vigor, further enhances trait expression when complementary alleles from distinct parental lines are combined in F1 hybrids, a principle widely exploited in crops like maize.

Breeding programs have historically selected high-yielding hybrids by evaluating performance across a target population of environments (TPE), recognizing that genotype-by-environment interactions critically influence trait outcomes. Similarly, for transgenic approaches targeting complex traits, transgene-by-environment interactions must be characterized to ensure reliable efficacy. The convergence of transgenic technology with insights from natural allelic diversity—particularly alleles already validated by breeding selection—offers a powerful strategy for sustainable yield improvement. This disclosure addresses this need by leveraging functional allelic variants of the ZAR1 gene, demonstrating that both native promoter and coding sequence differences contribute to differential expression and protein function, and that molecular stacking of heterozygous allelic combinations can recapitulate and enhance heterotic effects in transgenic contexts.

## BRIEF SUMMARY OF THE DISCLOSURE

The present disclosure introduces methods for optimizing transgene efficacy by utilizing functional allelic variants of yield-improving genes, particularly the Zea mays ARGOS1 (ZAR1) gene. Motivated by the observation that elite maize germplasm harbors distinct, breeding-selected alleles of ZAR1 partitioned between heterotic groups, the invention provides engineered constructs containing native promoter and coding sequences of these alleles—specifically the SS1 (Stiff Stalk) and NS1 (Non-Stiff Stalk) variants. The disclosure describes creating heterozygous allelic combinations through molecular stacking, wherein both SS1 and NS1 alleles are co-expressed in the same transgenic plant, mimicking the heterozygous state found in high-performing commercial hybrids.

These engineered transgenic plants exhibit enhanced agronomic performance, including increased grain yield, improved drought tolerance, reduced anthesis-silking interval, decreased kernel abortion, and greater plant vigor, particularly under abiotic stress conditions such as high planting density or water limitation. The invention further extends to genes from other plant species that are orthologous or functionally analogous to ZAR1, enabling broad applicability across crop species. Compositions provided include isolated polynucleotides encoding ZAR1 polypeptides, recombinant expression cassettes comprising native or synthetic promoters operably linked to ZAR1 coding sequences, and transgenic plant cells, tissues, and whole plants stably incorporating these constructs.

The disclosure also provides methods for controlling plant growth and development by modulating the expression or activity of yield improvement polypeptides. Specific embodiments include polynucleotides with at least 80% sequence identity to native ZAR1 sequences, expression cassettes designed for tissue-specific, inducible, or constitutive expression, and plant parts derived from transgenic events. By integrating insights from natural allelic variation and heterosis into transgenic design, the invention enables more predictable and robust yield improvements across diverse environmental conditions, bridging the gap between conventional breeding and modern biotechnology.

## EXAMPLES

### Example 1

Favorable alleles of maize growth-enhancing genes, particularly ZAR1, were identified through genetic association analysis in elite breeding germplasm. Over 400 inbred lines were genotyped, revealing two predominant haplotypes: haplotype 1 (NS1 allele) in Non-Stiff Stalk (NS) lines and haplotype 2 (SS1 allele) in Stiff Stalk (SS) lines. These alleles were associated with improved drought-tolerance traits, including reduced barrenness and increased kernel weight. Vectors were constructed containing the full-length coding sequences of each allele, driven either by the constitutive maize ubiquitin promoter or by their native promoter regions (~1.5 kb upstream). The native promoters exhibited haplotype-specific sequence variation, including indels in cis-regulatory regions, suggesting differential transcriptional regulation. Transgenic maize plants were generated for each construct, and transgene efficacy was evaluated in hybrid backgrounds across multiple environments. Plants expressing the SS1 allele showed robust growth enhancement but exhibited yield penalties in cooler, wetter environments, whereas NS1 allele expression conferred more stable performance across diverse conditions. These results confirmed that both coding and regulatory sequences contribute to functional allelic variation, providing a basis for selecting optimal alleles for transgenic deployment.

### Example 2

Different allele variants of ZAR1 were molecularly stacked to create heterozygous allelic combinations in transgenic maize. Constructs containing both SS1 and NS1 alleles—each with its native promoter and coding sequence—were co-transformed into maize embryos. Transgenic events were selected for single-copy integration and confirmed by PCR and Southern blot. Hemizygous and homozygous plants for each single allele, as well as the stacked heterozygous combination, were crossed to non-transgenic inbreds to generate hybrid test lines. Phenotypic attributes were measured across five field locations in 2011, including plant height, biomass, ear size, secondary ear growth, and grain yield. The heterozygous stack consistently outperformed single-allele transgenics, showing significant yield increases in four out of five locations, whereas single alleles showed positive but non-significant trends. Notably, the stacked plants exhibited enhanced plant vigor and productivity under high-density planting, with reduced tip kernel abortion and improved standability. Comparison with non-transgenic null segregants confirmed that the yield advantage was attributable to the transgenic alleles. Multi-location analysis demonstrated that the heterozygous combination provided greater stability and magnitude of effect, recapitulating the heterosis observed in conventional SS×NS hybrids.

### Example 3

Sequence variation in ZmARGOS1 (ZAR1) and ZmERECTA A was analyzed to understand functional divergence. Genomic DNA from SS1 and NS1 inbred lines was sequenced, revealing multiple SNPs and indels in both promoter and coding regions. Allele-specific expression analysis in F1 SS1/NS1 hybrids used RT-PCR primers flanking a 5′ UTR indel, producing distinct amplicon sizes (525 bp for SS1, 464 bp for NS1). Quantification showed biased expression favoring the SS1 allele in immature ear and seedling tissues, even under drought stress, indicating cis-regulatory differences. Protein alignment identified conserved motifs, including a leucine/methionine substitution in the core domain and an alanine/glycine change adjacent to a predicted flexible loop in SS1. Structural modeling suggested these alterations could affect protein folding or interaction partners, potentially explaining differential activity. These findings underscore that both expression level and protein function contribute to allelic effects, informing the design of optimized transgenes.

### Example 4

Immature maize embryos (Hi-II genotype) were prepared for transformation by surface sterilization and culture on callus induction medium. DNA constructs containing ZAR1 alleles fused to a DsRed visual marker (driven by aleurone-specific PromHvLTP2) were coated onto tungsten particles. Embryos were bombarded using a particle gun at 1100 psi, then transferred to selection medium containing bialaphos. After 4–6 weeks, transformed callus clones exhibiting red fluorescence were isolated. Regeneration was induced on media supplemented with benzylaminopurine and naphthaleneacetic acid. Resulting plants were screened for transgene integration and expression. Field trials under drought stress (water deprivation at flowering and grain fill) showed that ZAR1 transgenics maintained higher kernel-earing capacity and yield compared to controls. Meristem development was modulated, with increased meristematic activity observed histologically. Bombardment media contained MS salts, sucrose, and 2,4-D; culture media included L-proline and casein hydrolysate; regeneration medium featured silver nitrate to suppress ethylene responses.

### Example 5

Immature embryos were infected with Agrobacterium strain LBA4404 carrying ZAR1 T-DNA constructs. Co-cultivation occurred for 3 days on medium with acetosyringone to induce virulence genes. Embryos were then rested on antibiotic-free medium for 2 days to reduce bacterial overgrowth, followed by transfer to selection medium with cefotaxime (to kill Agrobacterium) and hygromycin (for transformant selection). After 4 weeks, hygromycin-resistant callus was subcultured and regenerated into whole plants using standard protocols. Transgenic status was confirmed by PCR and DsRed fluorescence. This method yielded a high frequency of single-copy integrations, facilitating downstream phenotypic analysis.

### Example 6

The function of ZmARGOS was tested using transgenic maize overexpressing ZAR1 under the maize ubiquitin promoter. RT-PCR confirmed transgene expression in leaf tissue at the V6 stage. Field evaluations across three years measured plant height, biomass accumulation, stem diameter, flowering date, and grain yield. Transgenics exhibited accelerated growth, reaching maturity faster than controls without altering flowering time. Biomass and grain yield were significantly increased in temperate and temperate-dry environments but reduced in cool, humid zones. Advanced generations (T3–T4) confirmed heritability of the trait, with consistent yield advantages under drought-prone conditions. These results validate ZAR1 as a potent modulator of complex agronomic traits with environment-dependent efficacy.

### Example 7

Somatic embryos were induced from soybean cotyledons on SHa medium with 2,4-D. Embryogenic suspension cultures were maintained in liquid medium with shaking. Particle gun bombardment delivered ZAR1 constructs with hygromycin resistance. Transformed tissue was selected on hygromycin-containing solid medium. Whole plants were regenerated via somatic embryogenesis, and transformed lines were propagated through recurrent embryogenic cultures. This protocol enabled efficient recovery of transgenic soybeans expressing maize ZAR1, demonstrating cross-species functionality.

### Example 8

Sunflower meristem tissues were transformed using embryonic axis explants from surface-sterilized seeds. Explants were bisected and cultured on GBA medium. Microprojectile bombardment or Agrobacterium co-cultivation introduced ZAR1 constructs. After selection on kanamycin, NPTII activity and transgene expression were assayed. Transformed shoots were grafted onto sunflower rootstock and acclimatized in the greenhouse. T0 plants showed sectorial transformation; progeny without chemical selection retained the transgene, confirming germline transmission. ARGOS activity in leaf extracts correlated with enhanced meristem development, indicating conserved function in dicots.

### Example 9

Variant nucleotide sequences of ARGOS/ERECTA were generated by altering amino acid sequences based on conserved domains. Protein alignments identified invariant residues (e.g., cysteines in disulfide bonds) and variable regions. Using a conservative substitution table, artificial sequences were designed with 80–100% amino acid identity to wild-type ZAR1. Perl scripts calculated percent identities after introducing substitutions like L→M or A→G. Variants with 80%, 85%, 90%, and 95% identity were synthesized and tested for activity in maize protoplasts. High-identity variants retained function, while lower-identity versions showed reduced efficacy, defining thresholds for functional conservation. These variants expand the toolkit for fine-tuning yield improvement traits.
