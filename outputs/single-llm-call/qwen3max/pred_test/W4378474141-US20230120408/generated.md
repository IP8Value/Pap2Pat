# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

### 1. Field of the Invention

The present invention relates generally to the field of superconducting thin film technology and, more specifically, to wafer-scale fabrication of magnesium diboride (MgB₂) thin films with controlled stoichiometry, enhanced superconducting properties, and tailored electrical characteristics suitable for high-frequency and quantum electronic applications. The invention further encompasses methods for producing such MgB₂ thin films on commercially viable substrates, including silicon wafers with buffer layers, through a combination of co-sputtering or layered deposition techniques followed by controlled thermal annealing under protective capping conditions. The resulting MgB₂ films exhibit high critical temperatures (Tc), tunable resistivity, low surface roughness, and uniform sheet resistance across large-area substrates, thereby enabling scalable integration into microwave resonators, kinetic inductance detectors, parametric amplifiers, and other superconducting microdevices.

### 2. Description of the Related Art

Conventional approaches to fabricating MgB₂ thin films have faced significant challenges that have hindered their practical implementation in commercial or scientific devices. Magnesium diboride is a promising superconductor due to its relatively high critical temperature of approximately 39 K in bulk form; however, translating this performance into thin-film architectures has proven difficult. Early attempts using in-situ magnetron sputtering often resulted in films with degraded superconducting properties—specifically, reduced Tc values below 25 K—due to oxidation, off-stoichiometry, and small grain sizes arising from the high vapor pressure and low sticking coefficient of magnesium at elevated deposition temperatures. Post-annealing processes were introduced to improve crystallinity and Tc, but these frequently led to excessive surface roughness (>10 nm rms), which compromises device yield and performance, particularly in nanoscale patterning. Moreover, magnesium evaporation during annealing above its melting point (~650 °C) caused compositional instability and void formation, further degrading film quality. Alternative strategies involving reactive sputtering or molecular beam epitaxy have been explored, yet they suffer from poor scalability, complex process control, or limited compatibility with standard semiconductor manufacturing infrastructure. Substrate selection has also posed limitations: while sapphire offers favorable lattice matching, oxygen diffusion from the substrate into the MgB₂ layer leads to interfacial degradation, especially in ultra-thin films. Silicon carbide provides excellent epitaxial growth conditions but is cost-prohibitive for large-scale production. Additionally, prior art lacks robust methodologies for tuning key electrical parameters such as resistivity and kinetic inductance without introducing impurities or relying on unstable gas-phase reactions, as seen in nitride-based superconductors like NbN or TiN. Consequently, there remains an unmet need for a reproducible, wafer-scale-compatible process capable of yielding smooth, stoichiometrically controlled, high-Tc MgB₂ thin films with engineered electromagnetic properties suitable for next-generation superconducting electronics.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a wafer comprising a superconducting magnesium diboride (MgB₂) film deposited on a substrate, wherein the film exhibits a sheet resistance uniformity of less than ±5% across a 100 mm diameter wafer, a thickness variation of less than ±3%, and a root-mean-square (rms) surface roughness of less than 1 nm. The MgB₂ film further demonstrates a room-temperature resistivity ranging between 25 and 100 microohm-centimeters (μΩ·cm), enabling precise control over kinetic inductance for microwave and quantum circuit design. In an alternative embodiment, the wafer comprises a superconducting MgB₂ film with a zero-resistance critical temperature (Tc,₀) exceeding 30 K, and in certain optimized configurations, achieving up to 37.2 K—the highest reported value for sputter-deposited MgB₂ films. A protective capping layer, composed of elemental boron or another high-melting-point, non-reactive material such as tantalum, is applied atop the as-deposited magnesium-boron precursor film prior to thermal annealing to prevent magnesium evaporation and maintain stoichiometric integrity. The method of making the MgB₂ film involves co-depositing magnesium and boron onto a buffered silicon substrate via radio-frequency (RF) magnetron sputtering at or near room temperature, optionally with a low-power RF substrate bias (e.g., 15 W) to enhance film density and reduce roughness. The atomic ratio of magnesium to boron in the precursor film is deliberately tuned—typically deviating from the ideal 1:2 stoichiometry—to achieve target post-anneal resistivity and Tc values. Deposition may be performed either by simultaneous co-sputtering or by sequential layering of magnesium and boron, with the latter approach facilitating enhanced grain growth upon annealing. Following deposition, the capped precursor film undergoes rapid thermal annealing at temperatures below 650 °C—preferably between 550 °C and 620 °C—for durations ranging from 30 seconds to 5 minutes, depending on the desired microstructure. This controlled annealing induces solid-state reaction between magnesium and boron to form phase-pure MgB₂ while preserving surface morphology. The resulting composition of matter is a polycrystalline or textured MgB₂ thin film exhibiting high critical current density (Jc), low microwave losses, and tunable kinetic inductance, suitable for integration into superconducting resonators, photon detectors, and quantum information processing circuits.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

### Technical Description

The invention begins with the deposition of magnesium and boron onto a suitable substrate, typically a high-resistivity silicon wafer coated with a thin inert buffer layer such as low-stress silicon nitride (Si₃N₄), deposited via low-pressure chemical vapor deposition (LPCVD) or plasma-enhanced CVD (PECVD). This buffer layer, preferably 30–100 nm thick, prevents undesirable reactions between magnesium and silicon, such as the formation of Mg₂Si, which would degrade superconducting performance. Magnesium and boron are deposited using RF magnetron sputtering from elemental targets under argon atmosphere at pressures between 1 and 2 mTorr. In one embodiment, co-sputtering is employed, wherein both magnesium and boron are simultaneously deposited at room temperature, with magnesium sputtering power varied between 50 W and 250 W while boron sputtering power is held constant at approximately 580 W to compensate for boron’s inherently low deposition rate due to its high melting point. To improve film smoothness, a low-level RF substrate bias of about 15 W is applied during deposition, reducing rms roughness from over 1.7 nm to below 0.5 nm without inducing excessive heating or etching that would disrupt stoichiometric control. Alternatively, magnesium and boron may be deposited as alternating nanoscale layers, with individual layer thicknesses selected to promote interdiffusion and large-grain MgB₂ formation during subsequent annealing. The Mg-to-B atomic ratio in the precursor film is precisely tuned—either by adjusting relative sputtering powers or layer thicknesses—to influence the final resistivity and Tc of the annealed MgB₂ film. For instance, boron-rich precursors yield higher resistivity post-anneal films due to residual unreacted boron acting as a scattering center, whereas magnesium-deficient compositions similarly increase resistivity through carrier depletion.

Following deposition, a capping layer is applied to the precursor film to suppress magnesium evaporation during thermal processing. Elemental boron is preferred as the capping material due to its high melting point (2076 °C), chemical inertness toward MgB₂, and ability to form a self-healing, viscous boron oxide layer above 450 °C that seals the surface without cracking. Tantalum may also be used but requires post-anneal removal due to its tendency to crack above 700 °C and potential interference with electrical measurements. The capping layer is typically 10–50 nm thick and deposited in situ without breaking vacuum to avoid oxidation. The capped structure is then subjected to rapid thermal annealing (RTA) using a system such as a Solaris 150 RTP, with ramp rates of 1–33 °C/sec and peak temperatures maintained below 650 °C—optimally between 580 °C and 620 °C—for hold times of 30 seconds to 2 minutes. During annealing, magnesium diffuses into adjacent boron layers, reacting to form MgB₂ while the capping layer confines volatile species. The resulting film is predominantly MgB₂, with minimal formation of higher borides such as MgB₄ or MgB₇, which are dielectric and do not significantly affect superconducting transport. After annealing, the sample is cooled under inert atmosphere or vacuum to room temperature. The capping layer is subsequently removed via argon ion milling or bromine-based plasma etching, revealing a smooth, phase-pure MgB₂ surface ready for device patterning.

The final composition of matter is a MgB₂ thin film exhibiting exceptional uniformity: sheet resistance varies by less than ±5% across a full 100 mm wafer, thickness deviation is under ±3%, and rms roughness is consistently below 1 nm—as illustrated in FIGS. 2A–2B, which depict atomic force microscopy (AFM) topographies of representative films. These properties enable high-yield lithographic definition of sub-micron features (down to 0.5 µm demonstrated). The film’s superconducting characteristics are highly tunable: Example 1 describes a film with uniform sheet resistance (±4%) and thickness (±2.5%) across 100 mm Si wafers, suitable for large-scale resonator arrays. Example 2 details a film with resistivity exceeding 100 μΩ·cm, achieved through boron-rich precursor stoichiometry, useful for high-kinetic-inductance circuits. Example 3 presents a film with Tc,₀ above 15 K, serving as a baseline for moderate-performance applications. Example 4 demonstrates a film with resistivity between 25–50 μΩ·cm, balancing low loss and adequate inductance for microwave transmission lines. Most notably, Example 5 achieves Tc,₀ of 37.2 K—the highest reported for sputtered MgB₂—by annealing alternating Mg/B layers under boron capping, albeit with increased roughness acceptable for small-active-area devices. The method of making the film includes selecting deposition parameters (power, bias, pressure), tuning Mg:B ratio, choosing capping material/thickness, setting annealing temperature/time, and optionally removing the cap post-anneal. Device fabrication proceeds via standard photolithography using maskless aligners, titanium-gold bilayer hardmasks for ion milling, and dielectric passivation (e.g., SiNₓ or AlN), culminating in functional superconducting microdevices such as coplanar waveguide resonators with internal quality factors (Qi) exceeding 10⁴ at 4.5 K.

## CONCLUSION

In conclusion, the preferred embodiment of the present invention provides a manufacturable, wafer-scale process for producing high-performance MgB₂ thin films with unprecedented control over stoichiometry, morphology, and electromagnetic properties. By combining room-temperature co-sputtering or layered deposition, boron capping, and optimized rapid thermal annealing on buffered silicon substrates, the invention overcomes longstanding barriers related to magnesium volatility, surface roughness, and stoichiometric instability. The resulting films exhibit high critical temperatures, tunable resistivity, low roughness, and excellent uniformity—attributes that collectively enable immediate deployment in advanced superconducting technologies, including high-temperature kinetic inductance detectors, quantum-limited amplifiers, and scalable quantum circuits. This approach leverages existing semiconductor infrastructure, ensuring compatibility with industrial fabrication standards while delivering superior performance metrics compared to conventional superconducting thin films.
