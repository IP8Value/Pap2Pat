# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNICAL FIELD

The present invention relates generally to the field of thermal management systems and, more specifically, to engineered surfaces for enhancing convective heat transfer while simultaneously reducing hydrodynamic drag in fluidic environments. The invention is particularly applicable to laminar and turbulent flows in heat exchangers, microchannel cooling systems, electronic thermal management devices, and other systems where efficient heat dissipation and low pumping power are critical design objectives. The disclosed technology leverages liquid-infused surface (LIS) architectures comprising substrates with precisely defined micro- or nano-scale grooves or cavities that are infused with a secondary immiscible liquid to create dynamic fluid-fluid interfaces. These interfaces facilitate slip at the wall, thereby reducing frictional losses, while enabling internal recirculatory convection within the surface texture that enhances conductive and convective heat transport across the boundary layer. The invention thus resides at the intersection of interfacial fluid dynamics, thermal engineering, and advanced materials science, providing a novel methodology for decoupling the traditionally antagonistic relationship between heat transfer augmentation and drag reduction.

## BACKGROUND

Efficient thermal management remains a persistent engineering challenge across numerous domains, including high-performance computing, aerospace systems, energy conversion, and biomedical devices. Conventional approaches to enhance heat transfer—such as surface roughening, extended surfaces (fins), or flow turbulators—typically incur significant penalties in terms of increased pressure drop and pumping power due to elevated wall shear stress. This trade-off arises because geometric modifications that increase surface area or disrupt thermal boundary layers also intensify momentum exchange near the wall, thereby increasing drag. Miniaturization of thermal systems offers another route to higher heat fluxes through increased surface-to-volume ratios, yet this too exacerbates viscous losses, often rendering such designs impractical for applications constrained by energy efficiency or space limitations. Prior art has explored superhydrophobic surfaces (SHS) as a means to reduce drag via trapped gas pockets that induce apparent slip; however, these suffer from poor thermal stability, limited durability under pressure or shear, and suboptimal thermal performance due to the extremely low thermal conductivity of gases. While some theoretical models have suggested that SHS or LIS could simultaneously improve heat transfer and reduce drag, these predictions often rely on idealized assumptions—such as isothermal solid textures or unity thermal conductivity ratios between phases—that are unattainable with real-world materials. Moreover, existing analytical frameworks neglect the contribution of internal convection within surface textures, particularly when the solid and infusing liquid possess comparable thermal conductivities. Consequently, there remains a critical need for a robust, manufacturable surface architecture that overcomes these limitations by actively harnessing internal fluid motion to augment heat transfer without sacrificing drag reduction benefits.

## BRIEF SUMMARY

The present invention provides a system and method for enhanced heat transfer with concurrent drag reduction by employing a substrate embedded with an array of grooves or cavities that are infused with a secondary liquid to form a liquid-infused surface (LIS). The system comprises a solid substrate having a structured topography defined by repeating groove features oriented transverse to the direction of external fluid flow. These grooves are filled with an infusing liquid that is immiscible with the working fluid flowing over the surface, creating stable liquid-liquid interfaces. Under an applied shear stress from the external flow, recirculatory vortices develop within the grooves, generating dispersive convection that significantly augments the total surface heat flux. The invention further provides a method for designing and operating such a system, which includes selecting substrate and infusing liquid materials with matched or favorable thermal conductivities, defining groove geometry (depth, width, pitch, and cross-sectional shape), and ensuring compatibility of fluid properties (viscosity, density, specific heat, and surface tension) to optimize both thermal performance and slip-induced drag reduction. Variations of the method include tailoring groove orientation (e.g., transverse, longitudinal, or hybrid patterns), adjusting the solid fraction of the surface, and integrating the LIS into thermal systems such as heat exchangers, microprocessors, or battery packs. The disclosed approach enables a net increase in heat transfer relative to a smooth reference surface while maintaining or even reducing hydraulic resistance, thereby achieving superior thermohydraulic efficiency unattainable by conventional surface modifications.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The principles underlying the present invention stem from the recognition that internal convection within the texture of a liquid-infused surface can serve as a potent mechanism for heat transfer enhancement, particularly when the thermal conductivities of the solid substrate and the infusing liquid are comparable. The scope of the invention encompasses any engineered surface wherein micro- or nano-scale cavities are filled with a liquid that forms a stable interface with an external working fluid, and wherein shear-driven recirculation within these cavities contributes meaningfully to the net heat flux. As used herein, “groove” refers to any recessed feature in the substrate surface, including but not limited to channels, pits, trenches, or dimples, regardless of cross-sectional geometry. “Infusing liquid” denotes a fluid phase that wets the groove walls and remains immiscible with the external working fluid, forming a distinct interface. “Dispersive convection” signifies time-averaged, spatially periodic fluid motion induced by surface heterogeneity, as opposed to random turbulent fluctuations. In exemplary embodiments, the system for increasing heat transfer comprises a planar or curved substrate fabricated from metals, polymers, ceramics, or composites, patterned with an array of transverse grooves having depths (k) ranging from 100 nanometers to 500 micrometers. The grooves may exhibit rectangular, U-shaped, V-shaped, rounded-corner, or pyramidal cross-sections, with widths (w) between 0.5k and 7k and pitches (p, center-to-center spacing) from 2k to 8k. The distance between adjacent grooves is selected to balance structural integrity with thermal performance, typically yielding a solid fraction (φ_s = w/p) between 0.125 and 0.75. Substrate materials include aluminum, copper, stainless steel, silicon, or polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS), chosen based on application-specific constraints such as weight, corrosion resistance, or flexibility. The working fluid may be water, air, oil, or refrigerants, while the infusing liquid is selected from fluorinated oils, silicone oils, liquid metals (e.g., gallium alloys), or ionic liquids, ensuring immiscibility and appropriate viscosity ratios (μ_i/μ_∞ between 0.1 and 10). Design criteria dictate that the thermal conductivity of the solid (κ_s) should be less than or equal to that of the infusing liquid (κ_i) to maximize heat flux enhancement, with optimal performance observed when κ_s ≈ κ_i. Thermal communication with a heat source—such as a semiconductor chip or temperature sensor—is achieved by bonding the substrate directly to the device or embedding thermal vias beneath the grooved region. The method for providing increased heat transfer involves fabricating the grooved substrate via lithography, etching, molding, or additive manufacturing; infusing the grooves with the selected liquid under vacuum or capillary action; and exposing the LIS to the working fluid under operational flow conditions. This process leverages the synergistic effects of reduced wall friction (due to interfacial slip) and augmented heat transfer (due to dispersive convection), resulting in a thermally efficient surface architecture suitable for next-generation thermal management solutions.
