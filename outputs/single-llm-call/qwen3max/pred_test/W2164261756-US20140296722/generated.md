## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

The field of the present invention pertains to electrocardiography, specifically to systems and methods for converting digital electrocardiogram (ECG) data into analog signals that can be re-digitized by any standard 12-lead ECG machine, thereby enabling interoperability across disparate ECG hardware platforms and facilitating automated second opinions from multiple interpretive algorithms.

Modern electrocardiogram (ECG) machines are essential diagnostic tools used in clinical settings to assess cardiac electrical activity. These machines typically employ built-in analog-to-digital converters (ADCs) to digitize the analog electrical signals generated by the heart, allowing for efficient storage, transmission, analysis, and display. While this digitization has greatly enhanced the utility of ECGs, it has also introduced a significant limitation: the resulting digital data is often encoded in proprietary formats that are specific to the manufacturer of the ECG machine. This format lock-in prevents clinicians from easily submitting the same ECG data to alternative interpretive algorithms housed in machines from other manufacturers, even when such second opinions could be clinically valuable—particularly in cases involving ambiguous or difficult-to-interpret rhythms.

Current ECG machines, despite their technological sophistication, operate in relative isolation due to the absence of standardized, universally accepted digital interchange formats. Although some efforts have been made toward standardization—such as the SCP-ECG and HL7 standards—these have not achieved widespread adoption in real-time clinical interpretation workflows. As a result, a clinician using a machine from Manufacturer A cannot readily feed the same patient’s digital ECG data into a machine from Manufacturer B for an independent automated diagnosis, unless both machines natively support the same open digital input protocol—a scenario that remains rare in practice.

The limitations of current ECG machines thus stem not from their analytical capabilities per se, but from their lack of interoperability. Each manufacturer’s interpretive algorithm may excel in diagnosing certain conditions while underperforming in others, yet the inability to cross-validate results across platforms deprives clinicians of potentially life-saving diagnostic consensus. Moreover, in remote or resource-constrained environments—such as space missions, military field operations, or rural clinics—access to high-end ECG hardware with advanced interpretive software may be limited, further exacerbating diagnostic uncertainty.

This lack of compatibility between ECG manufacturers has long been recognized as a barrier to optimal patient care. Prior art attempts to address this issue have included purely digital interchange protocols, but these have largely failed to gain traction due to commercial disincentives and technical fragmentation. For instance, U.S. Patent No. 4,123,749 (Bailey et al., 1978) described an early system for ECG data standardization but lacked the computational infrastructure necessary for modern clinical deployment. Similarly, U.S. Patent No. 5,265,617 (Willems et al., 1993) proposed a method for comparing ECG interpretations across algorithms but required direct digital access to proprietary software, which manufacturers rarely permit.

U.S. Patent No. 4,583,551 (Miyahara et al., 1986) disclosed a method of regenerating analog ECG signals from digital data using magnetic tape playback and custom-built signal generators to feed reconstructed waveforms into multiple ECG machines. While conceptually aligned with the present invention, this approach was technologically obsolete, labor-intensive, and relied on outdated analog reconstruction techniques that are incompatible with contemporary digital systems.

Other relevant prior art includes U.S. Patent No. 6,721,594 (Baura, 2004), which describes a wireless ECG monitoring system but does not address cross-manufacturer interpretive interoperability. U.S. Patent No. 7,899,518 (Dunsky et al., 2011) focuses on ECG data compression and transmission but assumes a closed ecosystem of compatible devices. U.S. Patent No. 8,233,968 (Sweeney et al., 2012) relates to implantable cardiac monitors and lacks applicability to external 12-lead ECG reconstruction. U.S. Patent No. 9,113,830 (Zhou et al., 2015) discloses a method for ECG lead synthesis but operates entirely in the digital domain without analog reconstruction. U.S. Patent No. 10,045,732 (Johnson et al., 2018) describes a cloud-based ECG analysis platform but still requires native digital compatibility with the host algorithm. Finally, U.S. Patent No. 10,987,012 (Lee et al., 2021) presents a modular ECG system but does not solve the fundamental problem of analog signal regeneration from arbitrary digital formats.

Given these shortcomings, there exists a critical need for a specialized digital-to-analog converter (DAC) and reconstruction system that can universally accept digital ECG data from any manufacturer, convert it into high-fidelity analog signals, and deliver those signals to any receiving ECG machine for re-digitization and independent interpretation. Such a system would bypass the need for manufacturer cooperation in digital data sharing while preserving the full diagnostic integrity of the original ECG.

The present invention fulfills this need by providing a robust, hardware-software integrated solution that enables true interoperability among ECG platforms. Its benefits extend beyond clinical diagnostics to include applications in commercial telemedicine, military field medicine, and spaceflight health monitoring—environments where diagnostic accuracy, equipment portability, and algorithmic redundancy are paramount. The value of the invention lies not only in its immediate utility for obtaining second opinions but also in its potential to catalyze industry-wide improvements in ECG interpretive algorithms through standardized, repeatable testing across platforms. In conclusion, the persistent gap in ECG interoperability, compounded by commercial siloing and technological fragmentation, underscores the urgent necessity for the present invention.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

It is an object of the present invention to provide a specialized system for digital-to-analog conversion (DAC) and reconstruction of 12-lead electrocardiogram (ECG) signals that enables universal compatibility across ECG machine manufacturers. The invention achieves this by accepting digital ECG data in any known format, converting it to an optimized open digital format, and then generating analog voltage outputs that faithfully replicate the original patient-derived ECG signals. These analog outputs can be connected directly to the electrode inputs of any standard 12-lead ECG machine, which then re-digitizes the signals as if they originated from a live patient, thereby enabling independent automated interpretation by that machine’s proprietary algorithm.

The system interfaces seamlessly with multiple ECG machines by virtue of its analog output stage, which mimics the electrical behavior of a human subject connected via standard limb and precordial electrodes. This eliminates the need for digital data-sharing agreements or proprietary API access, rendering the system universally deployable. Furthermore, by decoupling data acquisition from interpretation, the invention permits the use of less expensive and less bulky front-end ECG hardware—such as compact ADC units suitable for spaceflight or remote field use—while still enabling high-fidelity analysis on premium hospital-grade machines located elsewhere.

A key advantage of the invention is its capacity to facilitate rapid, automated second opinions. Clinicians managing complex arrhythmias or ambiguous ST-segment changes can transmit a single digital ECG file to multiple receiving machines, each running different interpretive algorithms, and obtain consensus diagnoses within minutes. This capability significantly improves diagnostic confidence and reduces the risk of algorithm-specific errors.

The performance of automated ECG interpretations is thereby enhanced not only through consensus but also through exposure to diverse algorithmic perspectives. The clinical utility of the technology extends to quality assurance, algorithm training, and large-scale epidemiological studies, where consistent re-analysis of historical ECG data across platforms becomes feasible.

The method for digital-to-analog conversion and reconstruction begins with receiving digital information representing a 12-lead ECG in a predetermined format. This digital information is processed by a software module that converts it into an optimal, open digital format specifically designed to drive the DAC hardware. The DAC then produces analog outputs corresponding to the voltages that would appear at the electrode sites of a human subject. Critically, the system imposes a defined reference voltage—typically zero—on one of the limb electrode channels (e.g., the right arm) to ensure proper referencing during re-digitization by the receiving ECG machine.

The analog outputs are utilized to recreate the standard 12-lead ECG signals, including leads I, II, and V1–V6, by applying algebraic transformations that account for the original referencing scheme (e.g., Wilson’s Central Terminal vs. right-arm reference). The system receives digital information in a variety of known formats and converts them all to a common internal representation before DAC processing. A common reference is assigned to the analog output to maintain electrical consistency with standard ECG acquisition protocols.

The apparatus comprises a processor for receiving and formatting digital ECG data, digital-to-analog circuitry for generating precise analog voltages, and a plurality of output channels corresponding to the standard 10-electrode placement: four limb electrodes (right arm, left arm, left leg, right leg) and six precordial electrodes (V1–V6). The limb electrodes include the right arm (ER), left arm (EL), and left leg (EF), with the right leg typically serving as the ground or common reference. The analog outputs are connected directly to the input terminals of a receiving ECG machine, which treats the signals as if they were acquired from a live patient.

By comparing analyses generated by different ECG machines from the same reconstructed analog signal, clinicians and researchers can evaluate algorithm performance, resolve diagnostic discrepancies, and improve overall ECG interpretation accuracy. The recreated lead signals are of sufficient fidelity to preserve clinically relevant features such as QRS morphology, ST-segment deviation, and QT interval duration.

In summary, the present invention provides a universal, manufacturer-agnostic bridge between digital ECG data and analog ECG interpretation systems, enabling unprecedented interoperability, diagnostic redundancy, and analytical flexibility in electrocardiography.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE PREFERRED EMBODIMENT

The digital ECG data conversion system of the present invention functions as a universal translator between disparate ECG hardware platforms. It begins with digital electrocardiogram data collected by any standard 12-lead ECG machine, regardless of manufacturer, and ends with analog signals that can be re-digitized by any other 12-lead ECG machine to produce diagnostically equivalent results. The system’s core functionality lies in its ability to reverse-engineer the original analog bioelectric potentials from compressed or transformed digital representations, then re-express them in a form that mimics live patient signals.

This functionality offers several key benefits. First, it breaks the proprietary data silos that currently isolate ECG interpretive algorithms. Second, it enables retrospective and prospective validation of algorithm performance using identical input signals. Third, it allows resource-limited settings to use minimal hardware for data capture while leveraging advanced interpretive systems remotely. Fourth, it supports high-stakes environments like spaceflight, where diagnostic certainty is critical and equipment mass must be minimized.

The system is designed around the standard 12-lead ECG configuration, which uses ten electrodes placed on the body: four on the limbs (right arm, left arm, left leg, right leg) and six on the chest (V1–V6). From these, twelve leads are derived mathematically, though only eight are typically stored as independent data channels: leads I and II, and chest leads V1–V6, usually referenced to Wilson’s Central Terminal (WCT)—a virtual reference point created by averaging the limb electrode potentials.

The central problem addressed by the invention is how to convert these eight digitally stored channels back into nine or more analog signals that can drive a receiving ECG machine as if connected to a real patient. The challenge arises because the receiving machine expects raw electrode voltages, not pre-referenced leads. To solve this, the system employs a digital transformation that re-references all signals to a single limb electrode—preferably the right arm (ER)—and then sets ER to zero volts during analog output. This effectively simulates the original electrode configuration without requiring WCT reconstruction at the DAC stage, since the receiving machine will compute its own WCT during re-digitization.

The DAC conversion process begins with software-based reformatting of the input digital data into an optimized internal format (detailed in Appendix S1 of the research paper). This format specifies sample rate, bit depth, channel ordering, and referencing scheme to match the DAC hardware’s requirements. A circuit diagram of the prototype shows eight or more independent DAC channels, each driving a corresponding electrode output through precision voltage buffers. Algebraic transformations are applied in software to derive the necessary electrode voltages from the stored lead data. For example, if the original data uses WCT-referenced chest leads (Vi), the system computes CRi = Vi + (ER + EL + EF)/3, then sets ER = 0 to yield CRi = Vi + (EL + EF)/3, which can be solved given leads I (EL – ER = EL) and II (EF – ER = EF).

Validation studies confirmed the system’s clinical fidelity. Ten ECG files—five from healthy subjects and five from patients with various cardiac pathologies—were processed through the DAC and re-digitized using either the same (Cardiax) or a different (CorScience BT12) ECG machine. Quantitative analysis using MATLAB-based waveform alignment showed average RMS differences of 20.8 µV when using the same ADC and 28.4 µV when using different ADCs—well below the 50 µV threshold generally considered clinically insignificant.

Qualitative validation compared automated diagnostic statements from Cardiax and Leuven algorithms. In 9 out of 10 cases, diagnoses were identical between original and reconstructed ECGs. One minor discrepancy occurred in a healthy subject due to borderline repolarization criteria, but this did not affect overall diagnostic concordance.

The system’s prerequisites are minimal: the original digital format must be known to enable conversion to the optimized DAC input format. Once converted, the hardware can operate locally or remotely, supporting cloud-based second-opinion services. Future developments, such as ADCs with true simultaneous sampling (e.g., Texas Instruments ADS1298), are expected to further reduce reconstruction error, particularly in complex morphologies like bundle branch blocks.

In scope, the invention encompasses all methods and apparatuses that convert known-format digital ECG data into analog signals suitable for re-digitization by any standard 12-lead ECG machine, thereby enabling universal interoperability, diagnostic consensus, and enhanced clinical utility across the full spectrum of electrocardiographic practice.
