# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD

The present invention relates generally to plasma systems measurement, and more particularly to a non-invasive, contact-free method and system for real-time monitoring of plasma characteristics in semiconductor manufacturing environments. The invention provides a novel approach for measuring critical plasma parameters—such as power, pressure, chamber wall cleanliness, and sheath dynamics—through the detection and analysis of radio frequency emissions generated by the plasma itself. This technique, herein referred to as Radio Emission Spectroscopy (RES), operates in the radio near-field regime and leverages electromagnetic induction via a loop antenna to capture time-varying magnetic fields associated with plasma currents. Unlike conventional optical diagnostics that rely on line-of-sight access through viewports, the RES method functions independently of optical transparency, thereby overcoming a major limitation in industrial plasma processing where viewport fouling is common. The invention is especially suited for integration into capacitively coupled plasma (CCP) reactors used in etching, deposition, and cleaning processes within semiconductor fabrication facilities, offering a robust, high-sensitivity diagnostic tool capable of continuous in situ monitoring without perturbing the plasma or requiring internal probes.

## BACKGROUND

Plasma usage in semiconductor manufacturing is ubiquitous, serving as the enabling medium for precision etching, thin-film deposition, surface modification, and chamber cleaning. These low-pressure plasmas, typically generated via radio frequency (RF) excitation in capacitively coupled configurations, must be precisely controlled to ensure process repeatability, yield, and device performance. Consequently, accurate diagnosis and real-time monitoring of plasma conditions are essential for both process development and high-volume manufacturing. Among the most widely adopted techniques for plasma monitoring is Optical Emission Spectroscopy (OES), which analyzes light emitted by excited species in the plasma to infer composition, temperature, and other properties. OES is non-intrusive and provides rich chemical information, motivating its widespread deployment in industrial tools. However, OES suffers from a critical limitation: it requires an optically transparent path from the plasma to the detector, usually through a quartz or sapphire viewport. In practice, these viewports become coated over time with process byproducts—polymers, oxides, or particulates—reducing transmission and eventually rendering optical signals unreliable or undetectable. This “clouding” phenomenon necessitates frequent maintenance, chamber venting, and recalibration, disrupting production and increasing cost-of-ownership.

To address these shortcomings, alternative non-optical monitoring techniques have been explored. One such approach is Radio Emission Spectroscopy (RES), which detects RF electromagnetic emissions naturally radiated by time-varying currents within the plasma. Unlike OES, RES does not depend on photon transmission and is therefore immune to viewport contamination. Early implementations of RES demonstrated its feasibility for capturing fundamental and harmonic emissions from single-frequency plasmas. However, prior art RES systems were limited in sensitivity, bandwidth, and analytical depth, often treating the signal as a qualitative indicator rather than a quantitative diagnostic. Furthermore, these systems lacked integration with modern data acquisition and signal processing capabilities necessary for real-time feedback control in advanced manufacturing nodes.

A notable advancement was disclosed in PCT/EP2018/057556, which introduced a near-field loop antenna configuration for capturing B-field components of plasma emissions with high spatial resolution and minimal external interference. This system demonstrated improved signal-to-noise ratio and the ability to correlate RES amplitude with bulk plasma current. Nevertheless, it did not fully exploit the technique’s potential for monitoring dynamic process variables such as pressure, power drift, or chamber wall state, nor did it address multi-frequency plasma environments increasingly common in next-generation tools.

Separately, Mandelis and colleagues proposed photoacoustic and photothermal methods for plasma characterization, but these approaches require modulated light sources and sensitive acoustic detectors, making them impractical for integration into standard plasma chambers. Moreover, the Mandelis publication fails to provide a viable solution for real-time, in situ monitoring under actual processing conditions, particularly in the presence of opaque viewports or complex RF driving schemes.

The RES system of the present invention comprises a loop antenna positioned proximate to a plasma chamber viewport, a high-speed digitizer, a fast Fourier transform (FFT) engine, and a signal processing unit. The antenna, typically circular with a diameter of approximately 21.6 mm, is oriented with its plane perpendicular to the viewport to maximize coupling to the azimuthal magnetic field generated by radial plasma currents. The system operates by continuously sampling the induced voltage in the antenna at rates up to 19 kHz, enabling millisecond-scale temporal resolution. Signal analysis focuses on the fundamental RF drive frequency (e.g., 13.56 MHz) and its harmonics or intermodulation products in multi-frequency systems. The RES system benefits from its passive, non-perturbative nature, high sensitivity to conduction currents, and immunity to optical obstructions.

Plasma chambers used in semiconductor processing include single-frequency CCP reactors like the Oxford Instruments PlasmaLab 100 and multi-frequency systems such as the Lam EXELAN 2300, which employs 2 MHz, 27 MHz, and 162 MHz sources to decouple ion energy and flux control. These chambers operate under precise gas flow, pressure, and power conditions, all of which influence plasma density, electron temperature, and sheath characteristics. The development of the RES system was motivated by the need for a universal, robust diagnostic that functions across diverse chamber types and process conditions without requiring internal modifications or suffering from signal degradation due to contamination.

## SUMMARY

The present invention introduces a Radio Emission Spectroscopy (RES) system for real-time, non-invasive monitoring of plasma characteristics in semiconductor manufacturing. The RES system comprises a near-field loop antenna, a high-bandwidth receiver, a digital signal processor, and a display or control interface. The loop antenna is positioned externally adjacent to a plasma chamber viewport and captures time-varying magnetic fields generated by plasma currents. The receiver amplifies and digitizes the induced signal, which is then processed via fast Fourier transform to extract amplitude and frequency information at the fundamental RF drive frequency and its harmonics or intermodulation products.

The operation of the RES system relies on the principle that the conduction current in the plasma bulk—responsible for ohmic heating and directly related to electron density and collision frequency—generates a proportional magnetic field detectable by the antenna. By monitoring changes in the RES signal amplitude at specific frequencies, the system infers variations in key process parameters such as applied RF power, chamber pressure, and wall contamination state.

The benefits of the RES system include immunity to viewport clouding, high sensitivity (detecting power changes as small as 5 W and pressure changes as low as 1 mTorr), real-time response (with data rates up to 19 kHz), and compatibility with both single- and multi-frequency plasma sources. Additionally, the system enables remote monitoring of sheath dynamics and stray capacitance changes through analysis of frequency heterodyning phenomena.

The invention further encompasses a method for measuring plasma characteristics, comprising the steps of: positioning a loop antenna near a plasma chamber viewport; igniting a plasma using one or more RF power sources; capturing the induced RF signal over time; performing spectral analysis to identify emission lines; and correlating signal amplitude or frequency shifts with plasma or process parameters. This method provides continuous, in situ feedback without perturbing the plasma.

A system for measuring plasma characteristics includes the aforementioned hardware components integrated into a compact module that interfaces with existing chamber control systems. The system operates autonomously, providing calibrated outputs for process control or fault detection.

Additionally, the invention includes a computer-readable medium encoded with instructions for executing the signal processing algorithms, including FFT computation, baseline correction, peak tracking, and sensitivity calibration. The medium may reside on a local processor or cloud-based server, enabling distributed monitoring across multiple tools.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE DRAWINGS

Figure 1 illustrates a schematic diagram of the RES system integrated with a plasma chamber. The loop antenna is shown mounted externally on a viewport, with its plane perpendicular to the chamber wall to optimize magnetic flux linkage. The antenna connects via coaxial cable to a signal conditioning unit containing amplifiers and anti-aliasing filters, which feed into a high-speed analog-to-digital converter. The digitized signal is processed by a field-programmable gate array (FPGA) implementing real-time FFT, with results displayed on a graphical user interface showing time-resolved spectra.

Antenna placement is critical: optimal sensitivity is achieved when the antenna is positioned midway between the powered and grounded electrodes, where bulk plasma currents are strongest. Distances of 1 mm from the viewport have proven effective, balancing signal strength against practical mounting constraints.

The plasma chamber components include a vacuum vessel, gas inlet manifold, exhaust pump, RF electrodes, and matching network. In the Oxford Instruments PlasmaLab 100 system, a single 13.56 MHz RF source drives the top electrode, while the bottom electrode and chamber walls are grounded. Operation involves introducing oxygen or argon gas at controlled flow rates (e.g., 50 sccm), evacuating to base pressure, igniting plasma at set power (50–500 W) and pressure (10–250 mTorr), and maintaining steady state for processing.

A custom sensor design features a shielded loop antenna with minimized electrostatic pickup, ensuring pure magnetic field detection. Benefits include reduced noise from stray electric fields, enhanced reproducibility, and compatibility with high-power RF environments.

The Oxford Instruments PlasmaLab 100 system operates as a single-frequency CCP reactor, ideal for demonstrating RES sensitivity to power and pressure. In contrast, the Lam EXELAN 2300 system employs three independent RF generators (2 MHz, 27 MHz, 162 MHz) connected via diplexers to the same electrode, enabling independent control of ion energy (low frequency) and plasma density (high frequency). RES measurements on this system reveal rich intermodulation spectra due to nonlinear sheath dynamics.

RES signal analysis focuses on amplitude and frequency of emission lines. Frequency mixing components arise from nonlinear plasma response, generating sidebands at sums and differences of drive frequencies. Heterodyning analysis involves tracking beat frequencies (e.g., 2 MHz spacing around 162 MHz) to infer sheath nonlinearity and electron temperature.

In multiple-powered electrode systems, each RF source contributes to the total current, and their interaction produces complex RES signatures. Operation involves simultaneous application of multiple frequencies, with RES capturing the resultant spectrum in real time.

The RES system offers significant benefits: no internal probes, no optical path required, high temporal resolution, and direct correlation to conduction current. The frequency heterodyning phenomenon—where sidebands appear at f₀ ± n·f₁—is analyzed to extract information about sheath impedance and electron kinetics. Applications include endpoint detection, chamber matching verification, and predictive maintenance based on wall condition trends.

## 1. Real-Time Monitoring of Power Variations in the Process Chamber Using RES

The RES technique exploits the proportionality between induced antenna voltage and bulk plasma conduction current, which scales with applied RF power. An experimental setup using an Oxford Instruments PlasmaLab 100 chamber with oxygen plasma (50 sccm, 100 mTorr) demonstrated RES signal amplitude variation from 50 W to 500 W. The signal spanned ~10 dB, corresponding to a tenfold linear increase, with sensitivity sufficient to resolve 5 W steps (<0.4% error). Real-time traces showed immediate response to step changes in power, confirming suitability for dynamic process control. The sensitivity of ~3.5 %/W arises because conduction current J_c ∝ n_e / ν_m, and electron density n_e increases nearly tenfold over this power range while collision frequency ν_m varies only ~50%, making n_e the dominant factor. Correlation with V-I probe measurements of I·cosΦ validated RES as a true current monitor.

## 2. Real-Time Monitoring of Pressure Variations in the Process Chamber Using RES

Pressure monitoring is vital for process stability. Experiments at fixed power (200 W) and gas flow (50 sccm O₂) varied pressure from 10 to 250 mTorr. RES amplitude increased monotonically with pressure, showing ~4 dB change (250% linear) between 10–25 mTorr, enabling detection of 1 mTorr changes (<0.1% error). Sensitivity of ~2.5 %/mTorr stems from rising n_e with pressure (from 2.75×10¹⁵ to 2.5×10¹⁶ m⁻³), outweighing decreasing ν_m. Real-time data confirmed rapid tracking of pressure ramps, proving RES utility for closed-loop pressure control in etch or deposition processes where even minor deviations affect feature profiles.

## 3. Real-Time Monitoring of Chamber Wall Cleanliness Using RES

Chamber wall contamination degrades process repeatability. To simulate this, photoresist-coated aluminum foil (~1.5% coverage) was placed inside the chamber. During O₂ plasma cleaning (500 W, 50 mTorr), RES amplitude gradually increased over 4.3 hours as resist was removed, asymptotically approaching the clean-wall baseline. A qualitative model attributes this to reduced dielectric film thickness t_f on the wall, lowering capacitive impedance Z_eff and increasing displacement current, which—via current balance—boosts conduction current and RES signal. This demonstrates RES capability for in situ endpoint detection in chamber cleans and wafer-to-wafer consistency assurance.

## 4. Use of RES to Monitor Plasmas in a Multiple Frequency Chamber

Multi-frequency chambers like the Lam EXELAN 2300 use 2 MHz and 162 MHz sources to control ion energy and flux independently. RES measurements on Ar/O₂ plasma revealed strong emission at 162 MHz with sidebands spaced at 2 MHz intervals, evidencing frequency heterodyning. A diode-mixer model explains this: nonlinear sheaths act as mixers, generating intermodulation products i_D ∝ exp(V_D/V_t), where V_D combines both RF voltages. Small-signal expansion yields terms at ω₂ ± nω₁, matching observed spectra. Sideband intensities depend on V_t (electron temperature) and saturation current I_S (related to n_e), suggesting future quantitative diagnostics. This confirms RES applicability to advanced multi-frequency tools.

## 5. Use of RES to Remotely Monitor Changes in Stray Capacitance, Chamber Conditions or Changes in the Sheath Characteristics of a Plasma

RES enables remote monitoring of sheath and chamber electrical properties. In power variation experiments, peak RES amplitude varied linearly with power, while peak frequency remained stable, indicating bulk-dominated response. However, in multi-frequency systems, frequency shifts and amplitude modulations reflect sheath nonlinearity. Frequency compensation characteristics—adjusting drive frequencies to maintain constant RES response—can stabilize processes against drift. Remote monitoring of capacitive changes (e.g., from wall coatings or electrode erosion) is achieved via impedance-induced current changes. Crucially, RES outperforms OES in obscured-viewport scenarios and provides direct electrical parameter correlation, offering superior robustness for industrial deployment.
