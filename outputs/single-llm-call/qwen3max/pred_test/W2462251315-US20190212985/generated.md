# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

Random number generation is a foundational requirement across a broad spectrum of modern technological applications, ranging from cryptographic key generation and secure communications to scientific simulations such as Monte Carlo methods, stochastic modeling, and even regulated domains like online gaming and lottery systems. In all such contexts, the quality of randomness directly influences the reliability, security, and integrity of the underlying system. High-quality random numbers must exhibit true unpredictability, uniform distribution, and independence between successive values—properties that cannot be guaranteed by deterministic computational methods. Pseudo-random number generators (PRNGs), which rely on mathematical algorithms seeded with initial values, are inherently predictable if the seed or internal state is known, rendering them unsuitable for high-assurance applications such as quantum-safe cryptography or national security protocols. Consequently, there is a critical need for hardware-based true random number generators (TRNGs) that derive entropy from fundamentally unpredictable physical processes.

Among physical phenomena, quantum mechanical processes offer the most robust source of intrinsic randomness due to the inherent indeterminacy described by quantum theory. Specifically, the emission and detection of individual photons represent a naturally stochastic process governed by Poisson statistics, making photon-based systems particularly well-suited for quantum random number generation (QRNG). Several hardware implementations have leveraged this principle, including systems based on beam splitters with single-photon detectors, homodyne detection of vacuum fluctuations, and more recently, optical sensors such as single-photon avalanche diodes (SPADs) and conventional CMOS image sensors (CIS). While these approaches have demonstrated acceptable randomness quality, they suffer from significant practical limitations. SPAD-based QRNGs often require high-voltage biasing (typically above 20 volts), are prone to after-pulsing artifacts, and exhibit limited scalability due to large pixel pitch and complex quenching circuitry. Conventional CIS-based QRNGs, though compatible with standard CMOS fabrication and low-power operation, lack single-photon sensitivity due to relatively high read noise (typically exceeding 1 electron rms), which obscures the quantized nature of photon arrivals and introduces thermal and electronic noise that degrades entropy quality.

Moreover, existing photon-emission-based QRNG technologies face challenges in achieving both high output data rates and long-term stability. The presence of dark current—a thermally induced signal unrelated to photon incidence—and 1/f noise in detector electronics introduces non-quantum sources of variability that compromise the purity of quantum randomness. These noise components are temperature-dependent and can fluctuate over time, necessitating complex calibration or cooling mechanisms. Additionally, many prior art systems do not provide real-time adaptability to environmental changes, such as variations in light intensity or sensor operating conditions, which can shift the statistical distribution of output bits and reduce entropy. Thus, despite progress in QRNG design, there remains an unmet need for a scalable, low-power, high-speed, and stable quantum random number generator that leverages mature semiconductor manufacturing while preserving the fundamental quantum randomness of photon detection without contamination from classical noise sources.

## SUMMARY OF SOME EMBODIMENTS

The present invention provides a quantum random number generator (QRNG) based on a Quanta Image Sensor (QIS), which overcomes the limitations of prior art SPAD and CIS-based systems by combining single-photon sensitivity, ultra-low read noise, high pixel density, and compatibility with standard CMOS fabrication processes. In some embodiments, the QRNG comprises a QIS pixel array composed of millions to billions of sub-micron photodetector elements known as “jots,” each configured to detect incident photons and generate a quantized electrical response corresponding to the number of photoelectrons generated during a defined exposure interval. The system further includes comparison circuitry that compares the analog output of each jot against a programmable threshold level to produce a binary decision—“0” if no photon is detected, and “1” if at least one photon is detected—thereby converting quantum-level photon arrival events into raw random bits.

In certain configurations, the QRNG incorporates an optional photon source, such as an LED or laser diode, to illuminate the QIS array under controlled conditions, ensuring a stable and tunable quanta exposure (H). An optional optical conditioner, such as a diffuser, neutral density filter, or microlens array, may be employed to homogenize illumination across the sensor or attenuate light intensity to achieve optimal entropy. The system may also include a randomness extractor, implemented in digital logic or firmware, which post-processes the raw bitstream using information-theoretically secure algorithms—such as universal hashing—to distill near-perfect randomness from input sequences with entropy slightly below unity. Control circuitry coordinates the operation of the QIS, photon source, optical conditioner, and extractor, enabling dynamic adjustment of key parameters including the comparison threshold level (Uₜ), the exposure time interval, the photon source emission intensity, and the optical conditioning characteristics.

The control circuitry may further implement real-time randomness monitoring by analyzing statistical properties of the raw or extracted bitstream and adjusting operational parameters to maintain maximum entropy. For instance, if fluctuations in ambient light or temperature cause a drift in quanta exposure H, the system can automatically recalibrate Uₜ to align with a local minimum in the probability density function of the readout signal, thereby preserving entropy stability. The QIS itself is characterized by in-pixel conversion gain sufficiently high to enable deep sub-electron read noise (DSERN)—preferably below 0.25 e⁻ rms—and minimal dark current, ensuring that the dominant source of signal variation is quantum-limited photon shot noise rather than classical electronic noise. As used herein, “quanta exposure H” refers to the average number of photoelectrons collected per jot per frame; “read noise” denotes the root-mean-square noise in the readout chain expressed in equivalent electrons; and “binary output data” signifies a sequence of bits derived from thresholded jot responses, intended for cryptographic or simulation use.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF SOME EMBODIMENTS

The disclosed QRNG architecture leverages the unique capabilities of the Quanta Image Sensor (QIS) to generate high-entropy random numbers at unprecedented data rates while maintaining robustness against environmental perturbations. Unlike SPAD arrays, which require high-voltage operation and suffer from after-pulsing, and conventional CMOS image sensors (CIS), which lack photon-counting resolution due to excessive read noise, the QIS operates at low voltage, exhibits negligible dark current, and achieves read noise levels below 0.25 e⁻ rms—enabling reliable discrimination between zero, one, or multiple photoelectron events. This allows the system to harness the intrinsic randomness of quantum photon emission without contamination from classical noise sources.

In operation, the QRNG generates random numbers based on the output of individual jots within the QIS pixel array. Each jot functions as an independent photon-counting unit, accumulating charge proportional to incident photons during a precisely controlled exposure interval. The accumulated charge is converted to a voltage via high in-pixel conversion gain and read out through a low-noise analog chain. In single-bit QIS embodiments, each jot outputs a binary value indicating whether zero or one-or-more photons were detected; in multi-bit embodiments, the jot provides a digital word representing the exact photon count. The readout chain includes analog-to-digital converters (ADCs), multiplexers, and amplifiers configured to preserve signal fidelity while enabling high-speed parallel readout—facilitating aggregate data rates exceeding gigabits per second in large arrays.

An optional photon source, such as a green LED array, provides controlled illumination to the QIS, with intensity regulated by precision voltage or current drivers under the supervision of control circuitry. An optional optical conditioner—comprising diffusers, filters, or beam-shaping optics—ensures spatially uniform and temporally stable illumination, critical for maintaining consistent quanta exposure H across the sensor. The control circuitry dynamically adjusts exposure duration, photon source intensity, and optical attenuation to maintain H near an optimal value such as 0.7 photoelectrons per jot per frame, where the probability of detecting zero versus one-or-more photons is nearly balanced, maximizing entropy.

Following analog readout, the digitized jot signals are processed by comparison circuitry that applies a threshold Uₜ—typically set near 0.5 e⁻ when H ≈ 0.7—to generate raw binary bits. This threshold is selected to coincide with a valley in the probability density function of the readout signal, minimizing sensitivity to minor fluctuations in H. In multi-bit QIS implementations, the ADC output is processed in the digital domain to emulate single-bit behavior or to extract higher-order entropy from multi-photon events. A randomness extractor, such as a Toeplitz matrix-based universal hash function, then compresses the raw bitstream into a shorter sequence of near-uniform random bits, with compression ratio determined by the measured minimum entropy.

The system embodies key design principles: signal distribution is dominated by Poisson photon statistics convolved with Gaussian read noise; quanta exposure H is maintained near 0.7 to balance P(“0”) and P(“1”); minimum entropy Sₘᵢₙ is computed in real time to guide extractor parameters; and the ideal random number generator condition—Sₘᵢₙ = 1—is approached through precise noise control and adaptive thresholding. Advantages include high data output rate (scalable with array size), low dark current error (<0.1 e⁻/s), and high stability against environmental drift. Variations include integration with on-chip processing, use of natural light sources, and hybrid architectures combining multiple jots per random bit to enhance throughput or redundancy.
