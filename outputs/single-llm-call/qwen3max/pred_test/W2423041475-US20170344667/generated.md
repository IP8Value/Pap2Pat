# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates generally to the field of structural engineering design and computational optimization, and more specifically to a computer-implemented method, system, and non-transitory computer-readable medium for performing failsafe topology optimization of three-dimensional structural continua. The invention enables the generation of structurally redundant designs that maintain load-carrying capacity under localized material loss—simulating scenarios such as crack propagation, impact damage, or manufacturing defects—by introducing a finite population of predefined damage volumes into the design domain during the optimization process. This approach is particularly applicable to safety-critical industries such as aerospace, automotive, and civil infrastructure, where structural integrity must be preserved even after partial failure of load-bearing components. The invention further provides an efficient computational framework leveraging parallel processing and hierarchical damage population strategies to render failsafe topology optimization tractable for industrial-scale finite element models.

## BACKGROUND

Topology optimization has evolved since its formal inception in 1988 as a powerful computational methodology for determining optimal material distribution within a prescribed design domain under given loads, boundary conditions, and performance constraints. By iteratively redistributing material to maximize structural efficiency—often minimizing compliance subject to a volume constraint—topology optimization routinely produces lightweight, high-performance structures resembling truss-like or organic forms. However, these optimized designs frequently exhibit minimal structural redundancy, rendering them vulnerable to catastrophic failure upon localized damage. This limitation is especially problematic in reliability-sensitive applications such as aircraft airframes, where regulatory standards mandate failsafe behavior: the ability to sustain operational loads even after the complete loss of a critical structural member or a defined volume of material.

Reliability-based design optimization (RBDO) has emerged as a broader framework to address uncertainties in loading, material properties, geometry, and manufacturing tolerances. Within this context, reliability-based topology optimization (RBTO) seeks robust designs by incorporating probabilistic models of uncertainty. Nevertheless, traditional RBTO approaches struggle to model failsafe requirements effectively due to the binary nature of structural failure—either a component carries load or it does not—which defies smooth statistical treatment. As noted in the literature, the discrete, deterministic character of member failure is incompatible with gradient-based optimization under continuous probability distributions.

Failsafe design philosophy, long institutionalized in aerospace engineering, mandates that structures withstand 80–100% of limit loads following either (1) the complete failure of any single discrete load-bearing member in a redundant system or (2) the partial loss of material from a monolithic component, simulating undetected crack growth between inspection intervals. Translating this requirement into topology optimization presents two core challenges: first, defining a meaningful “member failure” test before discrete structural members have even emerged from the optimization process; and second, developing a computationally feasible scheme to evaluate structural performance across a vast number of potential damage scenarios. Prior art attempted to address this by placing square voids at every finite element location, but this results in a damage population size equal to the number of elements—often exceeding hundreds of thousands—rendering the approach computationally prohibitive even on high-performance computing (HPC) clusters. Thus, there remains a critical need for a mathematically rigorous yet computationally efficient formulation of failsafe topology optimization applicable to real-world 3D structures.

## SUMMARY

The present invention addresses the fundamental challenge of integrating failsafe design requirements into topology optimization by redefining the problem through a novel computational framework based on a finite damage population derived from a hierarchical series. The invention introduces a mathematically grounded definition of damage as the complete removal of material within a spherical or cubic volume of prescribed size, randomly located anywhere within the structural continuum. This formulation inherently satisfies both failure modes defined in aerospace practice: discrete member loss and partial monolithic failure.

To render this infinite-population problem computationally tractable, the invention introduces a damage population series comprising Level-1 and Level-2 populations. The Level-1 population consists of a gapless tiling of damage cubes covering the entire design domain without overlap. The Level-2 population enriches this base set by adding damage cubes centered at the intersections of Level-1 cubes, thereby targeting the “best hideout” locations where material would otherwise survive all Level-1 damage instances. A partial Level-2 population (PB₂) is introduced that doubles the Level-1 count while capturing most of the modeling accuracy gain, offering superior return on computational investment.

The invention further provides simple analytical formulae for the maximum material survival rate—a key metric quantifying how much of a representative structural section remains intact under a given damage population. These formulae demonstrate that with a maximum length scale constraint limiting member cross-sections to half the damage cube edge length, the Level-2 population guarantees zero survival, thus strictly enforcing the failsafe requirement.

An efficient computer-implemented method is disclosed, wherein the structural continuum is represented via finite element analysis (FEA), and the damage volume is systematically introduced as a void in multiple independent FEA models. The optimization problem is formulated to minimize the maximum compliance (or total compliance across load cases) among all damaged and undamaged structural configurations. The SIMP (Solid Isotropic Material with Penalization) approach is employed, with density variables bounded below to avoid singularities and penalized via a power law to drive binary material distribution.

Critically, the invention leverages Message Passing Interface (MPI) parallel implementation to distribute the analysis and sensitivity computations for each damage scenario across multiple processors. A master process coordinates damage population generation, convergence monitoring, constraint screening, and iterative optimization updates. Practical measures are incorporated to preserve load conditions—such as excluding damage zones that eliminate point loads—and to reduce computational cost by filtering out damage instances containing negligible material.

The invention is embodied in a system comprising one or more data processing apparatuses, and in a non-transitory computer-readable medium storing instructions that, when executed, perform the failsafe topology optimization method. This enables engineers to generate structurally redundant, failsafe-compliant designs for complex 3D components using practical HPC resources, overcoming the prohibitive computational burden of prior approaches.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The concept of failsafe design requires that a structure remain functional after the sudden loss of a critical load path. In topology optimization, where structural members are not predefined, this necessitates a generalized failure test applicable to any emergent geometry. The invention defines this test through a damage model wherein a volume of material—represented as a sphere or cube of edge length *d*—is completely removed from the structure at a random location. This “damage” simulates scenarios such as fatigue crack coalescence, corrosion pits, or ballistic impact, and serves as a proxy for both discrete member failure (when *d* exceeds member cross-section) and partial monolithic failure.

Referring to FIG. 1A, a spherical damage model is illustrated, showing a void of diameter *d* embedded within a solid continuum. Alternatively, as shown in FIG. 1B, a cube-shaped damage volume of edge length *d* is used for computational simplicity and conservative modeling, as its circumscribed volume exceeds that of the sphere. The orientation of cube-shaped damage may be aligned with global axes or rotated to assess directional vulnerability, though axis-aligned cubes are typically sufficient for initial design exploration.

The effect of the damage volume is to eliminate all load-carrying capacity within its bounds, effectively creating a hole in the structure. The topology optimization problem is then formulated to ensure that, regardless of where this damage occurs within the domain Ω, the residual structure can still carry applied loads without excessive deformation or collapse.

Mathematically, the optimization seeks to minimize an objective function *f*(**x**)—typically the maximum compliance across all damage scenarios—subject to constraints such as volume fraction, stress limits, or displacement bounds. The design variables **x** = {*xᵢ*} represent normalized material densities for each finite element, constrained between a small lower bound (e.g., 0.01) and 1.0. The SIMP approach is employed, wherein the effective stiffness of each element is penalized as *Kᵢ* = *xᵢᵖKᵢ⁰*, with *p* ≥ 2 (commonly 3–4) to suppress intermediate densities.

While spherical damage is geometrically isotropic, cube-shaped damage is preferred for its ease of placement and analysis. Damage cubes may be placed with fixed orientation, though alternative shapes (cylindrical, ellipsoidal) could be used if justified by application-specific failure modes.

The solution strategy involves replacing the infinite set of possible damage locations with a finite, strategically selected damage population. The base population, termed Damage Series A Level-1 (DS-A₁ or PA₁), consists of non-overlapping, gapless cubes tiling the design domain, as illustrated in FIG. 2A. This ensures every point in the domain lies within at least one damage cube, though intersections between cubes create regions—the “best hideouts”—where material may survive all Level-1 damage instances.

To improve coverage, Damage Series B (DS-B or PB) is introduced as a partial subset of higher-level populations. Specifically, PB₂ includes all PA₁ cubes plus additional cubes centered at the eight-way intersections of PA₁ cubes, as shown in FIGS. 2B and 2C. This targets the exact locations where survival rates are highest under PA₁, dramatically reducing the maximum material survival rate with only a doubling of population size. Table 1 summarizes population sizes: PA₁ scales with (*L/d*)³, while PB₂ adds only a fraction of PA₂’s full count.

The reliability of the model is quantified by the maximum material survival rate—the largest fraction of a representative cube (size *d*) that remains intact under any damage instance in the population. For PA₁, this volumetric survival rate is 87.5%, with a 75% sectional survival rate in the worst direction. For PB₂, these drop to 62.5% and 50%, respectively. Crucially, when a maximum length scale constraint limits member thickness to *d*/2, PA₂ achieves zero survival, guaranteeing that no structural feature can evade damage.

The computational expense of evaluating dozens or hundreds of FEA models is mitigated through MPI parallelization. As illustrated in FIG. 3, a master process generates the damage population, distributes models to worker processes, collects compliance and sensitivity data, performs constraint screening (e.g., discarding damage zones with <10% material), and drives the optimizer. Load conditions are preserved by automatically excluding damage cubes that remove point loads.

The method is validated through a three-bar truss example: the standard topology eliminates the vertical bar, yielding a statically determinate two-bar system. In contrast, the failsafe design retains all three bars, ensuring load path redundancy. Compliance increases moderately in the undamaged state but remains bounded under any single-bar failure, demonstrating active constraint satisfaction.

### EXAMPLES

Example 1 considers a 2D rectangular plate (100×50) under shear load, with 20% volume fraction and a large 25×25 square damage. The standard design (FIG. 12a) forms a two-bar truss. Failsafe designs using PA₁ (FIG. 12b) and PB₂ (FIG. 12c) develop redundant diagonal members and central reinforcement. Compliance of the undamaged failsafe designs is ~44% higher than standard, but maximum damaged compliance is only ~120% above undamaged—significantly better than the 170% seen in the three-bar truss. Active damage zones (those governing the max compliance) cluster centrally, confirming the optimizer’s focus on critical vulnerabilities.

Example 2 replicates the cantilever plate from prior art, with damage sizes *d*=10 and *d*=22. For *d*=10, PA₁ uses 108 damage zones versus 7,701 in prior work; PB₂ uses 193. All methods produce similar redundant topologies (FIG. 18), proving PA₁ suffices for fine damage. For *d*=22, PA₁ (26 zones) fails to protect the load point, but PB₂ (42 zones) succeeds (FIG. 20), justifying its use for coarse damage. Computational savings exceed 99% versus element-density approaches.

Example 3 applies the method to a 3D control arm (327k tetrahedral elements, 30% volume, casting constraint). With 50³ damage cubes, PA₁ includes 45 zones; PB₂ adds 28 (FIG. 23). The failsafe design (FIG. 24) features a central rib and reinforced bearing mounts. Undamaged compliance rises from 162.3 to 193.7, but damaged compliance peaks at 756.8—highlighting narrow load paths near bearings. This insight guides designers to add supports if stricter failsafe performance is needed. Critically, the method handles this industrial-scale model with only 73 damage scenarios, whereas prior art would require >200,000.

Additional implementations include stress-constrained formulations, adaptive damage placement, and integration with additive manufacturing constraints. The invention is executable on standard HPC clusters via MPI, stored on non-transitory media (e.g., SSDs, cloud storage), and operable through commercial solvers like Altair OptiStruct. It enables failsafe-compliant design across aerospace, automotive, and biomedical engineering, transforming topology optimization from a pure efficiency tool into a safety-certifiable design methodology.
