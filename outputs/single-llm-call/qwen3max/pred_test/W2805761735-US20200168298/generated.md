# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD

The present invention relates generally to the field of computational genomics and pharmacogenomics, and more specifically to methods and systems for high-throughput sequencing (HTS)-based genotyping of genes that are subject to structural variation, copy number alterations, and hybridization with pseudogenes. The invention provides a novel framework for allelic decomposition—i.e., determining both the number of copies of a gene and the exact nucleotide sequence of each copy—in complex genomic regions such as those encoding drug-metabolizing enzymes, transporters, and targets (collectively referred to as ADME genes). The invention is particularly applicable to genes like CYP2D6 and CYP2A6, which exhibit high polymorphism, reside in genomic clusters with highly homologous pseudogenes, and frequently undergo structural rearrangements including partial deletions, duplications, and gene fusions. The disclosed methods enable accurate, rapid, and cost-effective genotype calling from whole-genome sequencing (WGS) or targeted capture sequencing data, thereby facilitating personalized medicine applications.

## BACKGROUND

Genetic testing has become increasingly integral to clinical diagnostics, therapeutic decision-making, and pharmacogenomic guidance. With the advent of high-throughput sequencing (HTS) technologies, it is now possible to interrogate the entire genome or targeted genomic regions at unprecedented depth and resolution. HTS platforms, such as those developed by Illumina, generate millions to billions of short DNA sequence reads that can be computationally aligned to a reference human genome to identify genetic variants. However, the analytical interpretation of HTS data remains a significant bottleneck, especially in genomic regions characterized by high sequence homology, repetitive elements, or structural complexity.

High-throughput sequencing encompasses both whole-genome sequencing (WGS), which sequences the entire genome, and targeted sequencing approaches, such as exome sequencing (WES) or custom capture panels like PGRNseq, which enrich specific regions of interest prior to sequencing. While WGS provides comprehensive coverage, it is often cost-prohibitive for routine clinical use. Targeted panels offer a more economical alternative by focusing sequencing power on functionally relevant genes, achieving very high coverage (e.g., 500× per chromosome) at lower cost. Nevertheless, these approaches introduce new analytical challenges, particularly non-uniform coverage due to biases in hybridization efficiency during capture.

A major challenge in analyzing HTS data lies in accurately genotyping genes that are highly polymorphic and structurally variable. Current computational tools for variant calling—such as those based on the Genome Analysis Toolkit (GATK) Best Practices workflow—are optimized for single nucleotide variants (SNVs) and small insertions/deletions (indels) in uniquely mappable regions. They perform poorly in regions with paralogs, pseudogenes, or segmental duplications, where reads cannot be unambiguously assigned to a single genomic locus. This mapping ambiguity severely compromises the accuracy of standard genotyping pipelines.

The importance of accurately genotyping ADME (Absorption, Distribution, Metabolism, Excretion) genes cannot be overstated. These genes encode proteins that determine individual responses to drugs, influencing efficacy, toxicity, and dosing. For example, CYP2D6 metabolizes approximately 25% of clinically used drugs, and its activity varies widely due to genetic polymorphisms. Incorrect genotype assignment can lead to inappropriate drug prescriptions, resulting in therapeutic failure or adverse events.

Existing array-based genotyping assays, such as Affymetrix DMET+ or Illumina ADME panels, detect only a predefined set of common variants. They fail to capture rare or novel alleles, structural variants, or complex haplotypes involving multiple mutations. Moreover, they cannot resolve hybrid genes formed by recombination between a functional gene and its pseudogene—a common occurrence in CYP2D6 due to its proximity to CYP2D7 and CYP2D8.

Targeted genotyping platforms like PGRNseq address some limitations by capturing entire pharmacogene loci, including flanking regions and pseudogenes. However, the resulting HTS data still require sophisticated computational methods to interpret, especially given the non-uniform coverage and the presence of structural variants. No existing tool can perform complete allelic decomposition—i.e., determine both copy number and the precise sequence content of each allele—for genes affected by partial duplications, deletions, or fusions with pseudogenes.

Algorithmically, the problem is compounded by the need to simultaneously resolve: (1) mapping ambiguity of reads originating from homologous regions; (2) estimation of local copy number across exons and introns; (3) detection of structural breakpoints; and (4) phasing of variants into star-alleles according to established pharmacogenomic nomenclature. Existing structural variant callers (e.g., Delly, Pindel) focus on large-scale events in unique regions and ignore pseudogene-mediated fusions. Gene-specific tools like Cypiripi and Astrolabe are limited to SNV-based genotyping or uniform coverage data and cannot handle partial gene alterations. Thus, there remains a critical unmet need for a computational framework capable of comprehensive allelic decomposition of structurally complex pharmacogenes from HTS data.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides methods and systems for genotyping structurally complex genes using high-throughput sequencing (HTS) data. The method begins by receiving HTS sequencing data corresponding to a target sample, wherein the data comprises nucleotide sequence reads derived from one or more genes of interest, particularly ADME genes such as CYP2D6. The reads are aligned to a reference genome augmented with allele-specific sequences from a curated database containing known star-alleles, pseudogenes, and structural configurations.

Following alignment, the method identifies nucleic acid sequence variants—including single nucleotide variants (SNVs) and short indels—within the target gene region. Concurrently, the method detects structural variants by estimating local copy number across exonic and intronic segments and comparing observed coverage profiles to known structural arrangements. This step enables the identification of full or partial gene deletions, duplications, and hybrid genes formed by fusion with pseudogenes.

The method then identifies gene-disrupting mutations—those that alter protein function—and uses them to infer the presence of major star-alleles. A combinatorial optimization framework selects a set of reference star-alleles whose mutational profiles best explain the observed gene-disrupting mutations and structural configurations. This selection is formalized as an integer linear programming (ILP) problem that minimizes discrepancies between observed and expected mutation copy numbers.

Subsequently, the genotype is refined by incorporating neutral mutations—those that do not affect protein function—to distinguish between minor star-alleles that share the same major allele designation. This refinement step resolves ambiguities arising from shared gene-disrupting mutations and assigns each neutral variant to its most likely allelic origin, potentially defining novel minor star-alleles when necessary.

The method is specifically designed for genotyping ADME genes, but is extensible to any gene with known allelic diversity and structural complexity. It can be repeated iteratively for multiple genes of interest within the same sample. The HTS data may originate from whole-genome sequencing, whole-exome sequencing, or targeted capture platforms such as PGRNseq, and may exhibit non-uniform coverage.

The invention further encompasses a computational system for predicting genotype, comprising modules for read alignment, variant calling, structural variant detection, star-allele identification, and genotype refinement. This system operates on suitably programmed computers and generates a final genotype report indicating the star-allele(s) present for each copy of the gene, including novel alleles when no known configuration fully explains the data. The system enables accurate, rapid, and scalable pharmacogenomic profiling suitable for clinical implementation.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention introduces a novel computational tool and associated methods for allelic decomposition of genes that are subject to structural variation, copy number changes, and pseudogene-mediated hybridization. Existing structural variation discovery tools are fundamentally limited in their ability to reconstruct the exact sequence content of each gene copy in complex loci. These tools typically rely on discordant read pairs, split reads, or de novo assembly, but they assume that structural variants occur in uniquely mappable regions and do not account for the presence of highly homologous pseudogenes. Consequently, they fail to detect or characterize hybrid alleles formed by recombination between a functional gene and its pseudogene—a common phenomenon in pharmacogenes like CYP2D6.

This limitation motivates the need for a new approach that integrates variant detection, copy number estimation, and star-allele matching within a unified combinatorial framework. The invention addresses this need through a method implemented in a software tool called Aldy, which performs comprehensive genotyping of ADME genes from HTS data.

The method leverages the PGRNseq capture protocol, a targeted sequencing platform that enriches 84 pharmacogenes, including exonic regions and several kilobases of upstream and downstream sequence. PGRNseq offers significant advantages over whole-genome sequencing (WGS) and whole-exome sequencing (WES) for pharmacogenomic applications: it achieves very high coverage (average 500–600× per chromosome) at a fraction of the cost of WGS, while providing more complete gene coverage than WES, which often misses regulatory regions. However, PGRNseq data exhibit non-uniform coverage due to capture biases, posing challenges for copy number estimation—a challenge explicitly addressed by the present invention.

Genotyping ADME genes is particularly challenging due to their inherent complexity. The CYP2D6 gene serves as a paradigmatic example: it is flanked by two highly homologous pseudogenes, CYP2D7 and CYP2D8, with which it frequently recombines to form hybrid alleles. CYP2D6 exhibits extensive allelic diversity, with over 100 star-alleles documented, many involving structural rearrangements such as *13 (a CYP2D6/CYP2D7 fusion), *36 (a partial duplication), and *68 (a complex rearrangement). Existing CYP2D6 genotyping tools like Cypiripi and Astrolabe are inadequate because they either assume uniform coverage or cannot model structural variants beyond full-gene duplications.

To standardize allele reporting, the pharmacogenomics community employs star-allele nomenclature. A star-allele is defined by a unique combination of mutations relative to the wild-type (*1) sequence. Mutations are categorized as either gene-disrupting (functional) or neutral (non-functional). Gene-disrupting mutations include nonsynonymous SNVs, frameshift indels, splice-site variants, and promoter/enhancer mutations that alter enzyme activity. Star-alleles defined solely by gene-disrupting mutations are termed major star-alleles (e.g., *2, *4, *10). Neutral mutations—such as synonymous SNVs or intronic variants with no functional impact—extend major alleles into minor star-alleles (e.g., *2A, *2B).

The genotyping method proceeds through a series of well-defined steps. First, HTS data for the target gene are received from the sample. These data are aligned to a reference genome allele database that includes sequences for all known major and minor star-alleles, pseudogenes, and hybrid configurations. The alignment is assessed for acceptability; if insufficient, a reference-guided assembly step may be invoked to improve mapping.

Next, nucleic acid variants are identified by comparing aligned reads to the reference. Structural variants are detected by estimating local copy number across gene segments and identifying deviations from diploid expectation. Gene-disrupting mutations are flagged based on annotations in the allele database.

A set of reference star-alleles is then selected to explain the observed mutations and structural profile. This is achieved by solving a Major Star-Allele Identification Problem (MSAIP) formulated as an integer linear program that minimizes the mismatch between observed and expected mutation copy numbers. The solution yields a candidate genotype comprising one or more major star-alleles.

This candidate genotype is refined by incorporating neutral mutations to distinguish between minor star-alleles. The Genotype Refining Problem (GRP) is solved as a quadratic integer program that assigns each neutral variant to the most probable major allele background, potentially defining novel minor alleles when necessary. The final genotype is the solution with the highest confidence score.

The method is performed by a suitably programmed computer and can genotype multiple genes simultaneously. In cases where alignment to the reference is unacceptable due to extreme divergence, the method employs reference-guided assembly of HTS reads to reconstruct allele sequences de novo before proceeding with variant and structural analysis. This ensures robustness even for highly divergent or novel alleles.

### CYP2D6

The CYP2D6 gene is a central focus of the present invention due to its clinical significance and structural complexity. CYP2D6 metabolizes a wide array of drugs, including antidepressants, antipsychotics, and beta-blockers, and its activity ranges from poor to ultrarapid metabolism based on genotype. Accurate CYP2D6 genotyping is therefore critical for dose optimization. Similarly, CYP2A6, a major nicotine metabolizer, exhibits structural variation and pseudogene interference, making it another key target. The methods described herein are directly applicable to CYP2A6 and other ADME genes, demonstrating flexibility across diverse genomic architectures. The underlying mathematical models and equations are generalizable, allowing adaptation to any gene with a curated allele database.

### HTS Data

The method begins by receiving high-throughput sequencing (HTS) data generated from a biological sample. This data may be produced internally using platforms like Illumina HiSeq or obtained from external sources such as public repositories (e.g., 1000 Genomes Project). The HTS data are aligned to a reference genome allele database that includes canonical sequences, pseudogenes, and all known star-alleles for the target gene. Prior to analysis, the sequencing process involves library preparation, target enrichment (if using a capture panel like PGRNseq), and sequencing on an appropriate platform. The reference genome allele database is obtained from authoritative sources such as the Human Cytochrome P450 Allele Nomenclature Database, PharmGKB, and dbSNP, and is regularly updated to include newly discovered alleles.

### Alignment/Read Mapping

Alignment of HTS reads is performed against each allele sequence in the reference genome allele database to resolve mapping ambiguity in homologous regions. Reads are initially mapped to the standard human reference genome (e.g., GRCh38) using aligners like BWA or CORA. Local indel realignment is conducted using tools such as GATK to improve variant calling accuracy. An appropriate alignment algorithm is selected based on read length and error profile. In difficult regions, reference-guided assembly may be employed to reconstruct contigs that better represent sample-specific alleles. Acceptable alignment is achieved when a sufficient fraction of reads map uniquely or can be probabilistically assigned to specific alleles. Nucleic acid variants are identified by comparing aligned reads to reference sequences, and the Genome Analysis Toolkit’s Best Practices workflow is used to confirm variant calls through base quality recalibration and variant filtration.

### Sequence Variant Calling

Nucleic acid variants—including single nucleotide variants (SNVs) and short insertions/deletions (indels)—are identified by scanning aligned reads for positions that differ from the reference genome allele database. Variant calling algorithms, such as those implemented in GATK HaplotypeCaller or FreeBayes, are used to distinguish true variants from sequencing errors. Identified variants are confirmed through statistical filters that assess read depth, base quality, mapping quality, and strand bias. Only high-confidence variants are retained for downstream genotyping steps.

### Detecting Structural Variants

Structural variants are detected by estimating gene copy number and identifying deviations from expected diploid coverage. Observed coverage is calculated across exonic and intronic segments of the target gene. Known possible gene arrangements—including full deletions, duplications, partial deletions/duplications, and hybrid genes—are obtained from the allele database. A database of structural variant signatures is used to match observed coverage patterns to known configurations. Copy number variations (CNVs) are detected by comparing normalized coverage to diploid baselines. Gene deletions and duplications are identified when coverage drops to zero or increases proportionally. Partial gene deletions/duplications affect only subsets of exons/introns. Hybrid genes—formed by fusion between the functional gene and a pseudogene—are defined by chimeric coverage patterns. An aggregate copy number profile is calculated by summing coverage across homologous regions. The number of whole gene copies, hybrid gene copies, and structural variant copies are determined by solving a combinatorial optimization problem. Copy number of each genomic region is estimated using normalized coverage depth, and the Copy Number Estimation Problem (CNEP) is solved to find the most parsimonious set of structural configurations explaining the data.

### Coverage Normalization

Non-uniform coverage in the PGRNseq platform is addressed through a specialized normalization procedure. Analysis of 96 samples revealed a consistent depth-of-coverage shape attributable to capture biases. A reference sample with known diploid copy number is used to characterize this shape. The sum of coverage depth across both chromosomal copies is calculated for each position. A scaling function Bs is rescaled to obtain a reference coverage depth function Rs that represents expected diploid coverage. The normalization factor η is estimated using genomic regions q known to have stable copy number (e.g., non-polymorphic control regions). FIGS. 4A–4C illustrate this rescaling process. Smaller gene regions are analyzed individually to account for localized structural changes. A binary vector v characterizes rearrangement configurations, with each element indicating the presence/absence of a specific exon/intron segment. Examples in FIGS. 5A–5C show how normalization enables accurate copy number inference. The optimal gene arrangement is identified by minimizing the difference between observed normalized coverage and known arrangements. The function cns denotes normalized copy number at each locus, while mutcn denotes the estimated copy number of a specific mutation. Both autosomes are accounted for in diploid organisms. The number of reads containing a mutation is normalized by the expected coverage of its locus to estimate mutcn.

### Major Star-Allele Identification

Major star-allele identification begins by obtaining sets of gene-disrupting mutations and their corresponding star-alleles from the database. For each gene copy, the method detects which major star-allele is present. The set M of all gene-disrupting mutations detected in the sample is identified. Major star-alleles containing mutations not present in M are filtered out, yielding a reduced set A of candidate alleles. Non-negative integer variables p1, p2, pt represent the number of copies of each candidate allele. The error Em quantifies the difference between estimated and observed copy numbers for each mutation m. A constraint ensures that if a gene-disrupting mutation is observed, at least one corresponding major star-allele must be present in the genotype. The set of major star-alleles that most closely matches M is selected by minimizing the total error. This Major Star-Allele Identification Problem (MSAIP) is formulated and solved as an Integer Linear Program (ILP).

### Genotype Refining

Ambiguity in major star-allele identification is resolved using neutral mutations to distinguish between minor star-alleles. The Genotype Refining Problem (GRP) takes as input the set of major star-alleles inferred by MSAIP. Its goal is to extend each major allele definition to a minor allele definition by assigning neutral mutations. The function mut(a) denotes the full set of mutations (both gene-disrupting and neutral) defining minor star-allele a. Binary variables xa,b indicate whether minor allele a is the correct extension of major allele b. Additional binary variables ea,b,m and fa,b,m model whether mutation m is present in the inferred allele. The objective minimizes a weighted difference between expected and observed mutation assignments, penalizing missing known mutations more heavily than adding novel ones. Each observed mutation is assigned to one or more major star-alleles. Gene-disrupting mutations are strictly conserved in all extensions. Over-calling—assigning more copies of a mutation than observed—is prevented by sentinel constraints. GRP is solved as a Quadratic Integer Program (QIP) to yield the final genotype.

## Complexity

The computational problems underlying the invention—specifically the Copy Number Estimation Problem (CNEP) and the Major Star-Allele Identification Problem (MSAIP)—are NP-hard in the general case. This is proven by reduction from the Closest Vector Problem (CVP), a well-known NP-hard problem in computational geometry. Even approximate solutions with additive guarantees are NP-hard under standard complexity assumptions. Despite this theoretical hardness, practical instances are efficiently solvable using state-of-the-art integer programming solvers like Gurobi or SCIP, which employ advanced heuristics, cutting planes, and branch-and-bound techniques. The NP-hardness underscores the necessity of the combinatorial optimization approach, as greedy or heuristic methods would fail to guarantee optimal or near-optimal solutions in complex cases.

## Systems

The invention is implemented in a genotype predictor system comprising interconnected hardware and software components. The system architecture includes a sample generator that prepares biological specimens for sequencing, a sequencer (e.g., Illumina HiSeq) that produces HTS data, and databases storing reference genomes, allele definitions, and structural variant catalogs. A sequence analyzer processes raw data through multiple analytical modules: a sequence aligner maps reads to references; a sequence variant identifier calls SNVs and indels; a structural variant identifier detects CNVs and fusions; a gene-disrupting mutation identifier flags functional variants; a star-allele identifier matches mutations to known alleles; a genotype caller assigns initial genotypes; and a genotype refiner resolves ambiguities. Input/output (I/O) devices facilitate user interaction, while a storage system archives data and results. A system controller orchestrates workflow execution. The user interface allows clinicians or researchers to upload samples, configure parameters, and view reports. Data analysis proceeds automatically once initiated, culminating in genotype report generation. Reports are displayed on-screen and can be delivered electronically or printed. A "module" refers to a software component performing a specific function, while the "system" encompasses all hardware and software elements. Software and firmware implement the genotyping algorithms, running on processing units configured with sufficient memory and storage. Communication links connect components via networks (e.g., LAN, internet), enabling remote access and cloud deployment.

## Examples

The invention was validated on three distinct datasets. Dataset 1 comprised 96 Coriell cell line samples from 32 family trios, captured with PGRNseq v.2 and sequenced to ~600× coverage; CYP2D6 genotypes were validated by PCR panels. Dataset 2 included 137 GeT-RM cell lines sequenced with PGRNseq v.1 at similar coverage, with genotypes for 10 ADME genes available for comparison. Dataset 3 consisted of 25 whole-genome samples from the Platinum Genomes, CEPH 1362, and 1000 Genomes projects, sequenced at 20–50× coverage. Performance evaluation showed that the method correctly genotyped >99% of samples across all datasets, significantly outperforming existing tools like Cypiripi and Astrolabe, which failed on 40–50% of structurally complex samples. The method accurately resolved deletions, duplications, and fusions, with no Mendelian inconsistencies observed in family trios. Novel alleles were discovered, demonstrating the method’s ability to extend beyond predefined variant sets.

### DISCUSSION

The present methods achieve superior accuracy in ADME genotyping compared to existing approaches. Discrepancies between predictions and validation panels were investigated and attributed to limitations of the panels themselves—e.g., TaqMan assays failed to detect key SNPs distinguishing *35/*45 from *2, or misidentified *15 due to pseudogene homology. In case (4) involving samples NA19834–NA19836, TaqMan incorrectly called copy number due to probe design limitations. Case (2) demonstrated accurate detection of the *13-like fusion allele *76, validated by orthogonal methods. Sample NA10860, predicted to carry a *4 duplication, was cross-validated using Illumina HiSeq X WGS data, confirming the call through consistent coverage profiles across the CYP2D6 region. No Mendelian inconsistencies were found in PGRNseq v.2 trio data, unlike previous analyses that relied on standard SNP callers. Comparisons with literature-validated genotypes from the CEPH 1463 family (FIG. 6) showed full concordance with inheritance patterns. CYP2A6 genotyping also achieved high accuracy. Computational overhead is minimal—each sample processes in <10 seconds with <100 MB memory—making the method suitable for clinical pipelines. Performance surpasses other tools, especially on CYP2D6, where structural complexity confounds conventional approaches. Across 10 ADME genes, accuracy exceeded 99%, with novel major star-alleles detected in several samples. Key advantages include comprehensive structural variant handling, pseudogene-aware alignment, combinatorial optimization for allele resolution, and support for both targeted and whole-genome data.
