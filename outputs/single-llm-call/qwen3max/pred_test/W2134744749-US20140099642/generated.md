# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNICAL FIELD

The present invention relates to the field of prenatal diagnostics and, more specifically, to noninvasive methods for detecting fetal genetic abnormalities using cell-free DNA isolated from maternal plasma. The invention provides a novel methodology that leverages massively parallel sequencing (MPS) combined with advanced bioinformatic correction for GC bias—a well-known technical artifact in high-throughput sequencing—to enable highly sensitive and specific detection of both autosomal and sex chromosomal aneuploidies. The disclosed method is particularly suited for identifying trisomies 13, 18, and 21, as well as sex chromosome abnormalities such as Turner syndrome (45,X), Klinefelter syndrome (47,XXY), and XYY syndrome. By integrating statistical modeling, coverage depth normalization, and fetal fraction estimation, the invention offers a robust, noninvasive alternative to conventional invasive diagnostic procedures like amniocentesis or chorionic villus sampling, which carry inherent risks of pregnancy loss. The technical field thus encompasses molecular diagnostics, computational genomics, reproductive medicine, and clinical laboratory science.

## BACKGROUND ART

Noninvasive prenatal diagnosis has emerged as a critical advancement in obstetric care, driven by the need to reduce the risks associated with invasive procedures while maintaining high diagnostic accuracy. Traditional methods for detecting fetal aneuploidies—such as karyotyping of amniotic fluid or chorionic villi—require invasive sampling and carry a small but significant risk of miscarriage, typically estimated at 0.5% to 1%. Noninvasive screening approaches based on maternal serum markers (e.g., alpha-fetoprotein, human chorionic gonadotropin) and ultrasound measurements (e.g., nuchal translucency) have been widely adopted but suffer from limited sensitivity and specificity, often necessitating confirmatory invasive testing for high-risk cases. The discovery of cell-free fetal DNA (cff-DNA) in maternal plasma by Lo et al. in 1997 opened a new avenue for truly noninvasive prenatal testing, as cff-DNA originates from the placenta and reflects the fetal genome. However, cff-DNA constitutes only a small fraction—typically 5% to 10%—of the total cell-free DNA in maternal plasma, posing a significant analytical challenge for detecting subtle genomic imbalances such as aneuploidies.

The advent of massively parallel sequencing (MPS) enabled genome-wide analysis of maternal plasma DNA, allowing for the detection of fetal aneuploidies by quantifying the relative representation of each chromosome. Early MPS-based approaches relied on z-score statistics to compare observed chromosome coverage against expected values derived from euploid controls. While effective for trisomy 21, these methods showed reduced performance for trisomies 13 and 18, largely due to technical biases introduced during library preparation and sequencing. One major source of such bias is the differential amplification and sequencing efficiency of DNA fragments based on their GC content—a phenomenon known as GC bias. Chromosomes with extreme GC content (either very high or very low) are underrepresented or overrepresented in sequencing data, leading to inaccurate coverage estimates and false-positive or false-negative results. Although several strategies have been proposed to correct for GC bias, including binning-based normalization and regression models, they often fail to fully account for the nonlinear and chromosome-specific nature of this artifact.

Moreover, the detection of sex chromosomal aneuploidies presents additional challenges, including the need to accurately determine fetal sex and estimate the fetal DNA fraction, especially in pregnancies with female fetuses where Y-chromosome markers are absent. Prior methods relying on internal chromosome controls or simple z-scores struggled with gender misclassification and insufficient resolution for low-prevalence conditions like 45,X or 47,XYY. These limitations highlight the need for an improved methodology that not only corrects for GC bias with high precision but also integrates fetal fraction estimation, gender classification, and a statistically rigorous framework for aneuploidy detection across all chromosomes. The present invention addresses these unmet needs by introducing a GC-correlation t-test approach that models the relationship between coverage depth and GC content using locally weighted regression (Loess), enabling accurate baseline prediction and enhanced sensitivity for both autosomal and sex chromosomal abnormalities.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a method for noninvasively detecting fetal genetic abnormalities in a pregnant female subject by analyzing cell-free DNA isolated from a biological sample, such as maternal plasma. The method begins by obtaining sequence information of polynucleotide fragments derived from the biological sample using massively parallel sequencing. These fragments are then assigned to their corresponding chromosomes based on alignment to a reference human genome. For each chromosome, the coverage depth—the number of uniquely mapped reads per unit length—is calculated, along with the GC content, defined as the percentage of guanine and cytosine bases in the genomic regions covered by the fragments. A key innovation of the invention is the establishment of a relationship between coverage depth and GC content using a non-linear regression model, such as the Loess algorithm, applied to a cohort of reference samples with known euploid status. This relationship is used to calculate a fitted coverage depth, representing the expected coverage for a given GC content in the absence of aneuploidy.

The method then compares the observed coverage depth to the fitted coverage depth to detect deviations indicative of chromosomal imbalance. Specifically, a Student’s t-statistic is computed using the residual (difference between observed and fitted coverage) and the standard deviation derived from the reference population. In one embodiment, a binary hypothesis testing framework is employed: a first t-test evaluates the null hypothesis that the fetus is euploid, while a second t-test evaluates the alternative hypothesis that the fetus is trisomic. The logarithmic likelihood odds ratio between these two hypotheses is used to make a confident diagnosis. Additionally, the invention includes steps to determine fetal gender by analyzing coverage on chromosome Y and to estimate the fetal DNA fraction using sex chromosome coverage in male-bearing pregnancies. For female fetuses, fetal fraction estimation is inferred indirectly through statistical modeling. The method further extends to the detection of sex chromosomal aneuploidies such as 45,X, 47,XXX, 47,XXY, and 47,XYY by applying chromosome-specific t-tests and incorporating fetal fraction estimates as discriminative markers. The invention also encompasses a computer-readable medium and a system configured to perform the aforementioned steps, enabling automated, high-throughput analysis of sequencing data for clinical prenatal diagnosis.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

### I. DEFINITIONS

As used herein, the term “chromosomal abnormality” refers to any deviation from the normal diploid chromosomal complement, including aneuploidies such as trisomy (an extra chromosome) or monosomy (a missing chromosome). “Reference unique reads” denote sequencing reads that map uniquely to a single location in the reference human genome and are used for coverage calculations. The terms “polynucleotide,” “oligonucleotide,” “nucleic acid,” and “nucleic acid molecule” are used interchangeably to refer to polymers of nucleotides, whether DNA or RNA, natural or synthetic, and include modified backbones or bases. “Massively parallel sequencing” refers to high-throughput sequencing technologies that simultaneously determine the sequences of millions to billions of DNA fragments, such as those provided by Illumina platforms. A “biological sample” includes any bodily fluid or tissue from which cell-free DNA can be extracted, with maternal plasma being the preferred source. “Monosomy X” (Turner syndrome) is a condition in which a female has only one X chromosome (45,X). “XXY syndrome” (Klinefelter syndrome) and “XYY syndrome” are sex chromosomal aneuploidies affecting males. “Trisomy 13” (Patau syndrome), “trisomy 18” (Edwards syndrome), and “trisomy 21” (Down syndrome) are autosomal aneuploidies with severe clinical consequences. The singular forms of terms used herein include plural references unless the context clearly dictates otherwise. The invention encompasses all aspects and embodiments described, including combinations of features and steps.

### II. ESTABLISHING A RELATIONSHIP BETWEEN COVERAGE DEPTH AND GC CONTENT

To implement the invention, sequence information from multiple polynucleotide fragments in a biological sample is obtained via massively parallel sequencing. These fragments are aligned to a reference human genome, and each is assigned to its corresponding chromosome. The coverage depth for each chromosome is calculated as the number of uniquely mapped reads divided by the chromosome length, normalized to account for differences in total sequencing depth across samples. Simultaneously, the GC content of each chromosome is determined as the proportion of G and C bases in the genomic regions covered by the fragments. Using a cohort of reference samples from pregnant women carrying euploid fetuses, the relationship between coverage depth and GC content is established. This relationship is typically non-linear and varies by chromosome; for example, chromosomes with GC content above 41% may show positive correlation, while those below 41% show negative correlation. The Loess (locally estimated scatterplot smoothing) algorithm is employed to fit a smooth curve to the data, capturing the complex, non-parametric relationship without assuming a specific functional form. The resulting model yields a fitted coverage depth for any given GC content, which serves as the expected baseline for euploid chromosomes. This step is repeated using multiple reference samples to ensure robustness and generalizability of the model.

### III. DETERMINING A FETAL GENETIC ABNORMALITY

In the diagnostic phase, sequence information from a test sample is processed identically: fragments are assigned to chromosomes, and coverage depth and GC content are calculated. The fitted coverage depth is then predicted using the pre-established GC-content model. The observed coverage depth is compared to this fitted value, and the residual (difference) is standardized by the standard deviation of residuals from the reference population to compute a Student’s t-statistic. A significantly elevated or reduced t-statistic indicates a deviation consistent with aneuploidy. For autosomes, a binary hypothesis test is applied: the null hypothesis assumes euploidy, while the alternative assumes trisomy. The logarithmic likelihood ratio of the two t-statistics provides a quantitative measure of confidence. For sex chromosomes, fetal gender is first determined using coverage on chromosome Y and logistic regression, after which sex-specific models are applied to detect abnormalities such as 45,X or 47,XXY. The method thus enables comprehensive, noninvasive detection of a wide range of fetal genetic abnormalities.

### IV. COMPUTER READABLE MEDIUM AND SYSTEM FOR DIAGNOSIS OF A FETAL GENETIC ABNORMALITY

The invention further includes a computer system and a non-transitory computer-readable medium encoded with instructions that, when executed, cause the system to perform the diagnostic method. The system receives raw sequencing data, aligns reads to the reference genome, assigns fragments to chromosomes, calculates coverage depth and GC content, retrieves the pre-trained GC-coverage model, computes fitted coverage, and performs statistical tests to determine the presence of fetal genetic abnormalities. The system may include modules for fetal gender classification, fetal fraction estimation, and quality control based on sequencing metrics such as Q20% and PCR duplication rate. The output is a diagnostic report indicating the presence or absence of specific aneuploidies with associated confidence scores.

### V. EXAMPLES

Example 1 demonstrates the strong correlation between chromosomal GC content and sequencing coverage in 300 euploid pregnancies, revealing a biphasic relationship centered at 41% GC. Example 2 details the application of the Loess algorithm to fit coverage depth as a function of GC content, yielding fitted values and standard deviations used in subsequent t-tests. Example 3 describes fetal fraction estimation using sex chromosome coverage in male pregnancies, with formulas accounting for contributions from maternal and fetal DNA. Example 4 analyzes the standard variation of chromosome residuals, showing stability with sufficient sample size (>100). Example 5 implements logistic regression for fetal gender prediction, achieving >99.9% accuracy. Subsequent examples validate the method on 903 clinical samples, showing 100% sensitivity for trisomies 13, 18, and 21, and high accuracy for sex chromosomal aneuploidies. The GC-correlation t-test approach outperforms prior z-score methods in both autosomal and sex chromosome detection, with theoretical sensitivity analyses confirming robustness across gestational ages and sequencing depths. The examples conclude that the invention provides a reliable, noninvasive platform for comprehensive prenatal aneuploidy screening.
