# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

Serial femtosecond crystallography (SFX) represents a transformative advancement in structural biology, enabling the determination of high-resolution protein structures from microcrystals and nanocrystals that are often too small or radiation-sensitive for conventional synchrotron-based X-ray crystallography. This technique leverages the ultrafast, high-intensity pulses generated by X-ray free-electron lasers (XFELs), which deliver femtosecond-duration X-ray bursts capable of capturing diffraction patterns before the onset of radiation-induced damage—a principle known as “diffraction before destruction.” Since its inception, SFX has facilitated the elucidation of previously intractable protein structures, particularly those of membrane proteins and large macromolecular complexes that resist crystallization in large, well-ordered forms. However, the success of SFX is critically dependent on the method by which protein crystals are delivered into the XFEL beam path, a challenge that has spurred the development of various delivery systems.

The SFX technique typically involves the continuous injection of a suspension of protein crystals into the interaction region of an XFEL beam. Early implementations relied on liquid jet injectors, such as the gas dynamic virtual nozzle (GDVN), which uses a coaxial sheath of inert gas to focus a liquid stream containing crystals into a narrow jet. While effective for soluble proteins, this approach suffers from extremely high sample consumption—often requiring 10 to 100 milligrams of purified protein per data set—due to the high flow rates (10–25 µl min⁻¹) necessary to maintain a stable jet at the repetition rates of modern XFELs (up to 120 Hz). This inefficiency is especially problematic for membrane proteins, which are notoriously difficult to express, purify, and crystallize in large quantities. Membrane proteins constitute approximately 60% of current drug targets, yet they account for less than 1% of all structures deposited in the Protein Data Bank (PDB), underscoring the urgent need for more efficient delivery methods that conserve precious sample.

To address these limitations, researchers have turned to viscous media that enable slower, more controlled delivery of crystals. Among these, lipidic cubic phase (LCP) has emerged as a leading platform for membrane protein crystallization and delivery. LCP is a bicontinuous lipidic mesophase formed by mixing monoacylglycerols with water, creating a three-dimensional network of lipid bilayers separated by aqueous channels that mimic the native membrane environment. Crystallization in LCP has been highly successful for G-protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs) and other membrane proteins, and its high viscosity allows for low-flow-rate delivery (∼100 nl min⁻¹), drastically reducing protein consumption. However, LCP presents significant challenges when applied to large, multi-subunit membrane protein complexes. The intrinsic curvature of the lipid bilayer in LCP impedes the incorporation and diffusion of large protein assemblies, and attempts to mix pre-formed detergent-solubilized crystals into LCP often result in crystal dissolution due to detergent partitioning into the lipid phase, destabilizing the protein–detergent micelles.

Alternative viscous media, including mineral oil-based grease and petroleum jelly, have been explored as potential carriers for SFX. While these materials can support crystal delivery at ambient pressure, they introduce substantial background scattering in the form of Debye–Scherrer rings at resolutions between 3.77 and 5 Å, which can obscure critical diffraction signals. Moreover, these media have not been validated for use with complex membrane proteins or under high-vacuum conditions typical of XFEL endstations. Additionally, existing crystal delivery systems lack compatibility with a broad range of crystallization conditions, particularly those involving low ionic strength, absence of precipitants, or sensitivity to organic solvents—conditions common in the crystallization of delicate photosynthetic complexes like photosystem I (PSI).

These limitations highlight a clear and unmet need for an inert, low-background, versatile crystal delivery medium that is compatible with both soluble and membrane proteins, functions across a wide temperature range, and supports stable jet formation under vacuum. Such a medium must preserve crystal integrity, minimize sample consumption, and produce negligible background scattering to enable high-fidelity data collection. The development of such a medium would significantly broaden the applicability of SFX to structurally and biologically important protein systems that are currently inaccessible due to sample scarcity or incompatibility with existing delivery platforms.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a novel method for preparing an inert crystal delivery medium suitable for serial femtosecond crystallography (SFX), comprising the steps of dissolving agarose in a solution containing a cryoprotectant and a crystallization buffer, heating the mixture to fully dissolve the agarose, cooling the solution to form a gel, and embedding pre-formed protein crystals into the gel matrix through mechanical mixing. In specific embodiments, the method includes optimizing the concentration of agarose and the type and concentration of cryoprotectant to achieve a viscosity compatible with injector systems while maintaining crystal stability. The method further encompasses the preparation of the agarose solution in the same buffer used for protein crystallization to prevent osmotic or chemical shock to the crystals.

The invention also introduces a crystal delivery medium composed of agarose, water, a cryoprotectant, and a crystallization buffer, wherein the agarose is present at a concentration between 5% and 9% (w/v), and the cryoprotectant is selected from glycerol, sucrose, or other polyols known to inhibit ice formation. In preferred embodiments, the medium contains 5.6% (w/v) agarose and 30% (v/v) glycerol, although alternative formulations using sucrose (e.g., 0.6 M) are employed for cryoprotectant-sensitive proteins such as photosystem I. The medium is characterized by its low X-ray scattering background, thermal stability over a range of 4°C to 30°C, and compatibility with a wide array of crystallization conditions, including high-salt, polyethylene glycol (PEG)-based, and low-ionic-strength buffers.

Additionally, the invention provides a system for preparing the crystal delivery medium, comprising a first syringe containing a suspension of protein crystals in their mother liquor, a second syringe containing a warm agarose solution, and a syringe coupler configured to connect the two syringes for bidirectional mixing. The system is designed to facilitate homogeneous distribution of crystals within the agarose gel without damaging the crystals, and it is compatible with standard laboratory equipment and XFEL injector hardware. The system enables precise control over the final concentration of agarose and cryoprotectant, allowing customization for different protein systems and experimental conditions.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

The present invention introduces an agarose-based crystal delivery medium specifically engineered for use in serial femtosecond crystallography (SFX). This medium is prepared by combining agarose, a natural polysaccharide derived from seaweed, with a crystallization buffer and a cryoprotectant. The resulting composition forms a thermoreversible gel that remains stable across a broad temperature range (4–30°C), enabling compatibility with diverse crystallization protocols. The crystal solution, defined as a suspension of pre-formed protein crystals in their mother liquor or crystallization buffer, is embedded into the agarose matrix post-crystallization, preserving crystal integrity while enabling low-flow-rate delivery.

Crystals suitable for use with the invention include nanocrystals (typically <1 µm) and microcrystals (1–10 µm), though larger crystals may also be accommodated. The crystallization buffer may contain any standard precipitant, including ammonium sulfate, sodium chloride, calcium chloride, PEGs of varying molecular weights (e.g., PEG 400 to PEG 8000), or organic solvents such as 2-methyl-2,4-pentanediol (MPD). The medium demonstrates exceptional compatibility with these components, maintaining gel stability and stream integrity even in the presence of up to 30% PEG or 1.25 M salt.

Cryoprotectants are essential for preventing ice formation during data collection in high-vacuum environments, where evaporative cooling can induce freezing. Common cryoprotectants include glycerol, ethylene glycol, sucrose, and low-molecular-weight PEGs. In the preferred embodiment, glycerol is used at 30% (v/v) due to its dual function as both a cryoprotectant and a viscosity enhancer. For cryoprotectant-sensitive proteins like PSI, sucrose (0.6 M) is substituted to avoid denaturation.

Agarose is employed at concentrations between 5% and 9% (w/v), with 5.6% being optimal after dilution during mixing. The agarose solution is prepared by dissolving ultralow-gelling-temperature agarose in a mixture of crystallization buffer and cryoprotectant, heated to above 85°C for complete dissolution. Upon cooling, the solution forms a viscoelastic gel with rheological properties suitable for extrusion through capillary injectors at flow rates as low as 160 nl min⁻¹.

The mixing process involves drawing the warm agarose solution into a first syringe and the crystal suspension into a second syringe, then connecting the syringes via a coupler and performing at least 40 bidirectional plunger movements to ensure homogeneous crystal distribution. This method prevents crystal loss, dehydration, and aggregation, which are common with manual mixing techniques. The final crystal density in the medium is typically 2 × 10¹⁰ crystals per milliliter, sufficient for high hit rates in SFX experiments.

A key advantage of the agarose medium is its low X-ray scattering background. Unlike LCP, which produces strong diffuse scattering at 4.5 Å and significant low-angle scattering (>30 Å), agarose exhibits minimal background, with only a weak diffuse ring at 3.3 Å. Quantitative analysis shows that agarose generates approximately 2.3 times less background than LCP in the critical 4–5 Å region, making it ideal for resolving medium-to-low resolution features and for studying large unit cells.

The system for preparing the medium includes standard syringes, a syringe coupler, and a heating source (e.g., boiling water bath). The entire process is compatible with ambient or vacuum operation, though vacuum is preferred for enhanced stream stability due to reduced turbulence. The agarose stream is stabilized using a high-velocity inert-gas sheath (e.g., nitrogen), which centers the jet in the XFEL beam path.

The scope of the invention extends to all equivalents, modifications, and alternative embodiments that achieve the same functional result—namely, the delivery of intact protein crystals in a low-background, viscous medium for SFX. This includes variations in agarose type, cryoprotectant selection, buffer composition, and mixing methodology. The invention concludes as a robust, general-purpose platform for SFX, enabling structural studies of previously inaccessible protein systems.

### EXAMPLE 1

To demonstrate the utility of the agarose-based delivery medium, phycocyanin (PC), a large soluble protein complex from *Thermosynechococcus elongatus*, was selected as a model system. PC, a trimeric light-harvesting complex composed of α and β subunits, was purified via cell disruption and ultracentrifugation, followed by concentration using 100 kDa cutoff filters. Crystals were grown by free interface diffusion using 1.0 M ammonium sulfate and 40 mM MES pH 6.4, yielding 1–5 µm crystals within 24 hours. These were filtered through a 10 µm stainless-steel mesh to remove aggregates.

Photosystem I (PSI) and photosystem II (PSII), large membrane protein complexes, were also purified using established protocols. PSI was stabilized in 5 mM MES pH 6.4 with 0.02% β-DDM, while PSII was crystallized in a buffer containing PIPES, CaCl₂, tocopherol, and PEG 2000. All crystals were embedded into agarose post-crystallization.

An agarose solution was prepared by dissolving 7% (w/v) ultralow-gelling-temperature agarose in a mixture of crystallization buffer and cryoprotectant (30% glycerol for PC and PSII; 0.6 M sucrose for PSI), heated in a boiling water bath for 30 minutes. The solution was drawn into a pre-warmed syringe, cooled to room temperature, and mixed with 5 µl of crystal suspension using a syringe coupler. After 40 mixing cycles, the homogeneous mixture was loaded into an LCP injector.

Data were collected at the Coherent X-ray Imaging (CXI) instrument at LCLS using a 50 µm capillary at 160 nl min⁻¹. For PC, 513,848 detector frames were processed using Cheetah to identify 41,100 hits (≥25 Bragg spots), of which 14,143 were indexed in space group P6₃ with unit-cell parameters a = b = 153.4 Å, c = 39.6 Å. The data were integrated using CrystFEL, with indexing ambiguity resolved by ambigator. A resolution cutoff of 2.5 Å was applied based on CC* and multiplicity.

Phasing was performed by molecular replacement using PDB entry 4gy3, followed by refinement in phenix.refine, including rigid-body, positional, and B-factor refinement, and water addition. The final model achieved R_work = 18.7% and R_free = 25.5%, with clear electron density for side chains and the phycocyanobilin chromophore.

Comparative tests of viscous media confirmed that agarose produced the most stable stream. Glycerol was identified as the optimal cryoprotectant, and agarose concentration was tuned to 5.6% post-mixing. The syringe mixing method proved superior to manual embedding, ensuring uniform crystal distribution and preventing dehydration. The agarose stream exhibited excellent stability in vacuum, outperforming ambient-pressure setups due to reduced turbulence.

Background scattering measurements revealed that agarose produced significantly lower background than LCP, mineral oil grease, or petroleum jelly, especially at low resolution. PC data statistics confirmed high data quality, with multiplicity of 250.6 overall and 12.5 in the highest shell. The structure was successfully solved, validating agarose as a viable medium for SFX.

In conclusion, the agarose delivery medium is suitable for SFX of both soluble and membrane proteins, offering low sample consumption, minimal background, and broad compatibility—enabling structural studies of scarce and complex biological systems.
