# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

Autoimmune diseases represent a broad class of disorders in which the body’s immune system mistakenly targets its own tissues, resulting in chronic inflammation, tissue damage, and functional impairment. These conditions can affect virtually any organ or system within the human body, ranging from the central nervous system to endocrine glands, joints, skin, and gastrointestinal tract. Collectively, autoimmune diseases afflict an estimated 15 to 24 million individuals in the United States alone, underscoring their significant public health burden and the urgent need for improved diagnostic and therapeutic strategies. Despite advances in immunomodulatory therapies, many patients experience delayed diagnosis, suboptimal treatment responses, and progressive disability due to irreversible tissue damage that often occurs before clinical symptoms become apparent.

Multiple sclerosis (MS) is a prototypical organ-specific autoimmune disease characterized by immune-mediated demyelination and neurodegeneration in the central nervous system (CNS). Among its clinical subtypes, primary progressive MS (PPMS) is marked by a steady worsening of neurological function from disease onset, without distinct relapses or remissions. In contrast, relapsing remitting MS (RRMS)—the most common form—features episodic inflammatory attacks (relapses) followed by periods of partial or complete recovery (remission). Over time, however, many RRMS patients transition to a secondary progressive phase with accumulating disability. Current diagnostic approaches for MS rely heavily on clinical evaluation, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) to detect CNS lesions, and cerebrospinal fluid analysis. While MRI is sensitive to structural changes such as new or enhancing lesions, it provides limited insight into the underlying immunopathological processes driving disease activity or treatment response.

A critical limitation of existing diagnostics is their inability to reliably detect subclinical immune activation that precedes overt relapse or to distinguish between different immunological endotypes of MS that may respond differently to available therapies. Moreover, long-term administration of immunosuppressive or immunomodulatory drugs—often initiated empirically—carries risks of serious adverse effects, including infections and malignancies, particularly when treatment is continued despite lack of efficacy. The absence of real-time, tissue-relevant biomarkers of immune dysregulation hinders the implementation of precision health approaches that could enable early intervention, therapy personalization, and dynamic adjustment based on individual disease trajectories. Consequently, there remains a profound unmet need for methods capable of monitoring the functional state of pathogenic immune cells within inflammatory microenvironments, ideally through minimally invasive means that permit longitudinal assessment in living subjects.

## SUMMARY

The present invention introduces a novel platform for the detection, diagnosis, prognosis, and monitoring of autoimmune diseases through the use of synthetic, implantable scaffolds that create engineered immunological niches (INs). These scaffolds, when implanted subcutaneously, recruit host immune and stromal cells to form a vascularized, inflammatory tissue that dynamically reflects systemic and local immune status. Critically, the molecular and cellular composition of this niche mirrors immunological events occurring in distant disease-affected organs—such as the CNS in multiple sclerosis—thereby serving as a surrogate biopsy site that is both accessible and representative of pathogenic processes.

In vivo, the implanted scaffold acts as a “molecular sensor” by capturing phenotypic and transcriptional changes in infiltrating immune cells, particularly innate immune populations like monocytes and dendritic cells, which play pivotal roles in autoimmune pathogenesis. Unlike circulating blood, which contains a heterogeneous mix of cells only partially relevant to tissue-specific inflammation, the IN enriches for disease-associated immune subsets and reveals alterations in gene expression that correlate with disease onset, progression, remission, and relapse. Biopsy of the IN enables comprehensive analysis of RNA, protein, or cellular markers, which are then processed through computational algorithms to generate a quantitative score indicative of autoimmune disease activity.

This scoring system is derived from multivariate gene expression signatures—such as a 21-gene panel identified in experimental autoimmune encephalomyelitis (EAE), a murine model of MS—that distinguish healthy from diseased states with high sensitivity and specificity. The signature is validated across disease models, including both adoptive transfer and active immunization paradigms, and demonstrates predictive power even in presymptomatic phases. By comparing measured expression levels to established control baselines, the invention enables not only diagnosis but also staging of disease (e.g., remission vs. relapse), prediction of therapeutic response, and real-time monitoring of treatment efficacy.

The invention further encompasses methods for determining appropriate therapeutic interventions based on the IN-derived score. For instance, a high score indicating active disease may prompt administration of aggressive immunosuppression (e.g., corticosteroids), while an intermediate score might warrant investigational tolerogenic therapies such as antigen-loaded nanoparticles. A low score consistent with remission could support a strategy of active surveillance rather than continuous drug exposure. Additionally, repeated measurements over time allow tracking of disease dynamics: increasing scores signal progression or impending relapse, stable scores suggest quiescent disease, and decreasing scores indicate therapeutic response or regression.

Thus, the invention provides a closed-loop system for precision management of autoimmune diseases: implant scaffold → biopsy IN → measure gene/protein expression → compute disease activity score → compare to controls → diagnose/stage disease → select/optimize treatment → re-biopsy to monitor response. This approach transforms autoimmune disease management from reactive to proactive, enabling preemptive intervention before irreversible damage occurs and facilitating personalized, data-driven therapeutic decisions.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

An autoimmune disease, as used herein, refers to any condition arising from an aberrant immune response against self-antigens, leading to inflammation and tissue injury. Non-limiting examples include multiple sclerosis, type 1 diabetes, rheumatoid arthritis, systemic lupus erythematosus, psoriasis, inflammatory bowel disease, myasthenia gravis, and Sjögren’s syndrome. Many of these diseases exhibit distinct stages or subtypes—for example, relapsing remitting versus primary progressive MS, or pre-diabetic versus overt diabetic states in type 1 diabetes—each requiring tailored diagnostic and therapeutic approaches.

The invention provides a method for detecting or diagnosing an autoimmune disease in a subject by measuring the expression level of one or more genes, RNAs, or proteins in a biological sample obtained from an engineered immunological niche. The niche is formed by subcutaneous implantation of a biocompatible synthetic scaffold, which recruits host immune cells—including CD45+ leukocytes such as T cells, B cells, monocytes, dendritic cells, and neutrophils—as well as stromal cells like fibroblasts and endothelial cells that support vascularization and tissue organization. The sample may comprise the entire scaffold with associated cellular infiltrate or isolated cell populations thereof.

Expression levels are measured using standard techniques such as quantitative reverse transcription PCR (RT-qPCR), RNA sequencing, microarrays, or immunoassays, and are compared to control levels derived from healthy subjects or historical baselines. A statistically significant deviation—either increased or decreased expression of specific signature genes—indicates the presence, type, or stage of autoimmune disease. Notably, the method can diagnose disease in symptomatic individuals, identify pre-symptomatic or prodromal states, and even detect genetic or immunological predisposition prior to clinical manifestation.

The invention further includes methods for monitoring autoimmune disease progression or regression by obtaining samples from the same subject at two or more time points, measuring expression levels, and comparing changes over time. An increase in disease-associated gene expression suggests progression or relapse, while a decrease indicates regression or remission. This enables tracking of disease flares, therapeutic responses, and long-term trajectories. Similarly, the stage of disease—e.g., remission, relapse, chronic active—can be identified based on the magnitude and pattern of the expression signature.

Treatment efficacy is assessed by measuring expression before and after therapeutic intervention; normalization of the signature toward control levels signifies effectiveness, whereas persistent or worsening dysregulation indicates treatment failure. Based on these findings, clinicians can adjust therapy—escalating, de-escalating, switching agents, or withholding treatment—thereby practicing adaptive, precision medicine.

Specific treatment methods are guided by the IN-derived score: subjects with high scores receive plasmapheresis or high-dose corticosteroids; those with intermediate scores are treated with disease-modifying agents such as interferon-beta, glatiramer acetate, or tolerogenic nanoparticles; and those with low scores undergo monitoring without immediate pharmacotherapy. Prophylactic treatment is also enabled in high-risk individuals showing early molecular signs of dysregulation.

Gene expression data are processed through algorithms—such as singular value decomposition (SVD) or machine learning models like bagged decision trees—to compress multivariate inputs into a single metric or score. This score, often combined with clinical variables, yields a graphical or numerical output that facilitates interpretation. Housekeeping genes (e.g., HMBS, POLR2A, UBC) are used for normalization to ensure accuracy.

Scaffolds are fabricated from biocompatible polymers such as poly(ε-caprolactone) (PCL) or poly(ethylene glycol) (PEG), engineered with controlled porosity, degradation rates, and surface chemistry to optimize cell recruitment. They may be functionalized with antigens, cytokines, or other agents to modulate immune responses or enhance disease relevance. Implantation sites include subcutaneous, intraperitoneal, or other accessible locations.

The system may be implemented via computer-readable media executing instructions to receive expression data, process it into scores, compare to controls, and generate reports. Control populations are defined using large cohorts of healthy and diseased subjects, with elliptical confidence regions in multivariate space delineating normal versus pathological states.

In sum, the invention provides a comprehensive, dynamic, and minimally invasive platform for the precision management of autoimmune diseases across their entire clinical spectrum—from risk prediction to diagnosis, staging, treatment selection, and longitudinal monitoring.
