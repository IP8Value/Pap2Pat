# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD

The present invention relates generally to the field of quantum computing, and more specifically to photonic architectures for scalable, fault-tolerant quantum computation. The invention provides a novel system and method for performing universal quantum computation using light as the physical medium for encoding and processing quantum information. In particular, the invention leverages a hybrid resource state comprising both discrete-variable qubits encoded in bosonic modes—specifically Gottesman-Kitaev-Preskill (GKP) states—and continuous-variable Gaussian states, namely momentum-squeezed vacuum states. This hybrid approach enables room-temperature operation, modular design, and compatibility with existing integrated photonic fabrication technologies, while overcoming the limitations imposed by the probabilistic nature of non-Gaussian state generation in optical systems. The invention further encompasses specialized decoding procedures tailored to the unique error structure of the hybrid resource state, thereby enabling robust quantum error correction and fault-tolerant logical operations.

## BACKGROUND

Quantum computing holds the promise of solving certain computational problems exponentially faster than classical computers, with applications ranging from cryptography and materials science to drug discovery and optimization. Among the various physical platforms being explored for quantum computation—including superconducting circuits, trapped ions, and neutral atoms—photonic systems offer distinct advantages that make them particularly compelling for scalable, fault-tolerant architectures. Photons are inherently stable at room temperature, exhibit low decoherence, and can be manipulated with high precision using well-established linear optical components such to beam splitters, phase shifters, and squeezers. Moreover, photonic qubits are naturally suited for long-distance quantum communication, enabling modular quantum computing architectures where multiple processing units are interconnected via optical fibers without the need for transduction between different physical modalities.

Despite these advantages, photonic quantum computing has historically faced significant challenges in achieving universality and fault tolerance. A key obstacle is the difficulty of generating non-Gaussian states of light, which are essential for implementing non-Clifford gates and enabling universal quantum computation. While Gaussian operations—such as squeezing, displacement, and linear interferometry—can be performed deterministically and with high fidelity using integrated photonics, they alone are insufficient for universal quantum computation due to the Gottesman-Knill theorem, which states that any quantum circuit composed solely of Clifford gates can be efficiently simulated classically. Non-Gaussian resources, such as GKP qubits or cubic-phase states, must therefore be introduced to achieve computational universality.

Current approaches to photonic quantum computing fall into two broad categories, each with its own set of limitations. The first category relies on continuous-variable (CV) cluster states—large-scale entangled Gaussian states that can be generated deterministically—as the computational resource. In this paradigm, quantum information is encoded into discrete-variable qubits (e.g., GKP states) that are injected into the CV cluster state. While this approach benefits from the scalability of CV cluster state generation, it demands that high-quality GKP qubits be available on-demand and deterministically coupled to the cluster, a requirement that is incompatible with the probabilistic nature of current GKP state generation methods based on post-selected Gaussian boson sampling (GBS). The second category involves directly generating entangled resource states composed entirely of bosonic qubits (e.g., GKP or cat states). Although such states offer intrinsic resilience to noise, their generation is highly challenging due to the probabilistic nature of both the qubit preparation and the entangling operations required to build large cluster states.

These limitations have hindered the development of practical, scalable photonic quantum computers. There is thus a pressing need for an architecture that reconciles the scalability of CV-based approaches with the error-resilience of bosonic qubit encodings, while accommodating the probabilistic character of non-Gaussian state sources. The present invention addresses this need by introducing a hybrid resource state that seamlessly integrates GKP qubits and squeezed vacuum states, along with a corresponding decoding framework that accounts for the distinct noise characteristics of each component. This approach not only reduces the hardware overhead associated with GKP state multiplexing but also maintains the local, planar structure necessary for fault tolerance and scalable fabrication.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides a scalable, fault-tolerant photonic quantum computing system based on a hybrid resource state that combines Gottesman-Kitaev-Preskill (GKP) qubits and momentum-squeezed vacuum states within a three-dimensional Raussendorf-Harrington-Goyal (RHG) lattice structure. The system comprises four primary modules: a state preparation module that generates approximate GKP qubits using multiplexed Gaussian boson sampling (GBS) devices; a multiplexing module that boosts the effective success probability of GKP state generation and substitutes failed attempts with guaranteed momentum-squeezed states; a computational module that entangles these single-mode states into a (2+1)-dimensional hybrid cluster state using deterministic continuous-variable controlled-Z (CZ) gates implemented via optical delay lines and interferometers; and a photonic quantum processing unit (QPU) that performs adaptive homodyne measurements to execute measurement-based quantum computation.

A key innovation of the invention is the “swap-out” mechanism, wherein any mode that fails to produce a GKP qubit is instead prepared in a momentum-squeezed vacuum state. Unlike erasure-based models that remove defective nodes from the lattice, this substitution preserves the entanglement topology and allows computation to proceed with reduced—but still manageable—noise. The invention further includes a two-tier decoding architecture: an inner decoder that processes continuous homodyne measurement outcomes to infer discrete qubit readouts while accounting for correlations induced by neighboring squeezed states, and an outer decoder that performs topological error correction on the RHG lattice using syndrome data derived from the inner decoder. This combined approach enables fault-tolerant quantum computation even when a significant fraction of nodes (up to approximately 23.6% in ideal conditions) are occupied by squeezed states rather than GKP qubits.

The system is designed for full integration on a planar photonic chip, operating at room temperature with minimal cryogenic requirements, and leverages the high speed and efficiency of homodyne detection to set the computational clock rate. By decoupling the demanding requirements of non-Gaussian state generation from the deterministic entangling and measurement operations, the invention achieves a modular architecture that is compatible with existing silicon photonics manufacturing processes and scalable to millions of qubits.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

### INTRODUCTION

Photonic quantum computing offers a promising pathway toward scalable, fault-tolerant universal quantum computation due to its inherent advantages of room-temperature operation, low decoherence, and seamless integration with optical communication networks. Among photonic approaches, those leveraging bosonic encodings—particularly the Gottesman-Kitaev-Preskill (GKP) code—stand out for their ability to encode discrete-variable qubits into the infinite-dimensional Hilbert space of optical modes while retaining compatibility with Gaussian operations. GKP qubits enable deterministic implementation of Clifford gates and Pauli measurements using only linear optics, homodyne detection, and squeezing, making them exceptionally well-suited for integrated photonic platforms. However, the generation of high-fidelity GKP states remains a significant experimental challenge, as it typically relies on probabilistic post-selection schemes such as Gaussian boson sampling (GBS), which suffer from low success probabilities and require extensive multiplexing to approach deterministic operation.

To address this bottleneck, the present invention introduces a hybrid photonic quantum computing architecture that strategically combines the strengths of continuous-variable (CV) and discrete-variable (DV) paradigms. Specifically, the architecture utilizes a hybrid resource state in which each node of a three-dimensional cluster lattice is occupied either by a GKP qubit or by a momentum-squeezed vacuum state, with the latter serving as a fallback when GKP state generation fails. This design overcomes the strict on-demand requirement of prior CV-based architectures while avoiding the prohibitive multiplexing overhead of all-GKP cluster state generation.

Photonic quantum computing architectures can broadly be classified into two main categories based on their resource state composition. The first class employs CV cluster states—deterministically generated, large-scale entangled Gaussian states—as the backbone for computation, into which non-Gaussian GKP qubits are injected to provide the necessary non-Clifford resources and error correction capabilities. While this approach benefits from the scalability of CV entanglement generation, it critically depends on the availability of high-quality GKP qubits at regular intervals, a condition that is difficult to satisfy given the probabilistic nature of current GKP state sources. The second class aims to construct cluster states entirely from pre-prepared bosonic qubits (e.g., GKP or cat states). Although such states offer enhanced noise resilience, their generation and entanglement are both probabilistic, leading to formidable multiplexing and fusion gate requirements that scale unfavorably with system size.

The present invention transcends these dichotomies by introducing a hybrid scheme that leverages the deterministic entanglement capabilities of CV systems while relaxing the stringent demands on GKP state availability. In this hybrid model, the computational resource is a lattice where GKP qubits and squeezed states coexist, with the latter acting as functional—but noisier—substitutes for missing GKP qubits. This approach preserves the entanglement structure of the cluster state even in the presence of GKP generation failures, thereby maintaining computational connectivity and enabling robust error correction through a tailored decoding strategy.

For scalability, the invention embodies several desirable features: a fully on-chip implementation using planar photonic circuits, a modular system configuration that separates state preparation, multiplexing, and computation into specialized components, and a theoretical foundation based on a planar system layout and a hybrid resource state compatible with topological error correction. The overall system configuration is designed to operate in (2+1) dimensions—two spatial and one temporal—ensuring that optical path lengths and loss remain constant regardless of system size, a critical requirement for indefinite scalability.

### Overview of the System Configuration

The photonic quantum computing system of the present invention comprises three principal modules working in concert to generate and utilize a hybrid resource state for fault-tolerant computation. These modules are: a state preparation module, a multiplexing module, and a main computational module, with an additional photonic quantum processing unit (QPU) responsible for measurement and feed-forward control.

The state preparation module is tasked with generating high-fidelity bosonic qubits, specifically GKP states, using arrays of Gaussian boson sampling (GBS) devices. Each GBS device operates by interfering displaced and squeezed vacuum states through a linear optical network and performing photon-number-resolving (PNR) detection on all but one output mode. Upon observing a specific photon detection pattern, the remaining mode is projected into an approximate GKP state. Due to the probabilistic nature of this process, multiple GBS devices are operated in parallel within the state preparation module to increase the likelihood of successful state generation.

The multiplexing module receives the outputs from the state preparation module and implements active switching to route successfully generated GKP states to designated output channels. Crucially, if none of the parallel GKP sources succeed in a given time bin, the multiplexing module substitutes the output with a momentum-squeezed vacuum state—a deterministic and easily producible Gaussian state. This “swap-out” functionality ensures that every temporal mode entering the computational module is occupied by either a GKP qubit or a squeezed state, thereby preserving the regular lattice structure required for cluster state computation.

The main computational module stitches together these single-mode states into a higher-dimensional hybrid cluster state. This is achieved through a combination of time-domain and space-domain entangling operations. A representative system, denoted as system 100, illustrates this process: a state factory generates GKP states (or substitutes squeezed states upon failure); a time stitch module implements optical delay lines to chain temporally adjacent qubits into one-dimensional cluster states via repeated application of CV CZ gates; a space stitch module multiplexes these temporal chains across multiple spatial waveguides and applies additional CZ gates between neighboring spatial modes to build a two-dimensional sheet; and finally, the photonic QPU entangles multiple such sheets into a three-dimensional RHG lattice through further layers of CZ interactions.

In this configuration, the time stitch employs tunable delay loops to synchronize optical pulses and implement temporal entanglement, while the space stitch uses integrated waveguide arrays and reconfigurable couplers to manage spatial multiplexing and inter-waveguide entanglement. The resulting hybrid resource state retains the topological properties of the RHG lattice, enabling fault-tolerant quantum error correction despite the heterogeneous composition of its nodes.

### Generation of Bosonic Qubits Using Multiplexed Gaussian Boson Sampling (“GBS”) Devices

The generation of non-Gaussian states of light, such as GKP qubits, is a foundational requirement for universal photonic quantum computation. The present invention employs Gaussian boson sampling (GBS) devices as the primary means of producing these states. A GBS device consists of an interferometer that mixes N input modes—each prepared in a displaced and squeezed vacuum state—followed by photon-number-resolving (PNR) detectors on N−1 of the output modes. When a specific pattern of photon detections is observed, the remaining undetected mode is heralded to be in a desired non-Gaussian state, such as a finite-energy approximation of a GKP qubit.

Although the success probability of generating a high-fidelity GKP state from a single GBS device is typically low (e.g., on the order of 1% or less for experimentally feasible parameters), this limitation can be mitigated through spatial and temporal multiplexing. In spatial multiplexing, M identical GBS devices are operated in parallel, and their outputs are routed through a binary tree of fast optical switches to a common output channel. Upon receiving a heralding signal from any one of the devices, the corresponding switch network directs the successful state to the output while discarding the others. The probability of obtaining at least one successful state scales as 1 − (1 − p_GBS)^M, where p_GBS is the success probability of a single device. Thus, by increasing M, the effective success probability can be made arbitrarily close to unity.

An exemplary multiplexed state generation setup includes an array of GBS sources, each comprising three-mode interferometers fed by momentum-squeezed vacuum states with 10–15 dB of squeezing, and PNR detectors capable of resolving up to seven photons. Such a configuration can produce |0⟩_GKP states with fidelities exceeding 90% at success probabilities of a few percent per device. When multiplexed across hundreds of parallel channels, the system achieves near-deterministic GKP state delivery at rates compatible with the computational clock cycle dictated by homodyne detection speeds (potentially in the GHz range).

Critically, the multiplexing module includes a fallback mechanism: if no GBS device succeeds in a given time bin, a pre-prepared momentum-squeezed vacuum state is swapped into the output channel. This ensures that the downstream computational module always receives a valid optical mode, albeit one that lacks the full error-correcting capabilities of a GKP qubit. This hybrid approach dramatically reduces the number of GBS devices required compared to architectures that demand perfect GKP coverage, thereby lowering hardware overhead and improving practical feasibility.

### Time Domain Generation of 1D Clusters

The generation of one-dimensional (1D) cluster states in the time domain is a key step in constructing the full (2+1)D hybrid resource state. This process leverages optical delay lines and deterministic CV entangling gates to sequentially entangle a stream of single-mode states—comprising both GKP qubits and momentum-squeezed states—into a linear cluster.

The setup for 1D cluster state generation includes an input port that receives the multiplexed single-mode states, a fast optical switch (e.g., a Mach-Zehnder interferometer with variable phase shifters), a tunable optical delay line, and a CV controlled-Z (CZ) gate implemented via a pair of inline squeezers and a 50:50 beam splitter. The operation proceeds as follows: the first incoming mode is switched into the delay line, where it circulates for one clock period. Upon its return, it interferes with the next incoming mode at the CZ gate, thereby entangling the two. The combined state is then partially switched back into the delay line, and the process repeats for each subsequent mode. After N modes have been processed, the final state is switched out of the loop and directed to the next stage of the system.

The CV CZ gate, defined as exp(i q̂₁ q̂₂), is implemented using a standard optical circuit consisting of two single-mode squeezers (with opposite squeezing parameters) sandwiching a 50:50 beam splitter. This gate is native to both GKP qubits and squeezed states, allowing seamless entanglement regardless of the specific state occupying each mode. The resulting 1D cluster state exhibits the canonical form |ψ⟩ ∝ ∫ dq exp(−½ q^T V q) |q⟩, where V is the adjacency matrix of the linear graph and the quadrature variances reflect the finite squeezing levels of the constituent states.

An equivalent spatial representation of this temporal cluster can be constructed by mapping each time bin to a separate spatial waveguide, with the delay line replaced by a series of fixed-length waveguide segments. However, the time-domain approach is preferred for scalability, as it minimizes the physical footprint and avoids the need for large numbers of parallel waveguides. A complete example device for generating 1D clusters includes integrated silicon photonic components such as thermo-optic phase shifters for switch control, low-loss spiral waveguides for delay lines, and on-chip squeezers based on four-wave mixing in microring resonators.

### GKP Cluster in 2+1 Dimensions

To enable fault-tolerant quantum computation, the one-dimensional temporal cluster states must be extended into a higher-dimensional lattice structure. The present invention achieves this by stitching together multiple 1D hybrid temporal cluster states in two spatial dimensions to form a (2+1)D cluster, where the third dimension corresponds to time.

This is accomplished using a two-dimensional array of 1D cluster generators, each producing a temporal chain of entangled modes. Between adjacent generators in the array, additional CZ gates are applied at regular intervals to entangle spatially neighboring modes. The timing of these spatial entangling operations is synchronized with the temporal clock cycle to ensure proper lattice formation. Specifically, the array is divided into two interleaved sublattices (e.g., colored green and yellow), with state preparation and CZ gate application alternating between even and odd clock cycles. This staggered approach prevents timing conflicts and ensures that each mode is entangled with its correct neighbors in both space and time.

An exemplary 2D chip layout for a (2+1)D cluster generator includes a grid of waveguide channels, each containing a time-stitch module (delay line + CZ gate) for temporal entanglement. Perpendicular to these channels, a network of reconfigurable couplers implements the spatial CZ gates. The entire structure is fabricated on a single silicon photonic chip using standard lithographic techniques, with phase shifters and modulators providing dynamic control over coupling strengths. By stacking multiple such 2D layers and applying inter-layer CZ gates, a full three-dimensional cubic lattice can be generated, suitable for implementing topological codes such as the surface code or the RHG lattice.

This (2+1)D architecture ensures that the optical depth traversed by any mode remains constant regardless of the total number of qubits, thereby avoiding the exponential loss scaling that plagues purely spatial or purely temporal approaches. Moreover, the planar nature of the layout—requiring only nearest-neighbor couplings and no waveguide crossings—facilitates scalable fabrication and preserves the locality of noise, a critical requirement for effective quantum error correction.

### Generation of a Hybrid Raussendorf-Harrington-Goyal (“RHG”) Lattice

The Raussendorf-Harrington-Goyal (RHG) lattice is a three-dimensional cluster state structure that serves as a universal resource for fault-tolerant measurement-based quantum computation. It is constructed by foliating layers of 2D surface code lattices and connecting them through additional entangling operations, resulting in a body-centered cubic geometry with primal and dual qubits arranged in alternating layers.

In the present invention, the RHG lattice is generated in a hybrid form, where each node is occupied either by a GKP qubit or by a momentum-squeezed vacuum state. The generation process begins with the creation of 1D temporal cluster chains as described previously. These chains are then arranged in a 2D spatial array corresponding to a single layer of the RHG lattice. Additional state preparation modules inject ancillary states at specific locations to form the primal and dual sublattices. Spatial CZ gates are applied between neighboring nodes according to the RHG connectivity pattern, which includes both intra-layer and inter-layer links.

Example representations of two consecutive layers of the RHG lattice show primal qubits (red dots) forming a square lattice, with dual qubits (blue dots) positioned at the centers of the primal plaquettes. The combined representation reveals the full 3D connectivity, where each primal qubit is connected to four dual qubits in the layers above and below, and vice versa. The chip layout for generating this structure includes a repeating unit cell comprising waveguides for primal and dual modes, delay lines for temporal synchronization, and integrated couplers for implementing the required CZ interactions.

Operation of the chip proceeds in a pipelined fashion: as new modes are injected into the temporal chains, previously entangled modes are advanced through the spatial network and eventually directed to the photonic QPU for measurement. The hybrid nature of the lattice—allowing squeezed states to occupy nodes where GKP generation fails—does not disrupt the topological structure, as both state types respond identically to the CZ entangling operations. This robustness enables the RHG lattice to serve as a fault-tolerant resource even under imperfect state preparation conditions.

### Passive Version of System Configuration

The present invention further encompasses a passive variant of the system configuration that eliminates the need for inline squeezing during the computational phase, thereby simplifying the hardware and reducing noise sources. This passive architecture is based on a hybrid continuous-variable/discrete-variable (CV-DV) framework that leverages pre-prepared resource states and passive linear optics to generate fault-tolerant cluster states.

Traditional photonic architectures often require active squeezing operations during computation, which introduce additional loss and noise due to the nonlinear processes involved. The passive version of the present invention circumvents this limitation by preparing all necessary non-Gaussian resources—namely GKP states and magic states—offline in the state preparation module, and then using only passive components (beam splitters and phase shifters) for entanglement and measurement.

The generation circuit for fault-tolerant resource states in this passive architecture exploits the symmetry of Gaussian operations and the structure of the GKP code. Specifically, the circuit is designed such that all squeezing is confined to the initial state preparation stage, and subsequent entangling operations are implemented via passive interferometers. This is made possible by the fact that the CV CZ gate can be decomposed into a sequence of passive transformations when acting on appropriately prepared input states.

The logical error rates of the passive system are analyzed under realistic noise models incorporating finite squeezing and photon loss. GKP encoding for optical bosonic modes is defined using finite-energy approximations, where each ideal Dirac comb is replaced by a Gaussian envelope. Single-mode states within the GKP code space are modeled under the influence of an additive Gaussian bosonic channel, which captures the effects of finite squeezing as random displacements in phase space.

Beam splitter and phase shifter operations are described by their standard symplectic transformations, while homodyne detectors are used to perform GKP Pauli measurements by projecting onto quadrature eigenstates. The CV CZ and CX gates for GKP qubits are implemented using passive networks that avoid inline squeezing, relying instead on the pre-squeezed nature of the input states. This passive approach not only reduces hardware complexity but also improves error performance by minimizing the introduction of additional noise during computation.

### 3D Hybrid Macronode Architecture

The invention further discloses a three-dimensional hybrid macronode architecture that enhances scalability and error resilience through built-in redundancy and optimized entanglement structures. In this architecture, the fundamental unit of the cluster state is not a single optical mode but a “macronode” comprising multiple entangled modes, typically four, arranged in a compact 3D configuration.

The primal unit cell of the 3D hybrid pair cluster state consists of two entangled macronodes, each containing a pair of GKP states and a pair of momentum-squeezed states. The entanglement within and between macronodes is engineered to provide inherent protection against local errors and to facilitate efficient decoding. Specifically, the two-mode entangled state used as the building block can be derived either from GKP states or from momentum-squeezed vacuum states, ensuring compatibility with the hybrid resource model.

Identities for two-mode cluster states are employed to simplify the description of the macronode structure, revealing that the overall state can be represented as a graph where edges correspond to CZ interactions. Magic states are inserted into the architecture by replacing selected GKP states with distilled non-Clifford resources, enabling universal computation. The entangled pairs are arranged in a 3D lattice using a 2D array of sources, with beamsplitters, delay lines, and phase delays applied to create the desired connectivity.

A fully connected 3D resource state is obtained by applying homodyne detection to ancillary modes, effectively projecting the system into the canonical hybrid cluster state. Circuit identities are used to migrate CZ gates and commute CX gates with squeezers, allowing the replacement of active components with passive equivalents. This economizes the description of the post-measurement state and reveals the underlying redundancy that enhances fault tolerance.

The noise model for the macronode architecture incorporates both photon loss and finite squeezing, treated as a single-mode Gaussian bosonic channel. Homodyne outcomes are rescaled to account for loss, and threshold calculations are performed using Monte Carlo simulations to estimate conditional qubit-level error probabilities. The architecture demonstrates improved swap-out tolerance compared to conventional designs, with the macronode structure providing natural error suppression through its multi-mode encoding.

The passive system configuration is generalized to accommodate arbitrary graph states by constructing interferometers from unitary matrices derived via the Reck decomposition. Beamsplitter networks are assembled using Fourier transform modules and phase shifters, enabling the realization of complex entanglement patterns without active squeezing. This generalization ensures that the passive architecture can support a wide range of quantum error-correcting codes and computational protocols.

### Method 1: Procedure for Performing Fault-Tolerant Quantum Computation

Fault-tolerant quantum computation in the present invention is performed through a structured procedure that integrates logical circuit compilation, state initialization, circuit implementation, and measurement outcome processing. First, the user’s quantum algorithm is compiled into a sequence of logical operations compatible with the surface code or RHG lattice. All Clifford gates are commuted to the end of the circuit and absorbed into the measurement bases, leaving only non-Clifford operations (e.g., T-gates) to be implemented via magic state injection.

Logical qubit states are initialized by measuring ancillary qubits in appropriate quadratures: |+⟩ states are prepared by measuring ancillas in the p-quadrature, while |0⟩ states result from q-quadrature measurements. The initialized patch is then subjected to rounds of error correction to ensure fault tolerance. Logical circuit implementation proceeds via lattice surgery techniques, where patches are merged and split to perform multi-qubit gates and measurements. Adaptive homodyne settings are updated in real-time based on previous measurement outcomes to maintain the correct logical frame.

Finally, measurement outcomes from the photonic QPU are processed by the classical controller to extract logical results. This involves applying the recovery operations determined by the outer decoder and adjusting for any Pauli frame updates accumulated during the computation. The entire procedure is designed to operate within the fault-tolerant threshold, ensuring that logical error rates decrease exponentially with code distance.

### Method 2: Procedure for Performing Quantum Error Correction

Quantum error correction in the hybrid architecture follows a two-stage process. First, a decoder is run on the syndrome data obtained from homodyne measurements. This decoder comprises an inner stage that converts continuous measurement outcomes into discrete qubit readouts, and an outer stage that identifies and corrects logical errors using topological decoding algorithms such as minimum-weight perfect matching (MWPM).

The error correction procedure begins by identifying unsatisfied stabilizers from the binned qubit outcomes. A matching graph is constructed with vertices representing these defects and edge weights reflecting the probability of errors connecting them. The MWPM algorithm is then applied to find the most likely error chain, and a recovery operation is enacted by flipping the corresponding qubit outcomes. This recovery is tracked in software via Pauli frame updates rather than physically applied, minimizing latency and hardware overhead.

### Method 3: Example Inner Decoder

The inner decoder processes raw homodyne data to produce reliable qubit readouts in the presence of correlated noise from neighboring squeezed states. It begins by identifying noisy directions in the measurement vector using the known noise covariance matrix. A change-of-basis transformation is applied to diagonalize the noise, isolating high-variance modes that are less trustworthy.

Binning is then performed along the transformed axes, with special care taken to enforce consistency constraints derived from the lattice structure. For example, in a configuration where a squeezed state is surrounded by four GKP qubits, the decoder ensures that the inferred qubit parities respect the expected correlations. The change-of-basis is undone to return to the original mode basis, and a binary string is obtained by taking the integer part of the binned values modulo 2. This string represents the best estimate of the underlying qubit states, incorporating both analog measurement precision and topological constraints.

### Method 4: Example Outer Decoder

The outer decoder operates on the binary strings produced by the inner decoder to perform topological error correction. Syndrome identification involves computing the parity of qubit outcomes around each stabilizer generator of the RHG lattice. Unsatisfied stabilizers (those with odd parity) are flagged as defects.

A matching graph is constructed with one vertex per defect, and edges connecting all pairs of vertices. Edge weights are assigned based on heuristic error probabilities that account for the number of neighboring squeezed states and the homodyne measurement values. The minimum-weight perfect matching is found using Edmonds’ algorithm, and the corresponding recovery operation is determined by flipping qubits along the matched paths. This recovery corrects both bit-flip and phase-flip errors at the logical level, provided the physical error rate is below threshold.

### Method 5: Example Inner Decoder for One p-Squeezed State Surrounded by GKP States

A specialized inner decoder is employed for local configurations where a single momentum-squeezed state is surrounded by four GKP qubits, a common motif in the RHG lattice. The decoder takes as input a five-component vector of homodyne outcomes and applies a tailored change-of-basis matrix whose columns are eigenvectors of the expected noise covariance.

The first transformed component, corresponding to the squeezed mode, is binned independently since it is uncorrelated with the others. Among the remaining components, the one with the lowest noise variance is used to infer the global parity (even or odd) of the GKP qubits. This parity constraint is then applied to bin the other components consistently. Finally, a modular arithmetic rule is used to resolve ambiguities in the second component, ensuring that the reconstructed integer vector respects the lattice symmetries. The result is a five-bit string that accurately reflects the underlying qubit states despite the presence of strong correlated noise.

## Technological Advantages

### Modularity

The architecture of the present invention facilitates a highly modular design by decoupling the distinct hardware requirements of state preparation, multiplexing, and computation. The state preparation module, which demands ultra-low-loss circuits and cryogenic-compatible photon-number-resolving detectors, can be implemented on a dedicated chip optimized for these constraints. The multiplexing module, requiring fast but moderately lossy switches, can operate at room temperature using standard integrated photonics. The computational module, involving reconfigurable homodyne detection and delay lines, is also room-temperature compatible and benefits from mature silicon photonic fabrication. This modularity enables independent optimization of each component, simplifies system integration, and supports incremental scaling by adding more state preparation units as needed.

### Minimal Cryogenic Requirements

Unlike superconducting or trapped-ion platforms that require extensive cryogenic infrastructure, the present invention imposes minimal cryogenic demands. Only the state preparation module—specifically the PNR detectors—may need cooling, and even this requirement could be eliminated with future room-temperature PNR technology. The remainder of the system, including all entangling operations and measurements, operates at ambient conditions. This drastically reduces the cost, complexity, and power consumption of the quantum computer, while enabling the use of commercially available cryostats for the small cooled section rather than custom-built “jumbo” systems.

### Homodyne Detection Sets Timescales

The computational clock speed of the system is governed by the timescales of homodyne detection, which can operate at GHz rates—orders of magnitude faster than photon-number-resolving or threshold detectors used in other photonic schemes. This high speed translates directly into shorter optical delay lines for temporal entanglement, which in turn reduces propagation losses and improves overall fidelity. As illustrated in FIGS. 2A, 2C, and 4C, the delay lines required for time-domain cluster generation are on the order of millimeters to centimeters for GHz clock rates, enabling full integration on compact photonic chips. The rapid feedback enabled by fast homodyne detection also supports real-time adaptive measurements, a critical feature for fault-tolerant computation.
