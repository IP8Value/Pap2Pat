# DESCRIPTION

## INTRODUCTION

Accurate estimation of a speaker’s location within an enclosed environment, such as the interior of a vehicle, is critical for enabling intelligent audio systems that can selectively enhance, suppress, or route speech signals based on spatial origin. In modern automotive applications, where multiple occupants may be speaking simultaneously amidst background noise from engines, wind, and road conditions, conventional single-microphone approaches are insufficient to isolate and process individual speech streams. Multimicrophone arrays offer a promising solution by leveraging spatial diversity to distinguish between coexisting acoustic sources. However, the performance of such systems heavily depends on accurate knowledge of the spatial characteristics of each source, particularly in reverberant or noisy environments. Beamforming—a signal processing technique that combines signals from multiple microphones with appropriate weights to enhance signals from a target direction while suppressing others—has emerged as a cornerstone of spatial audio processing. Traditional beamformers, such as the minimum variance distortionless response (MVDR) beamformer, rely on steering vectors derived from time differences of arrival (TDOA) to localize and enhance desired sources. Yet, in practical scenarios involving multiple concurrent speakers and non-stationary interference, these methods often fail to adequately suppress competing talkers. The linearly constrained minimum variance (LCMV) beamformer addresses this limitation by incorporating additional constraints to explicitly nullify interfering sources, provided that accurate models of the acoustic propagation paths from each source to the microphone array are available. These models are most effectively captured through relative transfer functions (RTFs), which encode the complex frequency-domain relationships between microphones for a given source location. The use of RTFs instead of simplified TDOA-based steering vectors significantly improves robustness across varying reverberation levels and source positions. Consequently, the ability to estimate and utilize RTFs corresponding to known speaker positions forms the foundation of a high-performance, location-aware speech enhancement system. This invention leverages the fixed seating geometry of vehicles to enable a calibration-based approach wherein RTFs associated with each seat position are pre-measured and stored, allowing real-time identification of active speakers and dynamic adaptation of the beamformer to extract the desired speech signal with minimal distortion.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides a method for estimating the location of an active speaker within a confined acoustic environment, such as an automobile cabin, using a multimicrophone array and a library of pre-calibrated relative transfer functions (RTFs). The method begins by designating one microphone of the array as a reference microphone. During a calibration phase, RTFs are estimated and stored for each predefined seat position in the vehicle; each RTF characterizes the acoustic path from a speaker at that seat to every microphone in the array relative to the reference microphone. In operation, a voice sample is captured by the microphone array during a speech-active time frame. From this sample, a speaker-specific RTF is estimated using generalized eigenvalue decomposition applied to the signal correlation matrix and a noise covariance matrix. This estimated RTF is then projected onto each RTF in the pre-stored library by computing the cosine distance between the estimated RTF and each calibrated RTF. The seat position corresponding to the calibrated RTF with the smallest cosine distance—that is, the highest similarity—is identified as the location of the active speaker. The invention also encompasses a system for performing this location estimation, comprising a microphone array installed in the vehicle, a memory storing the RTF library for each seat, and a processor configured to execute the RTF estimation, projection, and location determination steps. The calibration process is performed once, typically during vehicle manufacturing or initial setup, and involves capturing sound samples from known positions to compute and store the RTFs. Additional features of the system include integration with a voice activity detector (VAD) to identify speech-active frames, adaptive noise covariance estimation updated only during noise-only segments, and a post-processing stage employing a neural network-based speech presence probability (SPP) map to further enhance the extracted speech signal. The system is particularly effective in low-reverberation environments and is designed to handle scenarios with multiple potential speakers, including cases of double-talk, by refraining from RTF updates when multiple sources are simultaneously active.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

Location estimation of active speakers in a vehicle is motivated by the need for context-aware audio systems that can support hands-free communication, voice command recognition, and personalized in-cabin experiences. In such environments, passengers occupy fixed seats, and their positions relative to the microphone array remain approximately constant over time. This spatial predictability enables a calibration-based approach to speaker localization. The system comprises a microphone array, typically consisting of multiple omnidirectional microphones arranged in a U-shape or other configuration within the vehicle cabin, and a digital signal processor connected to the array. The calibration process is initiated by designating one microphone as the reference microphone—often chosen based on its central location or superior signal quality. During calibration, a test sound (e.g., broadband speech or noise) is emitted from each seat position sequentially while the microphone array records the resulting signals. For each seat, the acoustic transfer functions (ATFs) from the source to each microphone are estimated in the short-time Fourier transform (STFT) domain. The RTF for a given seat is then computed as the ratio of the ATF from that seat to each non-reference microphone divided by the ATF to the reference microphone, yielding a complex-valued vector per frequency bin that captures the relative phase and magnitude response across the array. These RTFs are stored in a library indexed by seat position. In the operational phase, when a voice sample is captured by the array, the system first employs a voice activity detector (VAD)—preferably based on a neural network-derived speech presence probability (SPP) map—to determine whether any speech is present. If speech is detected, the system estimates a speaker RTF from the current speech-active frames. This is achieved by computing the signal correlation matrix over the active frames and applying generalized eigenvalue decomposition (GEVD) with the noise covariance matrix; the dominant eigenvector, normalized appropriately, yields the estimated RTF. The system then performs RTF projection by calculating the cosine distance between the estimated RTF and each RTF in the pre-stored library. The cosine distance, defined as one minus the cosine of the angle between the two complex vectors in the frequency domain, serves as a robust similarity measure under low reverberation. The seat position associated with the RTF exhibiting the minimal cosine distance is designated as the location of the active speaker. If the cosine distances are ambiguous or if the VAD indicates simultaneous activity from multiple speakers, the system may withhold RTF updates to avoid contamination. The determined location can then be used to configure a linearly constrained minimum variance (LCMV) beamformer to extract the desired speaker’s signal while suppressing interference from other seats, thereby enabling precise, location-aware speech enhancement in automotive environments.
