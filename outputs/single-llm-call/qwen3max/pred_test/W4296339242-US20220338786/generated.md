## BACKGROUND

Deep brain stimulation (DBS) is a well-established neuromodulation therapy that delivers controlled electrical pulses to specific regions of the brain through chronically implanted electrodes. It is clinically approved for the treatment of movement disorders such as Parkinson’s disease, essential tremor, and dystonia, and is under active investigation for psychiatric conditions including obsessive-compulsive disorder and treatment-resistant depression. DBS operates by modulating pathological neural activity patterns, thereby alleviating symptoms and improving quality of life for patients. A critical frontier in the evolution of DBS is the development of closed-loop or adaptive systems that can dynamically adjust stimulation parameters—such as frequency, amplitude, and pulse width—in real time based on feedback from concurrently recorded neural signals. Such adaptive systems promise enhanced therapeutic efficacy, reduced side effects, and improved battery longevity compared to conventional open-loop approaches.

Despite its clinical utility, a major technical limitation of current DBS systems is the contamination of intracranial electrophysiological recordings by high-amplitude stimulation artifacts. These artifacts, which arise directly from the delivery of electrical pulses, often saturate recording amplifiers and obscure underlying neural activity, particularly local field potentials (LFPs) and other biomarkers of interest. This obscuration impedes both offline scientific analysis and real-time biomarker detection, thereby hindering the development and deployment of responsive neuromodulation therapies. Consequently, effective artifact removal is not merely a signal processing convenience but a foundational requirement for next-generation DBS platforms capable of closed-loop operation.

The need for robust artifact removal is further intensified by practical constraints inherent to implantable medical devices. Many existing and emerging DBS systems operate under stringent power budgets, necessitating low sampling rates—typically between 200 Hz and 250 Hz—to conserve energy during wireless data transmission and storage. At these sampling rates, the stimulation frequency (commonly set between 130 Hz and 185 Hz) often exceeds the Nyquist limit, resulting in aliasing that folds the artifact into frequency bands overlapping with biologically relevant neural oscillations (e.g., beta and gamma bands). This spectral overlap renders conventional filtering techniques ineffective, as they risk removing or distorting genuine neural signals along with the artifact.

Moreover, real-world DBS recordings are frequently fragmented into multiple discontinuous segments due to packet loss during wireless telemetry, intermittent device reconfiguration, or deliberate data buffering strategies. These gaps introduce unknown phase shifts between contiguous data segments, complicating the assumption of continuous periodicity that underpins many existing artifact removal algorithms. Traditional methods such as template subtraction, notch filtering, or discrete Fourier transform (DFT)-based approaches either fail to account for these phase discontinuities or require precise knowledge of the stimulation period—a parameter that, while nominally set by the device, often deviates slightly in practice due to clock drift, hardware tolerances, or communication latencies. Even minor inaccuracies in the assumed stimulation frequency can lead to catastrophic reconstruction errors when using harmonic models, as the artifact’s phase accumulates over time, causing misalignment and incomplete cancellation.

These combined challenges—low sampling rates, aliased artifacts, unknown phase shifts from missing data, and insufficiently precise stimulation frequency estimates—create a complex signal environment that existing artifact removal techniques are ill-equipped to handle comprehensively. There remains a pressing need for a unified, computationally efficient, and robust method capable of accurately estimating and removing stimulation artifacts under these realistic and demanding conditions, thereby enabling reliable extraction of neural biomarkers both offline and in real-time embedded systems.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides novel systems and methods for the removal of electrical stimulation artifacts from intracranial electrophysiological recordings, particularly in the context of deep brain stimulation (DBS). Central to the invention is a period-based approach that accurately determines the true stimulation period—even in the presence of unknown phase shifts and at low sampling rates—and uses this period to reconstruct and subtract the artifact from the recorded waveform data.

The method begins with an initial guess for the artifact period, which may be derived from the nominal stimulation frequency programmed into the DBS device. However, recognizing that this nominal value is often insufficiently precise for effective artifact removal, the invention employs a data-driven optimization procedure to determine the true stimulation period relative to the sampling rate of the recording system. This true period is estimated by maximizing the coherent energy across multiple discontinuous segments of the recorded signal, effectively aligning them in phase despite unknown temporal gaps.

Once the true stimulation period is determined, the invention identifies the stimulation artifact within the waveform data using a Nadaraya-Watson kernel regression framework or, more preferably, a parametric harmonic regression model. This model represents the artifact as a sum of sinusoidal components at the fundamental frequency and its harmonics, with coefficients optimized via least squares. The identified artifact is then subtracted from the original waveform data to generate filtered waveform data that is substantially free of stimulation contamination, thereby revealing the underlying neural signal of interest.

In addition to artifact removal, the invention introduces a complementary system for stimulation period-based artifact removal that is particularly suited for integration into implantable or wearable neuromodulation devices. This system receives raw waveform data from an intracranial electroencephalography (iEEG) sensing device, determines the stimulation period relative to the sampling clock, identifies the artifact using the aforementioned harmonic model, and outputs clean neural data in real time.

A further aspect of the invention is a method for period-based estimation of electrical stimulation artifacts that simultaneously accounts for multiple unknown phase shifts introduced by data segmentation or packet loss. This method formulates a joint optimization problem over the stimulation frequency, phase offsets, and harmonic coefficients, solving it via Newton-type descent to achieve high-fidelity artifact reconstruction.

Additionally, the invention includes a packet loss estimation method termed Periodic Estimation of Lost Packets (PELP). PELP leverages the periodic nature of the stimulation artifact to infer the locations and durations of missing data segments by fitting a harmonic regression model to the longest available continuous run and extrapolating phase coherence to shorter runs. This enables accurate reconstruction of the full time series even when significant portions of data are lost during wireless transmission, a common issue in bidirectional DBS systems that both stimulate and stream neural data.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF EXAMPLE EMBODIMENTS

Artifact removal is a critical enabler of closed-loop DBS therapies, which rely on real-time detection of neural biomarkers to titrate stimulation parameters dynamically. Without effective artifact suppression, these biomarkers remain obscured, preventing adaptive control and limiting therapeutic precision. Existing artifact removal methods suffer from several key limitations: they often assume continuous data without phase disruptions, require exact knowledge of the stimulation frequency, perform poorly at low sampling rates where aliasing occurs, or introduce excessive latency incompatible with real-time operation. These shortcomings render them inadequate for use in next-generation implantable systems designed for concurrent sensing and stimulation.

To address these challenges, the present invention introduces the Period-Based Artifact Reconstruction and Removal Method (PARRM). PARRM is specifically engineered to remove high-frequency DBS artifacts even when the stimulation frequency exceeds the Nyquist limit of the recording system, a scenario common in power-constrained devices that sample at 200–250 Hz. By modeling the artifact as a periodic function with an unknown but estimable period, PARRM avoids the pitfalls of fixed-frequency filters and adapts to the actual stimulation characteristics observed in the data.

Concurrent sensing and stimulation are essential for closed-loop neuromodulation, yet they are technically challenging due to artifact contamination. PARRM enables this concurrency by providing a computationally tractable, online-capable algorithm that recovers neural signals with high fidelity. A particular challenge arises in low sampling rate recordings, where traditional frequency-domain methods fail due to spectral folding. PARRM overcomes this by operating in the time domain with a flexible harmonic model that does not rely on spectral bin alignment.

Complementing PARRM, the invention introduces the Periodic Estimation of Lost Packets (PELP) method. Bidirectional DBS devices that both deliver therapy and stream neural data often experience packet loss during wireless transmission, resulting in discontinuous recordings with unknown gap durations. PELP exploits the periodicity of the stimulation artifact to estimate these lost intervals. By dividing the time series into continuous runs, determining the stimulation period for each, and fitting a harmonic regression model to the longest run, PELP infers the optimal size of packet losses across all segments. Experimental results demonstrate PELP’s effectiveness in accurately reconstructing missing data, even under conditions of amplitude variability, frequency drift, and low signal-to-noise ratios.

Accurate packet loss estimation is vital not only for data integrity but also for downstream analyses such as biomarker discovery. PELP’s ability to restore temporal continuity enhances the reliability of spectral and time-domain features extracted from LFP data. Moreover, PELP is broadly applicable to other stimulating devices beyond DBS, including spinal cord stimulators (SCS) and vagus nerve stimulators (VNS), wherever periodic artifacts and data loss co-occur.

PELP offers significant advantages over existing interpolation or gap-filling methods, which typically ignore the structured nature of stimulation artifacts. By leveraging periodicity, PELP achieves higher accuracy with fewer assumptions. Its potential applications extend to ecological monitoring, where patients move freely and wireless dropouts are frequent, enabling robust biomarker tracking in real-world environments. Streaming intracranial electrophysiology data is crucial for chronic monitoring, but existing streaming methods often discard or corrupt data during transmission. PELP provides a principled solution to these limitations by reconstructing lost packets based on the deterministic structure of the stimulation artifact, thereby preserving data utility without increasing bandwidth or power consumption.

### Period-Based Artifact Reconstruction and Removal for Deep Brain Stimulation

Closed-loop electrical neuromodulation therapies represent a paradigm shift in treating neurological and psychiatric disorders, promising personalized, responsive intervention based on real-time neural feedback. However, the identification and validation of reliable biomarkers—neural signatures predictive of symptom states—remain hindered by the pervasive contamination of recordings with stimulation artifacts. Existing implantable DBS and spinal cord stimulation (SCS) devices lack integrated, robust artifact removal capabilities, limiting their utility in adaptive control schemes.

The present invention addresses this gap through the Period-Based Artifact Reconstruction and Removal Method (PARRM), a novel algorithm designed to recover obscured biomarkers by accurately modeling and subtracting stimulation artifacts. PARRM demonstrates superior performance compared to conventional filters such as notch filters, adaptive templates, or wavelet denoising, particularly in challenging scenarios involving aliasing, phase discontinuities, and imprecise frequency knowledge.

In deep brain stimulation applications, PARRM enables the recovery of neural signals that would otherwise be buried beneath high-amplitude artifacts. For instance, beta-band oscillations in the subthalamic nucleus—a key biomarker in Parkinson’s disease—are often masked during DBS but can be successfully extracted using PARRM. This capability extends to online biomarker detection, allowing real-time estimation of neural features for immediate feedback control. DBS may be applied at various frequencies—commonly 130 Hz, 150 Hz, or 185 Hz—depending on the clinical indication, and PARRM adapts seamlessly to each by estimating the true period from the data itself.

The artifact estimation process in PARRM begins with a data-driven determination of the stimulation period. Rather than relying on the device-reported frequency, which may contain small but consequential errors, PARRM solves an optimization problem to find the period that maximizes the coherence of the artifact across all available data segments. This period is then used to construct a harmonic regression model of the artifact, expressed as a sum of cosine and sine terms at the fundamental frequency and its harmonics. The model is implemented as a linear filter whose design parameters include the number of harmonics K and convergence tolerances. Choosing K involves a trade-off: too few harmonics underfit complex artifact waveforms (e.g., rectangular pulses), while too many risk overfitting neural signal components. However, empirical evidence suggests that moderate overestimation of K has minimal adverse impact.

Method 300 embodies the core steps of PARRM: deep brain stimulation is applied to a patient-specific brain region; waveform data is received from sensing electrodes; the stimulation period is determined relative to the sampling rate; the stimulation artifact is identified via harmonic regression; and the artifact is removed to yield filtered data. Complementarily, method 400 details the period estimation subroutine: a candidate period is selected; a waveform template is estimated; the deviation from this template is quantified across segments; and the final period estimate minimizes this deviation. This process iteratively refines the period until convergence.

Experimental comparisons illustrate PARRM’s effectiveness. In simulated data containing sinusoidal neural signals obscured by DBS artifacts, PARRM recovers the underlying signal with significantly lower relative root mean squared error (RRMSE) than conventional filters. Parameter sweeps confirm robustness across a range of harmonic counts and noise levels. Time-domain RRMSE metrics consistently favor PARRM, demonstrating its ability to preserve neural dynamics while eliminating artifact contamination.

### Comparison of PARRM to Conventional Filters

Comparative evaluation of PARRM against conventional artifact removal filters was conducted using simulated electrophysiological data contaminated with realistic DBS artifacts. The averaged time-voltage series revealed that PARRM achieved near-complete artifact cancellation without distorting the underlying neural waveform, whereas notch filters introduced ringing artifacts and adaptive template methods exhibited residual contamination due to phase misalignment. Windowed power spectral density plots further confirmed that PARRM effectively suppressed power at the stimulation frequency and its harmonics, reducing spectral leakage into adjacent bands.

Filter performance was quantitatively assessed using time-domain RRMSE, defined as the root mean square of the difference between the true neural signal and the recovered signal, normalized by the energy of the true signal. Across multiple simulation trials, PARRM consistently achieved RRMSE values below 5%, while conventional methods ranged from 15% to over 50%, depending on the degree of aliasing and phase disruption. A parameter sweep varying the number of harmonics K from 3 to 10 demonstrated that PARRM’s performance plateaued beyond K=5, indicating that moderate model complexity suffices for most clinical waveforms.

These results conclusively establish PARRM’s effectiveness in removing stimulation artifacts under realistic recording conditions. Its superiority stems from its joint estimation of frequency, phase, and harmonic content, enabling precise alignment and subtraction even when the artifact is aliased or fragmented. This makes PARRM uniquely suited for deployment in next-generation closed-loop DBS systems requiring high-fidelity neural sensing during active stimulation.

### Periodic Estimation of Lost Packets From Deep Brain Stimulation Waveform Data

Packet loss is a common occurrence in wireless intracranial recording systems, where data is transmitted in discrete packets that may be dropped due to interference, power constraints, or buffer overflow. This loss manifests as gaps of unknown duration in the time series, disrupting continuity and complicating artifact removal. The present invention introduces the Periodic Estimation of Lost Packets (PELP) method to address this challenge.

PELP defines a systematic approach to infer the locations and sizes of missing data segments by exploiting the periodic nature of the stimulation artifact. Method 900 outlines the PELP procedure: waveform data is received in real time; packet loss locations and sizes are initially unknown; the time series is divided into continuous runs separated by gaps; the stimulation period is determined for each run; a harmonic regression model is fitted to the longest run to establish a phase reference; the optimal size of each packet loss is determined by aligning shorter runs to this reference; the model is applied to all runs; and run-specific loss estimates are aggregated to reconstruct the full timeline.

Experimental testing of PELP involved recording neural data from a human participant undergoing DBS for obsessive-compulsive disorder. Stimulation was simulated in post-hoc analysis to introduce controlled packet losses. Monte Carlo simulations modeled inaccuracies in period estimation, amplitude variability, and frequency drift to assess robustness. Results demonstrated that PELP accurately estimated loss durations within milliseconds, even under adverse conditions. Sinograms of stimulation model fitting visually confirmed phase coherence across reconstructed segments.

### Experimental Testing of the Period-Based Estimation of the Loss of Packets (PELP)

Experimental validation of PELP utilized local field potential (LFP) data recorded from a 37-year-old female participant with treatment-refractory obsessive-compulsive disorder, implanted with a Medtronic Summit RC+S device targeting the ventral capsule/ventral striatum (VC/VS). Neural data was acquired at 1000 Hz with bipolar sensing contacts adjacent to the stimulation electrode. Stimulation parameters were set to 150.6 Hz, 4.5 mA, 90 μs pulse width.

To simulate real-world telemetry limitations, the continuous recording was artificially segmented into ten blocks of 250 samples each, with random-length gaps inserted between them. PELP was then applied to estimate the duration of each gap. Monte Carlo simulations varied three key parameters: amplitude ratio (artifact-to-signal strength), amplitude variability (temporal fluctuations in artifact magnitude), and drift (slow changes in stimulation frequency). Three experimental sets were conducted, each isolating one parameter while holding others constant.

Accuracy of loss estimation was quantified by comparing PELP’s inferred gap durations against ground truth. Graphical results included sinograms showing phase-aligned artifact reconstructions, histograms of estimation errors across 1000 Monte Carlo trials, and heat maps plotting accuracy against uncertainty in period estimation. Findings indicated that PELP maintained high accuracy (>95%) when the amplitude ratio exceeded 1.2, degraded gracefully with increasing amplitude variability, and remained robust to drift rates up to 0.1 Hz/s.

Real LFP data with simulated packet losses was processed using PELP, yielding reconstructed time series that preserved neural oscillatory structure. The method’s applicability extends beyond DBS to any periodic stimulation paradigm, including SCS and cortical stimulation. Limitations include reliance on sufficient artifact energy and a minimum run length for harmonic fitting; potential extensions involve integrating probabilistic models or deep learning priors to handle extreme data sparsity.

### Period-Based Estimation of Electrical Stimulation Artifacts in the Presence of Phase Shifts

The presence of phase shifts—introduced by data gaps, device reprogramming, or asynchronous sampling—poses a fundamental challenge to artifact estimation, as it breaks the assumption of global periodicity. The present invention overcomes this by introducing systems and methods that simultaneously estimate multiple phase shifts alongside the stimulation artifact and its period.

Method 1600 formalizes this approach: waveform data from deep neural stimulation is received and characterized by multiple runs; a model incorporating periodic artifacts and run-specific phase shifts is constructed; initial estimates for artifact and phases are generated; a loss function quantifying reconstruction error is defined; and optimization proceeds via harmonic regression to jointly refine all parameters. The loss function is minimized with respect to harmonic coefficients for fixed frequency and phases, then optimized over frequency and phases using Newton’s descent.

This method contrasts with PELP, which assumes a known period and estimates only phase shifts (gap durations). Method 1600 is more general, estimating period, phases, and artifact shape concurrently. Experimental illustrations show that relative error in frequency estimation drops below 10⁻⁶% with 10⁵ samples, far surpassing DFT-based methods that suffer from grid mismatch. The invention thus enables high-precision artifact removal even when no prior knowledge of stimulation parameters is available.
