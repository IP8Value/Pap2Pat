# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND OF THE DISCLOSURE

Current imaging techniques employed during percutaneous cardiac interventions, particularly transcatheter procedures, suffer from significant limitations that hinder procedural accuracy, safety, and efficiency. Fluoroscopy remains the dominant intraoperative imaging modality due to its real-time capabilities and ability to visualize radiopaque markers on catheters and other interventional devices. However, fluoroscopy provides only a two-dimensional projection of the three-dimensional anatomy, thereby lacking depth perception—a critical deficiency when navigating complex intracardiac structures. Moreover, soft tissues such as the myocardium, valves, and septal walls are largely transparent under fluoroscopy, necessitating the repeated administration of iodinated contrast agents to transiently opacify regions of interest. This not only increases procedural time and radiation exposure but also introduces risks associated with contrast-induced nephropathy and allergic reactions. While echocardiography—particularly transesophageal echocardiography (TEE)—offers direct visualization of cardiac anatomy and hemodynamics, its use is constrained by operator dependency, limited availability in many clinical settings, and challenges in integrating TEE data with fluoroscopic guidance in a spatially coherent manner. Preoperative imaging modalities such as computed tomography (CT) and magnetic resonance (MR) provide high-resolution, three-dimensional anatomical models that can inform procedural planning. Yet, when these static 3D reconstructions are overlaid onto live 2D fluoroscopic images, they often obstruct the operative field without delivering true spatial registration or dynamic feedback. Furthermore, conventional display systems present all imaging data on flat, two-dimensional screens, forcing clinicians to mentally reconstruct spatial relationships between the catheter, target anatomy, and surrounding structures—an inherently error-prone cognitive task. Existing image registration methods, including intensity-based, gradient-based, and feature-based algorithms, struggle with cross-modality alignment due to fundamental differences in image contrast, resolution, and noise characteristics between preoperative CT and intraoperative fluoroscopy. Augmented reality (AR) platforms have emerged in orthopedic and neurosurgical domains but remain impractical for cardiac interventions due to their inability to support real-time, quantitative tracking of moving devices within a dynamic anatomical environment. These collective shortcomings underscore an unmet need for an integrated, real-time image guidance system that overcomes the dimensional, perceptual, and registration barriers inherent in current cardiovascular interventional practice.

## SUMMARY OF THE DISCLOSURE

The present disclosure introduces a novel image guidance method that enables real-time, three-dimensional visualization of both patient anatomy and interventional devices during percutaneous cardiac procedures through augmented reality. The method begins by receiving a first image data set acquired preoperatively using a volumetric imaging modality such as computed tomography, which captures detailed three-dimensional representations of the patient’s heart and spine. From this data set, a virtual model of the anatomical target—such as the left atrium, interatrial septum, or aortic valve—is generated through segmentation and mesh reconstruction. Concurrently, a model of the spine is extracted from the same data set, serving as a stable anatomical reference. During the intervention, a second image data set is received in the form of one or more fluoroscopic images captured intraoperatively using a C-arm imaging system. A mask of the spine is then generated from the fluoroscopic image through adaptive thresholding, morphological operations, and region-of-interest refinement. A virtual model of the medical device—typically a catheter equipped with radiopaque markers—is also constructed based on its known geometry and detected silhouette in the fluoroscopic image. A transformation matrix is determined that aligns the coordinate system of the preoperative CT-derived models with that of the intraoperative fluoroscopic image, leveraging the spine as a universal fiducial marker. This transformation accounts for scaling, rotation, and translation discrepancies arising from differences in imaging geometry and patient positioning. Using this transformation, the medical device is co-registered with the anatomical target in a unified three-dimensional space. Finally, an output image is generated that fuses the registered models into a composite holographic rendering, which is displayed via an augmented reality headset to provide the interventionalist with intuitive, depth-perceptive visual feedback. Variations of the method include the use of biplane fluoroscopy for continuous 3D tracking, integration of motion-correction algorithms to compensate for respiratory or cardiac motion, incorporation of machine learning for automated segmentation, and voice- or gesture-based user interaction with the AR environment.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The disclosed system comprises an integrated hardware-software platform designed to facilitate real-time, three-dimensional image guidance during minimally invasive cardiac interventions. The system overview encompasses multiple functional modules that operate in concert to transform disparate imaging inputs into a unified augmented reality experience. Key system components include at least one preoperative imaging device (e.g., a CT scanner), at least one intraoperative imaging device (e.g., a fluoroscopic C-arm), a processing server, a database, and an augmented reality display system such as Microsoft HoloLens. The system architecture is modular, enabling distributed computation and wireless communication between components over a secure hospital network.

The guidance system includes a model generator responsible for creating virtual three-dimensional representations of anatomical structures and medical devices. These virtual models are generated from segmented image data using surface meshing algorithms and exported in standard formats such as STL or OBJ. A segmentation engine performs the initial delineation of structures of interest from raw image volumes. For CT data, segmentation involves thresholding based on Hounsfield units, followed by noise removal, hole filling, and manual or semi-automated refinement to isolate the heart and spine while excluding ribs, vasculature, and other irrelevant anatomy. The segmentation process identifies the spine as a universal fiducial marker due to its high contrast in both CT and fluoroscopy, minimal physiological motion, and consistent anatomical position across imaging sessions when the patient is maintained in supine orientation.

A registration engine determines the spatial transformation that aligns the coordinate systems of preoperative and intraoperative images. This is achieved by comparing a 2D projection of the CT-derived spine model—rendered at the same C-arm angulation as recorded in the fluoroscopic metadata—with the actual fluoroscopic image of the spine. The engine employs a Fourier-Mellin transform pipeline: first, both images undergo 2D Fourier transformation; then, their spectral magnitudes are converted to polar-logarithmic coordinates to decouple rotation and scaling into translational shifts; finally, phase correlation in the transformed domain yields precise estimates of rotation angle, scale factor, and translation offsets. In some embodiments, the registration engine incorporates a machine learning algorithm trained on paired CT-fluoroscopy datasets to refine transformation parameters or handle low-contrast scenarios.

A motion correction engine addresses artifacts arising from patient respiration or cardiac contraction. By analyzing temporal sequences of fluoroscopic frames, the engine identifies quiescent intervals—such as the inter-beat period in the cardiac cycle—and selects images captured during these windows for registration and device tracking, thereby minimizing motion-induced misalignment.

The display generator synthesizes the final augmented reality output. It imports the registered 3D models of the heart, spine, and catheter, then renders them as stereoscopic holograms overlaid onto the user’s real-world view. The generator supports both 2D and 3D display modes and can produce secondary views—such as cross-sectional slices or endoluminal perspectives—from virtual cameras positioned at the catheter tip. Operational information, including catheter orientation (pitch, roll, yaw) and proximity to target structures, is embedded into the visualization.

A centralized database stores all intermediate and final data products, including raw image data sets, segmented models, spine masks, transformation matrices, and a training library of annotated cases used to improve segmentation and registration algorithms over time. The first imaging device, typically a CT scanner, captures pre-operative 3D images of the thorax with ECG gating to minimize motion blur. The second imaging device, a fluoroscopic C-arm, acquires intra-operative 2D images from one or more angles, embedding metadata such as gantry orientation, source-to-detector distance, and patient table position. The C-arm may be mounted on a robotic arm to enable rapid switching between orthogonal views, facilitating real-time 3D catheter localization.

The display system comprises head-mounted AR goggles featuring transparent waveguides, depth sensors, and spatial audio. These devices allow hands-free interaction via voice commands (e.g., “open” to reveal internal anatomy) or hand gestures (e.g., pinch-and-drag to rotate the 3D model). In a representative embodiment, a 12-French transseptal catheter with ring-shaped radiopaque markers is used. The pitch angle is calculated from the vertical displacement of markers in the AP view, roll from the symmetry of marker rings in oblique views, and yaw from lateral deviations in LAO/RAO projections.

Image processing pipelines detect the catheter in fluoroscopy by initializing a region of interest at the image base, applying edge detection perpendicular to the assumed catheter axis, identifying opposing edges via gradient peak detection, and iteratively advancing the region of interest upward to trace the centerline. Once the 2D positions are established in two angular views, 3D coordinates are triangulated using known C-arm rotation angles. The resulting point cloud is smoothed and rendered as a blue virtual catheter with red spherical markers at key locations.

The method workflow begins with receiving a first image data set (CT), generating anatomical and spinal models, receiving a second image data set (fluoroscopy), generating a spine mask, determining the transformation via Fourier-Mellin registration, registering all models into a common frame, and generating an output image for AR display. This sequence ensures accurate, real-time spatial correspondence between virtual anatomy and physical devices. The scope of implementations extends beyond transseptal puncture to include valve replacement, atrial appendage closure, and congenital defect repair, with potential adaptation to other anatomical regions where a stable bony fiducial exists.
