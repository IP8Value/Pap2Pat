# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD OF THE DISCLOSURE

The present disclosure relates generally to the field of imaging and vision systems, and more specifically to thermal management techniques for three-dimensional (3D) stacked image sensor architectures that integrate vision processing units (VPUs) in close proximity to the image sensor. The invention further pertains to fidelity-driven runtime control mechanisms that dynamically regulate sensor temperature to maintain image quality while optimizing system energy efficiency. This disclosure encompasses hardware-software co-designed solutions for managing thermal behavior in near-sensor processing systems, particularly those employed in mobile, wearable, or embedded vision applications such as lifeloggers, dashboard cameras, augmented reality devices, and autonomous navigation systems. The disclosed methods and apparatuses address the critical challenge of thermal noise induced by on-die or on-package processing, which degrades image fidelity and impairs the accuracy of downstream computer vision tasks. By introducing novel thermal management policies—such as stop-capture-go and seasonal migration—and a runtime controller named Stagioni, the invention enables high-performance, energy-efficient vision systems that adaptively balance computational throughput, power consumption, and image quality in response to dynamic environmental and application-specific demands.

## BACKGROUND

Imaging and vision systems have become ubiquitous in modern computing devices, enabling capabilities such as object detection, spatial mapping for augmented reality, and autonomous navigation. These systems rely on capturing high-resolution visual data at significant frame rates, often requiring data transfer rates exceeding 2 Gbps for 4K video at 30 frames per second. In traditional vision architectures, the image sensor is physically separated from the computational units—such as CPUs, GPUs, or dedicated VPUs—by interconnects like ribbon cables or standard camera serial interfaces (CSI). This separation necessitates extensive off-chip data movement, which constitutes a major bottleneck in both energy efficiency and processing latency. As a result, conventional vision systems typically operate at power levels ranging from several watts to over 100 watts, depending on the resolution, frame rate, and complexity of the visual tasks being performed.

Despite advances in convolutional neural network (ConvNet) acceleration using ASICs, FPGAs, and domain-specific processors, the fundamental limitation of moving large volumes of pixel data across chip boundaries remains a dominant source of energy consumption. Communication interfaces such as CSI and DDR memory buses consume orders of magnitude more energy per pixel than on-chip operations, with interface energy often exceeding 3 nJ per pixel compared to hundreds of picojoules for sensing and processing. This inefficiency has motivated the development of near-sensor processing architectures, where computation is moved closer to the image sensor to minimize data movement.

Recent innovations in 3D integrated circuit stacking have enabled the integration of image sensors, vision processors, and memory into a single package. Prototypes such as RedEye and ShiDianNao, as well as commercial products from Sony and Samsung, demonstrate the feasibility of performing ConvNet inference directly within the sensor stack. These 3D-stacked vision sensors offer substantial energy savings—up to 52% in modeled scenarios—by eliminating off-chip data transfers and enabling low-bandwidth communication of processed features rather than raw pixel data.

However, the tight thermal coupling between the sensor and the adjacent processing layers introduces a critical drawback: elevated temperatures increase thermal noise in the image sensor, manifesting as dark current and read noise. This noise not only degrades aesthetic image quality but also impairs the accuracy of computer vision algorithms, particularly under low-light conditions where high exposure and ISO settings amplify noise sensitivity. While dynamic thermal management (DTM) techniques exist for general-purpose processors—such as clock gating, voltage scaling, and task migration—these mechanisms are designed to enforce thermal design power (TDP) limits and do not account for the transient, fidelity-sensitive nature of image capture. Consequently, existing DTM approaches are ill-suited for vision systems where image quality must be preserved on demand, even during sustained high-temperature operation.

Moreover, prior work on 3D-stacked sensors has largely addressed thermal issues through static design-time partitioning of computation, without runtime adaptability. There remains a pressing need for intelligent, application-aware thermal management that can dynamically regulate sensor temperature in response to changing lighting conditions, ambient environments, and fidelity requirements. The present invention addresses this gap by introducing a comprehensive framework for modeling and controlling the thermal behavior of near-sensor processing systems, with a focus on preserving image fidelity while maximizing energy efficiency.

## SUMMARY

The present disclosure introduces a fidelity-driven runtime thermal management framework for 3D-stacked vision systems that integrate image sensors with near-sensor vision processing units (VPUs). This framework is motivated by the energy-efficiency benefits of near-sensor processing, which significantly reduces off-chip data movement but concurrently elevates sensor temperature, thereby increasing thermal noise and degrading image fidelity. The thermal implications of near-sensor processing are particularly acute because image sensors exhibit strong sensitivity to junction temperature, with noise levels rising exponentially with temperature—especially under low-light conditions requiring high ISO and exposure settings.

To address this challenge, the invention proposes novel thermal management control policies that dynamically adjust processing behavior based on real-time fidelity needs and thermal state. Specifically, two primary control policies are introduced: (1) *stop-capture-go*, which temporarily suspends near-sensor processing to allow rapid cooling of the sensor for high-fidelity image capture, and (2) *seasonal migration*, which shifts processing to a thermally isolated far-sensor VPU during high-temperature periods to maintain continuous computation while enabling sensor cooling.

These policies are orchestrated by a runtime controller, herein named *Stagioni*, which operates as a lightweight service on the near-sensor processor or as a dedicated hardware module. The Stagioni controller continuously monitors sensor temperature, ambient conditions, lighting environment, and application fidelity requirements to determine optimal duty cycles and switching schedules between processing modes. It implements an application programming interface (API) that allows vision applications to signal on-demand high-fidelity capture requests, triggering immediate thermal regulation.

The invention further encompasses a vision system embodiment comprising a 3D-stacked image sensor package with integrated sensor, DRAM, and VPU layers, coupled with a thermal resistance-capacitance (RC) model that accurately predicts transient and steady-state temperature behavior. This model reveals that while the package-level thermal time constant is on the order of seconds, the sensor junction exhibits a much faster thermal response—on the order of milliseconds—enabling rapid temperature drops (e.g., 13°C in 20 ms) upon removal of near-sensor processing power.

Additionally, the disclosure introduces a method for thermally managing a vision system that includes: characterizing the relationship between processing power, sensor temperature, and image noise; defining situational temperature thresholds based on lighting and ISO settings; computing required duty cycles to maintain fidelity; and executing control policies to minimize average system power while meeting fidelity deadlines. A vision circuitry embodiment is also described, featuring dual VPUs—one near-sensor and one far-sensor—enabling seamless migration of workloads without performance interruption.

Collectively, these innovations enable vision systems that achieve both high energy efficiency and high image fidelity, adapting dynamically to environmental and application demands—a capability previously unattainable with conventional thermal management approaches.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present disclosure describes embodiments of a thermal management system for near-sensor vision architectures, with a focus on 3D-stacked image sensors that integrate sensing, processing, and memory in a single package. The term “near-sensor processing” refers to the execution of vision algorithms—such as object detection, classification, or feature extraction—on a vision processing unit (VPU) that is physically and thermally coupled to the image sensor, typically through 3D stacking using through-silicon vias (TSVs). The term “fidelity” denotes the quality of captured images, quantified by metrics such as signal-to-noise ratio (SNR), and is influenced by thermal noise, which increases with sensor temperature. “Thermal management” encompasses any mechanism that regulates the temperature of the sensor element to preserve fidelity, including power gating, workload migration, and duty cycling.

This detailed description is structured to first motivate the need for near-sensor processing by contrasting it with traditional vision systems, then characterize its thermal and fidelity implications, and finally introduce and evaluate the proposed thermal management mechanisms. The disclosure is grounded in empirical modeling and hardware validation, ensuring practical relevance.

Traditional vision processing pipelines suffer from high energy consumption due to extensive off-chip data movement. In a conventional system, raw pixel data is transmitted from the sensor to a remote SoC via a CSI interface, then stored in off-chip DRAM before being processed by a VPU. Energy models derived from datasheets and literature show that communication interfaces consume over 3 nJ per pixel, whereas sensing and processing consume only hundreds of picojoules per pixel. Near-sensor processing mitigates this by integrating the VPU and DRAM beneath the sensor layer in a 3D stack, reducing data movement to a few bytes of processed features. This architecture can reduce system power by up to 52% for ResNet-based classification at 1080p and 34 fps.

However, this integration introduces thermal challenges. The VPU’s power dissipation—ranging from 100 mW to 2.5 W—directly heats the sensor through conduction. A thermal RC model of the 3D stack, validated against measurements on an OnSemi AR0330 sensor, shows that inter-layer thermal resistances are negligible, causing the entire stack to reach nearly uniform temperature. Steady-state temperature rises with VPU power and ambient conditions, but more critically, the sensor junction exhibits a fast thermal time constant (~20 ms), allowing rapid cooling when processing power is removed.

This rapid thermal response is key to the invention. Experiments confirm that removing 2.5 W of near-sensor power drops sensor temperature by 13°C within 20 ms. This enables on-demand fidelity: during continuous vision tasks (e.g., object tracking), the system can operate at higher temperatures for energy efficiency, but instantly cool for high-fidelity captures when triggered by an event (e.g., a person entering the scene).

Image fidelity is highly sensitive to temperature, especially under low light. Thermal characterization shows that noise correction circuits in commercial sensors fail above certain temperatures, with the noise “knee” shifting lower under high ISO/exposure. Histograms of captured images reveal increased pixel intensity variance and mean shift due to dark current, visibly degrading image quality. Vision task accuracy—tested with MobileNet on ImageNet—drops significantly with injected thermal noise.

These insights motivate three design principles for thermal management: (1) *situational temperature regulation*, where upper temperature bounds adapt to lighting and ISO; (2) *on-demand fidelity*, leveraging rapid cooling for high-quality captures; and (3) *system power minimization through duty cycle optimization*, balancing time spent in high-power (NSP) and low-power (CAP) modes.

Two control policies implement these principles. *Stop-capture-go* halts near-sensor processing (entering CAP mode) for a duration sufficient to cool the sensor, then resumes. It is architecturally simple, requiring only clock/power gating, but drops frames during stop periods, affecting performance. *Seasonal migration* shifts processing to a far-sensor VPU during high-temperature phases, maintaining continuous operation. It requires duplicated VPUs but incurs minimal switching overhead (~100 µs) via pre-copy migration.

The Stagioni runtime controller enforces these policies. It accepts fidelity requirements (e.g., SNR targets of 20–35 dB) and environmental inputs (ambient temperature, lighting) to compute dynamic temperature thresholds \(T_{high}\) and \(T_{low}\). For stop-capture-go, it gates the VPU upon threshold breach or capture request. For seasonal migration, it migrates tasks via message passing over Ethernet (emulating CSI). Duty cycles are derived from RC charging models, with warming/cooling times calculated based on thermal constants.

Evaluation via FPGA emulation—using ZCU102 boards to emulate stacked sensor and SoC—demonstrates that Stagioni reduces average system power by 22–53% across workloads, depending on fidelity needs and power profiles. Stop-capture-go achieves the lowest power but may drop frames; seasonal migration preserves performance with moderate overhead. Both policies adapt smoothly to ambient temperature changes and lighting transitions, as shown in temperature traces overlaid with dynamic thresholds.

In conclusion, the disclosed thermal management framework enables practical, high-efficiency near-sensor vision systems by reconciling the conflicting demands of energy, performance, and fidelity. The invention is extensible to other stacked architectures and lays the foundation for imaging-aware dynamic thermal management in future vision systems.
