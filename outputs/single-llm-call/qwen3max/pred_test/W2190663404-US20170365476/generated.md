# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

Silicon substrates are foundational materials in the semiconductor industry, widely employed in the fabrication of electronic and optoelectronic devices such as transistors, photodetectors, and solar cells. The utility of silicon arises from its favorable electronic properties, abundance, and well-established processing infrastructure. However, conventional silicon exhibits limited absorption of photons with energies below its bandgap (approximately 1.12 eV at room temperature), which restricts its performance in applications requiring sensitivity to infrared radiation or enhanced broadband light harvesting. To overcome this limitation, researchers have pursued strategies to extend silicon’s optical response into the sub-bandgap spectral region. One such approach involves hyperdoping—introducing dopant species at concentrations far exceeding their equilibrium solid solubility limits in silicon. Hyperdoped silicon can exhibit strong sub-bandgap optical absorptance due to the formation of intermediate bands or defect states within the bandgap, thereby enabling absorption of lower-energy photons.

Motivation for hyperdoping silicon stems from the potential to create high-performance, silicon-based infrared photodetectors and intermediate-band photovoltaic devices that leverage existing silicon manufacturing technologies while achieving functionality beyond the intrinsic limitations of pure silicon. When combined with surface texturing that produces light-trapping morphologies—commonly referred to as “black silicon”—hyperdoped layers can achieve near-unity absorptance across a broad spectral range, including both above- and below-bandgap wavelengths. This dual enhancement arises because the textured surface increases the effective optical path length through multiple reflections and scattering, while the hyperdoped layer provides intrinsic sub-bandgap absorption. Consequently, hyperdoped black silicon represents a promising platform for next-generation optoelectronic devices that require broadband spectral response without resorting to exotic or non-silicon materials.

Despite these advantages, hyperdoped black silicon fabricated via conventional methods—such as femtosecond laser irradiation in the presence of dopant gases—often suffers from significant microstructural defects. Specifically, the rapid melting and resolidification processes inherent to ultrafast laser processing typically produce a polyphasic surface layer comprising amorphous silicon, metastable high-pressure crystalline phases (e.g., Si-III and Si-XII), and conventional crystalline silicon (Si-I). While this complex microstructure supports high sub-bandgap absorptance, it simultaneously degrades electrical properties such as carrier mobility and lifetime, impeding efficient charge extraction in device applications. Moreover, post-processing thermal annealing—a standard technique used to improve crystallinity and activate electrical junctions—unintentionally deactivates the sub-bandgap optical absorptance by allowing dopant atoms to diffuse from optically active, non-equilibrium lattice sites to thermodynamically stable, optically inactive configurations. This trade-off between crystallinity and optical functionality has posed a fundamental challenge in the development of practical hyperdoped optoelectronic devices. Thus, there exists a critical need for a processing method that can enhance the crystallinity of hyperdoped black silicon while preserving or even restoring its sub-bandgap optical absorptance and light-trapping surface morphology.

## SUMMARY

The present invention introduces nanosecond pulsed laser annealing as a novel and effective method for processing hyperdoped semiconductor substrates, particularly hyperdoped black silicon, to simultaneously achieve high crystallinity, preserved light-trapping surface textures, and robust sub-bandgap optical absorptance. This technique addresses the longstanding conflict between structural order and optical functionality by leveraging the unique non-equilibrium thermodynamics of nanosecond-scale laser-material interactions. The invention is motivated by the need to maintain or restore sub-bandgap optical absorptance—a key metric for infrared photodetection and intermediate-band photovoltaics—while improving the electrical and structural quality of the doped surface layer.

In accordance with the invention, a method of processing a semiconductor substrate comprises incorporating a dopant into the semiconductor substrate to form a doped surface layer, wherein the dopant concentration exceeds the solid solubility limit of the dopant in the semiconductor. The doped surface layer is then optically annealed using nanosecond-duration laser pulses. This optical annealing step enhances the crystallinity of the doped surface layer by converting amorphous and pressure-induced crystalline phases into conventional single-crystalline or highly crystalline silicon, while simultaneously preserving the micron-scale light-trapping surface textures that are essential for broadband optical absorption. Critically, the optical annealing restores or maintains the sub-bandgap optical absorptance of the doped layer, which may have been degraded by prior thermal processing.

The method further includes optional thermal annealing prior to optical annealing, which may be employed to improve initial crystallinity or facilitate diode junction formation but often deactivates sub-bandgap absorptance. The subsequent nanosecond laser annealing reactivates this absorptance without compromising the structural improvements conferred by thermal treatment. Dopant incorporation may be achieved through various means, including exposure to dopant-containing compounds during laser texturing, application of a dopant-containing film to the substrate surface, or ion implantation followed by laser-induced melting and solute trapping. In preferred embodiments, the semiconductor substrate is silicon, and the dopant is a chalcogen such as sulfur, selenium, or tellurium, though the method is broadly applicable to other semiconductors—including germanium, silicon carbide, and gallium arsenide—and to both electron-donating (n-type) and hole-donating (p-type) dopant species.

The doped surface layer generated by this process exhibits a polyphase structure initially, which is transformed into a highly crystalline state through optical annealing. The resulting layer forms an electrically rectifying diode junction with the underlying bulk substrate and demonstrates sub-bandgap optical absorptance greater than 50%, often exceeding 70–90% across infrared wavelengths. Surface textures, characterized by undulations such as conical or pillar-like features on the micrometer scale, remain intact after optical annealing, ensuring continued light-trapping capability. The method is particularly advantageous because it decouples structural refinement from optical deactivation, enabling the fabrication of high-performance photodetectors and photovoltaic devices that leverage both enhanced crystallinity for charge transport and preserved sub-bandgap absorption for spectral response. The versatility of the approach allows integration into existing semiconductor manufacturing workflows and compatibility with a range of doping and texturing techniques, making it a scalable solution for advanced optoelectronic device fabrication.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

Semiconductor substrate processing according to the present invention begins with the preparation of a base semiconductor material, typically monocrystalline silicon, though germanium, silicon carbide, gallium arsenide, and other compound semiconductors are also suitable. The solid solubility limit refers to the maximum equilibrium concentration of a dopant species that can be incorporated into the crystal lattice of the semiconductor at a given temperature without precipitating secondary phases. In the context of this invention, “hyperdoping” denotes dopant concentrations significantly exceeding this limit—often by two to four orders of magnitude—achieved through non-equilibrium processing techniques that trap dopants in metastable lattice sites.

A polyphasic doped surface layer is formed by exposing the semiconductor substrate to conditions that induce localized melting and rapid resolidification in the presence of a dopant source. This may be accomplished by irradiating the substrate with short laser pulses (e.g., femtosecond or picosecond duration) in an atmosphere containing a dopant precursor gas such as sulfur hexafluoride, or by applying a thin film containing the dopant onto the surface prior to laser irradiation. Alternatively, ion implantation may be used to introduce dopant ions into a shallow surface region, followed by pulsed laser melting to dissolve the implanted layer and initiate resolidification. The resulting doped surface layer typically exhibits a complex microstructure comprising amorphous silicon, high-pressure crystalline phases (e.g., Si-III, Si-XII), and regions of conventional crystalline silicon, along with a high density of dopant atoms trapped in non-equilibrium configurations.

Optical annealing is motivated by the need to improve the crystallinity of this polyphasic layer without sacrificing its optically active dopant configuration or light-trapping morphology. Unlike conventional thermal annealing, which drives the system toward thermodynamic equilibrium and thereby deactivates sub-bandgap absorption, nanosecond pulsed laser annealing operates under non-equilibrium conditions that permit selective melting and rapid resolidification of the surface layer. During this process, the laser pulse energy is absorbed within a shallow depth (typically 50–200 nm), melting the amorphous and defective regions while leaving the underlying crystalline substrate intact. Resolidification proceeds epitaxially from the liquid-solid interface, yielding a highly crystalline doped surface layer in which dopant atoms remain trapped in optically active sites.

Crystallinity enhancement is quantified through Raman spectroscopy, which distinguishes between amorphous silicon (broad peak centered at ~480 cm⁻¹) and crystalline silicon (sharp peak at ~520 cm⁻¹). A crystalline material phase, as used herein, refers to a region of the semiconductor exhibiting long-range atomic order characteristic of the diamond cubic lattice of silicon (Si-I), as opposed to disordered (amorphous) or metastable high-pressure phases. Following optical annealing, Raman spectra show a marked reduction or elimination of the amorphous signal and pressure-induced phase signatures, accompanied by a narrowing of the crystalline peak width (FWHM), indicating reduced lattice stress and/or increased grain size.

Sub-bandgap radiation, as referenced in this disclosure, encompasses electromagnetic wavelengths longer than approximately 1100 nm (corresponding to photon energies below 1.12 eV for silicon). The absorptance of such radiation is a critical performance metric for infrared photodetectors and intermediate-band solar cells. The present method preserves or restores absorptance values greater than 50%—and often exceeding 80%—in the 1200–4000 nm range, even after prior thermal deactivation.

Referring to the flow chart of FIG. 1, the method proceeds in two principal steps. Step 1 involves applying short radiation pulses (e.g., femtosecond laser pulses) to the semiconductor substrate in the presence of a dopant source, thereby generating a textured, hyperdoped surface layer. This layer exhibits self-organized surface undulations—typically conical structures 1–10 µm in height—with a polyphase internal structure and dopant concentrations exceeding 10¹⁹ cm⁻³, far beyond the equilibrium solubility limit of ~5×10¹⁶ cm⁻³ for chalcogens in silicon. Step 2 entails optical annealing of this doped surface layer using nanosecond laser pulses (e.g., 4–25 ns duration, wavelengths of 193–355 nm, fluences of 0.6–2.2 J/cm²). The optical annealing pulses are rastered across the substrate surface using an x-y scanning mirror system or translation stage, ensuring uniform treatment. The process environment may be controlled via gas lines and pump lines to maintain inert or reactive atmospheres as needed.

The optical annealing enhances crystallinity by melting the amorphous skin and defective regions, followed by rapid resolidification that yields a highly crystalline doped layer. Crucially, the micron-scale surface textures are preserved because the laser fluence is calibrated to melt only the topmost nanometers to hundreds of nanometers, insufficient to disrupt the larger geometric features. Absorptance characteristics remain high due to the retention of both light-trapping morphology and optically active dopant configurations. Dopant concentration is preserved because the resolidification velocity exceeds the diffusion rate of dopant atoms, enabling solute trapping.

This processed material is ideally suited for photodetector applications. A photodetector structure comprises the highly crystalline, hyperdoped surface layer forming a homojunction with the underlying p-type or n-type substrate, with metal contacts (e.g., Ti/Ni/Ag on the doped side and Al on the back) enabling photocurrent detection under reverse bias. Similarly, in photovoltaic devices, the hyperdoped layer acts as an intermediate-band absorber, enhancing photocurrent generation from sub-bandgap photons.

Apparatus 100, configured for dopant incorporation, may include a femtosecond laser, vacuum chamber, dopant gas delivery system, and beam scanning optics. Apparatus 200, for optical annealing, comprises a nanosecond pulsed laser (e.g., excimer or Nd:YAG), beam shaping optics, and a scanning system. An integrated system may combine both lasers, with a beam splitter and mirrors directing pulses sequentially to the same substrate location. Ion implantation may alternatively be used to incorporate dopants, followed by laser texturing and optical annealing.

The method may include thermal annealing (e.g., 700°C in forming gas) prior to optical annealing to improve initial junction properties, albeit at the cost of absorptance deactivation. Subsequent nanosecond laser annealing reactivates absorptance while maintaining crystallinity. The final product is a semiconductor substrate with a highly crystalline, hyperdoped surface layer exhibiting light-trapping textures, sub-bandgap absorptance >50%, and rectifying electrical behavior—ideal for advanced optoelectronic devices.

### EXEMPLIFICATION

Example 1 illustrates the fabrication and characterization of sulfur-hyperdoped black silicon. A p-type silicon wafer was irradiated with femtosecond laser pulses (800 nm, 100 fs, 8 kJ/m² fluence, 200 pulses per site) in 500 Torr SF₆ gas, producing a black silicon surface with 10-µm conical structures and a 50–100 nm amorphous skin. The sample was then subjected to nanosecond laser annealing using a 355 nm Nd:YAG laser (4 ns pulse, 2.2 J/cm² fluence, single pulse per site). Prior to optical annealing, rapid thermal annealing (500°C, 30 sec, N₂) was performed after metal electrode deposition to ensure ohmic contact.

Crystallinity was assessed via Raman spectroscopy: the as-fabricated sample showed strong amorphous (480 cm⁻¹) and pressure-phase signals, which were eliminated after ns annealing. The FWHM of the crystalline peak decreased from 8.4 to 7.3 cm⁻¹, indicating improved crystalline quality. Surface electrical conductance increased by over an order of magnitude, consistent with reduced defect scattering. Current-voltage measurements revealed rectification ratios of 50±20 at ±10 V, with turn-on voltages of 0.5–1.5 V after rapid thermal annealing—characteristic of homojunction diodes rather than Schottky barriers. Optical absorption measurements (UV-VIS-NIR and FTIR) confirmed that above-bandgap absorptance (~95%) and sub-bandgap absorptance (>90% at 1.2–2.5 µm) were preserved after ns annealing. Surface morphology, examined by FESEM, showed nanoscale smoothing of granular features while retaining the 10-µm cones. These results demonstrate that ns laser annealing enhances electrical and structural properties without degrading optical performance.

Example 2 explores the sequential use of thermal and optical annealing. Hyperdoped black silicon was first fabricated as in Example 1. It was then thermally annealed at 700°C for 30 min in forming gas, which crystallized the surface but reduced sub-bandgap absorptance from ~80% to ~20%. Subsequent ns laser annealing (355 nm, 1.1 J/cm²) restored absorptance to 70–80%, as shown in FIG. 7, while maintaining high crystallinity (undetectable amorphous content by Raman). After metal electrode deposition and rapid thermal annealing (500°C, 30 sec), absorptance partially decreased again (~60%), but a second ns laser annealing step fully restored it to 70–80%. TEM and SAD confirmed conversion of the surface to single-crystalline silicon with [110] orientation. FESEM showed intact conical morphology, while current-bias measurements confirmed persistent rectification.

Additional experiments varied fs laser fluence during fabrication: at 2.5 kJ/m², 1-µm cones formed. Low-fluence ns annealing (0.6 J/cm²) crystallized the surface without damaging texture, boosting sub-bandgap absorptance to 80–90%. However, high-fluence ns annealing (2.2 J/cm²) destroyed the smaller cones, reducing absorptance to 60%, underscoring the importance of matching laser parameters to surface feature size.

Discussion of physical mechanisms reveals that ns laser annealing melts a ~100 nm surface layer, sufficient to dissolve the amorphous skin and amorphize pockets but not deep enough to flatten micron-scale cones. The resolidification traps sulfur in optically active sites, reactivating sub-bandgap absorption. The process is wavelength-insensitive (193–355 nm) due to similar optical penetration depths. The Johnson-Mehl-Avrami-Kolmogorov framework explains thermal deactivation as dopant diffusion to inactive sites, while ns annealing reverses this by re-melting and quenching. The combination of equilibrium (thermal) and non-equilibrium (laser) processing offers unprecedented control over metastable materials. Challenges remain in precise dopant concentration control (<0.4 at.% for intermediate band formation) and layer thickness optimization (<100 nm for carrier extraction), but the present method resolves the core conflict between crystallinity and optical activity.

## CONCLUSION

Nanosecond laser annealing provides a transformative approach to processing hyperdoped semiconductor materials, particularly black silicon, by enabling concurrent high crystallinity, preserved light-trapping morphology, and robust sub-bandgap optical absorptance. This technique overcomes the fundamental limitation of conventional thermal annealing, which sacrifices optical functionality for structural order. By leveraging rapid melting and resolidification dynamics, ns laser annealing reactivates optically active dopant configurations while eliminating amorphous and metastable phases, thereby enhancing both electrical and optical performance. The method is broadly applicable across semiconductor platforms and compatible with industrial fabrication processes, offering significant advantages for infrared photodetectors, intermediate-band solar cells, and other sub-bandgap optoelectronic devices. Although challenges persist in fine control of dopant concentration and layer thickness, the present invention establishes a critical pathway toward viable hyperdoped silicon devices. Variations in laser parameters, dopant species, and substrate materials are contemplated within the scope of this disclosure, reflecting the versatility and scalability of the disclosed approach.
