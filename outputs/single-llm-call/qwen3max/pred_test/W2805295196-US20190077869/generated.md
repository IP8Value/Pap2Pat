# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates to the field of immunotherapy for blood-borne cancers, particularly acute myeloid leukemia (AML). More specifically, the invention provides novel methods and compositions for the treatment of hematologic malignancies through the use of immune-checkpoint inhibitors targeting the CD112 (PVRL2/Nectin-2), CD155 (PVR/Tage4), TIGIT, Galectin-9, and TIM-3 pathways. The invention further encompasses pharmaceutical compositions comprising such inhibitors in combination with chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T cells or bispecific antibody constructs, such as BiTE® molecules, to enhance anti-leukemic immune responses. These therapeutic approaches are designed to overcome tumor-mediated immunosuppression by disrupting inhibitory signals that impair T-cell and natural killer (NK) cell cytotoxicity, thereby restoring or augmenting the immune system’s capacity to eliminate malignant cells. The invention is particularly directed toward patients with refractory or relapsed AML, where conventional therapies have failed, and represents a significant advancement in the development of precision immunotherapies for hematologic cancers.

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

Immune-checkpoint blockade has emerged as a transformative strategy in oncology, leveraging the body’s own immune system to recognize and destroy cancer cells. Under physiological conditions, immune checkpoints such as CTLA-4 and PD-1 serve to maintain self-tolerance and prevent excessive immune activation that could lead to autoimmunity. However, tumors frequently exploit these regulatory pathways to evade immune surveillance by upregulating checkpoint ligands that engage inhibitory receptors on T cells, leading to T-cell exhaustion, anergy, or apoptosis. The success of monoclonal antibodies targeting CTLA-4 (e.g., ipilimumab) and PD-1/PD-L1 (e.g., nivolumab, pembrolizumab) in melanoma, lung cancer, and other solid tumors has validated immune-checkpoint inhibition as a powerful therapeutic modality. Nevertheless, many hematologic malignancies, including AML, exhibit limited responsiveness to these agents, underscoring the need to identify alternative or complementary immune-regulatory axes.

T-cell activation is a tightly regulated process requiring both antigen recognition via the T-cell receptor (TCR) and co-stimulatory signals, such as those mediated by CD28 binding to B7 family members (CD80/CD86). Conversely, inhibitory signals from immune-checkpoint receptors—many of which belong to the B7 or TNF receptor superfamilies—can override activation signals. Members of the B7 family, including PD-L1, PD-L2, and B7-H3, bind to receptors like PD-1 and deliver suppressive signals that dampen T-cell proliferation, cytokine production, and cytotoxic function. Similarly, TNF family members, such as Galectin-9, interact with receptors like TIM-3 to induce T-cell tolerance and promote immune evasion. Cytokine signals further modulate this balance, with inflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL-2, IFN-γ) supporting effector functions, while immunosuppressive cytokines (e.g., TGF-β, IL-10) reinforce checkpoint-mediated inhibition.

In the context of blood-borne cancers, particularly AML, tumor immune resistance is a major barrier to durable remission. Leukemic blasts often express ligands that engage inhibitory receptors on immune effector cells, creating an immunosuppressive microenvironment that protects the malignant clone. Cancer immunotherapy seeks to reverse this suppression by either blocking inhibitory interactions or enhancing stimulatory signals. While bispecific T-cell engagers (BiTEs), such as blinatumomab for ALL and AMG 330 for AML, redirect T cells to tumor antigens independently of MHC presentation, their efficacy can be limited by concurrent checkpoint-mediated inhibition. Thus, rational combinations of BiTEs with checkpoint inhibitors represent a promising strategy to amplify anti-tumor immunity. The discovery that AML blasts overexpress ligands such as CD112 and CD155, which engage the inhibitory receptor TIGIT, provides a compelling rationale for targeting this axis to improve outcomes in AML and other hematologic malignancies.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides immunomodulating compounds that inhibit the interaction between immune-checkpoint ligands and their cognate receptors, thereby enhancing anti-tumor immune responses in blood-borne cancers. In one embodiment, the invention features a pharmaceutical composition comprising a population of CAR T cells engineered to target AML-associated antigens such as CD33, CD19, or Flt3, in combination with an inhibitor of CD112, CD155, TIGIT, Galectin-9, or TIM-3. In another embodiment, the pharmaceutical composition includes a bispecific antibody construct, such as a BiTE® molecule, that binds both CD3 on T cells and a tumor antigen on leukemic blasts, co-administered with an immune-checkpoint inhibitor. The invention is motivated by the urgent need to identify novel immune-checkpoints beyond PD-1 and CTLA-4 that are operative in AML, where traditional checkpoint inhibitors have shown limited efficacy.

Specifically, the invention discloses inhibitors against CD112 (PVRL2), CD155 (PVR), TIGIT, Galectin-9, and TIM-3, which may be administered alone or in combination with cellular or antibody-based immunotherapies. The inhibitor against CD112 may be a monoclonal antibody, a soluble receptor, or an RNAi molecule that reduces CD112 expression. Similarly, the inhibitor against CD155 may comprise a neutralizing antibody or a CRISPR/Cas9-mediated knockout construct. The inhibitor against TIGIT may block the extracellular domain of TIGIT to prevent its binding to CD112 or CD155. Inhibitors against Galectin-9 and TIM-3 may disrupt their interaction, thereby preventing TIM-3-mediated T-cell exhaustion.

The antibody construct of the invention is a bispecific molecule comprising a first binding domain specific for CD3 and a second binding domain specific for a tumor-associated antigen such as CD33, CD19, or Flt3. The CAR T cell of the invention is genetically modified to express a chimeric receptor that includes an extracellular antigen-binding domain (e.g., scFv against CD33) fused to intracellular signaling domains that activate T-cell effector functions. The invention also encompasses an immunostimulant that enhances T-cell proliferation or cytokine production, as well as iRNA or CRISPR/Cas9 techniques to genetically ablate checkpoint molecules in tumor or immune cells. These modalities act to modulate intracellular signaling pathways that govern T-cell activation, survival, and cytotoxicity. The resulting pharmaceutical composition is formulated for parenteral administration and is effective in treating refractory or relapsed AML.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention is not disclosed in the prior art, which fails to recognize the therapeutic potential of simultaneously targeting the CD112/CD155–TIGIT axis and employing bispecific T-cell engagers or CAR T cells in AML. Immune-checkpoint inhibitors described herein are distinct from known anti-PD-1 or anti-CTLA-4 agents and are specifically tailored for hematologic malignancies. The invention is particularly motivated by the high unmet need in treating blood-borne cancers, especially AML, which remains associated with poor survival despite intensive chemotherapy.

CD112 (PVRL2) and CD155 (PVR) are identified as key targets because they are consistently overexpressed on AML cell lines and primary blasts, including leukemia-initiating cells. Similarly, Galectin-9 and TIM-3 constitute a second inhibitory axis that contributes to T-cell dysfunction in AML. Clinical data demonstrate that high expression of PVR and PVRL2 correlates with negative prognostic outcomes in AML patients, confirming their role in immune evasion. Blockade of PVR, PVRL2, or TIGIT significantly enhances T-cell-mediated lysis of AML cells in vitro and in vivo. Although some studies report contradictory roles for Galectin-9 and TIM-3, the present invention establishes that their blockade also augments anti-leukemic immunity in defined contexts.

Flow cytometry reveals that PVR and PVRL2 are expressed at higher protein densities on AML cell lines (e.g., TF-1, Molm-13, MV4-11) compared to peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) from healthy donors. Primary AML blasts from untreated patients similarly show robust expression, with median fluorescence intensities indicating abundant surface presentation. This differential expression provides a therapeutic window for selective targeting. The immunosuppressive signal mediated by PVR and PVRL2 occurs through engagement of TIGIT on T cells and NK cells, triggering ITIM-dependent inhibition of cytotoxicity. Galectin-9 binding to TIM-3 similarly suppresses T-cell function through downstream phosphatase recruitment.

As used herein, “inhibit” or “inhibiting” means to reduce or abolish the biological activity of a target molecule, including by blocking protein-protein interactions, reducing gene expression, or preventing receptor signaling. “Blood-borne cancer” refers to malignancies originating in the hematopoietic system, such as AML, ALL, chronic myeloid leukemia, and lymphoma. While the invention is optimized for AML, it may also be applied to solid tumors expressing these checkpoints. Acute myeloid leukemia is defined as a clonal disorder of myeloid progenitors characterized by impaired differentiation and uncontrolled proliferation. Refractory AML denotes disease that fails to achieve remission after initial therapy, while relapsed AML refers to recurrence after a period of remission.

An immune-checkpoint inhibitor is any agent that interferes with the binding of an inhibitory ligand to its receptor. Modulation of intracellular signaling refers to altering kinase/phosphatase cascades downstream of immune receptors. Treatment of blood-borne cancer involves administering a therapeutically effective amount of the inhibitor to reduce tumor burden or prolong survival. Inhibition of ligand-receptor interaction may be achieved by antibodies, soluble decoys, or genetic knockdown.

The antibody construct is a recombinant protein comprising two antigen-binding domains. Antibody structure includes heavy and light chains, with variable regions conferring specificity. Fragments include Fab, F(ab′)2, Fd, Fv, dAb, and scFv. Bispecific single-chain constructs are engineered using peptide linkers, preferably flexible glycine-serine repeats, though single amino acid linkers may be used. These constructs are expressed in mammalian cells or bacteria and purified by chromatography.

Monoclonal antibodies are homogeneous populations derived from a single clone. Human antibodies minimize immunogenicity. Variants include humanized, affinity-matured, or effector-function-modified forms. In vitro generated antibodies may have engineered VH and VL domains, with CDRs defining antigen contact. Isolated proteins are purified away from native cellular components.

Experimental results confirm that inhibitors of CD112, CD155, TIGIT, Galectin-9, or TIM-3 enhance T-cell killing of AML cells. Inhibitors include protein-based agents (e.g., antibodies, soluble receptors) and non-protein compounds (e.g., small molecules). Soluble proteins are produced by recombinant expression. Polypeptide fragments act as mutual antagonists, designed using computational modeling. Small molecule inhibitors are typically <900 Da, orally bioavailable, and delivered intravenously or orally.

“Inhibitor” may also refer to nucleic acids that reduce expression via RNAi or CRISPR/Cas9. “Reduce expression” means decreasing mRNA or protein levels. RNAi is synthesized chemically or expressed from vectors and delivered via lipid nanoparticles. “Knock out” denotes permanent gene disruption. CRISPR/Cas9 uses guide RNAs to induce double-strand breaks, repaired by error-prone NHEJ.

CAR T cells are engineered to express a chimeric receptor targeting CD33, CD19, or Flt3. When combined with checkpoint inhibitors, synergistic lysis is observed. BiTE constructs, such as those with CD3 and CD33 binding domains, redirect T cells to blasts. In vitro killing assays demonstrate enhanced cytotoxicity upon checkpoint blockade, confirmed by CRISPR knockout and Granzyme B release.

“Treatment” includes prophylaxis and therapy. A “therapeutically effective amount” achieves a clinical benefit. Administration routes include intravenous, subcutaneous, or intraperitoneal. A “subject” is a human patient. Pharmaceutical formulations contain active agents, buffers, preservatives, surfactants, carbohydrates, and excipients. Co-therapy with chemotherapy, radiation, or stem cell transplantation is contemplated.

Efficacy is demonstrated by in vivo survival prolongation in NSG mice reconstituted with human T cells. Safety is evaluated by toxicity studies. Dose regimens are determined by pharmacokinetics, including half-life, distribution, and metabolism. Formulations may be lyophilized, sustained-release, or liposomal. Sterilization ensures parenteral safety. Salts are selected based on solubility and stability, guided by the Hofmeister series.

### Examples

The invention is exemplified by specific embodiments wherein patient samples are analyzed for CD112, CD155, TIGIT, Galectin-9, and TIM-3 expression by flow cytometry or RT-qPCR. Expression levels are correlated with clinical parameters, revealing that high PVR and PVRL2 are independent prognostic markers for poor survival in AML. In vitro killing assays show that blocking PVR and PVRL2 with antibodies enhances T-cell-mediated lysis of AML cell lines and primary blasts, an effect recapitulated by CRISPR/Cas9 double-knockout of PVR and PVRL2 in MV4-11 cells. Specificity is confirmed by using irrelevant antibodies and purified T cells, excluding ADCC. In vivo, NSG mice transplanted with PVR/PVRL2-knockout MV4-11 cells exhibit prolonged survival when reconstituted with human T cells, with or without AMG 330. mRNA analysis of two independent cohorts (n=139 and n=290) validates the negative prognostic impact of PVR and PVRL2. Protein expression on primary blasts confirms targetability. Granzyme B ELISA demonstrates increased T-cell activation upon checkpoint blockade, establishing a mechanistic link between TIGIT-PVR/PVRL2 inhibition and enhanced cytotoxicity.
