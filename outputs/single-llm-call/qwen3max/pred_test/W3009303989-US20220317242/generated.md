# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

The proliferation of wireless sensing technologies has enabled significant advances in ambient and ubiquitous computing, particularly in applications involving human activity monitoring. Such systems are increasingly vital for supporting aging populations, individuals with disabilities, and general wellness tracking in residential and institutional settings. Traditional approaches to human activity detection have relied on video surveillance, wearable sensors, or radar-based systems. However, each of these methods presents notable limitations that hinder their widespread adoption in private or domestic environments. Video-based monitoring raises serious privacy concerns, as continuous visual recording may capture sensitive or intimate moments, thereby discouraging user acceptance. Moreover, video processing demands substantial computational resources, making real-time analysis impractical in resource-constrained settings. Wearable sensors, such as accelerometers, gyroscopes, or infrared detectors, require consistent user compliance—users must remember to wear the devices, keep them charged, and tolerate their physical presence, which is often inconvenient during activities like sleeping or bathing. These constraints reduce long-term reliability and usability. Radar systems, while capable of non-contact sensing, remain prohibitively expensive and are not commercially accessible at scale, limiting their deployment to specialized research or military contexts. Radio Frequency Identification (RFID) technology offers a promising alternative due to its low cost, passive operation, and widespread industrial use in inventory and asset tracking. However, conventional RFID-based activity detection systems suffer from limited spatial sensitivity and short effective range, primarily because they rely on linearly polarized tags interrogated by circularly polarized readers—a configuration optimized for orientation-agnostic reading in logistics but suboptimal for detecting subtle environmental perturbations caused by human motion. This mismatch results in dominant line-of-sight (LOS) backscatter signals that overwhelm the weaker multipath reflections carrying information about human presence and movement. Consequently, existing RFID systems require dense tag deployments to achieve adequate coverage, increasing installation complexity and cost. These shortcomings underscore a critical need for an improved RFID-based remote sensing architecture that enhances signal-to-noise ratio (SNR), extends detection range, improves spatial sensitivity to subtle movements, and operates without requiring users to wear or carry any device—all while preserving user privacy through the absence of cameras or biometric data collection.

## SUMMARY

The present invention introduces a novel system for remote sensing of human activity using radio frequency identification (RFID) technology configured with cross circular polarization between the reader and the tag. The system comprises an RFID emitter, specifically an RFID reader equipped with a circularly polarized antenna, and a circular-polarized passive RFID tag designed to operate with opposite handedness relative to the reader’s polarization. The RFID reader emits circularly polarized radio frequency (RF) waves, which are received by the passive RFID tag. Due to the cross-polarized configuration—such as a right-hand circularly polarized (RHCP) reader antenna and a left-hand circularly polarized (LHCP) tag—the direct line-of-sight (LOS) backscatter signal is significantly suppressed, typically by more than 30 dB, minimizing interference from static environmental components. When a human body or other reflective object enters the environment, incident RF waves reflect off the conductive tissues of the body, causing a reversal in the handedness of the circular polarization. This reflected wave, now co-polarized with the reader antenna, is efficiently received, thereby enhancing the signal component associated with human activity while suppressing the static noise. The system further includes an RFID reflection analysis system that processes received signal strength indicator (RSSI) and phase data from the modulated backscatter signal to detect, classify, and monitor human movements. A key feature of the invention is the use of a spiral antenna in the RFID tag, which provides broadband circular polarization and stable radiation characteristics across the operating frequency band. The tag also incorporates a matching network and an integrated circuit (IC) optimized for conjugate impedance matching to maximize power transfer. The RFID transceiver device within the reader communicates with the tag via backscatter modulation, and the reflection analysis system employs calibration techniques to establish a steady-state model of the environment, enabling real-time deviation detection corresponding to moving objects. The method for remote sensing involves installing the RFID reader antenna and tag in a fixed physical arrangement—typically perpendicular to each other on opposing walls—and emitting circularly polarized RF waves. The tag receives these waves, modulates them according to its internal IC state, and re-emits modulated circularly polarized waves. The reader captures the reflected modulated signal, analyzes variations in RSSI and phase relative to a calibrated baseline, and triggers actions such as activity classification, alert generation, or data logging. This approach enables non-intrusive, privacy-preserving, and low-cost monitoring of both repetitive (e.g., respiration, hand waving) and non-repetitive (e.g., head nodding, crossing arms) human activities over extended ranges and large spatial areas using only a single tag.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The invention is embodied in an RFID system for object detection, particularly suited for human activity monitoring in indoor environments such as homes, hospitals, and offices. A primary advantage of this system is its strong privacy preservation: unlike camera-based systems, it does not capture visual or identifying information, and unlike wearables, it imposes no burden on the user. The RFID emitter, comprising an RFID reader and a circularly polarized antenna, is installed in a fixed location, typically mounted on a wall or ceiling. The circular-polarized passive RFID tag is similarly affixed to a surface in the environment, often on an opposing wall, and requires no power source or maintenance. The system exploits the physics of electromagnetic wave reflection: when circularly polarized RF waves encounter a conductive surface such as the human body, the handedness of the polarization reverses upon reflection. This phenomenon is leveraged by configuring the reader and tag with opposite circular polarizations (e.g., RHCP reader and LHCP tag). In this cross-circular polarization setup, the direct LOS path between reader and tag suffers severe polarization mismatch loss, reducing the static backscatter signal to near the noise floor. However, any RF wave that reflects off a human body flips its polarization and becomes co-polarized with the reader, allowing efficient reception of the activity-induced signal. The RFID tag is configured as a passive device containing an integrated circuit (IC)—such as an Impinj Monza R6 chip—and a spiral antenna fabricated on low-cost FR4 substrate. The spiral antenna provides frequency-independent circular polarization and a wide axial ratio bandwidth, ensuring consistent performance across the UHF RFID band (902–928 MHz in North America). A T-shaped matching network connects the IC to the antenna arms, achieving conjugate impedance matching to maximize power harvesting from incident RF energy. During operation, the RFID reader emits a continuous wave (CW) of circularly polarized RF energy. The tag harvests this energy, modulates the carrier wave by switching its load impedance, and re-radiates a modulated backscatter signal. Due to the cross-polarized alignment, only reflections that undergo polarization reversal—i.e., those interacting with moving objects—are received with high fidelity. The RFID reflection analysis system collects RSSI and phase data from the reader at high sampling rates (e.g., 250 reads per second). To mitigate artifacts from frequency hopping—a standard feature in commercial RFID readers that cycles through 50 channels every 10 seconds—the system performs calibration by recording a 10-second baseline in an empty, static environment. This baseline captures the steady-state RF profile, including multipath effects from walls, furniture, and fixtures. During monitoring, real-time measurements are compared against this model; deviations indicate the presence of moving objects. Specifically, RSSI values are normalized per channel by subtracting the calibrated mean, and phase data are remapped to a reference frequency to eliminate discontinuities. Variations in these corrected signals correlate with human gestures, posture changes, or respiration. The system benefits significantly from circular polarization: it suppresses static clutter, enhances sensitivity to subtle torso movements, and enables large-area coverage with a single tag—demonstrated to increase spatial sensitivity by over 230% compared to linearly polarized tags. Critically, the system requires no device attachment to the user, making it ideal for long-term, unobtrusive monitoring. External publications on spiral antennas and polarization reversal are incorporated by reference for antenna design details. System 100 refers to the core RFID-based remote sensing architecture, comprising an RFID reader device with a circularly polarized antenna, a passive RFID tag with spiral antenna, and a reflection analysis module. The reader’s transmitter emits RHCP waves, which the LHCP tag receives weakly due to polarization mismatch. Upon reflection from a human, the wave becomes LHCP and is strongly received by the RHCP reader. The tag modulates and retransmits the signal, which the reader analyzes for activity signatures. The reflection analysis system includes a calibration subsystem that creates a steady-state model, a comparison engine that computes deviations, and an external interface for alerts or data export. This interface may connect to a local computerized device or a cloud-based server via network infrastructure, enabling remote monitoring and integration with smart home or healthcare platforms. System 200 illustrates physical deployment scenarios: the reader antenna and tag are installed perpendicularly on adjacent or opposing walls, typically 1.2 meters above the floor, to maximize coverage while minimizing direct coupling. Alternative arrangements include ceiling-mounted readers with wall-mounted tags or vice versa, depending on room geometry. The system detects moving objects by analyzing transient changes in RSSI and phase; for example, respiration causes periodic chest expansion, altering the multipath profile. Perpendicular installation ensures optimal cross-polarization isolation. The system is highly flexible: multiple tags can be deployed for multi-zone monitoring, and the analysis algorithms can be customized for specific activities or user profiles. The RFID tag embodiment features a spiral antenna with 13 cm radius, a matching network for 20–145j chip impedance, and an IC for backscatter modulation. The circular-polarized passive RFID tag embodiment specifically uses an Archimedean spiral geometry to achieve broadband CP performance. The method for RFID-based remote sensing using cross circular polarization begins with installing the reader antenna and tag in the environment. The reader emits CP RF waves, which the tag receives and modulates. The tag emits modulated CP waves, which—after potential reflection and polarization flip—are received by the reader. The system analyzes these signals and performs actions such as logging, alerting, or triggering smart devices. A complementary calibration method involves entering a calibration mode, collecting steady-state data over 10 seconds across all frequency channels, creating a baseline model, and transitioning to monitoring mode. During monitoring, the system subtracts the steady-state model from live data, determines phase and RSSI changes due to moving objects, and assesses whether the environment is empty. If drift occurs, the system may re-enter calibration mode.
