# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

Chromosomal translocations represent a critical class of genomic structural variations that underlie numerous genetic disorders and malignancies. These rearrangements occur when segments of non-homologous chromosomes are exchanged, potentially disrupting gene function, creating novel fusion genes, or altering regulatory landscapes. One of the most well-characterized examples is the Philadelphia chromosome, a cytogenetic hallmark of chronic myeloid leukemia (CML). The Philadelphia chromosome arises from a balanced reciprocal translocation between chromosomes 9 and 22, designated t(9;22)(q34;q11), which juxtaposes the breakpoint cluster region (BCR) on chromosome 22 with the Abelson murine leukemia viral oncogene homolog 1 (ABL1) on chromosome 9. This translocation results in the formation of the BCR-ABL1 fusion gene, encoding a chimeric protein with constitutive tyrosine kinase activity that drives uncontrolled proliferation of myeloid cells.

Chronic myeloid leukemia is a clonal myeloproliferative disorder with an incidence of approximately 1 to 2 cases per 100,000 individuals annually, accounting for 15% to 20% of adult leukemias. The disease typically presents in a chronic phase characterized by marked leukocytosis and the presence of immature myeloid precursors in the peripheral blood. The BCR-ABL1 fusion protein, resulting from the Philadelphia translocation, is central to the pathogenesis of CML and serves as both a diagnostic marker and a therapeutic target. Breakpoints within the BCR gene are predominantly localized to a 5.8-kilobase region known as the major breakpoint cluster region (M-bcr), spanning exons 12 to 16, and are found in about 90% of CML cases. In contrast, breakpoints in the ABL1 gene occur within a large 90-kilobase intron 1, upstream of the tyrosine kinase domains encoded in exons 2 through 11.

Current diagnostic methods for CML rely heavily on detecting either the Philadelphia chromosome or the BCR-ABL1 fusion transcript. Conventional karyotyping remains the historical gold standard but requires actively dividing cells, typically obtained from bone marrow aspirates cultured for several days to induce metaphase arrest. This process is labor-intensive, time-consuming, and limited by the requirement for viable, proliferating cells. Fluorescent in situ hybridization (FISH) offers an alternative that can be applied to interphase nuclei from peripheral blood, bypassing the need for cell culture. FISH uses fluorescently labeled DNA probes to detect the physical proximity of BCR and ABL1 sequences, thereby identifying the translocation even when it is cytogenetically cryptic. However, neither karyotyping nor FISH yields a molecular biomarker suitable for sensitive, quantitative monitoring of minimal residual disease during therapy.

Real-time reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) provides high sensitivity for detecting BCR-ABL1 fusion transcripts and is widely used for monitoring treatment response in CML patients receiving tyrosine kinase inhibitors such as imatinib. Despite its sensitivity, RT-PCR is inherently dependent on RNA integrity, which is compromised by ubiquitous ribonucleases and suboptimal sample handling. Moreover, transcriptional silencing of the fusion gene—without elimination of the underlying leukemic clone—can yield false-negative results, raising uncertainty about whether molecular remission truly reflects eradication of disease. Studies have demonstrated the persistence of leukemic DNA even when BCR-ABL1 transcripts are undetectable by RT-PCR, underscoring the need for a stable, DNA-based biomarker.

A significant limitation of existing approaches is their inability to precisely map the genomic breakpoints at nucleotide resolution in a routine clinical setting. This lack of precision hinders the development of patient-specific assays for longitudinal monitoring and obscures potential correlations between breakpoint location and clinical outcomes. Furthermore, genetic heterogeneity among CML patients—with variations in exact breakpoint positions—complicates standardized assay design. There exists a clear and pressing need for a robust, scalable method capable of identifying chromosomal translocation breakpoints directly at the DNA level, producing personalized biomarkers that are stable, specific, and amenable to high-sensitivity detection in diverse sample types, including archived tissues and liquid biopsies. Such a method would address the shortcomings of current techniques by combining the genomic specificity of cytogenetics with the molecular precision and sensitivity required for modern precision oncology.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention introduces a novel method termed Anchored Chromosomal Paired-End Tag (Anchored ChromPET) sequencing, designed for the high-resolution detection, characterization, and monitoring of chromosomal structural variations, particularly translocations associated with genetic diseases such as chronic myeloid leukemia. This method integrates targeted enrichment, next-generation sequencing, and advanced bioinformatics to identify precise DNA breakpoints with unprecedented accuracy and efficiency. The core innovation lies in the selective capture of genomic regions of interest—such as the major breakpoint cluster region (M-bcr) of the BCR gene—using custom-designed RNA baits, followed by paired-end sequencing of enriched DNA fragments to interrogate structural rearrangements.

The Anchored ChromPET method begins with the construction of a chromosomal paired-end tag (chromPET) library from genomic DNA. This involves fragmentation of DNA, end repair, addition of adenine overhangs, and ligation of specialized Y-shaped adapters that incorporate unique molecular barcodes. These barcodes enable multiplexing of multiple patient samples within a single sequencing lane, significantly reducing cost and increasing throughput. The library is then hybridized to biotinylated RNA baits complementary to the target genomic region—in this case, the M-bcr locus. Hybridization allows selective enrichment of DNA fragments containing sequences from the region of interest, including those that span translocation junctions. Streptavidin-coated magnetic beads are used to isolate the bait-bound DNA fragments, which are subsequently eluted, converted to double-stranded DNA, and subjected to high-throughput paired-end sequencing.

A key advantage of the invention is its ability to produce patient-specific DNA biomarkers. By precisely mapping the genomic coordinates of translocation breakpoints at single-nucleotide resolution, the method enables the rational design of PCR primers that flank the unique junctional sequence. This junctional fragment serves as a stable, clonal DNA biomarker that is not subject to the variability of RNA expression or degradation. Unlike RNA-based biomarkers, DNA is inherently more stable in clinical specimens, including formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissues and cell-free DNA in serum, making it superior for retrospective analysis and liquid biopsy applications.

The invention further encompasses a sophisticated bioinformatics pipeline for analyzing sequencing data. Paired-end reads are demultiplexed using the embedded barcodes and mapped to reference sequences of the target loci (e.g., BCR and ABL1). Normal chromPETs—those mapping within the same locus—are distinguished from junctional chromPETs, which span two different genomic regions indicative of a translocation. An innovative algorithm then predicts the most probable breakpoint location by aggregating positional votes from each junctional chromPET based on empirical fragment length distributions derived from normal chromPETs. This computational approach enhances accuracy even with low-abundance junctional events.

The Anchored ChromPET technology is not limited to human diagnostics; it is equally applicable to the study of chromosomal rearrangements in animal models and microorganisms, facilitating research into genome instability and evolution. The method’s reliance on DNA, rather than RNA or cell culture, makes it broadly deployable across sample types, including non-dividing cells and archived materials. Additionally, the invention includes kits comprising all necessary reagents—such as barcoded adapters, RNA baits, streptavidin beads, and PCR primers—for performing the Anchored ChromPET assay. These kits streamline implementation in clinical and research laboratories, enabling rapid identification of structural variants for diagnostic, prognostic, and therapeutic monitoring purposes. In summary, the invention provides a comprehensive, high-resolution, DNA-based platform for detecting and tracking disease-associated chromosomal translocations with superior sensitivity, specificity, and practicality compared to existing methodologies.

## EMBODIMENTS

Chromosomal translocations are a major driver of oncogenesis, necessitating precise detection methods. Paired-end mapping techniques offer a powerful solution by capturing the ends of DNA fragments and determining their genomic origins. The basic principle of paired-end-tag (PET) sequencing involves generating short sequence reads from both ends of size-selected DNA fragments. When these PETs are mapped to a reference genome, concordant mappings—where the distance and orientation match expected parameters—indicate normal genomic architecture, whereas discordant mappings reveal structural variations such as translocations, deletions, or insertions.

In the context of yeast genome analysis, the ChromPET technique was adapted to study gross chromosomal rearrangements in Saccharomyces cerevisiae. This motivated a global approach for identifying chromosomal structural variation without prior knowledge of breakpoints. The system involved constructing a ChromPET library from fragmented genomic DNA, ligating adapters, and performing paired-end sequencing. Bioinformatic analysis began by extracting flanking sequences and identifying unique ChromPETs that map unambiguously to the yeast genome. Aberrant ChromPETs—those with unexpected orientations or distances—were flagged for further investigation. To generate an aberrant ChromPET profile, chimera products arising from ligation artifacts were excluded through stringent filtering. The analysis addressed challenges posed by the short length of tags and repetitive sequences by requiring multiple independent paired-end tags supporting the same structural variant. Tag density was calculated across genomic windows, and regions dense in abnormal tags were identified as candidate breakpoints. Lowering statistical cutoffs yielded a refined list of windows, and partner windows were tabulated to reconstruct translocation events. Abnormal linkages were considered, and thresholds were relaxed to capture low-frequency events, followed by application of a new cutoff to balance sensitivity and specificity.

This framework was extended to analyze specific aberrations: insertions were detected by clusters of PETs mapping to a single locus with one end unmapped; deletions were inferred from PETs spanning larger-than-expected distances. In validation experiments, a translocation in chromosome V reporter region, a HIS3 insertion in the XRS2 coding region, and Ty element insertions were successfully detected. PCR confirmation verified Ty element insertions, demonstrating the method’s reliability. To focus on clinically relevant translocations like BCR-ABL1, the ChromPET method was modified into Anchored ChromPET. An area of interest—such as the M-bcr—was selected using array CGH or prior knowledge. This region was purified by PCR and converted into a biotinylated RNA bait, with repetitive DNA eliminated using Cot-1 DNA. The bait was hybridized to test genomic DNA, and double-stranded DNA hybrids were captured on streptavidin-coated nanochips. Trapped DNA was eluted, ligated to sequencing adaptors, and subjected to paired-end sequencing. Aberrant links were analyzed to identify translocation junctions, and PCR primers were designed based on these findings to amplify junctional fragments from test DNA. Comparison across samples enabled biomarker identification for diagnosing associated diseases. Kits were developed for identification and diagnosis, with primers tailored to cancer-specific breakpoints. This approach supports personalized medicine by enabling patient-specific monitoring. The method is broadly applicable to any disease involving chromosomal structural variations. Numerical ranges—for example, fragment sizes of 300–700 bp or sequencing depths of 10,000–1,000,000 reads—are optimized for sensitivity. A comprehensive kit includes baits, adapters, enzymes, beads, and primers for end-to-end analysis.

## CONCLUSIONS

The detection of the BCR-ABL1 fusion gene is paramount for the diagnosis and management of chronic myeloid leukemia. While conventional cytogenetic methods like karyotyping and FISH provide valuable information, they lack the resolution and molecular specificity needed for sensitive monitoring. Paired-end sequencing offers a powerful alternative, enabling high-resolution, digital karyotyping with superior sensitivity and scalability. The Anchored ChromPET approach described herein overcomes the cost and complexity barriers of whole-genome sequencing by focusing on targeted regions of interest. By combining selective enrichment, barcoded multiplexing, and advanced bioinformatics, this method delivers precise breakpoint identification at the DNA level, yielding stable, patient-specific biomarkers. These biomarkers outperform RNA-based counterparts in challenging sample types, including FFPE tissues and liquid biopsies, and enable reliable detection of minimal residual disease. The Anchored ChromPET platform thus represents a transformative advance in the molecular diagnosis and longitudinal monitoring of translocation-driven diseases, with broad applicability across oncology and genetic medicine.
