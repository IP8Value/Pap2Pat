# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD OF INVENTION

Metabolic disorder diseases represent a broad category of conditions characterized by disruptions in normal biochemical processes that regulate metabolism, including the utilization and storage of carbohydrates, lipids, and proteins. Among these disorders, type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) stands out as a particularly prevalent and debilitating condition affecting millions worldwide. T2DM is primarily defined by chronic hyperglycemia resulting from a combination of insulin resistance in peripheral tissues—such as skeletal muscle, liver, and adipose tissue—and an inadequate compensatory response by pancreatic β-cells to secrete sufficient insulin. In the early stages of the disease, insulin resistance leads to impaired glucose uptake despite elevated circulating insulin levels; over time, β-cell dysfunction exacerbates the condition, culminating in progressive hyperglycemia and associated metabolic complications.

Insulin resistance fundamentally impairs the ability of insulin-sensitive tissues to respond appropriately to physiological concentrations of insulin, thereby disrupting glucose homeostasis. Under normal conditions, insulin binding to its receptor initiates a signaling cascade that culminates in the translocation of glucose transporter type 4 (GLUT4) to the plasma membrane, facilitating cellular glucose uptake. In insulin-resistant states, this pathway is blunted, leading to reduced glucose disposal and persistent elevation of blood glucose levels. This pathophysiological mechanism underlies many of the clinical manifestations of T2DM, including polyuria, polydipsia, fatigue, and long-term microvascular and macrovascular complications such as nephropathy, retinopathy, neuropathy, and cardiovascular disease.

Adiponectin, an adipokine secreted predominantly by adipose tissue, plays a critical role in modulating insulin sensitivity and lipid metabolism. Circulating adiponectin levels are inversely correlated with body fat mass and are typically reduced in individuals with obesity and T2DM. Adiponectin enhances insulin sensitivity through activation of AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK) and peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor alpha (PPARα) pathways, promoting fatty acid oxidation and glucose uptake in muscle and liver. Consequently, strategies aimed at increasing adiponectin secretion or activity have emerged as promising therapeutic avenues for managing insulin resistance and related metabolic disturbances.

Despite the availability of several pharmacological agents for treating T2DM—including sulfonylureas, biguanides (e.g., metformin), thiazolidinediones, meglitinides, and α-glucosidase inhibitors—current therapies are often limited by adverse side effects and narrow mechanisms of action. For instance, sulfonylureas can induce hypoglycemia and weight gain, while metformin may cause gastrointestinal distress and, rarely, lactic acidosis. Thiazolidinediones, although effective in improving insulin sensitivity via PPARγ agonism, carry risks of hepatotoxicity and fluid retention. Moreover, most existing drugs target only one aspect of the complex pathophysiology of T2DM, necessitating combination therapy to achieve adequate glycemic control—a practice that further increases the risk of cumulative toxicity and drug interactions.

In light of these limitations, there has been renewed interest in ethnobotanicals—plant-derived compounds traditionally used in folk medicine—as potential sources of novel antidiabetic agents. Ethnopharmacological approaches offer the advantage of leveraging centuries of empirical knowledge while providing structurally diverse bioactive molecules that may act through multiple complementary mechanisms. Among the numerous plants investigated for their antidiabetic properties, *Curculigo latifolia* (C. latifolia), a shrub native to Southeast Asia and commonly known as Lemba in Malaysia, has garnered attention due to its reported sweet-tasting fruits and preliminary evidence of antioxidant and metabolic-modulating activities. Although C. latifolia has historically been used as a natural sweetener suitable for diabetic patients, scientific validation of its therapeutic potential in metabolic disorders remains largely unexplored until recently.

## SUMMARY OF INVENTION

The present invention provides compositions comprising extracts derived from *Curculigo latifolia*, specifically from its fruit and root, for use in the treatment and management of metabolic disorders, particularly type 2 diabetes mellitus. These extracts demonstrate significant antidiabetic and hypolipidemic effects by ameliorating hyperglycemia, enhancing insulin and adiponectin secretion, improving glucose uptake in insulin-sensitive tissues, and modulating key genes involved in glucose and lipid metabolism. The invention encompasses methods of administering said extracts to subjects in need thereof, either alone or in combination with conventional antidiabetic agents, to achieve improved glycemic control and mitigate diabetic complications without the adverse effects commonly associated with synthetic pharmaceuticals.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE PREFERRED EMBODIMENTS

The invention relates to a method for ameliorating elevated blood glucose levels in a subject suffering from or at risk of developing a metabolic disorder, particularly type 2 diabetes mellitus. This method comprises administering to the subject a therapeutically effective amount of an extract prepared from *Curculigo latifolia*, preferably a mixture of fruit and root extracts in a 1:1 ratio. The administration may be oral, and the dosage may range from 50 mg/kg to 200 mg/kg body weight per day, based on preclinical data demonstrating dose-dependent reductions in fasting blood glucose and improvements in insulin sensitivity.

In vitro studies were conducted using established cell lines, including 3T3-L1 adipocytes and L6 myotubes, to evaluate the cytotoxicity and biological activity of C. latifolia extracts. Cell viability assays confirmed that the extracts exhibited no significant cytotoxic effects at concentrations up to 100 µg/mL, indicating a favorable safety profile. Furthermore, the extracts significantly enhanced both basal and insulin-stimulated glucose uptake in these cell models, suggesting dual mechanisms of action involving insulin sensitization and insulin-mimetic activity.

In vivo studies employed a well-characterized rat model of type 2 diabetes induced by a high-fat diet (HFD) combined with a low-dose streptozotocin (STZ) injection. This model recapitulates key features of human T2DM, including insulin resistance, impaired insulin secretion, hyperglycemia, and dyslipidemia. Diabetic rats treated with C. latifolia fruit:root extract showed marked reductions in fasting blood glucose levels, with the highest dose (200 mg/kg) achieving a 64.5% decrease compared to untreated diabetic controls. Concurrently, oral glucose tolerance tests revealed improved glucose clearance kinetics in treated animals, underscoring the extract’s efficacy in restoring glucose homeostasis.

Monitoring of metabolic parameters included regular assessment of fasting blood glucose, insulin, adiponectin, and lipid profiles throughout the treatment period. Notably, C. latifolia extract not only lowered glucose but also corrected dyslipidemia by reducing total cholesterol, triglycerides, and low-density lipoprotein (LDL) while elevating high-density lipoprotein (HDL). Additionally, plasma markers of hepatic and renal function—such as alanine aminotransferase (ALT), γ-glutamyltransferase (GGT), urea, and creatinine—were normalized following treatment, indicating protective effects against diabetic complications.

The envisioned benefits of C. latifolia extracts extend beyond glycemic control to include systemic metabolic improvements, reduced oxidative stress, and preservation of organ function. By simultaneously targeting multiple nodes in the metabolic network—including insulin signaling, adipokine regulation, and lipid metabolism—the invention offers a holistic approach to managing T2DM that addresses both primary symptoms and secondary complications.

### Example 1

To prepare the C. latifolia fruit extract, fresh fruits were harvested, cleaned, and mashed using a mortar and pestle. The mashed material was then extracted with distilled water (40 mL per gram of fruit) under continuous stirring at room temperature for 24 hours. The resulting slurry was filtered through Whatman No. 1 filter paper, and the filtrate was lyophilized to yield a dry powder stored at −80°C. Similarly, dried roots were ground into a fine powder and subjected to aqueous extraction under identical conditions. Both fruit and root extracts were subsequently mixed in a 1:1 (w/w) ratio to form the final composition used in all experimental and therapeutic applications described herein.

### Example 2

Cell culture experiments utilized 3T3-L1 preadipocytes and L6 rat skeletal muscle myoblasts maintained in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and antibiotics. Cells were differentiated into mature adipocytes or myotubes according to standard protocols. A cell viability assay was performed using the MTT reduction method after 24-hour exposure to varying concentrations of C. latifolia extracts. Cell counting and morphological differentiation were assessed microscopically. Results demonstrated that the extracts did not impair cell viability at concentrations up to 100 µg/mL and significantly enhanced differentiation markers, supporting their safety and bioactivity in relevant metabolic cell types.

### Example 3

Insulin secretion was evaluated in INS-1 pancreatic β-cells exposed to C. latifolia extracts under both basal and glucose-stimulated conditions. After 24 hours of treatment, insulin content in the culture supernatant was quantified using a rat-specific ELISA kit. The extract significantly potentiated glucose-induced insulin secretion in a dose-dependent manner, with maximal stimulation observed at 50 µg/mL. This effect suggests that C. latifolia components may protect or enhance β-cell function, contributing to improved systemic insulin availability.

### Example 4

Glucose uptake activity was measured in differentiated 3T3-L1 adipocytes and L6 myotubes using a fluorescent 2-deoxyglucose analog. Cells were serum-starved and then treated with C. latifolia extracts in the presence or absence of insulin. Fluorescence intensity, proportional to glucose uptake, was quantified by flow cytometry. The extract significantly increased both basal and insulin-mediated glucose uptake, with synergistic effects observed when co-administered with submaximal insulin doses. These findings confirm the extract’s capacity to improve cellular glucose disposal through insulin-sensitizing and insulin-mimetic mechanisms.

### Example 5

Adiponectin secretion was assessed in 3T3-L1 adipocytes following 24-hour treatment with C. latifolia extracts. Culture media were collected and analyzed using a commercial rat adiponectin ELISA kit. Treatment with the extract resulted in a significant, dose-dependent increase in adiponectin levels, with the highest concentration (100 µg/mL) inducing a 2.5-fold elevation compared to untreated controls. This upregulation of adiponectin provides a mechanistic basis for the observed improvements in insulin sensitivity and lipid metabolism.

### Example 6

An animal model of type 2 diabetes was established in male Sprague-Dawley rats by feeding a high-fat diet (comprising 50% normal chow, 24% corn oil, 20% full-cream milk powder, and 6% sugar) for four weeks, followed by a single intravenous injection of streptozotocin (40 mg/kg). Diabetic rats (fasting glucose >170 mg/dL) were randomized into treatment groups receiving daily oral gavage of C. latifolia fruit:root extract at 50, 100, or 200 mg/kg, or glibenclamide (10 mg/kg) as a positive control, for four weeks. Oral glucose tolerance tests were performed at baseline and endpoint. At sacrifice, blood and tissues were collected for biochemical and gene expression analyses. Treatment with C. latifolia extract significantly reduced fasting glucose, improved glucose tolerance, normalized lipid profiles, increased insulin and adiponectin levels, and upregulated expression of key metabolic genes—including IRS1, GLUT4, PPARγ, PPARα, AdipoR1, AdipoR2, LPL, and leptin—in adipose and muscle tissues, confirming its multifaceted antidiabetic and hypolipidemic actions.
