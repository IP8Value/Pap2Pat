# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates to the field of oncology, specifically to methods and compositions for classifying patients diagnosed with or suspected of having breast cancer based on gene expression profiles derived from tumor-associated stroma. The invention provides novel prognostic tools that enable the stratification of breast cancer patients into distinct clinical outcome groups by analyzing the transcriptional activity of a defined set of stromal genes. These methods facilitate more accurate prediction of disease recurrence, metastasis, and overall survival, thereby supporting personalized treatment decisions. The invention further encompasses diagnostic kits, nucleic acid compositions, polypeptide binding agents, microarrays, and computational systems designed to implement the disclosed stromal-derived prognostic predictor (SDPP) for clinical use.

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

Breast cancer remains one of the most prevalent malignancies among women worldwide, with significant heterogeneity in clinical behavior and therapeutic response. Despite advances in surgical techniques, radiation therapy, chemotherapy, and targeted biological agents, approximately 26% of patients diagnosed with early-stage breast cancer eventually develop metastatic disease and succumb to their illness. Current clinical parameters—such as tumor size, lymph node involvement, histological grade, and receptor status (estrogen receptor [ER], progesterone receptor [PR], and human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 [HER2])—provide only limited prognostic accuracy. Molecular profiling approaches, including gene expression signatures derived from whole tumor tissue or purified epithelial compartments, have improved risk stratification but often fail to account for the critical role of the tumor microenvironment in disease progression.

Recent genomic characterization of breast tumors has led to the identification of intrinsic molecular subtypes—luminal A, luminal B, HER2-enriched, basal-like, and normal-like—each associated with distinct clinical outcomes. However, these classifications primarily reflect epithelial cell biology and may overlook contributions from stromal components, which constitute the majority of tumor mass in many cases. The tumor stroma, comprising fibroblasts, immune cells, endothelial cells, and extracellular matrix, actively participates in carcinogenesis through modulation of angiogenesis, immune evasion, hypoxia responses, and extracellular remodeling. Yet, comprehensive gene expression analyses of morphologically normal or tumor-adjacent stroma have been scarce, largely due to technical challenges in isolating pure stromal populations from heterogeneous tissue sections.

A major unmet need persists for robust, stroma-informed prognostic methods capable of predicting cancer recurrence and metastasis with high specificity and sensitivity. Existing signatures often derive from bulk tumor analyses, conflating epithelial and stromal signals, or rely on cultured stromal cells that may not reflect in vivo biology. Consequently, there is a pressing demand for a clinically applicable predictor that leverages the unique transcriptional landscape of tumor-associated stroma to enhance outcome prediction beyond conventional markers.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention introduces a stroma-derived prognostic predictor (SDPP) developed through laser capture microdissection (LCM) to isolate tumor-associated stroma and matched normal stroma from breast cancer patients. Microarray analyses of these purified stromal samples identified a gene expression signature predictive of clinical outcome. This SDPP comprises a defined set of genes involved in pro-angiogenic processes, hypoxia responses, and T-cell-mediated immunity. The invention provides a method for identifying this gene expression signature by comparing stromal transcriptomes from patients with divergent clinical courses. It further discloses a method for predicting clinical outcome in a breast cancer patient by quantifying the expression levels of SDPP genes in a stromal sample obtained from the patient and applying a multivariate algorithm to generate a prognostic score.

The invention includes methods for determining prognosis, predicting disease outcome, diagnosing poor-prognosis breast cancer, estimating the probability of cancer recurrence or metastasis, and classifying tumor subtypes based on stromal gene expression. It enables assignment of patients to specific therapeutic regimens or clinical trials by integrating SDPP results with other clinical or molecular data. Treatment optimization and monitoring are facilitated by tracking changes in SDPP gene expression during therapy. The SDPP can be combined with existing predictors—such as the 70-gene signature or wound-response signatures—to improve overall predictive accuracy. The invention also encompasses compositions comprising nucleic acid sequences corresponding to SDPP genes, binding agents such as antibodies against SDPP-encoded polypeptides, diagnostic kits, expression arrays, and computer-implemented systems for executing the predictive algorithms.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

The invention centers on a breast cancer outcome predictor derived exclusively from tumor-associated stroma, addressing a critical gap in current prognostic methodologies that predominantly focus on epithelial compartments. The motivation for a stroma-derived predictor arises from growing evidence that the tumor microenvironment significantly influences disease progression, yet remains underutilized in clinical decision-making. The stroma-derived prognostic predictor (SDPP) is defined as a multivariate algorithm trained on gene expression data from microdissected stromal tissue, yielding a quantitative score correlated with clinical outcomes such as recurrence-free survival and distant metastasis.

The SDPP gene sets consist of transcripts differentially expressed between stromal samples from patients with good versus poor outcomes. These include pro-angiogenic factors (e.g., VEGF pathway components), hypoxia-inducible genes (e.g., HIF1α targets), and T-cell markers (e.g., CD3Z, CD8). Gene set selection was performed using rigorous statistical criteria, including false discovery rate correction, fold-change thresholds, and association with clinical endpoints via Cox regression. Example SDPP gene sets include THC2436642, A—24_P82805, ENST00000246228, and THC2269172, which encode novel polypeptides correlating strongly with disease outcome. The accuracy of SDPP gene sets has been validated in multiple independent cohorts, demonstrating superior or complementary performance relative to established signatures.

Clinical outcome is defined as recurrence, metastasis, or death within a defined follow-up period. Expression levels of SDPP genes are measured in RNA extracted from stromal tissue isolated via LCM, using platforms such as quantitative PCR or microarrays. A reference expression profile is established from training cohorts with known outcomes, enabling comparison with new patient samples. Acceptable sample types include fresh-frozen or optimally preserved archival tissue, provided stromal purity is confirmed histologically.

Class discovery was initiated by unsupervised clustering of stromal gene expression data, revealing subtypes associated with outcome. Class distinction employed differential expression analysis to identify genes separating these subtypes. Class prediction was implemented via supervised machine learning, notably multivariate naive Bayes classifiers, trained to assign new samples to prognostic categories. Accurate prediction is motivated by the need to spare low-risk patients from overtreatment while intensifying therapy for high-risk individuals. Gene weighting within the predictor reflects each gene’s independent prognostic contribution, derived from regression coefficients or information gain metrics.

Alternative methods for predicting outcome include support vector machines, random forests, or neural networks trained on the same gene set. The method for identifying the SDPP gene set involves LCM, RNA amplification, microarray hybridization, statistical filtering, and validation in independent datasets. An alternative identification method substitutes RNA-seq for microarrays. In summary, SDPP gene set identification integrates precise stromal isolation, genome-wide profiling, bioinformatic filtering, and clinical correlation to yield a robust prognostic tool.

### Identifying Classes and Genes for Predicting Clinical Outcome

Class discovery began with microarray experiments on LCM-isolated stromal samples from breast cancer patients. The top 200 most variable genes across samples were selected to reduce noise and highlight biologically relevant variation. Unsupervised hierarchical clustering of tumor stroma using these genes revealed distinct subtypes. Cluster significance was assessed via bootstrap resampling (e.g., pvclust), confirming robust separation. Class discovery thus defines intrinsic stromal subtypes without prior knowledge of clinical outcomes.

Class distinction was introduced to identify genes differentially expressed between discovered classes. Pairwise comparisons using LIMMA or SAM algorithms yielded candidate genes meeting stringent thresholds: FDR-adjusted p ≤ 0.01, fold change ≥2.0, and absolute expression above background. These genes formed the basis of a reference expression profile representing each class. A multivariate predictor was constructed using logistic regression or PAM, trained to distinguish classes based on gene expression. Bayes’ classifiers were trained by estimating prior probabilities and likelihoods from training data, enabling probabilistic outcome assignment.

Class prediction applies the trained predictor to new patient samples. SDPP implements this by inputting stromal gene expression data into the classifier, outputting a predicted class (e.g., “good prognosis” or “poor prognosis”) and associated confidence score. Gene weighting within the predictor ensures that genes with stronger prognostic power contribute more heavily to the final decision. In summary, class prediction transforms molecular measurements into actionable clinical insights through validated computational models.

### Cancers

Breast cancer is a heterogeneous disease encompassing multiple molecular subtypes with divergent behaviors. The invention applies SDPP to classify luminal, HER2-enriched, basal-like, and normal-like subtypes based on stromal gene expression, revealing that basal-like tumors often exhibit stromal profiles associated with poor outcome. SDPP is also applicable to other carcinomas—including ovarian, lung, and colorectal cancers—where stromal interactions drive progression. As used herein, “cancer” refers to malignant neoplasms characterized by uncontrolled cell growth, invasion, and metastatic potential.

### Nucleic Acid Compositions

Nucleic acid compositions of the invention comprise isolated polynucleotides corresponding to SDPP gene sequences or fragments thereof. These may be DNA or RNA, single- or double-stranded, and include cDNA, genomic DNA, and synthetic oligonucleotides. Compositions may be labeled for detection or immobilized on solid supports for array-based assays.

### SDPP Genes and Nucleic Acids

The SDPP gene set is defined as a collection of nucleic acid sequences whose expression in tumor stroma correlates with clinical outcome. Composition includes sequences such as THC2436642, encoding novel polypeptides with prognostic value. Isolated nucleic acids are purified away from native cellular context. Polynucleotide selection considers specificity, sensitivity, and assay compatibility. Hybridization conditions are defined by stringency—modulated via temperature, salt, and denaturant concentration—with Tm calculated based on sequence length and GC content. Products of SDPP genes include mRNA transcripts and encoded polypeptides, both serving as biomarkers. In summary, SDPP gene products provide molecular handles for diagnosis and monitoring.

### Nucleic Acids, Primers and Probes

Isolated nucleic acid sequences serve as templates or targets in diagnostic assays. Primers—short oligonucleotides flanking SDPP gene regions—enable amplification via PCR, particularly multiplex formats detecting multiple genes simultaneously. Probes—labeled oligonucleotides complementary to SDPP transcripts—facilitate hybridization-based detection in microarrays or in situ assays. Probe design ensures specificity through melting temperature matching and avoidance of secondary structures. In summary, primers and probes operationalize SDPP gene detection in clinical laboratories.

### Polypeptide Binding Compositions

Polypeptide products of SDPP genes are detected using binding compositions comprising antibodies or fragments thereof. Isolated polypeptides may serve as immunogens or standards. Binding agents include monoclonal/polyclonal antibodies, scFv fragments, and engineered scaffolds. Antibodies are produced by immunizing hosts with SDPP polypeptides or via phage display. Peptide mimetics—non-peptidic molecules mimicking epitopes—offer stability advantages. Binding agents may be fixed to solid supports (e.g., ELISA plates) for high-throughput screening. In summary, polypeptide binding compositions enable protein-level validation of SDPP predictions.

### Microarrays

Microarrays—comprising DNA or tissue specimens immobilized on chips—detect SDPP gene expression. DNA microarrays use oligonucleotide probes for transcript quantification; tissue microarrays allow spatial protein localization. Array composition includes SDPP gene probes alongside controls. Arrays predict clinical outcome by comparing patient stromal expression to reference profiles. In summary, microarrays provide a multiplexed platform for SDPP implementation.

### Methods of Diagnosis

SDPP gene sets diagnose breast cancer subtypes and predict prognosis, recurrence, and metastasis. “Patient” denotes an individual undergoing evaluation; “diagnosis” includes prognostic assessment. Gene expression is detected via qRT-PCR, microarrays, or RNA-seq. Quantitative multiplex PCR simultaneously measures multiple SDPP transcripts. Protein products are detected using antibodies in IHC or ELISA, with amounts quantified by densitometry or fluorescence. Combining nucleic acid and protein methods enhances accuracy. Integration with other signatures (e.g., Oncotype DX) refines outcome prediction.

### Methods of Assigning or Selecting Treatment

Treatment is assigned based on SDPP-predicted outcome: high-risk patients receive aggressive therapy (e.g., chemotherapy), while low-risk patients avoid unnecessary toxicity. Therapy is tailored to HER2/ER status in conjunction with SDPP. Treatment efficacy is monitored by tracking SDPP gene expression changes post-therapy. Agents are identified by screening compounds that reverse poor-prognosis stromal profiles—e.g., inhibiting hypoxia, Th2 responses, or angiogenesis. Cell culture models using stromal fibroblasts test agent effects. Agents promoting Th1 immunity or inhibiting matrix metalloproteinases are prioritized. In summary, SDPP guides precision oncology from diagnosis through therapeutic monitoring.

## EXAMPLES

### Example 1

Tissue samples from 34 invasive ductal carcinoma patients and 10 reduction mammoplasty donors were processed. Laser capture microdissection isolated tumor-associated and normal stroma. RNA was extracted, amplified, and hybridized to Agilent 44K microarrays. Analysis identified a tumor stroma subtype linked to recurrence and poor outcome. The top 200 most variable genes were used for clustering, revealing three stromal subtypes: good, poor, and mixed outcome. Clusters significantly associated with recurrence (p < 0.001). Differentially expressed genes included angiogenic factors (e.g., VEGFA), hypoxia markers (e.g., CA9), and T-cell genes (e.g., CD3D). A logistic regression predictor was constructed and evaluated via cross-validation, achieving 85% accuracy. Gene ontology analysis showed poor-outcome stroma enriched for macrophage chemoattractants and matrix remodeling enzymes; good-outcome stroma showed Th1 immune activation. qRT-PCR validated key genes (e.g., CD8A, MMP9). Immunohistochemistry confirmed elevated CD31 (angiogenesis) and reduced CD8+ T-cells in poor-outcome stroma. The SDPP classifier outperformed standard clinical factors in multivariate Cox regression and remained significant when combined with the 70-gene signature. Biological interpretation highlighted integrated dysregulation of immunity, hypoxia, and angiogenesis in aggressive disease.

### Example 2

Multiple predictors—including SDPP, wound-response, and hypoxia signatures—were integrated into a unified Bayes’ classifier. Posterior probabilities of recurrence were estimated by combining likelihoods from each predictor. SDPP added significant predictive value (p = 0.003) beyond existing models, increasing overall accuracy by 12%. This demonstrates that stromal biology captures complementary information to epithelial-centric signatures, necessitating an integrative approach for optimal prognostication.

### Example 3

Independent validation used samples from a second cohort. LCM, RNA isolation, and microarray hybridization followed identical protocols. A stroma subtype associated with recurrence was again identified. Differentially expressed genes overlapped significantly with Example 1 (e.g., ENST00000246228). A logistic regression predictor achieved 82% cross-validated accuracy. Gene ontology confirmed enrichment for immune and angiogenic pathways. Protein validation by IHC and gene validation by qRT-PCR corroborated microarray findings. SDPP performance remained robust across tissue processing variations.

### Example 4

A third cohort underwent LCM and microarray analysis. Stromal—but not epithelial—gene expression predicted recurrence, underscoring stroma-specific prognostic power. The predictor achieved 79% accuracy in stroma versus 52% in epithelium. IHC validated protein expression differences. Public dataset testing (n=295) confirmed SDPP’s independent prognostic value (HR=2.4, p=0.001). Survival analysis showed clear separation by SDPP class (p=4.89e-4). Cox regression confirmed independence from ER status. Poor-outcome stroma exhibited elevated angiogenic factors (e.g., ANGPT2) and diminished T-cell markers (e.g., CD3E). The combination of these features—not either alone—drove prognostic power, highlighting the multifactorial nature of stromal influence on outcome.
