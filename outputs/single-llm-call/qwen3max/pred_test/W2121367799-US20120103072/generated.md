## TECHNICAL FIELD

The present invention relates generally to the field of clinical diagnostics and prognostics, and more specifically to methods for predicting overall survival and mortality risk in maintenance hemodialysis (MHD) patients through the measurement and analysis of lipoprotein particle properties, including low-density lipoprotein (LDL) particle diameter and subfraction concentrations. The invention further pertains to the use of ion mobility analysis as a high-resolution technique for quantifying lipoprotein particle characteristics that serve as superior biomarkers for cardiovascular and all-cause mortality in end-stage renal disease populations, where conventional lipid measures such as LDL cholesterol (LDL-C) and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) fail to provide reliable prognostic information.

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

End-stage renal disease (ESRD) represents a terminal phase of chronic kidney disease requiring life-sustaining renal replacement therapy, most commonly in the form of maintenance hemodialysis. In the United States alone, over 400,000 individuals currently undergo hemodialysis, a number that continues to rise annually. Despite advances in dialysis technology and supportive care, the prognosis for these patients remains poor, with approximately two-thirds succumbing within five years of initiating treatment—a survival rate inferior to that of many common cancers. Cardiovascular disease (CVD) accounts for nearly half of all deaths in this population, underscoring the critical need for accurate risk stratification tools.

Hemodialysis treatment involves the periodic removal of metabolic waste products, excess fluid, and electrolytes from the blood via an extracorporeal circuit. While effective in sustaining life, this intervention does not fully correct the profound metabolic, inflammatory, and vascular derangements associated with ESRD. Consequently, traditional cardiovascular risk factors derived from the general population often exhibit paradoxical or attenuated associations with outcomes in dialysis patients. Notably, the well-established relationship between elevated serum cholesterol levels and increased CVD risk is reversed or absent in this cohort—a phenomenon termed the “lipid paradox” or “reverse epidemiology.” In fact, lower levels of total cholesterol and LDL-C are frequently associated with higher mortality, likely reflecting the confounding influence of malnutrition, inflammation, and protein-energy wasting rather than true cardioprotective effects of hypocholesterolemia.

Given these limitations, there is an urgent need for alternative biomarkers that more accurately reflect atherogenic potential and overall mortality risk in maintenance hemodialysis patients. Conventional lipid panels, which report only bulk chemical concentrations of cholesterol within lipoprotein classes, fail to capture critical structural and functional heterogeneity among lipoprotein particles. Lipoproteins exist as a continuum of subclasses varying in size, density, composition, and biological activity. Low-density lipoprotein (LDL), for instance, comprises very small, small, medium, and large subfractions, each with distinct metabolic fates and pathophysiological roles. Very small, dense LDL particles are highly atherogenic due to prolonged circulation time, enhanced endothelial penetration, and increased susceptibility to oxidation, whereas larger, buoyant LDL particles are less atherogenic and may even be protective in certain contexts.

Similarly, high-density lipoprotein (HDL) is not a uniform entity but consists of functionally diverse subclasses involved in reverse cholesterol transport, anti-inflammatory signaling, and antioxidant defense. Intermediate-density lipoprotein (IDL) and very-low-density lipoprotein (VLDL) also contribute to cardiovascular risk through triglyceride-rich remnant accumulation. Traditional assays that measure only total cholesterol content within these classes obscure these critical distinctions and are therefore inadequate for risk prediction in complex, chronically ill populations such as those with ESRD. Thus, novel methodologies capable of resolving lipoprotein particle size, concentration, and subclass distribution are essential to advance precision prognostication and guide targeted therapeutic interventions in hemodialysis patients.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a method for predicting overall survival and mortality risk in maintenance hemodialysis (MHD) patients by measuring specific lipoprotein particle properties that have been discovered to be strongly and independently associated with clinical outcomes. In one aspect, the method comprises measuring the LDL particle diameter (LDL-Pd) in a biological sample obtained from an MHD patient, comparing the measured LDL-Pd to a reference level, and predicting an increased or decreased mortality risk based on this comparison. Specifically, a smaller LDL-Pd is indicative of higher mortality risk, whereas a larger LDL-Pd correlates with improved survival.

In another aspect, the invention involves measuring the concentration of very small LDL particles (vs-LDL-Pc) in a patient sample, comparing this concentration to a reference vs-LDL concentration, and identifying an increased mortality risk when the measured concentration exceeds the reference. Conversely, the method includes measuring the concentration of large LDL particles (l-LDL-Pc), comparing it to a reference l-LDL concentration, and identifying a reduced mortality risk when the measured concentration is above the reference level.

In a further embodiment, the method entails simultaneously measuring both vs-LDL-Pc and l-LDL-Pc concentrations, comparing each to their respective reference concentrations, and integrating these values to generate a composite mortality risk assessment. This dual-parameter approach enhances predictive accuracy by capturing the opposing prognostic influences of atherogenic versus benign LDL subfractions.

Additionally, the invention encompasses methods that incorporate LDL particle size or HDL concentration as supplementary or alternative biomarkers. For instance, HDL particle concentration—particularly total HDL-Pc—may be evaluated in conjunction with LDL parameters to refine risk stratification, although its independent prognostic value may vary depending on clinical context. The core innovation lies in the recognition that particle-based metrics, rather than cholesterol mass, provide superior prognostic information in MHD patients, thereby overcoming the limitations of the lipid paradox and enabling more reliable identification of high-risk individuals who may benefit from intensified monitoring or novel therapeutic strategies.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

### Introduction to Maintenance Hemodialysis Patients and Lipoprotein-Mortality Associations

Maintenance hemodialysis (MHD) patients represent a unique clinical population characterized by accelerated atherosclerosis, chronic inflammation, protein-energy wasting, and dysregulated lipid metabolism. Unlike the general population, where elevated LDL cholesterol is a well-established risk factor for cardiovascular events, MHD patients exhibit a dissociation between conventional lipid measures and mortality outcomes. This paradox necessitates the development of alternative biomarkers that reflect the true atherogenic burden. The present invention is grounded in the discovery that specific physical and quantitative properties of lipoprotein particles—namely LDL particle diameter and subfraction concentrations—are robust predictors of overall survival in this high-risk group. These properties are measurable using advanced analytical techniques such as ion mobility analysis and provide clinically actionable insights independent of traditional lipid panels, nutritional status, or inflammatory markers.

### Definitions

As used herein, the singular forms “a,” “an,” and “the” include plural referents unless the context clearly dictates otherwise. The term “about” means plus or minus 10% of the indicated value, unless otherwise specified. A “biomarker” or “LDL property” refers to any measurable characteristic of LDL particles, including but not limited to particle diameter, concentration of specific subfractions (e.g., very small, large), or ratios thereof, that correlates with clinical outcomes. “Clinical factors” encompass demographic variables (age, sex, race), comorbidities (diabetes, cardiovascular disease), dialysis vintage, laboratory parameters (albumin, CRP, IL-6), and nutritional indices. “Comparable” or “corresponding” denotes similarity in clinical context, such as matching for age, sex, or disease severity. A “control population” is a cohort of MHD patients used to establish reference ranges for lipoprotein properties, typically derived from the same geographic and clinical setting as the test subject.

“Determining,” “measuring,” “assessing,” and “assaying” are used interchangeably to describe the quantitative or qualitative evaluation of a biomarker using validated analytical methods. “Diagnosis” refers to the identification of a disease state, whereas “prognosis” denotes the prediction of future clinical outcomes, such as mortality risk or survival time. A “difference of the level” or “difference in properties” signifies a statistically significant deviation from a reference value. “Ion mobility analysis” (IMA) is a high-resolution technique that separates and counts individual lipoprotein particles based on their size-to-charge ratio in the gas phase, enabling precise determination of particle diameter and concentration across subfractions.

A “lipoprotein” or “lipoprotein particle” is a macromolecular complex comprising a hydrophobic core of triglycerides and cholesteryl esters surrounded by a phospholipid monolayer and apolipoproteins; classes include VLDL (very-low-density lipoprotein), IDL (intermediate-density lipoprotein), LDL (low-density lipoprotein), and HDL (high-density lipoprotein), each defined by characteristic size and density ranges. A “population” refers to a defined group of subjects, such as MHD patients enrolled in a clinical study. “Overall survival” (OS) is the length of time from sample collection or study enrollment until death from any cause. A “reference level” is a threshold value—such as a median, quartile, or population mean—against which a patient’s biomarker level is compared to infer risk. A “sample” or “test sample” is a biological specimen (e.g., plasma or serum) obtained from a subject for analysis. A “subject” is a human patient undergoing maintenance hemodialysis. “Substantially the same as” indicates functional or clinical equivalence within acceptable variance. “Treating,” “treatment,” or “alleviation” refers to any intervention aimed at improving prognosis, though the present invention is primarily diagnostic and does not require therapeutic action.

These definitions collectively establish the conceptual and operational framework for implementing the prognostic methods disclosed herein.

### Sample Collection, Preparation, and Purification

Biological samples for lipoprotein analysis are obtained from maintenance hemodialysis patients during routine clinical visits, preferably in the fasting state and on a mid-week dialysis day to minimize diurnal and procedural variability. Blood is drawn predialysis into standard anticoagulant tubes (e.g., EDTA), and plasma or serum is separated by centrifugation within two hours of collection. Sample preparation involves the removal of non-lipoprotein components to isolate intact lipoprotein particles. This is achieved through sequential ultracentrifugation steps under controlled conditions: typically, samples are subjected to centrifugal forces ranging from 100,000 to 500,000 × g for durations of 16 to 72 hours at temperatures between 4°C and 10°C, using density gradient media such as potassium bromide solutions adjusted to specific densities corresponding to VLDL (<1.006 g/mL), LDL (1.019–1.063 g/mL), and HDL (1.063–1.21 g/mL). Following ultracentrifugation, the LDL-containing fraction is carefully extracted and may undergo additional purification steps, including dialysis against physiological buffers to remove residual salts and small molecules that could interfere with downstream analysis.

Sample dilution is performed as needed to bring lipoprotein concentrations within the dynamic range of the analytical instrument. The purified LDL fraction is then analyzed by ion mobility analysis (IMA), a technique that aerosolizes the sample into charged droplets, desolvates them to produce gas-phase ions, and separates these ions based on their collision cross-section as they drift through an electric field in a buffer gas. The resulting mobility spectrum is converted into particle size distributions using calibration standards, allowing precise determination of LDL particle diameter (LDL-Pd) and concentration of subfractions defined by specific diameter ranges: very small LDL (180.0–208.2 Å), small LDL (208.2–214.1 Å), medium LDL (214.1–220.0 Å), and large LDL (220.0–233.3 Å). The IMA apparatus comprises a nebulizer, desolvation chamber, drift tube, and particle detector, all integrated into a controlled environment to ensure reproducibility. This high-resolution method directly counts individual particles, providing absolute concentrations (e.g., in nmol/L) and mean diameters with interassay coefficients of variation below 1% for major subfractions.

### Determining Prognosis

Prognosis, as used herein, refers to the predicted likelihood of survival or risk of death over a defined follow-up period, typically expressed as hazard ratios, survival probabilities, or risk categories (e.g., low, intermediate, high). Prognostic assessment integrates measured lipoprotein properties with relevant clinical factors to generate a comprehensive risk profile. Statistical significance of associations between biomarkers and outcomes is determined using established methods, including Cox proportional hazards regression models adjusted for case-mix variables (age, sex, diabetes, comorbidity score), conventional lipids, malnutrition-inflammation complex syndrome (MICS) indicators, and inflammatory markers (CRP, IL-6, TNF-α).

The method involves measuring LDL particle size and/or LDL subfraction concentrations in a patient’s sample and comparing these values to reference levels derived from a control population of MHD patients. Elevated vs-LDL-Pc (e.g., above the third quartile) or reduced LDL-Pd (e.g., below the median of 216.5 Å) signals increased mortality risk, while elevated l-LDL-Pc indicates reduced risk. Reference ranges may be established using population medians, quartiles, or clinically validated thresholds. Temporal changes in these properties can also inform prognosis, with worsening profiles indicating disease progression.

Statistical analyses employ Kaplan-Meier survival curves, chi-squared tests for categorical associations, and multivariate regression to isolate the independent effect of lipoprotein measures. Software tools, including custom Visual Basic for Applications (VBA) programs, may automate data processing, risk calculation, and report generation. Simple algorithms combine multiple prognostic factors—such as LDL-Pd, vs-LDL-Pc, and l-LDL-Pc—into a unified risk score. For example, a patient with below-median LDL-Pd and above-median vs-LDL-Pc would be classified as high risk, whereas one with above-median LDL-Pd and l-LDL-Pc would be low risk. The final prognosis is reported in a clinical analysis report, enabling clinicians to monitor patient status and consider risk-adapted management strategies.

## EXAMPLES

### Introduction to Study and Analytical Methods

The following examples illustrate the application of the invention in a prospective cohort of 235 maintenance hemodialysis patients enrolled in the Nutritional and Inflammatory Evaluation in Dialysis (NIED) Study. Lipoprotein subfractions were resolved using ion mobility analysis as previously described, with assay characteristics demonstrating high precision for major subfractions (interassay CV <1% for LDL-Pd). Lipoprotein fractionation covered a continuous size spectrum from small HDL (76.5–105.0 Å) to large VLDL (424.0–520.0 Å), with LDL subfractions defined as: very small (180.0–208.2 Å), small (208.2–214.1 Å), medium (214.1–220.0 Å), and large (220.0–233.3 Å). All measurements were performed on archived baseline plasma samples, and patients were followed for up to six years for vital status ascertainment.

### Example 1

The study population comprised 235 MHD patients (mean age 54 ± 14 years; 45% women; 26% African American) with a median dialysis vintage of 44 months. Baseline laboratory values showed mean total cholesterol of 143 ± 42 mg/dL, LDL-C of 76 ± 29 mg/dL, and HDL-C of 37 ± 12 mg/dL. Over six years, 71 patients (31%) died. Conventional lipid measures showed no significant association with mortality in multivariate-adjusted models, consistent with the lipid paradox observed in ESRD populations.

### Example 2

Correlation analyses revealed that conventional LDL-C correlated modestly with large LDL-Pc (r = 0.34, P < 0.01) but poorly with very small LDL-Pc. HDL-C was negatively associated with small and medium LDL-Pc and positively with large HDL-Pc. LDL particle diameter (LDL-Pd) correlated positively with both very small and large LDL-Pc, suggesting complex remodeling of LDL subclasses in uremia.

### Example 3

Quartile-based hazard ratio analyses demonstrated that patients in the highest quartile of vs-LDL-Pc had a 2.43-fold higher death risk (95% CI: 1.03–5.72) compared to the lowest quartile, after full adjustment for clinical confounders. Conversely, the highest quartile of l-LDL-Pc was associated with a 62% lower mortality risk (HR = 0.38, 95% CI: 0.15–0.96). Increasing quartiles of LDL-Pd showed progressively lower mortality: HRs of 1.00 (ref), 0.93, 0.43, and 0.45 for quartiles 1 to 4, respectively. Cubic spline analyses confirmed a nonlinear inverse relationship between LDL-Pd and mortality, with risk rising sharply below 215 Å.

### Example 4

Combination analyses revealed that above-median LDL-Pd conferred protection regardless of LDL-C level, but above-median LDL-C was paradoxically harmful when LDL-Pd was below median (HR = 1.47). Similarly, above-median total LDL-Pc combined with above-median LDL-Pd yielded the lowest mortality (74% reduction vs. double-low group). Conventional LDL-C and HDL-C showed no predictive value across LDL-Pd strata, underscoring the superiority of particle-based metrics.

## SUMMARY OF RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

The results demonstrate that conventional lipid measures fail to predict mortality in MHD patients, consistent with the lipid paradox wherein low cholesterol levels reflect underlying illness rather than reduced cardiovascular risk. In contrast, novel lipoprotein particle properties—specifically smaller LDL particle diameter, higher very small LDL concentration, and lower large LDL concentration—are strongly and independently associated with increased death risk. These findings highlight the critical importance of particle phenotype over cholesterol content in this population.

The invention’s scope encompasses all methods that utilize LDL particle size or subfraction concentration to predict survival in hemodialysis patients, including combinations with other biomarkers.
