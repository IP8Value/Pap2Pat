# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention resides in the field of immunopharmacology and relates to the therapeutic and diagnostic use of naltrexone and its analogues as antagonists of Toll-like receptor 9 (TLR9). More specifically, the invention provides methods for treating disorders characterized by TLR9 overexpression or overactivity, including certain cancers and chronic inflammatory conditions, through the administration of low-dose naltrexone (LDN) or related compounds. The invention further encompasses pharmaceutical compositions comprising naltrexone or its derivatives formulated for systemic or localized delivery, as well as methods for modulating immune responses via TLR9 inhibition. Additionally, the invention includes diagnostic approaches for identifying subjects who may benefit from TLR9-targeted therapy based on the expression or activity levels of TLR9 in biological samples. This field intersects oncology, immunology, and pharmacology, offering a novel mechanism-based strategy for managing diseases driven by dysregulated innate immune signaling.

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

Naltrexone is a synthetic opioid antagonist originally developed for the treatment of opioid and alcohol dependence. Chemically, naltrexone hydrochloride is designated as 17-(cyclopropylmethyl)-4,5α-epoxy-3,14-dihydroxymorphinan-6-one hydrochloride, with a molecular formula of C20H23NO4·HCl and a molecular weight of 377.86 g/mol. It functions primarily as a competitive antagonist at mu-opioid receptors and, to a lesser extent, delta-opioid receptors, thereby blocking the euphoric and reinforcing effects of opioids and alcohol. Despite its established role in addiction medicine, naltrexone has been used off-label at significantly lower doses—typically 1–5 mg daily, referred to as low-dose naltrexone (LDN)—for a variety of non-addiction-related conditions. Clinical reports and small-scale studies have suggested therapeutic benefits of LDN in autoimmune and inflammatory disorders such as Crohn’s disease, multiple sclerosis, and fibromyalgia, although the underlying mechanisms remained poorly understood.

In the context of oncology, anecdotal and preliminary clinical evidence has indicated that LDN may exert anti-tumor effects in certain malignancies, including B-cell lymphoma and pancreatic cancer. These observations prompted investigations into potential non-opioid receptor-mediated mechanisms of action. Prior research demonstrated that naltrexone and its structural analogue naloxone can inhibit Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4) signaling in microglial cells, reducing neuroinflammation and neuropathic pain. This effect was attributed to direct binding of naltrexone to MD-2, a co-receptor essential for TLR4 activation by lipopolysaccharide (LPS). However, the scope of naltrexone’s interaction with other members of the TLR family had not been systematically explored in human immune cells.

Toll-like receptors are key pattern recognition receptors of the innate immune system, responsible for detecting pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) and damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs). Among them, TLR9 is localized intracellularly within endosomal compartments and recognizes unmethylated CpG dinucleotides in bacterial and viral DNA, as well as self-DNA in pathological contexts such as autoimmunity and cancer. Aberrant TLR9 signaling has been implicated in the pathogenesis of several diseases, including psoriasis, systemic lupus erythematosus, and various solid and hematologic malignancies. In the tumor microenvironment, TLR9 overexpression can promote angiogenesis, immune evasion, and metastasis, making it an attractive therapeutic target. While TLR4 antagonism by naltrexone was previously documented, the discovery that naltrexone also inhibits TLR9—and, to a lesser extent, TLR7 and TLR8—represents a significant expansion of its immunomodulatory profile and provides a mechanistic rationale for its observed clinical effects in cancer and inflammatory diseases.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention is based on the unexpected discovery that naltrexone acts as a functional antagonist of Toll-like receptor 9 (TLR9), inhibiting downstream pro-inflammatory signaling and cytokine production in human immune cells. Contrary to prior assumptions that naltrexone’s immunomodulatory effects were limited to TLR4, experimental data demonstrate that naltrexone selectively suppresses cytokine release (including IL-6 and TNFα) induced by TLR7, TLR8, and TLR9 ligands, but not by TLR4 or interleukin-1 receptor (IL-1R) agonists, in peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs). This selectivity suggests a mechanism distinct from general MyD88 pathway inhibition and points to a specific interaction with intracellular TLRs, particularly TLR9. The invention thus provides novel therapeutic applications of naltrexone and its analogues for the treatment of disorders characterized by TLR9 overexpression or overactivity, including certain cancers (e.g., melanoma, prostate cancer, pancreatic carcinoma, B-cell lymphoma), autoimmune diseases (e.g., psoriasis, lupus), and chronic inflammatory conditions. Furthermore, the invention encompasses the use of naltrexone as an immunomodulator in vaccine adjuvant strategies to enhance cytotoxic T-cell responses by tempering excessive TLR9-driven inflammation that may otherwise impair adaptive immunity.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

The present invention introduces naltrexone as a previously unrecognized antagonist of Toll-like receptor 9 (TLR9), a critical sensor of unmethylated CpG DNA motifs in endosomal compartments of immune cells. TLR9 is predominantly expressed in B lymphocytes and plasmacytoid dendritic cells (pDCs), where it plays a pivotal role in initiating type I interferon and pro-inflammatory cytokine responses upon recognition of microbial or self-nucleic acids. Under pathological conditions, such as in the tumor microenvironment or autoimmune settings, TLR9 can be aberrantly activated by self-DNA complexed with antimicrobial peptides or autoantibodies, leading to chronic inflammation, immune dysregulation, and tumor progression.

TLR9-mediated signaling proceeds through the adaptor protein MyD88, culminating in the activation of NF-κB and IRF7 transcription factors, which drive the expression of cytokines like IL-6, TNFα, and type I interferons. Plasmacytoid dendritic cells (pDCs) are especially potent producers of type I interferons in response to TLR9 ligation and are central to antiviral immunity but also contribute to autoimmune pathology when chronically activated. Overactivity of TLR9 signaling—defined herein as sustained or exaggerated cytokine production in response to endogenous or exogenous ligands beyond physiological norms—is associated with poor clinical outcomes in several cancers and inflammatory diseases.

The inventors have discovered that naltrexone inhibits TLR9-mediated TNFα production in pDCs and IL-6 secretion in B cells following stimulation with CpG oligodeoxynucleotides (CpG-ODN), a canonical TLR9 agonist. This inhibition occurs without compromising cell viability or inducing apoptosis, indicating a specific modulatory effect rather than cytotoxicity. Importantly, naltrexone does not inhibit TLR4 signaling in human PBMCs under the same experimental conditions, distinguishing its mechanism from prior reports in murine microglia and suggesting cell-type or species-specific differences in TLR4 modulation.

As used herein, “agonist” refers to a compound that binds to and activates a receptor, while “antagonist” denotes a compound that binds to a receptor and blocks its activation by natural or synthetic agonists. “Overexpression of TLR9” means elevated levels of TLR9 mRNA or protein in a biological sample relative to healthy controls, detectable by quantitative PCR, flow cytometry, or immunohistochemistry. “Overactivity of TLR9-mediated signaling” refers to enhanced or prolonged downstream signaling events (e.g., NF-κB nuclear translocation, cytokine secretion) in response to ligand stimulation.

Techniques to assess TLR9 expression include RT-qPCR for TLR9 transcripts, intracellular flow cytometry using anti-TLR9 antibodies, and immunohistochemical staining of tissue sections. Signaling activity can be evaluated by measuring cytokine levels (e.g., IL-6, TNFα, IFN-α) in supernatants or by phospho-flow analysis of signaling intermediates.

“Treating” or “treatment” encompasses prophylactic, palliative, or curative interventions aimed at preventing, reducing, or eliminating disease symptoms or progression. “Supportive care” refers to therapies that alleviate symptoms or improve quality of life without directly targeting the underlying disease process.

“Cancer” or “cancerous” denotes malignant neoplasms characterized by uncontrolled cell growth, invasion, and metastasis. A “tumour” or “neoplasm” is an abnormal mass of tissue; “tumourigenic” describes cells capable of forming tumours. The “tumour/cancer microenvironment” includes stromal cells, immune infiltrates, cytokines, and extracellular matrix components that influence tumour behavior.

A “subject” is a human or non-human mammal, preferably a human patient. An “immunomodulator” is any agent that alters immune system activity.

Disorders amenable to treatment include those with documented TLR9 overexpression or overactivity: melanoma, prostate cancer, pancreatic cancer, B-cell lymphoma, psoriasis, lupus, and Crohn’s disease. Diagnostic tests involve assessing TLR9 levels or function in blood or tissue biopsies to stratify patients for naltrexone therapy.

Pharmaceutical compositions of the invention comprise naltrexone or a pharmaceutically acceptable salt (e.g., hydrochloride), ester, or analogue thereof, optionally combined with excipients, carriers, or other active agents (e.g., vitamin D3, Mycobacterium vaccae). Preferred formulations are oral tablets or capsules containing 1–4.5 mg naltrexone for LDN regimens. Optional features include sustained-release matrices or nanoparticle delivery systems for targeted tissue distribution.

The method of treating a subject involves administering a therapeutically effective amount of naltrexone to inhibit TLR9 signaling. Preferred doses range from 0.5 to 10 mg daily, more preferably 1–5 mg, administered orally at bedtime to coincide with endogenous opioid rhythm. Routes include oral, sublingual, transdermal, or intravenous, though oral is most practical for chronic use.

The method of providing supportive care uses naltrexone to reduce inflammation-related symptoms (e.g., pain, fatigue) in cancer or autoimmune patients, improving tolerability of primary therapies.

Naltrexone may also serve as a vaccine adjuvant by dampening excessive TLR9-driven inflammation that can suppress cytotoxic T-cell priming. Vaccine compositions combine tumor antigens or pathogen-derived peptides with naltrexone to promote balanced Th1/Tc1 responses.

Example 1 demonstrates naltrexone’s TLR9 antagonism using PBMC assays showing dose-dependent inhibition of IL-6 and TNFα after CpG stimulation, confirmed in isolated B cells and pDCs. Example 2 reports a melanoma patient achieving stable disease on LDN (4.5 mg/day) plus vitamin D3, with reduced inflammatory markers. Example 3 describes a prostate cancer patient on LDN and Mycobacterium vaccae exhibiting PSA stabilization and improved quality of life.

In conclusion, this invention establishes naltrexone as a selective TLR9 antagonist with broad therapeutic potential in oncology and immunology. Final remarks emphasize the novelty of repurposing an approved drug for TLR9-mediated disorders, offering a safe, cost-effective treatment strategy supported by mechanistic and clinical evidence.

## EXAMPLES

### Example 1—Identification of Naltrexone as an Antagonist of TLR9

Agonist and antagonist assays were performed using human peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) stimulated with specific ligands for Toll-like receptors (TLRs) and nucleotide-binding oligomerization domain (NOD)-like receptors. PBMCs were isolated from healthy donors and cultured in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum. Cells were pre-treated with naltrexone hydrochloride at concentrations ranging from 1 to 200 µM for one hour prior to stimulation with TLR ligands: lipopolysaccharide (LPS, 1 ng/mL) for TLR4, R848 (1 µM) for TLR7/8, and CpG-ODN 2395 (1 µM) for TLR9. After 24 hours, cell-free supernatants were collected and analyzed for interleukin-6 (IL-6) by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA). Naltrexone did not inhibit IL-6 production following TLR4 stimulation but significantly suppressed IL-6 release induced by TLR7/8 and TLR9 ligands at 200 µM (p < 0.05). Intracellular cytokine staining confirmed that naltrexone reduced TNFα production in plasmacytoid dendritic cells (pDCs) after TLR9 activation and IL-6 in monocytes after TLR7/8 stimulation. Control experiments with IL-1 receptor agonist showed no inhibition, ruling out general MyD88 pathway blockade. Cell viability assays using annexin V/7-AAD staining confirmed that naltrexone did not induce apoptosis or reduce cell survival at tested concentrations. These results collectively identify naltrexone as a functional antagonist of TLR9, with additional activity against TLR7 and TLR8, but not TLR4 or IL-1R, in primary human immune cells.

### Example 2—LDN in the Treatment of Melanoma

A 62-year-old male patient with stage IV metastatic melanoma, refractory to standard immunotherapies, was initiated on low-dose naltrexone (LDN) at 4.5 mg orally once daily at bedtime, in combination with high-dose vitamin D3 (5000 IU/day). Baseline imaging showed multiple hepatic and pulmonary metastases. After three months of therapy, the patient reported improved energy levels and reduced pain. Laboratory evaluation revealed a decline in C-reactive protein (CRP) from 18 mg/L to 6 mg/L and erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) from 45 mm/hr to 22 mm/hr, indicating reduced systemic inflammation. Serial CT scans at six months demonstrated stable disease with no new lesions and slight reduction in the size of existing metastases. The patient remained on LDN for over 18 months with maintained stability and minimal side effects, primarily mild insomnia during the first week of treatment. This clinical response, in the absence of conventional anti-cancer therapy, supports the hypothesis that TLR9 antagonism by LDN modulates the tumor-promoting inflammatory microenvironment, thereby inhibiting melanoma progression.

### Example 3—LDN in the Treatment of Prostate Cancer

A 70-year-old male diagnosed with high-risk prostate adenocarcinoma (Gleason score 8, PSA 28 ng/mL) declined immediate androgen deprivation therapy and instead opted for an integrative approach. He began treatment with low-dose naltrexone (3 mg orally at bedtime) combined with heat-killed Mycobacterium vaccae (as an immunostimulant) administered subcutaneously weekly for four weeks, then monthly. Within two months, his PSA level decreased to 19 ng/mL, and by six months, it stabilized at 15 ng/mL. The patient reported improved urinary symptoms and enhanced overall well-being. Repeat prostate biopsies at one year showed reduced inflammatory infiltrate and lower Ki-67 proliferation index compared to baseline. No significant adverse events were observed. This case illustrates the potential of LDN, possibly through TLR9 inhibition, to attenuate chronic inflammation in the prostate tumor microenvironment, thereby slowing disease progression and enhancing the efficacy of adjunctive immunotherapies.
