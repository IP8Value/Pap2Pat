# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNICAL FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention resides in the technical field of oncology and targeted drug delivery, specifically relating to methods and compositions for enhancing the selective accumulation of therapeutic or imaging agents within solid tumors through pharmacological modulation of tumor vascular permeability. More particularly, the invention provides a strategy for amplifying the Enhanced Permeability and Retention (EPR) effect—commonly observed in solid tumors—by co-administering vascular-active permeability agents that induce a more pronounced and therapeutically exploitable vascular disruption, herein termed Enhanced EPR (E2PR). This approach enables significantly improved tumor-to-background ratios for macromolecular and nanoparticle-based agents, thereby increasing therapeutic efficacy while minimizing systemic toxicity.

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

Accurate and effective diagnosis and treatment of cancer remain among the most pressing challenges in modern medicine. Solid tumors are often resistant to conventional chemotherapeutic regimens due to poor drug penetration, systemic toxicity at effective doses, and the presence of biological barriers such as the blood-brain barrier in central nervous system malignancies. A key physiological feature distinguishing many solid tumors from healthy tissues is their abnormal vasculature, which exhibits increased permeability and defective lymphatic drainage. This phenomenon, known as the Enhanced Permeability and Retention (EPR) effect, has been leveraged to improve the tumor-selective delivery of macromolecular drugs and nanoparticles. The EPR effect permits passive accumulation of particles typically in the size range of 10 to 200 nanometers within tumor interstitium, while sparing most normal tissues. Clinically, this principle underlies the success of formulations such as Doxil, a sterically stabilized liposomal encapsulation of doxorubicin approved for several cancer indications. Despite these advances, the magnitude of EPR-mediated accumulation is often modest and highly variable across tumor types and individual patients, limiting its therapeutic utility.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention introduces a novel method for significantly enhancing the delivery of therapeutic or imaging agents to solid tumors by co-administering a vascular-active permeability agent that induces a state of Enhanced EPR (E2PR). In one embodiment, the method comprises administering to a subject in need thereof a composition comprising a nanoparticle loaded with a therapeutic or imaging agent together with a vascular-active permeability agent, wherein the vascular-active permeability agent increases vascular permeability selectively within the tumor microenvironment, thereby promoting enhanced accumulation and retention of the nanoparticle within the tumor. The invention further encompasses kits comprising a first container holding a pharmaceutical composition of a nanoparticle formulation and a second container holding a vascular-active permeability agent, optionally with instructions for co-administration. In a specific embodiment, the nanoparticle is a sterically stabilized liposome approximately 100 nanometers in diameter, and the vascular-active permeability agent is Tumor Necrosis Factor-alpha (TNF-α). The benefits of this approach include markedly improved tumor-to-blood and tumor-to-normal tissue ratios of the administered agent, leading to superior therapeutic outcomes, including complete tumor regression and long-term cures in preclinical models, even in highly aggressive and treatment-resistant cancers such as glioblastoma multiforme.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

The invention is predicated on the discovery that vascular-active permeability entities can be used to pharmacologically amplify the natural but limited EPR effect observed in solid tumors. These entities, when administered systemically, induce a transient and localized increase in vascular permeability specifically within the tumor vasculature, without causing widespread systemic vascular leakage at clinically tolerable doses. This induced state, referred to herein as Enhanced EPR (E2PR), facilitates the delivery of therapeutic or imaging agents that are conjugated to or encapsulated within nanoparticles. The administration of these agents may be performed intravenously, intra-arterially, or via other systemic routes, and is typically synchronized with or shortly follows the administration of the vascular-active permeability agent to maximize synergistic accumulation. Nanoparticles suitable for use in the invention generally fall within a size range of about 10 nanometers to about 1 micrometer in diameter, with optimal performance observed for particles between 50 and 200 nanometers. Exemplary nanoparticle compositions include liposomes, polymeric nanoparticles, micelles, dendrimers, and inorganic nanoparticles such as gold or iron oxide cores coated with biocompatible polymers. Therapeutic or imaging agents may be attached to the nanoparticle surface via covalent linkages, electrostatic interactions, or hydrophobic embedding, or may be encapsulated within the nanoparticle core or aqueous interior. Therapeutic agents encompass a broad spectrum of molecules, including chemotherapeutic drugs, radiosensitizers, immunomodulators, and nucleic acid-based therapeutics. Specific chemotherapeutic agents include anthracyclines such as doxorubicin, taxanes such as paclitaxel, platinum compounds such as cisplatin, and vinca alkaloids such as vinorelbine. Imaging agents suitable for use include radionuclides such as iodine-125, iodine-131, technetium-99m, and gallium-68, as well as contrast agents for magnetic resonance imaging (e.g., gadolinium chelates), computed tomography (e.g., iodinated compounds), and optical imaging (e.g., near-infrared fluorophores). Non-invasive detection techniques such as SPECT-CT, PET-CT, MRI, and fluorescence imaging may be employed to monitor agent distribution and tumor response. Vascular-active permeability agents are defined as substances that modulate endothelial cell junctions, cytoskeletal integrity, or inflammatory signaling to increase vascular leakiness. Examples include cytokines such as TNF-α, interleukin-2, and interferon-gamma; bacterial components such as lipopolysaccharide or live attenuated bacteria like Clostridium novyi-NT; and vascular-disrupting agents such as combretastatin A4 phosphate (CA4P) and vinorelbine. These agents act through mechanisms involving activation of endothelial nitric oxide synthase, induction of actin stress fibers, downregulation of tight junction proteins, or recruitment of inflammatory cells that release secondary mediators. Kits for assessment and therapeutic use may include pre-formulated vials of nanoparticle-drug conjugates and vials of lyophilized or liquid vascular-active agents, along with dosing guidelines and compatibility information. The invention is applicable to a wide array of solid tumors, including carcinomas of the breast, lung, colon, ovary, and prostate, as well as sarcomas, melanomas, and primary brain tumors such as glioblastoma multiforme. Conventional chemotherapeutic agents suffer from dose-limiting toxicities that prevent eradication of all malignant cells; the E2PR approach addresses this limitation by concentrating the agent preferentially within the tumor, thereby allowing lower systemic doses to achieve higher intratumoral concentrations. The E2PR strategy represents a significant advancement over baseline EPR, as demonstrated in murine models where TNF-α co-administration increased tumor-to-blood radioactivity ratios to over 22-fold for liposomal agents, compared to less than 5-fold with EPR alone. In an orthotopic glioblastoma model, E2PR enabled substantial accumulation of liposomes across the blood-brain barrier and extended median survival from less than 50 days to over 100 days. These results underscore the versatility of the approach, which is compatible with diverse nanoparticle platforms, therapeutic payloads, and vascular-active triggers. In summary, the invention provides a robust, translatable platform for improving cancer therapy by harnessing pharmacologically induced vascular permeability to enhance tumor-selective drug delivery.

### Example 1

The materials and methods employed to demonstrate the invention included standard cell culture techniques using established murine tumor cell lines. CT26 murine colorectal adenocarcinoma cells were maintained in McCoy’s 5A medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum under standard incubation conditions. GL261 murine glioma cells were cultured in DMEM with identical serum supplementation. Reagents used included recombinant murine TNF-α, Bolton-Hunter reagent, radioiodines (125I and 131I), IODO-GEN, monoclonal antibodies against CD20 and liposomase, Doxil, and liposomal components such as HEPC, cholesterol, and DSPE-PEG2000. Animal models consisted of female BALB/c mice for subcutaneous CT26 tumors and C57BL/6 mice for orthotopic GL261 brain tumors. In the subcutaneous model, 5 million CT26 cells were injected into the right flank, and tumors were allowed to reach ~100 mm³ before intervention. For the orthotopic model, 20,000 GL261 cells were stereotactically implanted into the frontal lobe. A monoclonal antibody against C. novyi-NT liposomase (clone JHU009-5F5) was generated by immunizing mice with synthetic peptides and screening hybridomas by ELISA. Antibodies were radioiodinated using IODO-GEN-coated vials, followed by purification on Sephadex G-25 columns. Liposomes were prepared by thin-film hydration of lipid mixtures, extrusion through 100-nm filters, and dialysis to establish transmembrane pH gradients. Bolton-Hunter reagent was radioiodinated via chloramine-T oxidation and extracted into toluene. Encapsulation was achieved by incubating arginine-loaded liposomes with 125I-BH, exploiting the protonation of arginine at low pH to trap the labeled reagent intraliposomally. Biodistribution assays involved injecting radiolabeled agents, sacrificing mice at defined timepoints, harvesting tissues, and quantifying radioactivity via gamma counting. SPECT-CT imaging was performed using a Gamma Medica-Ideas X-SPECT system with pinhole collimators and co-registered CT. Statistical analysis of survival data was conducted using Log-rank tests. All animal procedures complied with institutional ethical guidelines.

### Example 2

An initial observation that motivated the invention was the unexpected accumulation of control immunoglobulins in tumors infected with Clostridium novyi-NT, an anaerobic bacterium known to colonize hypoxic tumor regions. In experiments, mice bearing subcutaneous CT26 tumors received intravenous injections of C. novyi-NT spores alongside either a radiolabeled anti-liposomase antibody or a non-specific IgG control. Both antibodies showed significant enrichment in infected tumors compared to uninfected controls, indicating that the bacterial infection itself—not antigen specificity—drove enhanced vascular permeability and macromolecular accumulation. This finding suggested that the inflammatory milieu generated by the infection could be harnessed to improve drug delivery.

### Example 3

To identify a more controllable and clinically feasible trigger than live bacteria, the pro-inflammatory cytokine TNF-α was investigated due to its known role in endotoxin-induced vascular leak and its ability to cause hemorrhagic necrosis in tumors. When TNF-α was co-administered with radiolabeled IgG to CT26 tumor-bearing mice, significant tumor-selective IgG accumulation was observed, peaking at 72–96 hours. Sterically stabilized liposomes (~100 nm) labeled via the Bolton-Hunter/arginine trapping method also showed enhanced tumor retention with TNF-α, with peak accumulation at 24 hours and tumor-to-blood ratios exceeding 22:1. Particle size dependence was confirmed: free arginine showed no enhancement, while micron-sized C. novyi-NT spores required TNF-α for tumor accumulation. E2PR was effective regardless of tumor volume and tolerated a 12-hour window between liposome and TNF-α administration, though pre-treatment with TNF-α abolished the effect. Microtubule-targeting agents like CA4P and vinorelbine also induced E2PR, albeit less potently than TNF-α.

### Example 4

Therapeutic validation was performed in CT26 and GL261 models. In CT26 tumors, single-agent Doxil or radiolabeled IgG delayed growth but did not cure. Combined with TNF-α, however, both induced complete regression and >75% long-term cures. Similarly, 131I-labeled SSLs only cured tumors when paired with TNF-α. In the aggressive orthotopic GBM model, Doxil alone offered minimal survival benefit, but Doxil plus TNF-α extended median survival to 103 days, with significant liposomal accumulation in brain tumors only in TNF-α-treated animals. These results confirm that E2PR translates into profound therapeutic gains, even in settings where conventional therapy fails.
