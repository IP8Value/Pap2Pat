# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates to electron gas electronic circuit elements, and more particularly to gated semiconductor devices that utilize two-dimensional electron gases (2DEGs) as the active conducting medium. The invention provides a novel fabrication methodology for creating high-performance electrostatic gate structures—such as split gates, quantum point contacts (QPCs), and Fabry-Pérot interferometers—on ultra-high mobility 2DEG systems without subjecting the sensitive 2DEG material to conventional lithographic or chemical processing steps. This approach is especially suited for applications in quantum transport, low-temperature condensed matter physics, and next-generation nanoelectronic devices where preservation of electron mobility and material integrity is paramount.

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

Two-dimensional electronic gases (2DEGs) represent a class of quantum-confined electron systems that form at the interface between two semiconductors with different bandgaps, such as GaAs and AlGaAs. These systems exhibit exceptional electron mobility due to the spatial separation of electrons from ionized dopants, which minimizes scattering and enables ballistic transport over micrometer-scale distances at cryogenic temperatures. The motivation for utilizing 2DEGs stems from their unique ability to manifest quantum mechanical phenomena—such as conductance quantization, the integer and fractional quantum Hall effects, and quantum interference—that are foundational to both fundamental research and emerging quantum technologies.

In contrast to 2DEGs, two-dimensional hole gases (2DHGs) also exist but typically exhibit significantly lower mobilities due to the heavier effective mass of holes and stronger spin-orbit coupling. Electron mobility is a critical parameter in semiconductor devices, as it directly influences conductivity, switching speed, and coherence length; thus, preserving and maximizing mobility is essential for high-fidelity quantum measurements.

Split gates—nanoscale metallic electrodes patterned in close proximity on the surface of a 2DEG—are widely used to electrostatically define constrictions such as QPCs. A QPC acts as a tunable one-dimensional channel whose conductance is quantized in units of \(2e^2/h\) under ballistic conditions. These structures are instrumental in probing quantum transport, forming quantum dots, and enabling interferometry in the quantum Hall regime. Ballistic transport through such constrictions occurs when the mean free path exceeds the device length, allowing electrons to traverse without scattering—a condition readily achieved in high-mobility 2DEGs at millikelvin temperatures.

In the presence of strong magnetic fields, 2DEGs exhibit the quantum Hall effect, where the Hall resistance becomes quantized in precise steps while the longitudinal resistance vanishes. At even higher mobilities and lower temperatures, the fractional quantum Hall effect (FQHE) emerges, revealing exotic quasiparticle excitations with fractional charge and statistics. These phenomena require pristine, undisturbed 2DEG systems, making device fabrication methods that avoid contamination or damage critically important.

Traditional fabrication of gated 2DEG devices relies on direct patterning of metal gates onto the semiconductor heterostructure using electron-beam (e-beam) or optical lithography. However, this process introduces several limitations: resist residues from lithography can trap charges and induce density fluctuations; e-beam exposure can cause radiation damage, including heating, electrostatic charging, and hydrocarbon contamination; and thermal mismatch between metal gates and the semiconductor substrate can induce strain during cooldown, degrading mobility—particularly in narrow constrictions. Prior art approaches have attempted to mitigate these issues through careful cleaning protocols, low-dose lithography, or alternative gate dielectrics, but none fully eliminate the exposure of the 2DEG to potentially damaging processes.

The inventors have developed a novel methodology that circumvents these limitations by decoupling gate fabrication from the 2DEG wafer. In this approach, all lithographic and metallization steps are performed on a separate substrate, which is then mechanically mounted onto the 2DEG surface in a non-destructive manner. This technique preserves the intrinsic properties of the 2DEG while enabling flexible, reconfigurable, and high-yield device integration.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

It is an object of the present invention to provide a method and device for fabricating high-performance gated electronic structures on ultra-high mobility two-dimensional electron gas systems without degrading the electronic properties of the underlying material. The invention achieves this by employing a flip-chip architecture wherein electrostatic gate patterns are fabricated on a separate carrier substrate and subsequently aligned and mounted onto the 2DEG wafer.

The method of the invention comprises fabricating gate electrode structures—including split gates, QPCs, and interferometer gates—on a mechanically robust, insulating substrate such as sapphire, using standard lithographic and metallization techniques. The 2DEG wafer, which remains untouched by any chemical or radiation-based processing, is prepared with ohmic contacts using non-lithographic methods such as shadow-mask evaporation or indium diffusion. The processed gate chip is then aligned and brought into proximity with the 2DEG surface using a precision mechanical fixture that applies gentle, uniform pressure without inducing strain or damage.

The resulting device is a hybrid gated electronic structure that combines the quantum transport capabilities of pristine 2DEGs with the design flexibility of conventional microfabrication. Other aspects of the invention include the reusability of the 2DEG wafer, the ability to test multiple gate designs on the same material, and compatibility with ultra-high mobility heterostructures required for observing delicate quantum Hall states.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The invention relates specifically to electron gas electronic circuit elements that exploit the quantum properties of two-dimensional electron gases. Central to the operation of such devices are metal-insulator-semiconductor (MIS) layered structures, of which metal-oxide-semiconductor (MOS) configurations are a well-known subset. In a MOS field-effect transistor (MOSFET), an applied gate voltage modulates the carrier density in a channel, thereby controlling current flow. In high-mobility III-V semiconductor heterostructures like GaAs/AlGaAs, a 2DEG forms naturally at the interface due to band bending and modulation doping, eliminating the need for a traditional inversion layer.

Other systems exhibiting 2DEG behavior include Si/SiGe heterostructures and oxide interfaces such as LaAlO₃/SrTiO₃, but GaAs/AlGaAs remains the gold standard for ultra-high mobility applications. The semiconductor industry has long relied on lithography—optical, e-beam, and nanoimprint—to define nanoscale features. However, for quantum devices, the trade-off between feature resolution and material integrity becomes acute. Non-silicon-based devices, particularly those based on GaAs, offer superior mobility but are more susceptible to processing-induced damage.

Prior art manufacturing methodologies for gated 2DEG devices involve direct lithography on the heterostructure, followed by metal deposition and lift-off. This exposes the 2DEG to solvents, plasmas, and radiation, often degrading mobility. The new method of the invention avoids all such steps on the 2DEG wafer. Instead, a silicon or sapphire substrate is patterned with the desired gate geometry using standard e-beam lithography: a chromium layer is first deposited for charge dissipation, followed by MMA/PMMA resist spinning, exposure, development, and Ti/Au evaporation. After lift-off and chromium etching, a thin aluminum oxide layer is deposited to prevent gate leakage.

Ohmic contacts are fabricated on the 2DEG wafer using shadow-mask evaporation of Ge/Au/Ni/Au or indium diffusion, ensuring no lithography is performed on the sensitive surface. The gate chip is then assembled onto the 2DEG wafer using a test fixture comprising a sample holder, BeCu springs, and a top sapphire plate. Optical interference fringes enable sub-micron alignment accuracy. Bonding is purely mechanical, avoiding adhesives or high-temperature processes that could induce strain or contamination.

This method offers multiple benefits: it prevents chemical contamination from resists and developers; eliminates radiation damage from lithography; avoids thermal strain from differential contraction between metal gates and GaAs; and allows the 2DEG wafer to be reused or reconfigured. A prototype split-gate device was fabricated using this approach, and its conductance was measured across a QPC, demonstrating clear quantization and pinch-off behavior.

### Embodiment 1

In a proof-of-principle experiment, a GaAs/AlGaAs wafer with a 2DEG located 100 nm below the surface was used. Ohmic contacts were formed by evaporating Ge/Au/Ni/Au through a shadow mask, followed by annealing. A separate sapphire chip was processed to define a 1-μm-long QPC using e-beam lithography: 50 nm Cr was deposited, then MMA/PMMA resist spun, exposed, and developed. Ti (5 nm)/Au (150 nm) was evaporated, lifted off, and the Cr layer etched. A 50 nm Al₂O₃ layer was deposited by atomic layer deposition. The sapphire chip was cleaned in oxygen plasma and solvents, then mounted on the 2DEG wafer using a fixture with BeCu springs applying ~10 mN force. Conductance measurements at 25 mK showed clear quantization in steps of \(2e^2/h\), with pinch-off voltages between −6 V and −40 V. No hysteresis was observed, indicating minimal charge trapping.

### Embodiment 2

The flip-chip technique was further refined using e-beam lithography on sapphire substrates. Chromium (50 nm) was deposited for charge control, followed by MMA/PMMA resist spinning at 4000 rpm. After e-beam exposure and development in MIBK:IPA, Ti (5 nm)/Au (150 nm) was deposited by e-beam evaporation. Lift-off was performed in warm acetone, and Cr was etched in ceric ammonium nitrate. A 30–100 nm Al₂O₃ layer was deposited to insulate the gates. Ohmic contacts were made on the GaAs/AlGaAs wafer via shadow-mask evaporation. The flip-chip was aligned using optical interference fringes and mounted with BeCu springs. Devices were tested on wafers from Sandia and Princeton, showing pinch-off and conductance quantization. Although gate efficiency was lower than conventional devices due to a 50–200 nm air gap, performance in the quantum Hall regime was excellent, with clear IQH and FQH plateaus up to 9 T. The methodology is extensible to 3D stacked circuits and other materials, enabling planarized gate electrodes on interposers for complex quantum architectures.
