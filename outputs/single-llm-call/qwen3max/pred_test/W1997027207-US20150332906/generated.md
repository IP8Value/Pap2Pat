# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

Matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight (MALDI-TOF) mass spectrometry is a widely employed analytical technique for the characterization of biomolecules, particularly peptides, proteins, and other large organic compounds. The fundamental principle underlying MALDI-TOF involves the ionization of analyte molecules embedded within a crystalline matrix through irradiation with a pulsed laser, followed by acceleration of the resulting ions into a field-free drift region where they separate according to their mass-to-charge ratios based on differences in their time-of-flight to a detector. This method enables rapid, high-throughput analysis with relatively minimal sample preparation, making it especially valuable in clinical diagnostics, microbiology, proteomics, and natural product discovery. Modern MALDI-TOF instruments often incorporate advanced features such as delayed extraction and ion reflectors—also known as reflectrons—to improve resolution and mass accuracy by compensating for initial kinetic energy spread among ions of the same mass. These enhancements have enabled routine performance specifications exceeding 15,000 in full-width at half-maximum (FWHM) resolution and sub-5 parts-per-million (ppm) mass accuracy when internal calibration standards are used. Despite these impressive capabilities, practical implementation reveals limitations that undermine the theoretical performance envelope, particularly in terms of reproducibility and consistency across replicate measurements.

## SUMMARY

Conventional MALDI-TOF mass spectrometers, while theoretically capable of high mass accuracy and resolution, frequently exhibit significant deviations in replicate mass measurements—even when internal calibration is applied—thereby compromising reliability in applications requiring precise mass determination, such as peptide mass fingerprinting or biomarker identification. These deviations manifest as uncorrelated errors across multiple peptides within a single spectrum and inconsistent results between repeated acquisitions of the same sample. The root cause of this variability lies not in the calibration algorithm itself, which has been shown to be deterministic and repeatable, but rather in the analog-to-digital (A/D) conversion process inherent to the detector system. Specifically, each acquisition cycle resets the temporal alignment of data bins relative to the ion flight times due to electronic jitter associated with the internal clock synchronization of the A/D converter. This discontinuous sampling introduces small but statistically significant shifts in measured peak positions, which propagate through the calibration function and ultimately degrade mass accuracy. To address this limitation, the present invention introduces a novel system and method for improving mass accuracy in MALDI-TOF mass spectrometry by leveraging the statistical properties of replicate spectral data. In one embodiment, the method comprises acquiring multiple independent spectra from the same sample under identical conditions, storing each spectrum as binned intensity data indexed by discrete time intervals, and then generating a composite spectrum through averaging of corresponding bins across all stored spectra. In another embodiment, the system includes a MALDI-TOF mass spectrometer operatively connected to a computing device configured to execute software instructions that automate the acquisition, storage, statistical analysis, and presentation of averaged spectral data. By exploiting the random nature of bin repositioning error—which follows a normal Gaussian distribution—the invention achieves enhanced mass accuracy and identifies potentially erroneous measurements through descriptive statistical metrics such as standard deviation and outlier detection tests.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

MALDI-TOF mass spectrometers operate by generating ions from a solid-phase sample co-crystallized with a UV-absorbing matrix, accelerating those ions into a flight tube, and measuring their arrival times at a detector to infer mass-to-charge ratios. Contemporary instruments typically employ an ion reflector to correct for variations in initial ion velocities, thereby increasing resolution and mass accuracy. However, despite these refinements, conventional MALDI-TOF systems suffer from intrinsic limitations related to the analog-to-digital conversion of ion signals. The detector output is sampled at discrete time intervals dictated by the internal clock of the A/D system, which partitions the continuous ion signal into fixed-width “bins” representing counts of ion impacts over defined temporal windows. Because the start of each acquisition sequence is synchronized independently with the laser pulse, minor timing discrepancies—referred to as jitter—cause slight shifts in the placement of these bins relative to the true ion arrival times. Although each shift may be less than the width of a single bin, the cumulative effect across multiple acquisitions leads to measurable inconsistencies in mass assignments, even when internal calibration is performed. This variability is exacerbated by the fact that calibration functions are derived from the same discretized data, meaning interpolation errors affect both unknown analytes and calibrant peaks simultaneously. Consequently, there exists a critical need for a method that mitigates the impact of binning-induced inaccuracies without requiring hardware modifications or changes to existing experimental protocols.

The operation of a MALDI-TOF mass spectrometer begins with the ionization of the sample via a pulsed laser, which triggers the release of analyte ions from the matrix surface. These ions are accelerated through an electric field into the flight region, where lighter ions arrive at the detector sooner than heavier ones. Upon impact, the ions generate electrical signals that are amplified and digitized by the A/D system. This system consists of a high-speed clock, typically operating in the gigahertz range, which governs the rate at which voltage levels are sampled and converted into digital values. Each digital value corresponds to a bin representing a narrow window of flight time, and the collection of bins forms a histogram-like representation of the ion signal intensity versus time. Due to the finite sampling rate, the resulting spectrum is inherently discontinuous, approximating what would otherwise be a smooth, continuous peak profile. The spacing between adjacent bins correlates directly with ion flight time and thus varies inversely with the square root of mass; hence, bin widths increase with higher m/z values. When the laser fires repeatedly during a single acquisition, the accumulated signal is summed across shots, but the bin positions remain fixed once the acquisition window is initiated. Crucially, however, when a new acquisition is started—whether manually or automatically—the bin grid is realigned based on the current state of the internal clock, introducing a small positional offset relative to prior acquisitions.

This discontinuity has profound implications for mass accuracy. Since peak fitting algorithms must interpolate between bins to estimate true peak centroids, any misalignment introduces systematic bias that cannot be corrected post-acquisition using standard calibration procedures. Moreover, because the magnitude and direction of the offset vary randomly from one acquisition to the next, the resulting mass errors appear stochastic and uncorrelated across replicates. Increasing the number of laser shots per acquisition improves signal-to-noise ratio but does not resolve the underlying binning artifact, as the bin structure remains static throughout the acquisition. Therefore, the only viable strategy to overcome this limitation is to treat each acquisition as an independent measurement subject to random error and apply statistical methods to extract a more accurate consensus value. Toward this end, the present invention discloses a multi-step process wherein the spectrometer is reset between successive data acquisitions, allowing the bin grid to reorient randomly with respect to the ion flight times. Multiple spectra are collected in this manner, each comprising a set of binned ion counts indexed by time. These individual spectra are then stored digitally and subsequently analyzed using statistical techniques appropriate for normally distributed data.

In practice, the method involves initiating a first spectral acquisition by firing the laser and detecting ion molecule impacts over a predefined data collection period governed by the internal clock. The resulting spectrum is stored in memory as a series of binned intensity values. The spectrometer is then reset—either automatically or via user command—and a second acquisition is performed under identical instrumental settings. This cycle is repeated a predetermined number of times (e.g., 10–25), yielding a population of independent spectra. Following acquisition, the stored spectra are processed by aligning corresponding bins and computing the arithmetic mean of intensities at each bin position. Optionally, additional statistical parameters such as standard deviation, variance, and outlier flags (e.g., via Grubbs’ test) are calculated to assess data quality and flag potentially unreliable measurements. The final composite spectrum, reflecting the averaged signal with reduced noise and improved centroid estimation, is then provided to the user for interpretation or further analysis. This approach effectively transforms the inherent randomness of bin repositioning from a source of error into a mechanism for enhancing precision through ensemble averaging.

An exemplary MALDI-TOF mass spectrometer system implementing the disclosed method includes a vacuum chamber housing an ion source, a flight tube equipped with an ion reflector, a microchannel plate detector, and an analog-to-digital converter linked to a control computer. The computer executes software that orchestrates laser triggering, data acquisition, binning, storage, and statistical processing. In one configuration, the system features a processor, random access memory (RAM), read-only memory (ROM), secondary storage (e.g., hard disk or solid-state drive), and input/output interfaces including display, keyboard, and network connectivity modules. The software may reside on a non-transitory computer-readable medium and include executable instructions for performing the steps of the invention. In more complex implementations, the system may comprise multiple interconnected computers or servers operating in a distributed or cloud-based environment, optionally utilizing virtualization technologies to allocate computational resources dynamically. Such architectures enable scalable deployment across laboratory networks or remote diagnostic platforms.

The transformation of a general-purpose computer into a specialized machine for executing the disclosed method occurs through the loading and execution of specific program code that configures the hardware to perform the unique sequence of operations described herein. This includes synchronizing laser pulses with the A/D clock, managing data buffers for bin storage, applying statistical models to spectral populations, and rendering composite outputs. The resulting system provides a robust solution to a long-standing problem in MALDI-TOF mass spectrometry, delivering performance closer to theoretical instrument specifications without necessitating costly hardware upgrades. Furthermore, the methodology is broadly applicable across different MALDI-TOF platforms and can be extended to other time-of-flight instruments employing similar A/D detection schemes, including LC-TOF and Q-TOF systems. Thus, the scope of the disclosure encompasses not only the specific embodiments detailed above but also any equivalent systems or methods that achieve improved mass accuracy through statistical aggregation of independently acquired, binned spectral data.
