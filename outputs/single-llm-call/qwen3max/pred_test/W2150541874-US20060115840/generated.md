# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

DNA arrays represent a powerful high-throughput genomic technology that enables the simultaneous measurement of the expression levels of thousands of genes across diverse biological conditions. These platforms have become indispensable tools in functional genomics, toxicology, pharmacology, and clinical diagnostics, offering researchers the ability to profile global transcriptional responses to stimuli such as chemical exposures, disease states, or developmental cues. The utility of DNA arrays lies in their capacity to detect relative changes in mRNA abundance by hybridizing labeled nucleic acid samples—typically cDNA or cRNA derived from biological sources—to immobilized nucleic acid probes affixed to a solid support. The resulting hybridization signals are quantified and interpreted to infer gene expression patterns, thereby facilitating the identification of biomarkers, pathways, and molecular mechanisms underlying biological phenomena.

Macroarrays and microarrays constitute the two primary categories of DNA arrays, distinguished chiefly by feature density and detection methodology. Macroarrays, historically developed earlier, typically consist of larger spots (often millimeters in diameter) printed on nylon membranes and are probed with radioactively labeled targets, followed by detection via autoradiography. In contrast, microarrays feature much smaller spot sizes (ranging from tens to hundreds of micrometers) and are fabricated on glass or silicon substrates, enabling higher probe density and compatibility with fluorescent labeling and laser-based scanning. Microarrays, particularly those employing oligonucleotide probes, offer superior sensitivity, reproducibility, and dynamic range, making them the preferred format for modern genomic investigations.

The microarray experiment process generally involves several key steps: (1) selection and design of gene-specific probes; (2) printing of these probes onto a solid support in a spatially defined grid; (3) extraction and labeling of RNA from experimental and reference samples; (4) hybridization of the labeled nucleic acids to the array under controlled conditions; (5) washing to remove non-specifically bound material; and (6) scanning and quantification of fluorescence signals. Data analysis then proceeds through image processing, background correction, normalization, and statistical testing to identify differentially expressed genes. Despite the sophistication of this workflow, the accuracy and reliability of microarray results are highly dependent on the quality of each step, particularly normalization, which corrects for systematic technical variations unrelated to biological differences.

Current microarray analyses suffer from significant limitations, especially when applied to focused or custom-designed arrays used in specialized fields such as toxicogenomics. Commercially available whole-genome microarrays often include thousands of genes irrelevant to the specific biological question under study, diluting statistical power and increasing the multiple-testing burden. Conversely, focused arrays—designed to interrogate a limited set of biologically relevant genes—frequently lack adequate internal controls, replicate features, and robust normalization strategies. This is particularly problematic in toxicological studies, where chemical stressors may induce widespread transcriptional changes, invalidating assumptions of global transcript stability that underpin many conventional normalization methods.

To address normalization challenges, external control targets have been introduced into microarray workflows. These are exogenous nucleic acid sequences—absent from the organism under study—that are spiked into the sample at known concentrations and hybridized against complementary probes on the array. By measuring the signal from these external controls, researchers can adjust for variations in labeling efficiency, hybridization kinetics, and scanner performance. However, existing implementations of external controls often rely on complex mixtures of multiple spike-in RNAs at varying concentrations, which increases cost, complexity, and potential for cross-hybridization or inconsistent behavior across the intensity range.

Moreover, the use of external controls in current systems is hampered by suboptimal probe design, inadequate spatial distribution, and insufficient replication. Many arrays place external controls in isolated regions, failing to account for local spatial artifacts such as uneven hybridization, edge effects, or print-tip variability. Additionally, the absence of a continuous dilution series spanning the full dynamic range—from background to saturation—limits the ability to perform intensity-dependent normalization across the entire spectrum of signal intensities.

These drawbacks motivate the need for an improved microarray system that integrates a robust, flexible, and spatially distributed external control architecture specifically tailored for focused arrays. Such a system must enable accurate normalization even when a large proportion of genes are differentially expressed, provide built-in quality control metrics for printing and hybridization, and maintain high sensitivity for low-abundance transcripts—all while remaining cost-effective and adaptable to diverse biological contexts.

International Patent Application WO2004/064482 discloses a method for normalizing microarray data using external RNA controls. However, this approach employs a mixture of multiple control RNAs at predetermined concentrations, requiring complex preparation and lacking the granularity needed for fine-tuned, intensity-resolved normalization. Furthermore, the control probes are not systematically integrated into the array layout to monitor spatial or printing artifacts, limiting their utility in quality assurance.

Current microarray analysis limitations thus persist in three key areas: (1) inadequate normalization strategies for focused arrays where housekeeping genes may be unstable and global assumptions fail; (2) insufficient technical controls to detect and correct for printing carryover, spatial hybridization bias, and background noise; and (3) lack of a unified, scalable framework that combines external controls, negative controls, and replicate features into a single, optimized design. The present invention directly addresses these gaps by introducing a novel array architecture centered on a single-gene external control dilution series printed directly onto the array surface, enabling precise, spatially aware, and dynamic-range-spanning normalization and quality control.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The object of the invention is to provide an improved microarray system and associated methods that overcome the limitations of current microarray technologies, particularly in the context of focused gene expression studies such as toxicogenomics. The invention achieves this by integrating a novel external control architecture directly into the array design, enabling robust normalization, enhanced quality control, and increased sensitivity without reliance on unstable internal references or complex multi-component spike-in systems.

The invention provides a microarray comprising a solid support onto which a plurality of nucleic acid probes are immobilized in a spatially defined pattern. These probes include sample probes—designed to hybridize to target nucleic acids from a biological sample of interest—as well as an external control probe derived from a nucleic acid sequence not endogenous to the organism under study. Critically, the external control probe is printed in a dilution series spanning a wide range of concentrations, from sub-threshold to saturating levels, thereby generating a continuous calibration curve across the full dynamic range of the detection system.

The invention further provides a hybridizing reagent comprising a labeled nucleic acid sample and a known amount of an external control target nucleic acid that is complementary to the external control probe. This external control target is added to the hybridizing mixture at a fixed concentration, ensuring that the observed signal variation across the dilution series arises solely from differences in probe concentration on the array, not from fluctuations in target input.

The addition of the external control target to the hybridizing mixture serves as a universal reference that enables normalization of technical variability arising from differences in labeling efficiency, hybridization conditions, scanner settings, and other experimental factors. Because the external control target is absent from the biological sample, its signal is unaffected by biological variation, making it an ideal anchor for data adjustment.

The external control probe is printed in duplicate or triplicate within each sub-grid of the array, and its dilution series is spatially randomized among the sample probes. This design ensures that every local region of the array contains representative points along the calibration curve, allowing for the detection and correction of spatial artifacts such as uneven hybridization, edge effects, or print-tip-specific biases. The printing of the external control probe in a systematic dilution series—ranging, for example, from 0.000015 μM to 100 μM—ensures that the resulting signal intensities cover the entire measurable range, from background to saturation, without gaps.

Variation in hybridization signal intensities across the array, whether due to technical or spatial factors, is addressed by the invention through the use of the external control dilution series as a real-time calibration standard. By fitting a curve to the observed signals from the external control spots, one can derive a normalization function that adjusts all sample probe signals based on their corresponding intensity-dependent correction factor. This approach is particularly effective for focused arrays where traditional global normalization methods fail due to widespread differential expression.

The advantages of the invention are manifold. First, it enables accurate normalization even when a large fraction of genes are differentially expressed, a common scenario in toxicology and stress response studies. Second, it provides built-in quality control metrics for assessing printing fidelity, cross-spot contamination, background noise, and hybridization uniformity. Third, it enhances sensitivity by allowing precise estimation of detection thresholds and signal-to-noise ratios. Fourth, it reduces costs by eliminating the need for multiple spike-in RNAs and enabling the use of custom, focused arrays with high statistical power. Finally, it improves reproducibility and comparability across experiments, slides, and laboratories.

The array system components include: (1) a solid support (e.g., glass slide); (2) a plurality of sample probes specific to genes of interest; (3) an external control probe printed in a dilution series; (4) negative control features (e.g., buffer-only spots, random oligonucleotide pools); and (5) optional housekeeping gene probes. The system is designed with spatial redundancy, featuring multiple sub-grids containing replicated probe sets to enhance statistical reliability.

The process of normalizing an array system according to the invention involves: (a) hybridizing a labeled sample containing a fixed amount of external control target to the array; (b) measuring the fluorescence intensities of all spots, including the external control dilution series; (c) fitting a curve (e.g., LOWESS) to the external control signals to model the expected relationship between probe concentration and signal intensity; and (d) applying this model to adjust the signals of sample probes based on their intensity level, thereby correcting for technical variation.

The invention also provides a kit for performing microarray experiments, comprising: (1) the microarray as described; (2) instructions for use; (3) optionally, the external control target nucleic acid; and (4) hybridization buffers and reagents. The kit enables standardized, high-quality gene expression profiling with integrated normalization and quality control.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE PREFERRED EMBODIMENT

### Definitions:

As used herein, the term “gene” refers to a segment of DNA that encodes a functional product, such as a protein or RNA molecule, including regulatory regions necessary for its expression. A “probe” is a nucleic acid sequence immobilized on a solid support that is capable of specifically hybridizing to a complementary target sequence. A “target” is a nucleic acid molecule in the sample that is labeled and hybridized to the array for detection. “Complement” refers to a nucleic acid sequence that is fully or partially base-paired with another sequence under hybridization conditions. An “oligonucleotide” is a short, single-stranded synthetic nucleic acid, typically 20–100 nucleotides in length. “Specifically hybridizing” means that a probe binds preferentially to its intended target over non-target sequences under defined stringency conditions. A “toxicant” is any chemical or physical agent that causes adverse biological effects. “Normalization” is the process of adjusting raw signal intensities to correct for non-biological technical variation. “External” refers to nucleic acid sequences not naturally occurring in the biological sample under study. A “hybridizing mixture” is the solution containing labeled nucleic acids applied to the array for hybridization. “Labelled” means covalently or non-covalently attached to a detectable moiety, such as a fluorophore. A “fluorescent label” is a dye that emits light upon excitation, commonly Cy3 or Cy5. An “array” is a solid support with spatially addressable nucleic acid probes. “Expression” refers to the process by which information from a gene is used to synthesize a functional product. A “nucleic acid” is a polymer of nucleotides, including DNA and RNA. A “polynucleotide” is a nucleic acid chain of significant length, typically over 100 nucleotides. A “kit” is a packaged set of components for performing a specific assay.

The kit components include the microarray, external control target RNA, hybridization buffers, labeling reagents, and user instructions. The array components comprise the solid support, sample probes, external control probe dilution series, and negative controls. The hybridizing mixture components include labeled cRNA from the sample and reference, external control target RNA, fragmentation buffer, and hybridization buffer.

### General Overview of the Invention

The invention introduces a paradigm shift in microarray design by embedding a single-gene external control dilution series directly onto the array surface. This approach decouples normalization from biological assumptions and provides a physical calibration standard for every experiment.

### Description of Preferred Embodiments

The array system consists of a glass slide onto which oligonucleotide probes are printed in a grid of 12 sub-grids, each replicated four times for a total of 48 sub-grids. The solid support is a PowerMatrix slide, chosen for its low autofluorescence and high binding capacity. The external control probe is a 70-mer oligonucleotide derived from Arabidopsis thaliana chlorophyll synthase, selected for its absence in mammalian genomes. The hybridizing mixture contains Cy5-labeled sample cRNA, Cy3-labeled reference cRNA, and 5 ng of in vitro transcribed A. thaliana chlorophyll synthase RNA. Sample probes are 70-mer oligonucleotides representing approximately 1600 mouse genes relevant to toxicological responses, including cytochrome P450s, DNA repair genes, and stress response elements.

Printing is performed using a Virtek ChipWriterPro robotic arrayer with 12 print tips, operating at 65% humidity. Each sub-grid has a 16×12 layout. The external control probe is printed in 18 dilutions ranging from 0.000015 μM to 100 μM, with each dilution printed in duplicate or triplicate. Sample probes are printed at 40 μM in ArrayIT spotting solution. Spatial randomization ensures that EC spots are evenly distributed among sample probes to capture local hybridization conditions. Duplicate printing of all probes enhances statistical reliability. Buffer spots (containing only spotting solution) assess background and carryover. Empty spots serve as additional negative controls. Housekeeping gene probes (e.g., β2-microglobulin) are included but not relied upon for normalization. A random pool of 70-mer oligonucleotides, validated to lack homology to mouse genes, serves as a negative control for non-specific binding. The EC dilution series is prepared as a continuous log-scale series to ensure smooth calibration curves.

Normalization involves measuring EC spot intensities, fitting a LOWESS curve to the log(concentration) vs. log(intensity) data, and using this curve to adjust sample probe signals. The amount of sample target is determined by comparing its normalized intensity to standard curves or reference samples. The kit includes the array, EC RNA, labeling kits, hybridization buffers, and software for normalization.

## EXAMPLES

### A. Materials and Methods

Genes predictive of toxicant exposure were selected from literature and in-house data, focusing on xenobiotic metabolism, oxidative stress, and apoptosis. Custom 70-mer oligonucleotides were synthesized with 5′-C6 amino modifications. The test set included 1600 mouse genes and 50 housekeeping genes. Arrays were printed using the ChipWriterPro, and hybridization was performed on a TECAN HS4800 station with 15 μg of fragmented cRNA per channel.

### B. Results

PowerMatrix slides and ArrayIT buffer yielded optimal spot morphology and signal intensity. The optimal oligo printing concentration was 40 μM. The EC series enabled robust normalization, outperforming standard LOWESS in RT-PCR validation. The EC oligo dilution series covered 0.000015–100 μM, producing a full-range calibration curve. Features were printed in randomized positions within sub-grids. Spatial periodicity in spot intensity revealed hybridization gradients, correctable via EC normalization. Print-tip differences caused intensity variation, mitigated by per-tip LOWESS. The ToxArray design included 48 sub-grids with EC and negative controls. Quality tests confirmed low carryover (0.6%) and high sensitivity (1:2,000,000). The quality test result showed that EC-normalized data had 79% agreement with RT-PCR, versus 32% for standard LOWESS.
