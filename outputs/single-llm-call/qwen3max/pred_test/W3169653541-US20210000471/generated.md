# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD

Arteriovenous grafts are synthetic vascular conduits surgically implanted to provide reliable and repeated vascular access for patients undergoing chronic hemodialysis due to end-stage renal disease. These grafts function by creating a direct connection between an artery and a vein, typically in the upper extremity, thereby enabling high-flow extracorporeal circulation through a dialysis machine. The most commonly used material for such grafts is expanded polytetrafluoroethylene (ePTFE), which offers biocompatibility, durability, and ease of percutaneous cannulation. Arteriovenous grafts are distinct from autogenous arteriovenous fistulas, which directly anastomose native vessels without synthetic material, and are often employed when a patient’s venous anatomy is unsuitable for fistula creation. The present invention relates specifically to the geometric design of arteriovenous grafts, with particular emphasis on the configuration of the venous-end anastomosis—the junction where the graft is surgically attached to the vein—as a critical determinant of long-term graft patency and resistance to thrombosis.

## BACKGROUND

Hemodialysis is a life-sustaining medical procedure required by over 300,000 individuals annually in the United States alone who suffer from end-stage renal disease. This therapy compensates for the loss of kidney function by removing metabolic waste products, excess fluids, and electrolytes from the bloodstream via an external filtration system known as a hemodialyzer. To facilitate this process, a high-flow vascular access site must be established to allow rapid withdrawal and return of blood during each dialysis session. While autologous arteriovenous fistulas represent the gold standard for vascular access due to superior long-term patency and lower complication rates, many patients—particularly those with advanced age, diabetes, or peripheral vascular disease—lack suitable superficial veins for fistula construction. In such cases, synthetic arteriovenous grafts serve as a necessary alternative. Despite decades of clinical use and material advancements, these grafts remain plagued by unacceptably high failure rates. Specifically, brachio-axillary arteriovenous grafts, which connect the brachial artery near the elbow to the axillary vein in the upper arm, exhibit primary patency rates as low as 40–57% at one year post-implantation. The predominant cause of graft failure is thrombosis, which frequently arises secondary to stenosis at the venous anastomosis. This stenosis is driven by pathological hemodynamic forces that induce endothelial injury, promote myointimal hyperplasia, and activate coagulation cascades. Current surgical practices leave the angle of venous anastomosis to the surgeon’s intraoperative discretion, based largely on anatomical constraints rather than hemodynamic optimization. Consequently, there exists a critical unmet need for an arteriovenous graft design that incorporates a scientifically validated, optimized venous anastomosis geometry to mitigate pathological flow patterns and enhance long-term patency.

## BRIEF SUMMARY

The present invention provides an optimized arteriovenous graft configured to reduce the incidence of pathological wall shear strain rates at the venous anastomosis, thereby improving graft patency and reducing the risk of thrombosis. The graft comprises a tapered tubular conduit formed from a biocompatible material, with a first end adapted for anastomosis to an artery and a second end adapted for anastomosis to a vein. Critically, the venous end of the graft is pre-formed or pre-cut at a specific anastomosis angle relative to the longitudinal axis of the graft, wherein this angle is selected to minimize regions of both pathologically low shear strain rates (≤50 s⁻¹) and pathologically high shear strain rates (≥1000 s⁻¹) along the adjacent venous wall. In preferred embodiments, the anastomosis angle is approximately 30 degrees. Additionally, the venous end may incorporate micro-digit grooves and/or a lachrymiform (teardrop-shaped) indent to further modulate the local flow field and reduce shear stress perturbations. The invention also encompasses methods of manufacturing such grafts and surgical techniques for their implantation using the pre-defined optimal geometry.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present patent disclosure describes an improved arteriovenous graft designed to address the persistent clinical challenge of early graft thrombosis due to suboptimal hemodynamics at the venous anastomosis. As used herein, “arteriovenous graft” refers to a synthetic vascular conduit that establishes a shunt between an artery and a vein to enable repeated vascular access for hemodialysis. “Anastomosis angle” denotes the acute angle formed between the central longitudinal axis of the graft and the central longitudinal axis of the vein at the point of surgical attachment. “Wall shear strain rate” refers to the rate at which adjacent layers of fluid move past one another parallel to a solid boundary—in this context, the inner wall of the vein—and is expressed in reciprocal seconds (s⁻¹). Pathologically low shear strain rates are defined as ≤50 s⁻¹, a range associated with fibrin deposition and thrombus formation, while pathologically high shear strain rates are defined as ≥1000 s⁻¹, which can cause endothelial denudation and platelet activation.

Life-sustaining hemodialysis requires the diversion of arterial blood through an extracorporeal circuit at flow rates typically ranging from 300 to 600 mL/min, though higher flows up to 1000 mL/min may be used. In brachio-axillary configurations, arterial inflow originates from the brachial artery (diameter 3–5 mm, peak velocity 60–100 cm/s), while venous outflow occurs into the axillary vein (diameter 6–10 mm, mean velocity ~15 cm/s). To prevent arterial steal syndrome—where excessive flow is diverted away from distal tissues—grafts are commonly tapered, for example from 4 mm at the arterial end to 7 mm at the venous end.

Various graft configurations exist, but the present invention focuses on straight, tapered conduits implanted in the upper arm. Graft dimensions are standardized to accommodate typical human vasculature: total length of approximately 150 mm, arterial end inner diameter of 4 mm, venous end inner diameter of 7 mm. However, the key innovation lies not in overall size but in the precise geometry of the venous anastomosis.

Clinical data indicate that up to 80% of graft failures originate from stenosis at the venous anastomosis, primarily due to disturbed flow fields that induce intimal hyperplasia. Computational fluid dynamics (CFD) analyses reveal that the anastomosis angle is a dominant factor influencing wall shear strain distribution. Angles greater than 40° produce excessive high shear zones, particularly on the distal venous wall opposite the graft inflow, while angles below 20° create large regions of stagnant or recirculating flow with critically low shear rates just downstream of the anastomosis.

To mitigate these effects, the present invention specifies an optimized anastomosis angle of approximately 30 degrees. At this angle, CFD simulations demonstrate a significant reduction in both high and low pathological shear regions compared to conventional 90° or highly oblique (<15°) configurations. The venous end of the graft is pre-cut or pre-molded during manufacturing to this fixed angle, eliminating reliance on intraoperative estimation and ensuring consistent, hemodynamically favorable geometry across patients.

Further refinement includes the incorporation of surface features at the venous end. Micro-digit grooves—shallow, radially oriented channels with depths of 0.1–0.3 mm and widths of 0.2–0.5 mm—are formed circumferentially near the anastomotic rim. These grooves disrupt coherent jet impingement and promote smoother flow transition into the vein. Additionally, a lachrymiform indent—a teardrop-shaped depression with a maximum depth of 0.4 mm and length of 2–4 mm oriented along the flow direction—is integrated at the apex of the anastomosis. This feature helps redirect the high-velocity arterial jet away from direct impact on the venous wall.

When combined, these geometric modifications work synergistically to homogenize the velocity profile, reduce flow separation, and maintain wall shear strain rates within the physiological range of 50–1000 s⁻¹ over the majority of the venous wall adjacent to the anastomosis. The graft is constructed from biocompatible materials such as expanded polytetrafluoroethylene (ePTFE), polyurethane, or silicone, all of which are well-established in vascular surgery.

The importance of the venous-end anastomosis angle cannot be overstated: it is the primary determinant of local hemodynamics and, consequently, the biological response of the venous wall. By tailoring this angle to 30 degrees and incorporating flow-modifying surface features, the present invention provides a method for enhancing graft patency and reducing thrombosis risk. Surgeons implanting such grafts select the appropriate pre-angled model based on intraoperative assessment of vein diameter and trajectory, then perform a standard end-to-side anastomosis without needing to manually shape the graft end.

Illustrative examples include grafts with venous anastomosis angles of 30°, 25°, and 35°, all demonstrating superior hemodynamic profiles compared to 90° or 13° controls. These optimized designs represent a paradigm shift from empirical surgical technique to engineered vascular access solutions grounded in computational biomechanics.

### EXAMPLES

Computational fluid dynamics (CFD) simulations were conducted to evaluate the hemodynamic performance of various arteriovenous graft designs. The flow field of blood within a brachio-axillary graft system was modeled under pulsatile conditions representative of human physiology. Blood was assumed to have a density of 1060 kg/m³ and a dynamic viscosity of 0.0035 Pa·s for initial Reynolds number estimation. Using a characteristic arterial diameter of 4 mm and a peak velocity of 1.5 m/s, the Reynolds number was calculated as approximately 1800, confirming laminar flow conditions suitable for transient CFD analysis.

Idealized graft geometries were constructed using ANSYS DesignModeler software. Each model featured a 4 mm arterial inlet, an 8 mm venous segment, and a tapered graft transitioning from 4 mm to 7 mm over 150 mm. Venous anastomosis angles were systematically varied by adjusting the major axis of the elliptical intersection between graft and vein, yielding angles of 90°, 60°, 45°, 30°, 15°, and 13°. Tapering was simulated to reflect clinical practice, and the venous segment was extended sufficiently (72 mm total) to capture fully developed flow upstream and downstream.

To refine hemodynamic performance, micro-digit grooves and a lachrymiform indent were digitally incorporated into the 30° graft model. A high-fidelity computational mesh was generated using ANSYS Fluent, with boundary layer inflation (seven prism layers, expansion ratio 1.2) to resolve near-wall gradients. Mesh independence was confirmed through iterative refinement until solution convergence was achieved with 200,000–250,000 elements.

Blood was modeled as a non-Newtonian, incompressible fluid governed by the Navier-Stokes equations. The Bird-Carreau constitutive law was applied to capture shear-thinning behavior, with parameters: zero-shear viscosity μ₀ = 0.0056 Pa·s, infinite-shear viscosity μ∞ = 0.0035 Pa·s, relaxation time λ = 3.313 s, and power index n = 0.3568. Boundary conditions included a pulsatile arterial inlet derived from clinical Doppler waveforms (Fourier series fit, 700 mL/min average flow), constant venous outflow (15 cm/s), no-slip walls, and rigid vessel assumptions.

Simulations were solved using ANSYS Fluent with SIMPLE/PISO pressure-velocity coupling, second-order spatial discretization, and a time step of 0.0025 s over one cardiac cycle (0.8 s). Hybrid initialization via Laplace’s equation ensured stable convergence.

Multiple examples were analyzed. Example 1 (90° anastomosis) showed extensive high shear (>1000 s⁻¹) on the distal venous wall during systole. Example 2 (13°) exhibited large low-shear (<50 s⁻¹) zones during diastole. Examples 3–6 (45° to 15°) demonstrated intermediate behavior. Example 7 (30°) revealed minimal pathological shear: high-shear regions were reduced by 62% and low-shear areas by 58% compared to 90°. Cross-sectional views at 0.1 s (peak high shear) and 0.3 s (peak low shear) confirmed optimal flow alignment.

Example 8 incorporated micro-digit grooves (0.2 mm deep, 0.3 mm wide) and showed further reduction in shear perturbations. Example 9 used color-coded wall shear maps to visualize the 30° graft’s uniform physiological shear distribution. Example 10 quantified metrics Γ_low and Γ_high—integrals of pathological low and high shear exposure—and demonstrated minima at 30°.

These results conclusively identify 30° as the optimal anastomosis angle for minimizing thrombogenic flow disturbances. The benefits include reduced endothelial injury, suppressed intimal hyperplasia, and prolonged graft patency. While the disclosure focuses on brachio-axillary grafts, the principles apply to other configurations. Modifications such as variable tapering or alternative materials are within scope.
