# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNICAL FIELD

The present invention relates to the field of aluminum production via electrolysis, and more particularly to carbonaceous anodes used in Hall-Héroult electrolytic cells. Specifically, the invention provides a novel method for manufacturing carbon anodes with enhanced resistance to oxidation by air and carbon dioxide during operation in aluminum smelting cells. The improved anodes are produced by impregnating coke particles—either partially or entirely—with a boron-containing aqueous solution prior to forming and baking the anode body. This pre-impregnation approach ensures that the protective boron species are distributed throughout the anode microstructure, thereby significantly reducing surface and bulk reactivity with atmospheric oxygen and CO₂ at typical anode exposure temperatures ranging from 400°C to 600°C. The resulting anodes exhibit markedly lower rates of carbon consumption, reduced dusting, and improved structural integrity during service, which collectively contribute to increased process efficiency, extended anode life, and decreased environmental emissions in industrial aluminum production.

## BACKGROUND

Carbonaceous anodes are essential consumable components in the Hall-Héroult process for primary aluminum production, where they serve both as electrical conductors and as reactants in the electrochemical reduction of alumina. During normal cell operation, the anode is exposed to high temperatures (typically 950–980°C in the bath) and reactive environments. However, the upper portion of the anode, which extends above the molten electrolyte, is exposed to ambient air at temperatures between 400°C and 600°C. Under these conditions, the carbon anode undergoes non-faradaic oxidation reactions with atmospheric oxygen, producing CO and CO₂ without contributing to aluminum production. These parasitic reactions represent a significant source of carbon overconsumption, leading to increased raw material usage, higher operating costs, and greater greenhouse gas emissions.

The air reactivity of carbon anodes, along with their reactivity toward CO₂ generated in the electrolytic reaction, constitutes the dominant mechanism of excess anode consumption beyond the stoichiometric requirement of the Hall-Héroult process. Conventional anodes are manufactured from a mixture of calcined petroleum coke (typically 60–75%), recycled anode butts (0–30%), and coal tar pitch binder (12–16%). After mixing, the paste is formed into green anodes and baked at approximately 1100–1200°C to carbonize the pitch and develop mechanical strength and electrical conductivity. Despite optimization of raw materials and processing parameters, the resulting anodes remain susceptible to oxidative attack due to their inherent porosity and the high reactivity of the pitch-derived binder matrix relative to the coarse coke aggregates.

Several strategies have been explored to mitigate this issue. One approach involves applying external physical barriers, such as coatings of alumina powder or solidified cryolite bath, which are commonly used in smelters to partially shield the anode surface. However, these coatings are porous and offer only transient, limited protection. More engineered solutions, such as alumina-based spray coatings developed by CSIRO, have shown promise in laboratory settings but have not achieved industrial adoption, likely due to cost, durability, or scalability constraints.

An alternative strategy focuses on reducing the intrinsic reactivity of the carbon itself through chemical modification. Doping with elements like phosphorus or boron has been shown to inhibit carbon oxidation. Phosphorus, however, is incompatible with the Hall-Héroult bath due to its detrimental impact on current efficiency and metal purity. Boron, in contrast, is considered acceptable, and three mechanisms have been proposed for its protective effect: (a) electronic modification of graphite layers through boron substitution, reducing electron density at reactive sites; (b) promotion of graphitization, which yields a more oxidation-resistant structure; and (c) formation of a boron oxide film that blocks active carbon sites. However, mechanisms (a) and (b) are largely irrelevant for conventional anodes, which are baked well below graphitization temperatures (~2500°C) and cannot incorporate sufficient boron substitutionally without prohibitively expensive elemental boron additives.

This leaves mechanism (c)—surface passivation by boron oxide—as the most viable pathway. Moltech and Alcan have previously patented methods involving post-baking impregnation or coating of anodes with boron-containing solutions, often combined with binders or other additives. While effective in small-scale tests, these approaches face practical challenges in industrial implementation, particularly regarding uniform penetration into large anode blocks and long-term coating adhesion under thermal cycling. Moreover, laboratory testing typically uses small cores (e.g., 50 mm diameter), which may overestimate impregnation efficacy due to favorable surface-to-volume ratios not representative of full-scale anodes (which can exceed 1.5 meters in height). Thus, despite promising results, no boron-based protection method has been widely deployed in commercial smelters.

The present invention addresses these limitations by integrating boron protection at the particle level during anode manufacturing, ensuring homogeneous distribution and deep penetration without reliance on post-baking treatments.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides a process for manufacturing a carbonaceous anode for use in aluminum electrolysis cells, wherein the anode exhibits reduced reactivity toward air and carbon dioxide. The process comprises contacting coke particles with a boron-containing solution to produce boron-impregnated coke particles, mixing the boron-impregnated coke particles with coal tar pitch to form an anode paste, and forming a green anode from the anode paste. In one embodiment, the coke particles are divided into a first fraction and a second fraction, and only the first fraction is contacted with the boron-containing solution prior to mixing with pitch and the second fraction. In another embodiment, both fractions are impregnated, optionally after grinding and sieving to achieve desired particle size distributions. The boron-containing solution may comprise boric acid or boron oxide dissolved in water, typically at concentrations ranging from 0.5 to 5 wt%, and impregnation may be carried out at temperatures from ambient to boiling. Following impregnation, the boron-impregnated coke particles are dried to remove solvent, then combined with pitch and formed into a green anode, which may subsequently be baked at 1000–1300°C to yield the final carbonaceous anode. The resulting anode contains boron species distributed throughout its microstructure, particularly within the binder-rich regions, providing enhanced oxidation resistance without compromising electrical or mechanical properties.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The scope of the present invention encompasses methods for producing oxidation-resistant carbon anodes and the anodes themselves. As used herein, the term “about” means within ±10% of a stated value unless otherwise indicated. The invention is based on the discovery that impregnating coke particles—especially fine fractions—with a boron-containing aqueous solution prior to anode fabrication leads to superior protection against air and CO₂ reactivity compared to post-fabrication coating methods. This is because the boron species become integrated into the internal pore network of the particles and, upon mixing with pitch and baking, reside at critical interfaces where oxidation initiates.

The process begins with the preparation of boron-impregnated coke particles. Calcined petroleum coke, optionally blended with recycled anode butts, is provided in particulate form. The coke may be pre-calcined at 1200–1350°C to remove volatiles. In a preferred embodiment, the coke is separated into at least two size fractions: a fine fraction (e.g., <1 mm) and a coarse fraction (e.g., 1–20 mm). Only the fine fraction is impregnated, as it constitutes the majority of the binder matrix and is most vulnerable to oxidation. Alternatively, both fractions may be impregnated to maximize protection.

Impregnation is performed by immersing the coke particles in an aqueous solution containing boric acid (H₃BO₃) or boron oxide (B₂O₃) at concentrations of 0.5–5 wt%, more preferably 1–3 wt%. The solution may be heated to 50–100°C to enhance penetration. Contact time ranges from 1 to 60 minutes, with longer durations (e.g., 10–20 min) yielding greater boron loading and better protection. After impregnation, the particles are dried at 80–150°C to evaporate water, precipitating boron hydroxide or oxide within the pores. The dried, boron-impregnated particles are then mixed with coal tar pitch (typically 10–16 wt% of total mix) to form an anode paste. The paste is compacted into molds to produce green anodes, which may be baked at 1000–1300°C in inert atmosphere to carbonize the pitch and develop final properties. The resulting anode contains 50–500 ppm boron, uniformly distributed, and exhibits air reactivity reduced by up to 95% compared to untreated anodes.

## EXAMPLES

### Example 1: Anode Preparation

Anode samples were prepared using industrial-grade calcined petroleum coke (65 wt%), recycled anode butts (22 wt%), and coal tar pitch (13 wt%). For treated anodes, the fine coke fraction (<1 mm) was impregnated by immersion in a 2 wt% boric acid solution at 50°C for 15 minutes, followed by drying at 100°C overnight. The impregnated fines were then mixed with coarse coke and pitch to form a paste, which was compacted into cylindrical molds (50 mm diameter) to produce green anodes. These were baked at 1200°C for 20 hours under nitrogen. Untreated control anodes were prepared identically but without boron impregnation.

### Example 2: Anode Characterization

Treated and untreated anodes were analyzed using Time-of-Flight Secondary Ion Mass Spectrometry (ToF-SIMS), confirming the presence of boron throughout the impregnated samples. X-ray fluorescence (XRF) showed boron content of approximately 200 ppm. X-ray diffraction (XRD) revealed no significant change in crystallite height (Lc), indicating that boron did not alter the graphitic structure, consistent with the absence of high-temperature graphitization.

### Example 3: Air Reactivity Measurements

Air reactivity tests were conducted in a muffle furnace at 500°C under flowing air (4 L/min). Samples (100 g) were weighed periodically over 160 hours. Untreated anodes exhibited a burning rate of ~4%/h and disintegrated completely by 40 hours. Treated anodes showed a rate of ~0.2%/h, retaining structural integrity even after 6 days, with only 30% mass loss after 100 hours.

### Example 4: CO₂ Reactivity Measurements

CO₂ reactivity was assessed by exposing samples to 100% CO₂ at 970°C for 1 hour. Mass loss was measured before and after. Treated anodes showed 40% lower CO₂ reactivity than controls, demonstrating cross-protection against both oxidants.

### Example 5: Electrical Resistivity Measurements

Electrical resistivity was measured using a four-point probe method. Treated anodes exhibited resistivity of 65 μΩ·m, comparable to untreated anodes (62 μΩ·m), confirming that boron impregnation does not impair electrical performance.
