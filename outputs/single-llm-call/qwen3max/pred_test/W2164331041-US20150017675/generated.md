## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

Cell penetrating peptides (CPPs) represent a class of short polypeptides that possess the remarkable ability to traverse cellular membranes and facilitate the intracellular delivery of diverse molecular cargoes, including nucleic acids, proteins, and small molecules. These peptides have garnered significant interest in both diagnostic and therapeutic contexts due to their capacity to bypass conventional endocytic pathways and achieve direct cytosolic access. Among the earliest and most extensively studied CPPs is the Model Amphipathic Peptide (MAP), originally designed by Steiner and colleagues. MAP comprises the amino acid sequence KLALKLALKALKAALKLA-NH₂ and is characterized by its propensity to adopt an alpha-helical conformation in which hydrophobic residues align along one face of the helix while positively charged lysine residues occupy the opposing face. This amphipathic architecture underpins its interaction with lipid bilayers and contributes to its efficient translocation across plasma membranes.

The mechanisms governing the internalization of CPPs such as MAP remain a subject of active scientific inquiry. Initial studies suggested a non-endocytic mode of entry based on observations of peptide uptake at low temperatures (e.g., 0°C) and under conditions of cellular energy depletion, which typically inhibit classical endocytosis. However, subsequent investigations revealed a more nuanced picture: while certain inhibitors of endocytosis partially reduced uptake, they did not abolish it entirely, indicating the involvement of both energy-dependent and energy-independent pathways. Quantitative analyses have shown that, upon exposure to cells, approximately 50% of cell-associated MAP remains membrane-bound, 30% inserts into the lipid bilayer, and only about 20% achieves full internalization into the cytoplasm. Notably, experiments using giant unilamellar vesicles—membrane systems devoid of endocytic machinery—demonstrated that MAP can still penetrate lipid bilayers, confirming a genuine capacity for direct translocation. Once internalized, MAP distributes between the cytosol and nucleus, enabling its utility as a delivery vector for nuclear-targeted agents.

The functional versatility of MAP has been demonstrated in numerous applications. For instance, MAP conjugated to polylysine effectively delivered siRNA into cells, outperforming commercial transfection reagents like Lipofectamine®. In another study, MAP was linked via a disulfide bond to peptide nucleic acid (PNA), and this construct successfully silenced gene expression; notably, co-treatment with chloroquine enhanced efficacy, implying partial entrapment in endosomes and the potential for redox-triggered release upon disulfide reduction in the reducing cytosolic environment. Similarly, cytochrome c conjugated to MAP through a reducible disulfide linkage induced apoptosis upon proteasome inhibition, whereas a non-reducible thioether linkage failed to do so, underscoring the critical role of intracellular reduction in cargo activation. These findings collectively highlight the importance of redox-sensitive linkages in controlling the bioactivity of CPP-conjugated therapeutics. Building upon this foundation, the present invention introduces a novel conjugate—termed “reductide”—that exploits the redox-dependent cleavage of a disulfide bond within a CPP-cargo construct to simultaneously report on both cellular uptake and intracellular redox state.

### Invention

The present invention provides novel conjugates for detecting and monitoring the cellular redox state in living cells. These conjugates, referred to herein as “reductides,” are bifunctional molecules comprising two distinct segments linked by a redox-sensitive disulfide bond. The first segment consists of a cell-penetrating peptide (CPP), specifically the Model Amphipathic Peptide (MAP) or a functional variant thereof, labeled with a first fluorophore—preferably 5(6)-carboxytetramethylrhodamine (TAMRA)—at its N-terminus. The second segment comprises a non-cell-penetrating cargo peptide, such as CLKANL, labeled with a second fluorophore—preferably fluorescein amidite (FAM)—also at its N-terminus. The two segments are covalently joined via a disulfide bond between cysteine residues present in each moiety. This design creates a fluorescence resonance energy transfer (FRET) pair wherein TAMRA acts as a quencher of FAM fluorescence when the disulfide bond is intact. Upon cellular internalization and exposure to reducing conditions, the disulfide bond is cleaved, separating the two fluorophores and relieving quenching, thereby generating a measurable increase in FAM emission.

The cell-penetrating peptide segment facilitates rapid and efficient entry into a wide variety of mammalian cells, including fibroblasts and cardiomyocytes. Exemplary CPPs include MAP (KLALKLALKALKAALKLA-NH₂), TAT (GRKKRRQRRRPQ), penetratin (RQIKIWFQNRRMKWKK), and transportan, as well as derivatives thereof that retain membrane-translocating activity. The cargo peptide, while lacking intrinsic cell-penetrating ability, serves as a reporter whose intracellular release is contingent upon reduction of the disulfide linker. Representative cargo peptides include CLKANL, GGGC, or other short sequences terminating in cysteine to enable disulfide formation. The redox-sensitive linkage is specifically a disulfide bond, which is stable in oxidizing extracellular environments but readily reduced in the reducing cytosol, where glutathione (GSH) concentrations are high.

The method of detecting cellular redox state involves contacting live cells with the reductide conjugate under physiological conditions, allowing time for uptake and potential reduction, and then measuring the fluorescence signals from both TAMRA and FAM. TAMRA fluorescence reports on total cellular association and internalization of the conjugate, independent of redox state, while FAM fluorescence specifically indicates successful disulfide reduction and thus reflects the intracellular reducing capacity. By comparing the ratio or absolute intensities of these two signals over time, one can monitor dynamic changes in cellular redox status. This method is applicable to real-time optical imaging using fluorescence microscopy, high-throughput screening in multiwell plates via plate readers, and quantitative single-cell analysis by flow cytometry.

Applications of the reductide conjugate and associated methods are extensive. They include the discovery and validation of redox-modifying agents, such as antioxidants or pro-oxidants, by assessing their impact on FAM signal intensity. The system is particularly useful in identifying cardiac antiarrhythmic agents that modulate redox balance in cardiomyocytes, given the critical role of redox signaling in cardiac electrophysiology. Additionally, the conjugate can be adapted for in vivo optical imaging by using near-infrared fluorophores, or for scintigraphic imaging by replacing fluorophores with radionuclide chelators, thereby enabling non-invasive assessment of tissue redox states in disease models.

### Materials and Methods

Reagents used in the preparation and validation of reductide include N-acetylcysteine (NAC), 1-chloro-2,4-dinitrobenzene (CDNB), L-buthionine sulfoximine (BSO), diamide, reduced glutathione (GSH), oxidized glutathione (GSSG), Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM), fetal bovine serum (FBS), puromycin, and the Screen-Well™ REDOX compound library. Peptide synthesis was performed using standard Fmoc solid-phase chemistry. The Cys-MAP-TAMRA and Cys-CLKANL-FAM moieties were synthesized separately, purified by reverse-phase HPLC, and characterized by mass spectrometry. The two purified peptides were then combined under controlled oxidative conditions to form the disulfide-linked reductide conjugate.

Human BJ and IMR90 fibroblasts and rat H9c2 cardiomyocytes were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS and essential amino acids. Stable H9c2 cell lines expressing glutaredoxin-1–redox-sensitive green fluorescent protein (Grx1-roGFP) were generated via retroviral transduction and selected with puromycin. For in vitro redox assays, reductide was diluted into Tris-buffered saline (TBS) containing defined ratios of GSH and GSSG to mimic physiological redox potentials, and fluorescence was monitored over time using a plate reader with appropriate excitation/emission filters (485/528 nm for FAM; 530/590 nm for TAMRA).

Live-cell fluorescence microscopy was conducted on cells seeded in chambered coverglasses, treated with redox modulators, and imaged over several hours using a confocal microscope maintained at 37°C and 5% CO₂. Reductide performance was benchmarked against roGFP by performing sequential imaging and plate reading on the same cell populations. Plate reader assays in 96-well formats enabled quantification of FAM signal in response to various redox-altering treatments, with parallel assessments using monochlorobimane (for GSH levels) and Alamar Blue (for viability). Flow cytometry was employed to analyze single-cell TAMRA and FAM fluorescence intensities following reductide incubation under different redox conditions. Statistical analyses were performed using Student’s t-test for pairwise comparisons and one-way ANOVA for multiple groups, with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation, and significance is denoted accordingly in figures and tables.

### Statistical Analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted to evaluate the significance of observed differences in fluorescence signals across experimental conditions. For comparisons involving two groups, an unpaired, two-tailed Student’s t-test was applied. When more than two groups were compared, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by post-hoc testing (e.g., Tukey’s test) was used to determine which specific groups differed significantly. All experiments were repeated at least twice with triplicate measurements per condition. A p-value less than 0.05 was deemed statistically significant, and a p-value less than 0.01 was considered highly significant. Data variability is represented as standard deviation around the mean, and graphical representations include error bars reflecting this dispersion.

## Results

### Effects of GSH/GSSG on Reductide Redox-Dependent Fluorescence

Reductide was designed such that FAM fluorescence is quenched by proximal TAMRA when the disulfide bond remains intact. In acellular buffer systems, addition of reductide to solutions containing reduced glutathione (GSH) resulted in a time-dependent increase in FAM emission at 528 nm upon excitation at 485 nm, confirming disulfide reduction and fluorophore separation. When the buffer contained a fixed total glutathione pool (5 mM, calculated as [GSH] + 2×[GSSG]) but varying GSH/GSSG ratios, the rate and magnitude of FAM signal development were inversely proportional to the concentration of oxidized glutathione (GSSG). Specifically, buffers with higher GSSG content exhibited slower fluorescence kinetics and lower maximal FAM intensity, consistent with a more oxidizing environment impeding disulfide reduction. In contrast, TAMRA emission at 590 nm (excitation 530 nm) increased with GSH concentration but showed no time dependence, indicating that TAMRA signal reflects total reductide presence rather than redox status. In live-cell imaging, BJ fibroblasts treated with the reducing agent NAC displayed earlier and stronger TAMRA and FAM signals compared to those treated with the oxidant CDNB, suggesting redox-dependent modulation of both uptake and reduction. Over time, the FAM-labeled cargo peptide was observed to accumulate in exocytic vesicles and eventually be expelled into the extracellular space, while the TAMRA-labeled CPP remained intracellularly retained. This differential trafficking underscores the utility of dual-fluorophore design in distinguishing internalization from reduction events.

### Flow Cytometry

Flow cytometric analysis of IMR90 fibroblasts incubated with reductide revealed a time-dependent increase in both TAMRA and FAM fluorescence intensities. Cells pretreated with NAC exhibited the highest TAMRA signal, consistent with enhanced uptake under reducing conditions. FAM signal, though detectable, showed less temporal resolution due to active export of the cleaved cargo peptide—a phenomenon corroborated by microscopy. Importantly, no increase in cell death markers (e.g., DAPI positivity or altered side-scatter profiles) was observed in reductide-treated cells, even under oxidative stress, confirming that the conjugate is non-toxic at working concentrations.

### Reductide Response to a Small Library of Redox Modifying Compounds

Screening of 84 redox-modifying compounds from the Screen-Well™ library demonstrated that reductide is highly responsive to pharmacological perturbations of cellular redox state. At a screening concentration of 50 μM over 24 hours, 77.4% of compounds (primarily classified as antioxidants) significantly increased FAM signal, indicating a net reductive shift. Conversely, 10.7% of compounds decreased FAM signal, suggesting pro-oxidant effects. Notably, some compounds induced cell death yet still produced elevated FAM signals, demonstrating that reductide response is not merely a surrogate for viability. Dose-response re-evaluation of select compounds (e.g., carvedilol, GERI-BP002A) confirmed that apparent pro-oxidant effects at high concentrations could reverse to antioxidant effects at lower doses, highlighting the importance of concentration in redox pharmacology.

## Discussion

### Reductide Uptake as Well as Reduction Depends on Cellular Redox State

The reductide system reveals that both cellular internalization and intracellular disulfide reduction are influenced by the cellular redox environment. While disulfide cleavage is inherently redox-sensitive, the observation that TAMRA signal—reporting total conjugate uptake—also varies with redox state indicates that the initial translocation step is redox-modulated. This is supported by live imaging showing earlier TAMRA appearance in reduced cells and by plate reader assays demonstrating diminished FAM signal when redox modifiers are applied after reductide internalization. These findings challenge the notion that CPP uptake is purely passive and suggest a regulatory role for redox-sensitive membrane properties, possibly involving electrical potential gradients modulated by redox-active ion channels.

### Pro-Oxidants Activate an Antioxidative Response

Treatment with low-dose hydrogen peroxide (200–400 μM) paradoxically increased reductide FAM signal after 24 hours, indicative of an adaptive antioxidative response. This biphasic effect—reduction at low doses, oxidation at high doses—is consistent with literature showing that sublethal oxidative stress upregulates Nrf2-mediated antioxidant genes, thioredoxin, catalase, and glutathione synthesis. In contrast, high-dose H₂O₂ overwhelms these defenses, leading to net oxidation and decreased FAM signal. The discordance between reductide signal and Alamar Blue viability readouts further confirms that redox state and cell survival are distinct parameters. This adaptive response underscores the physiological relevance of reductide in capturing dynamic, homeostatic redox regulation rather than static oxidative damage.
