# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

The intestinal barrier is a critical physiological structure that maintains homeostasis between the internal environment of the host and the external luminal contents of the gastrointestinal tract. This barrier is composed of multiple interdependent components, including a mucus layer secreted by goblet cells, a single layer of epithelial cells interconnected by tight junctions, and a dense network of resident immune cells in the lamina propria. Together, these elements function to prevent the uncontrolled translocation of luminal pathogens, toxins, microbial metabolites, and dietary antigens into systemic circulation. The integrity of this barrier is essential for normal digestive function, immune tolerance, and overall health. Disruption of the intestinal barrier—commonly referred to as “leaky gut” or increased intestinal permeability—has been implicated in a wide array of pathological conditions, ranging from localized gastrointestinal disorders to systemic inflammatory and neurodegenerative diseases. Loss of intestinal barrier function can result from various insults, including chronic inflammation, oxidative stress, dysbiosis of the gut microbiota, dietary factors such as high-fat or high-sugar intake, and aging. When barrier integrity is compromised, luminal contents gain access to the submucosal compartment, triggering immune activation, systemic inflammation, endotoxemia, and potentially contributing to the pathogenesis of metabolic syndrome, autoimmune diseases, and even neurodegenerative conditions through the gut-brain axis. Given the central role of the intestinal barrier in maintaining systemic health, therapeutic strategies aimed at preserving or restoring its integrity represent a promising avenue for the prevention and treatment of numerous chronic diseases. The present invention addresses this need by identifying a specific molecular source of oxidative stress—mitochondrial superoxide production at site IIIQo of complex III—and demonstrating that targeted suppression of this source using S3QELs (Suppressors of site IIIQo Electron Leak) effectively mitigates diet-induced and age-related increases in intestinal permeability in both invertebrate and mammalian models.

## SUMMARY

The present invention introduces S3QELs—small-molecule compounds specifically designed to suppress electron leak and superoxide generation at site IIIQo of mitochondrial complex III without inhibiting normal electron transport or oxidative phosphorylation. These compounds have been discovered to significantly reduce intestinal permeability in models of metabolic stress and aging. In experimental studies, administration of S3QELs to Drosophila melanogaster fed a high-yeast extract diet resulted in a marked reduction in intestinal permeability, as measured by the Smurf assay, along with a concomitant decrease in enterocyte apoptosis and a 10–20% extension of median lifespan. Similarly, in mice fed a high-fat diet (60% kcal from fat), oral administration of S3QELs significantly reduced intestinal permeability, as evidenced by decreased plasma levels of FITC-dextran and reduced fecal albumin, while also restoring expression of tight junction and mucin genes in the colon and distal small intestine. These findings demonstrate that mitochondrial superoxide production at site IIIQo is a key driver of intestinal barrier dysfunction under conditions of nutritional excess. Based on these discoveries, the invention provides multiple embodiments of methods for treating or preventing increased intestinal permeability. Embodiment 1 encompasses a method for treating intestinal permeability in a subject by administering an effective amount of an agent that suppresses superoxide generation at site IIIQo of mitochondrial complex III. Embodiment 2 specifies that the agent partially suppresses superoxide generation without inhibiting mitochondrial respiration. Embodiment 3 identifies the agent as an S3QEL. Embodiment 4 defines the S3QEL as containing a sulfanyloxoquinazoline structural group. Embodiments 5 through 22 enumerate specific S3QEL compounds, including but not limited to S3QEL1.2, S3QEL2.2, S3QEL3, and structurally related analogs featuring pyrazolopyrimidine or other heterocyclic cores known to confer site IIIQo specificity. Embodiment 23 incorporates a delivery vehicle to enhance bioavailability or target intestinal epithelial cells. Embodiment 24 specifies that the S3QEL is formulated in a lipid or liposome-based system to improve stability and absorption in the gastrointestinal tract. Collectively, these embodiments provide a robust therapeutic platform for addressing intestinal barrier dysfunction across a spectrum of clinical conditions.

## Definitions

As used herein, the term “mammal” refers to any member of the class Mammalia, including but not limited to humans, non-human primates, rodents (e.g., mice, rats), canines, felines, bovines, ovines, and porcines. The term “subject” denotes a mammal, preferably a human, undergoing treatment or prophylaxis according to the methods described herein. An “effective amount” is that quantity of an active agent sufficient to produce a desired biological or therapeutic response, which may be prophylactic or therapeutic in nature. A “therapeutically effective amount” specifically refers to a dose that ameliorates one or more symptoms of a disease or condition associated with increased intestinal permeability. A “prophylactically effective amount” is a dose that prevents or delays the onset of such a condition. The term “treatment” includes both therapeutic and prophylactic interventions aimed at reducing, stabilizing, or preventing the progression of intestinal barrier dysfunction. “Mitigating” means reducing the severity, frequency, or duration of symptoms or pathological markers associated with increased intestinal permeability. The “intestinal barrier” refers to the functional and structural integrity of the gastrointestinal epithelium, including mucus layers, tight junctions, adherens junctions, and the underlying immune surveillance system. “Intestinal permeability” denotes the degree to which the intestinal barrier allows passage of luminal substances into the systemic circulation, typically measured by the translocation of non-digestible markers such as FITC-dextran, lactulose, or albumin.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

Experimental evidence demonstrates that S3QELs protect against intestinal permeability and reduce apoptotic cell numbers in enterocytes. In Drosophila fed a high-yeast diet, S3QEL administration halved the incidence of intestinal permeability and reduced apoptosis by approximately 50%, without affecting cell proliferation. In mice fed a high-fat diet, S3QELs significantly lowered plasma FITC-dextran and fecal albumin levels, indicating restored barrier integrity. These effects were accompanied by normalized expression of tight junction proteins (e.g., occludin, claudins) and mucins (e.g., Muc2), as well as the transcription factor Klf4, which regulates goblet cell differentiation. Superoxide production from complex III in enterocytes was identified as a key mediator, as genetic knockdown of Sod1 or Sod2 in enterocytes increased permeability and apoptosis, which were reversed by S3QELs. The invention thus provides a method for the treatment or prophylaxis of age-related and/or pathology-associated increases in intestinal barrier permeability by administering an S3QEL. Specific embodiments include methods for treating diet-induced intestinal permeability (e.g., due to high-fat or high-sugar diets), age-related increases in permeability, and pathology-associated permeability linked to conditions such as celiac disease, ulcerative colitis, irritable bowel syndrome, Parkinson’s disease, and Alzheimer’s disease. Therapeutic treatment according to the invention ameliorates one or more symptoms of these pathologies, including systemic inflammation, endotoxemia, glucose intolerance, and cognitive decline, by targeting the root cause of barrier dysfunction—mitochondrial superoxide overproduction.

### Celiac Disease and Ulcerative Colitis (UC)

Intestinal barrier dysfunction is a hallmark of celiac disease and ulcerative colitis (UC). In celiac disease, gluten exposure triggers zonulin release, leading to tight junction disassembly and increased paracellular permeability, which permits gliadin peptides to cross the epithelium and activate adaptive immune responses. Similarly, in UC, chronic inflammation disrupts epithelial integrity through proinflammatory cytokines (e.g., TNF-α, IFN-γ), which downregulate tight junction proteins and induce epithelial apoptosis. Increased apoptotic events and insufficient mucosal healing further exacerbate barrier loss. Prion protein mislocalization has also been observed in IBD, correlating with barrier defects. In irritable bowel syndrome (IBS), particularly post-infectious IBS, increased permeability is linked to low-grade inflammation and altered epithelial structure, including microvillus shortening and tight junction remodeling. The present invention addresses these conditions by suppressing mitochondrial superoxide-driven signaling that amplifies inflammation and barrier breakdown.

### Parkinson's Disease and Alzheimer's Disease.

Emerging evidence implicates the gut-brain axis in neurodegenerative diseases. In Parkinson’s disease (PD), gut dysbiosis and increased intestinal permeability precede motor symptoms, allowing bacterial endotoxins like LPS to enter circulation, activate the innate immune system, and promote α-synuclein aggregation in the enteric nervous system. Adaptive immune disturbances, including T-cell infiltration, further contribute to neuroinflammation. In Alzheimer’s disease (AD), microbiome-derived LPS has been detected in amyloid plaques, suggesting that gut-derived endotoxins may seed or accelerate neurodegeneration. By restoring intestinal barrier integrity, S3QELs may reduce systemic endotoxemia and mitigate neuroinflammatory cascades.

### Active Agents.

The invention involves administering S3QELs, which selectively inhibit superoxide production at site IIIQo of complex III—a major source of ROS in mitochondria. High-throughput screening identified S3QELs as non-toxic, non-inhibitory modulators of electron flow. Illustrative S3QELs include S3QEL1.2, S3QEL2.2, and S3QEL3, commercially available or synthesizable via established routes. Desirable properties include oral bioavailability, intestinal epithelial targeting, and lack of interference with redox signaling at other sites.

### Permeability Assays

Permeability is assessed using non-digestible markers. FITC-dextran (4 kDa) is administered orally; serum levels reflect paracellular leakage. The lactulose-mannitol test measures urinary excretion ratios to assess small intestinal permeability. Fecal albumin indicates transcellular leakage. Biomarkers include zonulin (tight junction regulator), calprotectin (neutrophil marker), A1AT (protein-losing enteropathy), LPS (endotoxemia), EndoCAb (barrier damage), and plasma D-lactate (bacterial translocation).

### Pharmaceutical Formulations.

S3QELs are formulated as pharmaceutically acceptable salts (e.g., hydrochloride, sodium) or esters/amides to enhance solubility. Compositions include carriers, carbohydrates, antioxidants, chelators, lipids, and excipients. Oral formulations may be enteric-coated for delayed release. Typical daily doses range from 0.1 to 100 mg/kg, administered once or twice daily. Liposomal or emulsion systems enable sustained release.

### Kits.

Kits contain S3QELs in vials or blister packs, with instructions for use, dosing guidelines, and warnings regarding potential adverse reactions. Packaging ensures stability and compliance.

## EXAMPLES

### Example 1

The intestinal epithelial barrier is vital for health, and its dysfunction is driven by oxidative stress. Using Drosophila and mice on high-nutrient diets as models, we showed that dietary yeast extract (YE) in flies increases intestinal permeability (Smurf assay), apoptosis, and proliferation, correlating with shortened lifespan. Gene expression analysis confirmed upregulation of antimicrobial peptides (Dpt, Drs, Def), Upd3, and septate junction genes. Antioxidant genes (Sod1, Sod2, Cat) were also elevated, indicating oxidative stress. We hypothesized mitochondrial superoxide from complex III site IIIQo as the source. S3QELs (S3QEL1.2, S3QEL2.2, S3QEL3) co-administered with 5% YE diet reduced permeability by ~50%, halved apoptotic cells, and extended lifespan by 10–20%, without affecting food intake. S1QELs had no effect, confirming site specificity. In enterocyte-specific Sod1/Sod2 knockdown flies, S3QELs rescued permeability, apoptosis, and lifespan. Succinate feeding worsened phenotypes, implicating succinate-driven superoxide. Hif-1α stabilization was identified as a downstream mediator: Hif-1α knockdown improved, and overexpression worsened, outcomes; S3QELs were most effective when Hif-1α was overexpressed. In mice on 60% high-fat diet, S3QELs reduced FITC-dextran and fecal albumin, restored tight junction and mucin gene expression, and alleviated ER stress, without altering body weight or glucose tolerance—suggesting hyperglycemia acts upstream of superoxide. Methods included fly husbandry, Smurf assay, Acridine Orange/Ethidium Bromide staining for apoptosis, PH3 staining for proliferation, qRT-PCR, and mouse gavage with FITC-dextran, fecal albumin ELISA, and glucose tolerance tests. Results confirm that S3QELs protect the intestinal barrier by targeting mitochondrial superoxide at site IIIQo, offering a novel therapeutic strategy for barrier-related diseases.
