# DESCRIPTION

The present invention introduces a novel class of retro-inverso (RI) peptides designed for the treatment of cancer, particularly by targeting the metastatic cascade. These peptides are engineered to inhibit key biological processes that drive tumor progression, including cell migration, invasion, and angiogenesis. Unlike conventional linear peptides derived from natural protein sequences, which are often rapidly degraded in physiological environments, the retro-inverso peptides of the invention exhibit enhanced metabolic stability due to their all-D-amino acid composition and inverted backbone orientation. This structural modification preserves the spatial arrangement of critical side-chain functionalities necessary for receptor interaction while rendering the peptides resistant to proteolytic cleavage. The invention specifically targets the interaction between the urokinase plasminogen activator receptor (uPAR) and Formyl Peptide Receptor 1 (FPR1), a signaling axis known to promote tumor cell motility and metastasis. By disrupting this interaction, the retro-inverso peptides effectively block downstream signaling pathways that facilitate extracellular matrix degradation, endothelial transmigration, and neovascularization—hallmarks of aggressive cancers such as sarcomas, melanomas, and epithelial ovarian carcinomas. The lead compound, RI-3, demonstrates potent inhibitory activity at nanomolar to picomolar concentrations, significantly reducing tumor growth, vascular infiltration, and circulating tumor cell dissemination in preclinical models without affecting normal cell proliferation or viability. Thus, the retro-inverso peptides represent a promising therapeutic strategy for anti-metastatic intervention, offering improved pharmacokinetic properties and high specificity compared to earlier peptide-based inhibitors.

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

Cancer remains a leading cause of mortality worldwide, with metastatic disease accounting for the vast majority of cancer-related deaths. Despite advances in surgical techniques, radiation therapy, and molecularly targeted agents, current treatments predominantly address primary tumor burden and often fail to prevent systemic dissemination. A major limitation in oncology is the lack of effective therapies that specifically target the metastatic process—a complex, multistep phenomenon involving local invasion, intravasation into blood or lymphatic vessels, survival in circulation, extravasation, and colonization of distant organs. Traditional chemotherapeutics and many targeted drugs primarily inhibit cell proliferation but do not effectively impede the migratory and invasive capabilities of malignant cells, which are central to metastasis.

Cell motility is a fundamental biological process essential for embryonic development, wound healing, and immune surveillance. However, when dysregulated in pathological contexts such as cancer, it becomes a driver of metastasis. Tumor cells acquire the ability to detach from the primary mass, degrade the surrounding extracellular matrix (ECM), and migrate directionally toward chemoattractants present in the microenvironment. This process, known as cell migration, is tightly coordinated through dynamic cytoskeletal rearrangements and integrin-mediated adhesion turnover. In solid tumors, aberrant activation of motility pathways enables cancer cells to invade adjacent tissues and enter the vasculature, initiating the metastatic cascade.

A critical mediator of cell migration in cancer is the urokinase plasminogen activator receptor (uPAR). uPAR is a glycosylphosphatidylinositol (GPI)-anchored cell surface protein composed of three domains (DI–DIII) that binds urokinase plasminogen activator (uPA), thereby localizing plasmin generation to the cell surface. Plasmin, in turn, degrades ECM components and activates other proteases, facilitating tissue invasion. Beyond its role in pericellular proteolysis, uPAR functions as a signaling hub by forming complexes with integrins, vitronectin, and G protein-coupled receptors—most notably the Formyl Peptide Receptors (FPRs). Elevated levels of soluble uPAR in serum correlate strongly with poor prognosis and increased metastatic risk across multiple cancer types, underscoring its clinical relevance as a prognostic marker.

Formyl Peptide Receptors (FPRs) are a family of seven-transmembrane G protein-coupled receptors originally identified in phagocytic leukocytes for their role in sensing bacterial formylated peptides. Among them, FPR1 has emerged as a key player in tumor biology. FPR1 is expressed not only in myeloid cells but also in various non-hematopoietic cancer cells, where it responds to endogenous ligands—including fragments derived from uPAR—to trigger intracellular signaling cascades that promote chemotaxis, invasion, and angiogenesis. Activation of FPR1 leads to phosphorylation of kinases such as Akt, ERK1/2, and p38 MAPK, which regulate cytoskeletal dynamics, gene expression, and cell survival.

Given the pivotal role of the uPAR–FPR1 axis in driving metastasis, disrupting this interaction represents a rational therapeutic strategy. Previous efforts yielded linear peptide antagonists based on the uPAR84–95 sequence (e.g., SRSRY), which mimics a natural FPR1 agonist. Substitution of Ser90 with Glu generated inhibitory peptides like RERF that block FPR1 activation and reduce migration. However, these peptides suffer from significant limitations: they are susceptible to rapid enzymatic degradation in human serum, limiting their in vivo half-life, and some exhibit off-target toxicity due to unintended interactions with integrins.

To overcome these drawbacks, the retro-inverso (RI) concept was applied. In an RI peptide, the amino acid sequence is reversed, and all residues are replaced with their D-enantiomers. This inversion maintains the topological orientation of side chains—critical for receptor binding—while reversing the direction of the peptide backbone amide bonds. Although this may disrupt main-chain hydrogen bonding patterns, the all-D configuration confers exceptional resistance to proteolysis. While successful RI analogs have been reported in other contexts, their applicability is not guaranteed and must be empirically validated.

Prior art peptides targeting uPAR/FPR1, including UPARANT and RERF, demonstrate proof-of-concept but lack optimal drug-like properties. There exists a clear need for new peptide therapeutics that combine high potency, metabolic stability, and minimal toxicity. Preventing the uPAR–FPR1 interaction offers a selective means to inhibit metastasis-associated signaling without broadly suppressing FPR1’s physiological roles. Retro-inverso peptides thus hold significant promise as next-generation anti-metastatic agents, provided they retain biological activity while overcoming the pharmacokinetic shortcomings of their L-peptide predecessors.

## DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides novel retro-inverso (RI) peptides that function as potent antagonists of the uPAR–FPR1 interaction, thereby inhibiting cancer cell migration, invasion, and angiogenesis. These peptides are specifically designed to mimic the spatial disposition of side-chain functional groups found in previously identified uPAR-derived inhibitory sequences, while incorporating structural modifications that enhance stability and bioavailability. The general formula of the peptides encompasses tetrapeptides and tripeptides composed entirely of D-amino acids, arranged in reverse sequence relative to their parent L-peptides, and optionally capped with aromatic acyl groups to preserve key pharmacophoric elements.

Preferred compounds within the scope of the invention include RI-1 (Ac-D-Tyr-D-Arg-D-Glu-D-Arg-NH₂), RI-2 (Ac-D-Tyr-D-Arg-D-Ser-D-Arg-NH₂), RI-3 (Ac-D-Tyr-D-Arg-Aib-D-Arg-NH₂), RI-4 (Phenylacetyl-D-Arg-Aib-D-Arg-NH₂), and RI-5 (3-Phenylpropionyl-D-Arg-Aib-D-Arg-NH₂), wherein Aib denotes α-aminoisobutyric acid, a non-natural, α,α-disubstituted amino acid that enhances conformational rigidity. Among these, RI-3 exhibits superior biological activity and stability, making it the lead candidate for therapeutic development.

The peptides are synthesized using standard solid-phase peptide synthesis (SPPS) methodologies employing Fmoc/t-Bu chemistry. Following chain assembly on resin, the peptides are cleaved, purified by reversed-phase high-performance liquid chromatography (RP-HPLC), and characterized by ultra-performance liquid chromatography coupled with mass spectrometry (UPLC-MS) to confirm identity and purity. The incorporation of D-amino acids and Aib ensures resistance to exo- and endoproteases, a critical feature for in vivo efficacy.

Biologically, the RI peptides act by competitively inhibiting the binding of uPAR-derived agonists (such as SRSRY or fMLF) to FPR1. This blockade prevents FPR1 activation, internalization, and downstream signaling through PI3K/Akt and MAPK pathways. Consequently, the peptides suppress directional cell migration induced by serum or chemotactic stimuli. In vitro assays demonstrate that RI-3 inhibits fibrosarcoma HT1080 cell migration with an IC₅₀ in the femtomolar range and reduces Matrigel invasion by over 50% at 10 nM. Importantly, this effect is not due to cytotoxicity or antiproliferative activity, as confirmed by MTS and real-time proliferation assays.

Furthermore, the peptides inhibit angiogenesis—the formation of new blood vessels that supply nutrients to growing tumors. RI-3 blocks both fetal bovine serum (FBS)- and VEGF-induced endothelial tube formation in human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs), reducing capillary-like structure formation by up to 75%. This anti-angiogenic property directly contributes to reduced tumor vascularization observed in vivo.

The therapeutic potential extends beyond sarcomas to other malignancies that co-express uPAR and FPR1, including melanoma and epithelial ovarian carcinoma. Additionally, the peptides inhibit trans-endothelial migration of tumor cells and monocytes, a critical step in intravasation and inflammatory recruitment to tumor sites. Due to their dual action on both tumor and endothelial cells, the RI peptides may serve as theranostic agents—combining diagnostic imaging (e.g., via fluorescent tagging) with therapeutic intervention in metastatic disease.

## EXAMPLE 1. SYNTHESIS OF THE RETRO-INVERSO PEPTIDES RI-1, RI-2, RI-3, RI-4, RI-5

The retro-inverso peptides RI-1 through RI-5 were synthesized using automated solid-phase peptide synthesis on a Rink amide MBHA resin with Fmoc-protected D-amino acids. For RI-1, the sequence Ac-D-Tyr-D-Arg-D-Glu-D-Arg-NH₂ was assembled by sequential coupling of Fmoc-D-Arg(Pbf)-OH, Fmoc-D-Glu(OtBu)-OH, Fmoc-D-Arg(Pbf)-OH, and Fmoc-D-Tyr(tBu)-OH using HBTU/HOBt/DIEA activation in N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF). After final Fmoc deprotection, the N-terminus was acetylated with acetic anhydride in DMF. Cleavage from the resin and side-chain deprotection were achieved using a cocktail of trifluoroacetic acid (TFA)/water/triisopropylsilane (95:2.5:2.5) for 2 hours at room temperature. The crude peptide was precipitated in cold diethyl ether, dissolved in water/acetonitrile, and purified by preparative RP-HPLC on a C18 column using a gradient of 5–60% acetonitrile in 0.1% TFA over 30 minutes. Lyophilization yielded white powders with >95% purity as confirmed by analytical HPLC and UPLC-MS (observed MH⁺: 664.4).

RI-2 (Ac-D-Tyr-D-Arg-D-Ser-D-Arg-NH₂) was synthesized similarly, substituting Fmoc-D-Ser(tBu)-OH for the glutamic acid derivative. Purification and characterization confirmed the expected mass (MH⁺: 620.4). RI-3 (Ac-D-Tyr-D-Arg-Aib-D-Arg-NH₂) required incorporation of Fmoc-Aib-OH, a sterically hindered residue that necessitated extended coupling times (2 × 60 min) and double activation. Despite this, high yield and purity were achieved (MH⁺: 622.3). RI-4 and RI-5, being tripeptides, omitted the N-terminal D-Tyr and instead used phenylacetic acid or 3-phenylpropionic acid for N-terminal capping to mimic the aromatic side chain of tyrosine. These were coupled after full chain assembly using PyBOP/DIEA in DMF. Final products RI-4 (MH⁺: 533.5) and RI-5 (MH⁺: 547.5) were obtained in high purity following identical cleavage and purification protocols.

## EXAMPLE 2. RETRO-INVERSO PEPTIDES DO NOT PROMOTE CELL MIGRATION

To assess whether the RI peptides possess intrinsic agonist activity, human fibrosarcoma HT1080 cells—which express high levels of uPAR and FPR1—were subjected to real-time migration analysis using the xCELLigence RTCA system. Cells were seeded in upper chambers of CIM-plates, and lower chambers contained either 10 nM N-formyl-methionyl-leucyl-phenylalanine (fMLF, a known FPR1 agonist), 10 nM of each RI peptide, or vehicle control. Impedance changes, reported as Cell Index, were monitored continuously for 18 hours. fMLF induced a robust and sustained increase in Cell Index, reflecting active cell migration toward the chemoattractant. In stark contrast, none of the RI peptides (RI-1 to RI-5) elicited any significant change in Cell Index above baseline levels observed in serum-free medium. This result demonstrates that the RI peptides lack intrinsic chemotactic activity and do not activate FPR1 or related motility pathways, confirming their antagonist nature.

## EXAMPLE 3. RETRO-INVERSO PEPTIDE RI-3 INTERFERES WITH FPR1 BIOLOGICAL ACTIVITY BY BLOCKING AGONIST/FPR1 INTERACTION AND BY PREVENTING AGONIST-INDUCED FPR1 INTERNALIZATION

To evaluate RI-3’s mechanism of action, competitive binding and internalization assays were performed using rat basophilic leukemia RBL-2H3 cells stably transfected with human FPR1 (RBL-2H3/ETFR). Cells were pre-incubated at 4°C (to prevent internalization) with vehicle, excess unlabeled fMLF (positive control), RERF (known antagonist), scrambled ERFR (negative control), or RI-3, followed by exposure to fluorescein-labeled fMLF (FITC-fMLF). Fluorescence intensity, measured by plate reader, showed that RI-3 reduced FITC-fMLF binding to levels comparable to fMLF and RERF, indicating direct competition for the FPR1 ligand-binding site. In parallel experiments conducted at 37°C to allow internalization, confocal microscopy revealed that untreated cells exhibited punctate intracellular fluorescence indicative of FPR1 internalization upon FITC-fMLF stimulation. Pre-treatment with RI-3 prevented this internalization, with fluorescence remaining diffuse at the plasma membrane—similar to the effect of RERF. Western blot analysis further showed that RI-3 inhibited fMLF-induced phosphorylation of Akt at Ser473, a key downstream effector of FPR1 signaling. These findings collectively confirm that RI-3 acts as a functional antagonist by blocking both ligand binding and subsequent receptor internalization and signaling.

## EXAMPLE 4. RETRO-INVERSO PEPTIDES INHIBIT CELL MIGRATION IN A DOSE DEPENDENT MANNER

Dose-response studies were conducted using Boyden chamber assays to quantify the inhibitory effect of RI-3 on HT1080 cell migration toward 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS). Cells were placed in the upper chamber, and lower chambers contained FBS alone or FBS supplemented with increasing concentrations of RI-3 (10⁻¹⁵ to 10⁻⁶ M). After 4 hours, migrated cells on the lower filter surface were fixed, stained, and counted. RI-3 inhibited migration in a concentration-dependent manner, with significant reduction observed at 10⁻¹³ M and maximal inhibition (~50%) achieved at 10⁻⁹ M. Real-time monitoring via xCELLigence confirmed these results, showing progressive attenuation of migration kinetics with increasing RI-3 concentration. The IC₅₀ for migration inhibition was determined to be approximately 1 × 10⁻¹³ M, highlighting the extraordinary potency of RI-3.

## EXAMPLE 5. RETRO-INVERSO PEPTIDES INHIBIT CELL INVASION IN A DOSE DEPENDENT MANNER

Invasion assays were performed using Matrigel-coated Boyden chambers to assess the ability of RI-3 to block HT1080 cell penetration through a reconstituted basement membrane. Cells were seeded in serum-free medium in the upper chamber, and lower chambers contained 10% FBS with or without RI-3 at concentrations ranging from 10⁻¹³ to 10⁻⁷ M. After 6 hours, invading cells were quantified. RI-3 significantly reduced invasion in a dose-dependent fashion, with an IC₅₀ of ~1 × 10⁻¹¹ M and ~50% inhibition at 10⁻⁹ M. Real-time invasion tracking over 20 hours using E-plates coated with Matrigel corroborated these findings, demonstrating sustained suppression of invasive behavior throughout the assay period. These results confirm that RI-3 effectively impedes the proteolytic and motile capabilities required for tumor cell invasion.

## EXAMPLE 6. RETRO-INVERSO PEPTIDE RI-3 DOES NOT AFFECT CELL PROLIFERATION

To rule out cytostatic or cytotoxic effects as the basis for reduced migration and invasion, HT1080 cell proliferation was assessed in the presence of 10 µM RI-3—a concentration far exceeding the IC₅₀ for migration inhibition. Using the MTS colorimetric assay, cells were cultured for 96 hours with daily medium replacement containing RI-3 or vehicle. Absorbance readings at 24-hour intervals showed no difference in metabolic activity between treated and control groups. Parallel experiments using xCELLigence real-time proliferation monitoring over 85 hours yielded identical growth curves. These data conclusively demonstrate that RI-3 does not impair cell viability or proliferation, confirming that its anti-migratory and anti-invasive effects are specific to motility pathways.

## EXAMPLE 7. RETRO-INVERSO PEPTIDE RI-3 IS STABLE IN HUMAN SERUM

Stability in biological fluids is critical for peptide therapeutics. To evaluate RI-3’s resistance to proteolysis, the peptide was pre-incubated at 10⁻³ M in human serum at 37°C for 18 hours, then tested in Matrigel invasion assays. HT1080 cells exposed to serum-preincubated RI-3 retained 81% of the inhibitory activity compared to freshly prepared RI-3. In parallel, real-time migration assays using bovine serum-preincubated RI-3 showed overlapping Cell Index curves with non-incubated peptide, indicating no loss of function. These results confirm that RI-3 is highly stable in serum, a key advantage over earlier L-peptide inhibitors that degrade rapidly under similar conditions.

### EXAMPLE 8. RETRO-INVERSO PEPTIDE RI-3 PREVENTS MATRIGEL INVASION BY HUMAN SARCOMA, MELANOMA AND EPITHELIAL OVARIAN CARCINOMA CELLS

The broad applicability of RI-3 was tested across multiple cancer cell lines expressing uPAR and FPR1. Human fibrosarcoma HT1080, osteosarcoma Saos-2, chondrosarcoma Sarc, melanoma A375, and epithelial ovarian carcinoma OVCAR-3 cells were subjected to Matrigel invasion assays in the presence of 10 nM RI-3. Western blotting confirmed variable but detectable expression of uPAR and FPR1 in all lines except Saos-2 (low uPAR). RI-3 significantly inhibited invasion in HT1080 (64%), Sarc (47%), A375 (58%), and OVCAR-3 (52%) cells, while showing minimal effect on Saos-2 (24%), consistent with its dependence on uPAR/FPR1 co-expression. These findings establish RI-3 as a pan-inhibitor of invasion in uPAR/FPR1-positive malignancies.

### EXAMPLE 9. RETRO-INVERSO PEPTIDE RI-3 PREVENTS IN VITRO TRANS-ENDOTHELIAL MIGRATION BY SARCOMA, MELANOMA AND EPITHELIAL OVARIAN CARCINOMA CELLS AS WELL AS BY HUMAN MONOCYTES

Trans-endothelial migration (TEM)—a surrogate for intravasation/extravasation—was assessed using HUVEC monolayers grown to confluence on E-plates. GFP-labeled Sarc, A375, or OVCAR-3 cells, or human THP-1 monocytes, were added to the endothelial layer with or without 10 nM RI-3. Real-time impedance monitoring over 10 hours showed that RI-3 reduced TEM by 24–35% across all cell types. Confocal z-stack imaging confirmed fewer tumor cells penetrating beneath the endothelium in RI-3-treated samples. This demonstrates RI-3’s ability to block a critical step in hematogenous metastasis and inflammatory cell recruitment.

### EXAMPLE 10. RETRO-INVERSO PEPTIDE RI-3 INHIBITS BOTH FBS AND VEGF-TRIGGERED ENDOTHELIAL TUBE FORMATION

Angiogenesis was modeled by seeding HUVECs on Matrigel in the presence of pro-angiogenic stimuli: 10% FBS or 40 ng/mL VEGF₁₆₅, with or without 10 nM RI-3. After 6 hours, tube networks were imaged and quantified by counting cord-like structures >100 µm. FBS and VEGF increased tube formation by 352% and 620% over baseline, respectively. RI-3 reduced these responses by 61% and 75%, indicating potent anti-angiogenic activity. This effect likely contributes to the reduced tumor vascularization observed in vivo.

### EXAMPLE 11. RETRO-INVERSO PEPTIDE RI-3 EXERTS ANTI-METASTATIC EFFECT REDUCING IN VIVO TUMOR GROWTH AND VASCULAR INFILTRATION BY SARCOMA CELLS

In a xenograft model, immunodeficient nude mice were subcutaneously injected with 1 × 10⁶ GFP-tagged Sarc cells. Starting one day post-implantation, mice received daily intraperitoneal injections of 6 mg/kg RI-3 or vehicle for 10 days. RI-3 treatment significantly reduced tumor volume (185 ± 82 mm³ vs. 330 ± 163 mm³ in controls) and weight without affecting body weight, indicating good tolerability. Immunohistochemistry revealed decreased CD31-positive microvessel density in RI-3-treated tumors, confirming anti-angiogenic effects in vivo. Moreover, circulating tumor cell (CTC) counts, quantified by Alu-sequence qPCR, were markedly lower in RI-3-treated mice (2.6 ± 2.7 CTCs/mL vs. 10.04 ± 3.7 in controls), with 50% of treated animals showing no detectable CTCs. In a separate 12-day study, RI-3—but not the comparator RERF—completely prevented lung metastasis formation, as confirmed by MacroFluo imaging and H&E staining. These results validate RI-3 as a potent anti-metastatic agent in vivo.
