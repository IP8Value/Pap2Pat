## BACKGROUND

The innate immune system employs an array of sentinel proteins known as pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) that detect and respond to microbe-associated molecular patterns and danger-associated molecular patterns. These molecular patterns frequently originate from intracellular pathogens, particularly viruses, and include various pathogen constituents or replication intermediates. Upon engagement with these molecular signatures, PRRs initiate signaling cascades that lead to the expression or processing of cytokines, chemokines, and effector molecules that directly inhibit microbial replication while simultaneously facilitating adaptive immune responses. Consequently, PRRs constitute a critical first line of immunological defense against infection and represent attractive targets for both microbial immune evasion strategies and pharmacologic manipulation for therapeutic intervention.

A primary outcome of PRR-mediated signaling is the synthesis and secretion of interferon (IFN) proteins, which include multiple subtypes of IFNα and β (collectively termed type I IFN) as well as IFNλ1–3 (type III IFN). These interferons exert their biological effects through cognate cell surface receptors that trigger phosphorylation cascades involving Janus kinases (Jak1, Tyk2) and signal transducer and activator of transcription factors (STAT1/2), ultimately amplifying the expression of antiviral effector genes and other immune stimulatory molecules conventionally designated as interferon-stimulated genes (ISGs). The PRR-mediated expression of IFNβ is particularly well characterized and requires the phosphorylation of the transcription factor interferon regulatory factor 3 (IRF3) by the serine kinases TANK-binding kinase 1 (TBK1) and I kappa B kinase ε (IKKε). This phosphorylation event occurs primarily through signaling pathways that utilize specific adaptor proteins serving as integration points for upstream PRRs.

Stimulator of interferon genes (STING), also known as MITA, TMEM173, MPYS, or ERIS, functions both as a pattern recognition receptor for cyclic dinucleotides through a binding pocket in its C-terminal cytoplasmic domain and as an adaptor molecule for multiple cytoplasmic receptors that detect double-stranded DNA. Other well-characterized adaptor proteins include TIR-domain-containing adaptor-inducing IFNβ (TRIF or TICAM1), which is required for signals initiated by Toll-like receptors 3 and 4, and interferon promoter stimulator 1 (IPS-1, also called MAVS, VISA, or Cardif), which is employed by the cytoplasmic RNA sensors RIG-I and MDA5. Given the fundamental importance of these pathways in innate immune activation and antimicrobial protection, they have become the focus of extensive research aimed at understanding their physiological roles and harnessing their therapeutic potential.

Pharmacologic stimulation of the interferon system represents a promising broad-spectrum antiviral strategy due to the ability of interferons to render cells and tissues refractory to replication of diverse virus types while coordinating adaptive immune responses. Various approaches to achieve this therapeutic goal have been explored, including the use of Toll-like receptor agonists that have demonstrated efficacy in blocking replication of certain chronic viruses and enhancing vaccine immunity. Similarly, synthetic nucleic acids that stimulate the RIG-I/MDA5/IPS-1 pathway can be employed for antiviral outcomes against diverse acute viral infections. Notably, two synthetic small molecules—10-carboxymethyl-9-acridanone (CMA) and 5,6-dimethylxanthenone-4-acetic acid (DMXAA)—have been identified as capable of activating the STING pathway, demonstrating antiviral activity against multiple viruses, including drug-resistant strains. DMXAA additionally exhibits other immunotherapeutic properties, including vaccine adjuvanticity, anti-angiogenic vascular disruption promoting tumor necrosis, and immune-mediated clearance of solid tumors. However, both CMA and DMXAA were subsequently discovered to function exclusively in murine cells and tissues, lacking efficacy in human systems, which ultimately led to their failure in clinical trials. Despite the high therapeutic potential of targeting the STING pathway, no published biological characterization of novel synthetic molecular entities capable of activating human STING-dependent innate responses existed prior to the present invention.

Members of the Alphavirus genus represent significant public health threats as mosquito-transmitted agents that are re-emerging worldwide and can cause substantial morbidity and mortality. Chikungunya virus (CHIKV), despite its evolutionary origin in the Old World, has experienced severe outbreaks in the Caribbean, Central, and South America since its introduction to the Western hemisphere in December 2013, with over one million suspected and confirmed cases reported. CHIKV disease is characterized by debilitating joint pain that can persist for months to years. Venezuelan equine encephalitis virus (VEEV), a related New World alphavirus, has caused numerous outbreaks in South and Central America as well as southern Texas, with fatality rates approaching 20% overall and reaching up to 35% in children. Critically, no FDA-approved antiviral drugs or vaccines currently exist for either virus. Both alphaviruses demonstrate extreme sensitivity to type I interferon, and as RNA-based viruses, their infection triggers IRF3/IFN activation via the IPS-1 pathway, suggesting they may lack robust evasion mechanisms directed against the cytoplasmic DNA-based STING pathway. This vulnerability presents a compelling opportunity for therapeutic intervention through pharmacologic activation of IRF3/IFN via STING stimulation. The present invention addresses this unmet medical need through the identification and characterization of a novel small molecule capable of stimulating IRF3 phosphorylation and interferon production in human cells, effectively preventing alphavirus replication. Through comprehensive reverse genetic studies employing CRISPR/Cas9-mediated gene editing, the invention demonstrates that this molecule requires STING for its innate gene induction and antiviral activity, representing the first synthetic compound definitively capable of activating the STING pathway in human cells. Furthermore, in vivo stimulation of the STING pathway has been shown to prevent CHIKV replication, establishing the therapeutic potential of pharmacologically targeting this critical immunological protein.

## SUMMARY

Stimulator of interferon genes (STING) functions as both a pattern recognition receptor and an essential adaptor protein in innate immune signaling pathways that culminate in the activation of interferon regulatory factor 3 (IRF3) and subsequent interferon production. STING-dependent signaling represents a crucial mechanism for host defense against various pathogens and has emerged as a promising target for therapeutic intervention across multiple disease contexts. Small molecules capable of activating the STING pathway can induce potent antiviral and antitumor immune responses, making them valuable candidates for pharmaceutical development. The present invention discloses the first synthetic small molecule STING activator that demonstrates specific activity in human cells, addressing a critical gap in the field where previously identified compounds such as CMA and DMXAA exhibited exclusive murine specificity.

The invention provides methods for preventing alphavirus replication through administration of compositions containing the novel STING-activating compound designated G10. This compound effectively blocks replication of both Old World alphaviruses such as Chikungunya virus (CHIKV) and New World alphaviruses such as Venezuelan equine encephalitis virus (VEEV), demonstrating broad-spectrum antiviral activity against clinically relevant pathogens for which no approved therapeutics currently exist. The invention further encompasses in vivo stimulation of the STING pathway as a viable therapeutic strategy, with demonstrated efficacy in preventing viremia in animal models of alphavirus infection when administered within appropriate temporal windows relative to viral exposure.

Additionally, the invention provides methods for identifying STING agonists through high-throughput screening approaches combined with comprehensive genetic validation using CRISPR/Cas9-mediated gene editing to confirm pathway specificity. This systematic approach enables the discovery and characterization of novel compounds that specifically target the human STING pathway, overcoming the limitations of previous murine-specific activators. The therapeutic applications extend beyond antiviral treatment to include potential uses in cancer immunotherapy, vaccine adjuvanticity, and other immune-mediated conditions where controlled activation of the STING pathway could provide clinical benefit. The invention thus represents a significant advancement in the field of innate immune modulation, providing both a novel therapeutic agent and a validated methodology for discovering additional human-specific STING activators.

## EXAMPLES

### Example 1 Identification of a Novel IFN/IRF3-Inducing Molecule by High Throughput In Vitro Screening

A high-throughput in vitro screening methodology was employed to discover novel small molecules capable of stimulating innate immune signaling in human cells. Human fibroblasts stably transfected with constitutively expressed human telomerase reverse transcriptase (THF cells) and engineered to express luciferase downstream of a promoter element responsive to both type I IFN-dependent and IRF3-dependent transcription (THF-ISRE cells) were utilized as the screening platform. These cells were plated in 384-well format and screened against a library of 51,632 chemically diverse compounds tested in duplicate. Each screening plate included sixteen positive control wells treated with 1000 U/mL IFNβ and sixteen negative control wells treated with 1% DMSO to establish baseline luminescence readings. Compound activity was designated as significant if the luminescence reading exceeded fifty percent of the difference between the average positive and negative control readings. Among the compounds demonstrating significant activity, a molecule designated G10, chemically identified as 4-(2-chloro-6-fluorobenzyl)-N-(furan-2-ylmethyl)-3-oxo-3,4-dihydro-2H-benzo[b][1,4]thiazine-6-carboxamide, exhibited the third highest luminescence signal across all screened compounds. Subsequent cytotoxicity assessment revealed that G10 maintained low cytotoxicity even at high concentrations, supporting its selection for comprehensive biological characterization. This screening approach successfully identified a novel compound capable of inducing innate immune responses through IRF3-dependent pathways, representing a significant advancement in the discovery of human-specific STING pathway activators.

### Example 2—G10 Induces IFN/IRF3- but not NF-κB-Dependent Transcription in Human Fibroblasts

Validation studies confirmed that G10-mediated induction of IFN/IRF3-dependent luciferase expression occurred in a dose-dependent manner in THF-ISRE reporter cells. To verify that G10 triggered expression of endogenous genes dependent on IRF3 and IFN signaling pathways, semi-quantitative RT-PCR analysis was performed to examine transcription of established IRF3/IFN-responsive genes including ISG54, ISG56, ISG15, and Viperin. Exposure of cells to G10 induced transcription of these cellular genes with kinetics and magnitude similar to those observed following treatment with UV-inactivated human cytomegalovirus (UV-CMV), a known stimulus of IRF3 and IFN activation that occurs through both STING and ZBP1/DAI pathways. Since many molecular patterns that activate IRF3 simultaneously induce NF-κB signaling pathways, the potential for G10 to stimulate NF-κB-dependent transcription was specifically examined. Using THF cells stably transduced with an NF-κB-dependent luciferase reporter construct, no G10-associated stimulation of NF-κB-dependent transcription was detected even at high compound concentrations, in contrast to the robust responses observed with established NF-κB-inducing stimuli such as Sendai virus, TNFα, and LPS. Furthermore, G10 failed to stimulate mRNA synthesis of endogenous NF-κB-dependent genes including IL8, IL1β, and MIP1α. These results collectively demonstrate that G10 selectively activates IRF3-dependent signaling pathways without concomitant activation of canonical NF-κB pathways in human fibroblasts, suggesting a focused mechanism of action that may provide therapeutic advantages by minimizing pro-inflammatory side effects associated with broad immune activation.

### Example 3—G10 Elicits Antiviral Activity Against New and Old World Alphaviruses

Given that G10 stimulated expression of IRF3- and IFN-dependent antiviral effector genes, its capacity to establish a cellular state refractory to viral replication was investigated. THF cells were pre-exposed to non-toxic concentrations of G10 for six hours, followed by infection with West Nile Virus (WNV), Vaccinia Virus (VACV), Chikungunya virus (CHIKV), or Venezuelan equine encephalitis virus (VEEV). While G10 demonstrated modest inhibitory effects against VACV (approximately one log reduction) and WNV (less than one log reduction), it exhibited potent antiviral activity against alphaviruses, reducing CHIKV replication by over three logs with an IC90 of 8.01 μM. Similarly, G10 potently blocked VEEV replication with an IC90 of 24.57 μM. This differential antiviral activity is particularly significant because CHIKV and VEEV represent highly divergent alphavirus clades—CHIKV belonging to the Old World lineage and VEEV to the New World lineage—yet both were effectively inhibited by G10 treatment. The relative resistance of WNV and VACV to G10-mediated antiviral effects may reflect either differential susceptibility to the specific antiviral genes induced by G10 or the presence of more robust innate immune evasion mechanisms in these viruses compared to alphaviruses. These findings establish G10 as a potent and selective inhibitor of alphavirus replication, addressing a critical unmet medical need given the absence of FDA-approved therapeutics for these emerging pathogens.

### Example 4—IRF3 is Essential to G10-Induced Transcription and Anti-Alphaviral Activity

To determine the transcription factors essential for G10-mediated effects, THF-ISRE cells were engineered using CRISPR/Cas9-mediated gene editing to generate stable knockout lines lacking either STAT1 or IRF3 protein expression. Immunoblot analysis confirmed complete absence of the respective proteins, and functional validation demonstrated loss of STAT1-dependent luciferase expression following IFNβ treatment in STAT1-deficient cells and loss of IRF3-dependent expression following UV-CMV exposure in IRF3-deficient cells. When tested with G10, luciferase expression was maintained in STAT1-deficient cells but completely abolished in IRF3-deficient cells, indicating that G10-induced transcription requires IRF3 but not direct Jak/STAT signaling. Direct evidence of IRF3 activation was obtained through immunoblot analysis using an antibody specific for phosphorylated IRF3 residue S386, which demonstrated that G10 stimulates IRF3 phosphorylation with kinetics similar to established stimuli such as UV-CMV and Sendai virus. The essential role of IRF3 in G10-mediated antiviral activity was confirmed by examining alphavirus replication in IRF3-deficient cells: while IFNβ pretreatment maintained potent antiviral activity against CHIKV, VEEV, and Sindbis virus (SINV) in these cells, G10 completely lost its ability to inhibit viral replication. These results definitively establish that both the transcriptional activation and antiviral effects of G10 are strictly dependent on IRF3, positioning IRF3 activation as the primary mechanism underlying G10's biological activity.

### Example 5—G10-Mediated IRF3 Activation and Anti-Alphaviral Activity Occur Independently of IPS-1/MAVS-Dependent Signaling

To characterize the cellular pathway targeted by G10, THF cells were engineered to lack the adaptor protein IPS-1 (also known as MAVS) using CRISPR/Cas9-mediated gene disruption. Immunoblot confirmation of IPS-1 knockout demonstrated that IRF3 activation in response to IPS-1-dependent stimuli such as poly(I:C), ppp-dsRNA, and Sendai virus infection was abolished in these cells, while responses to STING-dependent stimuli (UV-CMV and 2'3'-cGAMP) remained intact. Critically, G10-induced IRF3 phosphorylation was fully preserved in IPS-1-deficient cells, indicating that G10 stimulates a response independent of the IPS-1 signaling apparatus. The anti-alphaviral activity of G10 was similarly unaffected by IPS-1 deletion, with pretreatment reducing replication of CHIKV, VEEV, and SINV by multiple logs in IPS-1-deficient cells to the same extent observed in wild-type cells. These findings conclusively demonstrate that G10-mediated IRF3 activation and antiviral activity occur independently of the IPS-1/MAVS pathway and, by extension, do not require engagement of upstream IPS-1-associated pattern recognition receptors such as RIG-I, MDA5, or POL3. This pathway specificity distinguishes G10 from other innate immune activators and suggests a unique mechanism of action targeting alternative signaling components.

### Example 6—STING is Required for G10-Mediated IRF3 Activation, Gene Expression, and Anti-Alphaviral Activity

To determine whether the adaptor protein STING is essential for G10-mediated effects, THF-ISRE cells were engineered to lack STING expression through CRISPR/Cas9-mediated gene disruption. Immunoblot validation confirmed complete absence of STING protein, and functional testing demonstrated loss of IRF3 S386 phosphorylation in response to STING-dependent stimuli (UV-CMV and 2'3'-cGAMP) while preserving responses to IPS-1-dependent stimuli (Sendai virus and poly(I:C)). Intriguingly, STING deletion completely abolished G10-induced IRF3 S386 phosphorylation, establishing STING as essential for G10-mediated IRF3 activation. This requirement extended to transcriptional responses, as G10 failed to induce luciferase expression or endogenous IRF3-dependent gene transcription (ISG54, ISG15, Viperin) in STING-deficient cells, while responses remained intact in IPS-1-deficient controls. Most critically, the anti-alphaviral activity of G10 was completely lost in STING-deficient cells, with CHIKV and VEEV replication proceeding at levels equivalent to untreated controls despite maintained responsiveness to IFNβ treatment. These comprehensive genetic studies definitively establish STING as the essential adaptor protein required for all G10-mediated biological effects, confirming G10 as a STING pathway activator.

Investigation into whether G10 directly binds STING was conducted using differential scanning fluorimetry to examine thermal stability changes in purified STING C-terminal domain (STING-CTD) upon G10 incubation. Unlike validated ligands such as 2'3'-cGAMP (for human STING) or DMXAA (for mouse STING), G10 failed to increase the thermal stability of either human or mouse STING-CTD, suggesting no direct binding interaction. Additionally, G10 did not induce IRF3 activation in THP-1 cells despite their expression of functional STING, further arguing against direct STING binding. These observations suggest that G10 may engage upstream pattern recognition receptors that signal through STING, potentially including ZBP1/DAI or IFI203, rather than acting as a direct STING ligand. This indirect mechanism of STING activation represents a novel paradigm for pharmacologic stimulation of this critical innate immune pathway.

### Example 7—G10 Induces STING-Dependent Synthesis and Secretion of Bioactive Interferon

To elucidate the contribution of interferon signaling to G10-mediated effects, examination of type I and III interferon expression revealed that G10 treatment induced transcription of both IFNβ and IFNλ1 mRNAs in THF-ISRE cells. Extended treatment periods (18 hours) demonstrated substantial induction of IFNα/β-dependent genes Mx2 and OAS, consistent with autocrine/paracrine interferon signaling following secretion. A cell-based reporter assay using THF-ISRE-ΔIRF3 cells (which cannot produce interferon but respond to exogenous interferon) confirmed that G10 treatment induced secretion of bioactive interferon species capable of activating Jak/STAT signaling. This interferon secretion was completely dependent on STING, as STING-deficient cells failed to secrete interferon in response to G10, while IPS-1-deficient cells maintained G10-induced secretion (though at diminished levels compared to wild-type cells). These findings establish that G10 triggers a complete STING-dependent interferon response cascade, including transcription, synthesis, and secretion of bioactive interferon that subsequently amplifies antiviral gene expression through autocrine/paracrine signaling mechanisms.

### Example 8—STAT1 is Required for G10-Mediated Anti-Alphaviral Activity and ISG Expression

Despite G10's ability to induce IRF3-dependent gene expression independently of STAT1, examination of antiviral activity in STAT1-deficient cells revealed that G10 completely lost its ability to inhibit CHIKV, VEEV, and SINV replication, while IFNβ maintained partial antiviral activity even in the absence of STAT1. Investigation of interferon-stimulated gene (ISG) expression showed that while SeV, UV-CMV, and IFNβ could still induce Mx2 and ISG56 protein expression in STAT1-deficient cells, G10 failed to do so. This unexpected finding suggests that G10's antiviral activity depends on STAT1-mediated amplification of the interferon response, even though initial IRF3 activation occurs independently of STAT1. The differential requirement for STAT1 between G10 and other stimuli may reflect differences in the spectrum or quantity of interferon subtypes secreted, leading to distinct downstream signaling requirements. These results establish that while G10 initiates signaling through IRF3, its full antiviral efficacy requires the complete interferon amplification loop involving STAT1-dependent gene expression.

### Example 9—G10 Triggers IRF3-Dependent Activity with Less Potency than 2′3′-cGAMP

Comparative analysis between G10 and the canonical STING ligand 2'3'-cGAMP revealed differences in both kinetics and potency of IRF3 activation. G10 induced detectable IRF3 S386 phosphorylation at 30 minutes post-exposure, slightly earlier than 2'3'-cGAMP (1 hour), potentially reflecting differences in cellular permeability or uptake mechanisms. However, dose-response analysis demonstrated that 2'3'-cGAMP elicited stronger mRNA synthesis of IRF3- and IFN-dependent genes (IFIT1, IFIT2, ISG15, Viperin, OAS, Mx2) at lower concentrations than G10, indicating greater potency for the natural cyclic dinucleotide. Interestingly, IFNβ transcription showed more comparable dose-dependence between the two compounds. These differences may relate to their distinct mechanisms of STING pathway engagement—direct binding versus indirect activation—or differences in physicochemical properties affecting cellular distribution and target engagement. Despite lower potency, G10's ability to activate human STING represents a significant therapeutic advance over murine-specific compounds like DMXAA.

Examination of G10 activity in primary human cells confirmed its immunostimulatory capacity beyond immortalized cell lines. In human peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs), G10 induced dose-dependent expression of IRF3-, IFN-, and NF-κB-dependent genes (IFIT1, IFIT2, IFNβ, ISG15, OAS, Viperin, MIP1α, IL1β), demonstrating activity in physiologically relevant immune cells. Similar responses were observed in primary human umbilical microvascular endothelial cells. The induction of NF-κB-dependent genes in primary cells but not fibroblasts suggests cell type-specific differences in signaling pathway activation, potentially related to differential expression of pattern recognition receptors or signaling components between stromal and hematopoietic lineages.

In vivo validation of STING pathway activation as an antiviral strategy was achieved using DMXAA in a murine CHIKV infection model, since G10 lacks activity in murine cells. DMXAA administration (25 mg/kg) at 3 hours pre-infection or 6 hours post-infection completely prevented detectable viremia at 72 hours post-infection, while treatment at 24 hours post-infection showed diminished but non-significant reduction in viral titers. These results demonstrate that timely STING pathway activation can effectively block alphavirus replication in vivo, supporting the therapeutic potential of human-specific STING activators like G10. The temporal dependence of efficacy likely reflects the window before viral establishment of immune evasion mechanisms, highlighting the importance of early intervention for optimal therapeutic outcomes.

### Example 10

Primary human cells including peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) and human umbilical microvascular endothelial cells were examined for G10-induced gene expression to validate activity in physiologically relevant cell types beyond immortalized fibroblast lines. In PBMCs, G10 triggered dose-dependent expression of IRF3-dependent genes (IFIT1, IFIT2, ISG15, Viperin), interferon genes (IFNβ), and interferon-stimulated genes (OAS), confirming its immunostimulatory capacity in primary human immune cells. Notably, unlike in fibroblasts, G10 also induced NF-κB-dependent gene expression (MIP1α, IL1β) in PBMCs, suggesting cell type-specific differences in signaling pathway activation that may reflect differential expression of pattern recognition receptors or signaling adaptors between stromal and hematopoietic lineages. Similar patterns of IRF3-, IFN-, and NF-κB-dependent gene induction were observed in primary human umbilical microvascular endothelial cells, further supporting the broad applicability of G10 across diverse human cell types. These findings demonstrate that G10's immunostimulatory effects are not limited to transformed cell lines and provide strong evidence for its potential therapeutic efficacy in vivo.

### Example 11

The therapeutic potential of STING agonism was validated in vivo using DMXAA in a murine model of CHIKV infection, since G10 lacks cross-species activity. DMXAA administration (25 mg/kg intraperitoneally) at 3 hours pre-infection completely prevented detectable viremia at 72 hours post-infection, with similar efficacy observed when administered at 6 hours post-infection. Treatment initiated at 24 hours post-infection showed reduced viral titers compared to controls, though the difference did not reach statistical significance. These results demonstrate that pharmacologic activation of STING-dependent signaling represents a viable therapeutic strategy for preventing alphavirus replication in vivo, with optimal efficacy achieved when administered early relative to viral exposure. The temporal dependence of antiviral efficacy likely reflects the critical window before viral establishment of interferon antagonism mechanisms, supporting the clinical rationale for early intervention with STING activators in acute viral infections. This in vivo validation provides strong preclinical evidence supporting the development of human-specific STING activators like G10 for therapeutic applications against emerging alphavirus threats.
