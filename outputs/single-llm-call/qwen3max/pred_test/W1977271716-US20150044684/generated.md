## TECHNICAL FIELD

The present invention relates to a method for immobilizing and retrieving living microorganisms from complex environmental matrices such as soil and deep-sea sediment using electrochemical techniques. More specifically, the invention provides a method for selectively attaching viable microorganisms to an electrode surface through the application of a constant negative electric potential and subsequently detaching them in a viable state by applying a high-frequency triangular wave potential. The invention further encompasses systems and protocols for the separation, concentration, and downstream molecular analysis of microorganisms that are otherwise difficult to culture or isolate using conventional microbiological methods. This technology is particularly useful in the fields of environmental microbiology, metagenomics, bioprospecting, and microbial ecology, where access to intact, living microbial cells from natural habitats is essential for functional and phylogenetic characterization.

## BACKGROUND ART

Metagenomic analysis has emerged as a powerful approach to explore the vast, uncultivated microbial diversity present in environmental samples such as soil and marine sediments. Unlike traditional culture-based methods, metagenomics enables the direct study of genetic material extracted from environmental samples, thereby circumventing the limitations imposed by the inability to cultivate the majority of environmental microorganisms under laboratory conditions. However, a significant challenge in metagenomic workflows lies in the quality and representativeness of the extracted nucleic acids. Direct DNA extraction from soil or sediment often results in co-purification of humic substances and other PCR inhibitors, which severely compromise downstream applications such as polymerase chain reaction (PCR), cloning, and sequencing. Indirect extraction methods—wherein microorganisms are first separated from the environmental matrix before cell lysis—can yield higher-purity DNA but suffer from low recovery rates, typically capturing only 25% to 35% of the total microbial community. This bias arises because many microorganisms adhere strongly to soil aggregates or mineral particles, making physical separation inefficient and non-representative.

Various single-cell isolation techniques have been developed to address these limitations, including dilution-to-extinction, micromanipulation, flow cytometry, microfluidics, and compartmentalization. While effective in controlled settings, these methods are generally incompatible with raw environmental samples due to the strong adhesion of microbes to particulate matter and the heterogeneous nature of such matrices. Consequently, there remains a critical need for a gentle, efficient, and scalable method to separate living microorganisms from soil and sediment without disrupting their viability or altering community composition. The present invention addresses this unmet need by leveraging electrochemical principles to achieve selective, reversible, and non-destructive immobilization and retrieval of viable microorganisms from complex environmental samples.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

### Object to be Achieved by the Invention

It is an object of the present invention to provide a reliable, reproducible, and non-invasive method for isolating living microorganisms from environmental samples such as soil and deep-sea sediment, thereby enabling accurate metagenomic and physiological analyses. Another object is to overcome the limitations of current DNA extraction and microbial separation techniques by providing a means to obtain high-purity, inhibitor-free nucleic acids from a representative subset of the native microbial community. A further object is to facilitate the cultivation, screening, and functional characterization of previously unculturable microorganisms by preserving their viability throughout the separation process.

### Means for Achieving the Objects

The present invention achieves these objectives through the discovery of a novel electrochemical attachment method wherein living microorganisms are selectively attracted to and immobilized on an electrode surface upon application of a constant negative electric potential in a non-nutritive electrolyte solution. Concurrently, the invention discloses a complementary detachment method involving the application of a high-frequency triangular wave potential to release the attached microorganisms while maintaining their viability. The method begins by suspending a soil or sediment sample in a suitable buffer or artificial seawater, followed by introduction into an electrochemical chamber containing a working electrode, a counter electrode, and a reference electrode. A constant negative potential, typically ranging from −0.2 V to −0.5 V versus Ag/AgCl, is applied to the working electrode for a defined period, resulting in the selective attachment of viable microorganisms to the electrode surface while excluding inert particles and dead cells. After washing away unbound material, a high-frequency triangular wave potential—preferably at 9 MHz with an amplitude of ±4 mV to ±10 mV versus Ag/AgCl—is applied to detach the microorganisms. The retrieved cells can then be used directly for cultivation, staining, viability assays, or nucleic acid extraction. The invention further specifies optimal ranges for key parameters, including electrolyte composition, pH, salt concentration, electrode material (e.g., indium tin oxide), potential magnitude, and application duration, to maximize attachment efficiency and post-detachment survival.

### Effect of the Invention

The invention offers several significant advantages over existing methodologies. First, it enables the selective isolation of living microorganisms from complex environmental matrices with high fidelity to the original community structure, as demonstrated by 16S rRNA gene phylogenetic analyses showing >87% overlap at the class level between electrically retrieved and directly extracted communities. Second, the method yields microbial cells free from humic acids and other PCR inhibitors, facilitating robust downstream molecular analyses. Third, the electrochemical process is non-cytotoxic under optimized conditions, with survival rates exceeding 90% for many bacterial types. Fourth, the technique is broadly applicable across diverse microbial taxa, including Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria, actinomycetes, and deep-sea extremophiles. Finally, the invention supports iterative attachment and detachment cycles, enabling precise spatial control over microbial positioning—an attribute valuable for biosensor development, microbial fuel cells, and high-throughput screening platforms. These benefits collectively enhance the accuracy, efficiency, and scope of environmental microbiology research and biotechnological applications.

## MODES FOR CARRYING OUT THE INVENTION

### <Method for Immobilizing Living Microorganisms>

The method for immobilizing living microorganisms comprises disposing a suspension containing microorganisms onto the surface of a working electrode, typically fabricated from indium tin oxide (ITO) on a glass substrate, and applying a constant negative electric potential to the electrode. The microorganisms are thereby attracted to and firmly attached to the electrode surface in a viable state. The constant potential is preferably maintained within the range of −0.2 V to −0.5 V versus an Ag/AgCl reference electrode, with optimal values of −0.4 V for terrestrial samples in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and −0.3 V for marine samples in artificial seawater. The electrolyte composition is critical and must be non-nutritive to prevent microbial metabolism that could interfere with attachment; suitable electrolytes include Dulbecco’s PBS(−), MOPS buffer, tricine buffer, or artificial seawater. The artificial seawater comprises 30 g/L NaCl, 0.7 g/L KCl, 5.3 g/L MgSO₄·7H₂O, 10.8 g/L MgCl₂·6H₂O, and 1 g/L CaCl₂·2H₂O. The buffer solutions are prepared at a concentration of 10 g/L and adjusted to a pH range of 6.8 to 7.2, with pH 7.0 being optimal. The electrode is typically patterned with ITO regions surrounded by insulating silicon dioxide layers, forming discrete attachment zones. The electrode layer may be shaped as squares, circles, or reticulated arrays, with individual features ranging from 5 μm to several millimeters in size. The constant potential is applied for a period of 2 to 24 hours, depending on sample type and desired yield. The microorganism density in the initial suspension is typically 1×10⁶ cells per 5 mL for pure cultures or 50 μg of soil/sediment per 5 mL for environmental samples. The method effectively immobilizes a wide range of microorganisms, including *Escherichia coli*, *Bacillus subtilis*, *Shewanella violacea*, and uncultured soil bacteria, with survival rates exceeding 90%. In seawater applications, the same principles apply, though the optimal potential shifts slightly due to ionic strength effects. Post-attachment, microorganisms remain metabolically active, as confirmed by dehydrogenase activity staining, and exhibit characteristic fibrous appendages that mediate adhesion.

### <Method for Preparing Living Microorganisms>

The method for preparing living microorganisms involves first immobilizing them via the aforementioned attachment protocol and then detaching them in a viable state using a high-frequency electrical stimulus. Specifically, after attachment, a high-frequency triangular wave potential is applied to the same electrode. This potential oscillates at a frequency of 9 MHz with an amplitude ranging from ±4 mV to ±20 mV versus Ag/AgCl, though ±10 mV is preferred for maximal viability. The detachment occurs in the same or fresh electrolyte—PBS(−) for soil or artificial seawater for marine samples—with salt concentrations matching those used during attachment. The detachment process is conducted at ambient or sample-specific temperatures (e.g., 4°C for deep-sea samples) for 30 to 60 minutes. Under these conditions, over 98% of attached microorganisms are released, with survival rates of 83% for *E. coli* and 47% for *B. subtilis*, as measured by live/dead staining and colony-forming unit assays. The detached microorganisms can be retrieved by gentle aspiration or scraping and are suitable for immediate use in cultivation, PCR, or sequencing. The method is compatible with various microorganism types, including extremophiles and uncultured species. Detachment occurs within micrometers of the electrode surface, ensuring minimal mechanical stress. Electrodes may be planar or patterned, and multiple cycles of attachment and detachment can be performed iteratively at identical locations. This reversibility enables precise microbial handling for analytical or industrial purposes.

## EXAMPLES

### Example 1

A garden soil sample was suspended in PBS(−) at 50 μg/mL and introduced into a three-electrode chamber. A −0.4 V potential was applied for 24 hours, resulting in dense microbial attachment to the ITO electrode. Subsequently, a ±10 mV, 9-MHz triangular wave was applied for 60 minutes, detaching 99.8% of cells. Staining with SYTO9/propidium iodide revealed 91% viability among residual cells. Retrieved microorganisms were counted via hemocytometer, yielding 3.0×10⁹ viable cells per gram of soil.

### Example 2

Cultured *E. coli* and *B. subtilis* were suspended in PBS(−), attached at −0.4 V for 24 hours, and detached with ±10 mV triangular wave. Viability staining showed 83% and 47% survival, respectively. Colony counts confirmed 88% and 48% viable retrieval rates, demonstrating method efficacy for pure cultures.

### Example 3

Soil samples were subjected to varying attachment potentials (−0.1 V to −0.6 V). Maximum attachment occurred at −0.4 V in PBS(−), with negligible binding at positive potentials, confirming the necessity of negative polarization.

### Example 4

Detachment efficiency was tested across amplitudes (±4 mV to ±10 mV). ±10 mV yielded near-complete detachment (>99%) with highest viability, whereas ±8 mV caused significant cell death despite lower detachment.

### Example 5

Soil suspensions in PBS(−), MOPS, tricine, and LB were tested. Attachment occurred robustly in non-nutritive buffers but not in LB, underscoring the requirement for non-metabolizable electrolytes.

#### (1) Various Buffers

PBS(−), MOPS, and tricine all supported efficient attachment, with PBS(−) yielding the highest density.

#### (2) Artificial Seawater

Marine-adapted microbes attached optimally at −0.3 V in artificial seawater, reflecting ionic environment effects.

### Example 6

Deep-sea sediment was processed using large ITO electrodes. After two cycles of attachment (−0.3 V, 2 h) and detachment (±10 mV, 30 min), DNA was extracted via CTAB, amplified by 16S rRNA PCR, cloned, and sequenced. Over 130 clones were analyzed, revealing broad phylogenetic diversity.

### Example 7

Phylogenetic analysis showed 19 bacterial phyla in electrically retrieved samples, with 95% phylum-level overlap to direct extraction. Comparative Example 1 (no potential) showed no attachment. Comparative Examples 2-1 and 2-2 (positive potential or no detachment wave) failed to retrieve viable cells, validating the invention’s specificity.
