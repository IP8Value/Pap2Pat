# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

Anomaly detection refers to the identification of rare items, events, or observations that deviate significantly from the majority of data and do not conform to a well-defined notion of normal behavior. This task is critical in numerous real-world domains such as manufacturing quality control, cybersecurity intrusion detection, and financial fraud prevention. In practice, anomaly detection systems must operate under varying degrees of label availability, which gives rise to distinct learning paradigms. The fully supervised setting assumes access to labeled examples for both normal and anomalous instances. While this paradigm enables the training of high-performing binary classifiers, it suffers from significant practical limitations due to the high cost and difficulty of obtaining comprehensive labels for rare anomalies. Moreover, class imbalance between normal and anomalous samples often necessitates specialized techniques such as weighted loss functions or resampling strategies to prevent model bias toward the dominant class.

One-class classification represents a special case of the supervised setting where only labeled normal samples are available during training. Methods such as one-class support vector machines (OC-SVM) and autoencoders are commonly employed in this context, aiming to learn a decision boundary that encapsulates the normal data distribution while excluding outliers. Despite their theoretical appeal, these approaches cannot leverage any available anomalous labels, thereby discarding potentially valuable information that could improve detection accuracy. At the opposite end of the spectrum lies the fully unsupervised anomaly detection setting, wherein no labeled data is provided. Techniques like Isolation Forests, Local Outlier Factor (LOF), and deep generative models attempt to infer anomalies purely from data structure. However, the absence of any ground truth guidance typically results in performance degradation compared to supervised alternatives, limiting their reliability in safety-critical applications.

The semi-supervised anomaly detection setting seeks to balance labeling cost and model performance by utilizing a small set of labeled examples—often exclusively normal samples—alongside a larger pool of unlabeled data. Traditional semi-supervised methods assume that labeled and unlabeled data are drawn from the same underlying distribution, enabling consistent generalization. However, this assumption frequently fails in real-world scenarios due to various forms of distribution mismatch. For instance, in positive-unlabeled (PU) or negative-unlabeled (NU) settings, the labeled subset contains only one class, creating an inherent distributional divergence with the unlabeled set that includes both classes. Additionally, temporal evolution of anomalies—as seen in adaptive fraud schemes or changing manufacturing defects—can cause newly collected unlabeled data to exhibit novel anomaly types absent from the original labeled set. Furthermore, human labeling practices often introduce selection bias: annotators tend to label only clear, easy-to-classify instances, leaving ambiguous or hard-to-detect anomalies in the unlabeled pool. These mismatches violate the core assumptions of conventional semi-supervised learning frameworks, leading to biased pseudo-labels and suboptimal model performance. Existing approaches either ignore the labeled anomalies, treat all unlabeled data as normal (risking contamination), or rely on domain adaptation techniques that require substantial labeled source data—conditions rarely met in practical anomaly detection deployments.

## BRIEF SUMMARY

The present invention introduces a novel semi-supervised anomaly detection framework designed to overcome the limitations of existing methods under realistic conditions of distribution mismatch between labeled and unlabeled data. Central to this framework is an ensemble of one-class classifiers (OCCs) that collectively generate robust pseudo-labels for unlabeled samples without overfitting to the limited labeled data. Unlike conventional pseudo-labeling mechanisms that employ binary classifiers trained on scarce labeled examples—thereby inheriting their distributional biases—the disclosed approach decouples pseudo-label generation from the final anomaly predictor, enhancing generalization to unseen anomaly types and shifted data distributions.

The method begins by receiving a dataset comprising a small set of labeled samples and a larger set of unlabeled samples, where the feature distributions of these two subsets may differ significantly. A pseudo-labeler, constructed as an ensemble of multiple OCCs, analyzes the unlabeled data to assign provisional binary labels—normal, anomalous, or unknown—based on consensus among the ensemble members. Specifically, a sample is assigned a positive (anomalous) pseudo-label only if all OCCs classify it as anomalous; similarly, a negative (normal) pseudo-label requires unanimous agreement among all OCCs. Samples lacking consensus remain unlabeled and are excluded from supervised training phases, thereby mitigating error propagation from unreliable pseudo-labels.

Concurrently, a machine learning model—comprising an encoder, a predictor, and optionally a projection head for self-supervised representation learning—is trained using a composite loss function. This loss integrates three components: (1) a supervised loss computed over the original labeled data, (2) a pseudo-supervised loss computed over confidently pseudo-labeled unlabeled samples, and (3) a self-supervised auxiliary loss applied to the entire dataset to enhance feature representation quality. Critically, the thresholds governing pseudo-label assignment (ηₚ for positive and ηₙ for negative labels) are not treated as user-defined hyperparameters requiring validation data. Instead, they are automatically determined via a partial distribution matching technique that minimizes the Wasserstein distance between the anomaly score distributions of labeled samples and candidate subsets of unlabeled data. In settings where only one class is labeled (e.g., PU learning), Otsu’s method is employed to estimate the missing threshold in an unsupervised manner.

This integrated approach enables effective utilization of both labeled and unlabeled data while remaining robust to distribution shifts. By leveraging ensemble-based pseudo-labeling, partial distribution matching for threshold selection, and joint supervised/self-supervised training, the invention achieves state-of-the-art anomaly detection performance across diverse real-world scenarios—including evolving fraud patterns, labeling based on sample “easiness,” and PU learning—without requiring additional validation sets or extensive hyperparameter tuning.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The Semi-supervised Pseudo-labeler Anomaly Detection with Ensembling (SPADE) framework addresses the critical challenge of anomaly detection under distribution mismatch between labeled and unlabeled training data—a common yet under-addressed issue in practical deployments. Real-world applications such as financial fraud detection, industrial defect inspection, and network intrusion monitoring often involve dynamic environments where anomaly characteristics evolve over time. Consequently, labeled data collected at an earlier stage may not reflect the anomaly types present in subsequently gathered unlabeled data. Moreover, human labeling workflows inherently introduce bias, as annotators preferentially label unambiguous cases, leaving complex or novel anomalies unlabeled. These factors result in P<sup>l</sup><sub>X</sub> ≠ P<sup>u</sup><sub>X</sub>, violating the i.i.d. assumption of standard semi-supervised learning methods and degrading their performance.

To mitigate this, SPADE introduces a carefully architected pipeline illustrated in FIG. 1, which depicts data distributions under various mismatch scenarios: (Left) PU/NU settings with class-imbalanced labeling, (Middle) temporal shifts introducing new anomaly types, and (Right) labeling bias favoring easy samples. Conventional binary classifiers trained on such skewed labeled sets develop decision boundaries heavily biased toward observed anomalies, as shown in FIG. 2(b,c), failing to generalize to unseen types. In contrast, FIG. 2(d) demonstrates how SPADE’s OCC-based pseudo-labeler maintains broader coverage, enabling better adaptation.

As detailed in FIG. 5, the SPADE architecture comprises four key modules: an encoder (502), a predictor (504), a pseudo-labeler (506), and a projection head (508). The encoder maps input features x ∈ X into a latent representation r = h(x), implemented via multi-layer perceptrons for tabular data or convolutional networks for images. The predictor q(r) outputs an anomaly score, forming the final detection model f(x) = q(h(x)). The projection head g(r) supports self-supervised pretraining through tasks like contrastive learning or reconstruction, improving representation quality without labels.

The pseudo-labeler (506), depicted in FIG. 6, is an ensemble of K one-class classifiers (e.g., Gaussian Distribution Estimators). Each OCC is trained solely on labeled normal samples (or labeled anomalies in NU settings). For an unlabeled sample x<sub>u</sub>, pseudo-label v(h(x<sub>u</sub>)) is assigned as follows: +1 (anomalous) if all OCCs flag it as anomalous; 0 (normal) if all agree it is normal; −1 (unknown) otherwise. This unanimity requirement ensures high-confidence pseudo-labels, reducing noise.

Thresholds η<sub>p</sub> and η<sub>n</sub> controlling OCC decisions are determined via partial distribution matching. Given labeled normal data D<sub>0</sub><sup>l</sup>, η<sub>n</sub> is selected to minimize the Wasserstein distance between the anomaly score distribution of D<sub>0</sub><sup>l</sup> and a subset of unlabeled data, effectively identifying unlabeled samples most similar to known normals. Similarly, η<sub>p</sub> uses labeled anomalies D<sub>1</sub><sup>l</sup>. In PU settings lacking D<sub>0</sub><sup>l</sup>, Otsu’s method thresholds the unlabeled anomaly score histogram to separate presumed normals and anomalies.

Training optimizes the combined loss: ℒ = ℒ<sub>sup</sub> + αℒ<sub>pseudo</sub> + βℒ<sub>self</sub>, where ℒ<sub>sup</sub> is binary cross-entropy on labeled data, ℒ<sub>pseudo</sub> applies the same loss to pseudo-labeled samples (weighted by indicator 1{v ∈ {0,1}}), and ℒ<sub>self</sub> is a self-supervised loss (e.g., CutPaste for images). Hyperparameters α, β are fixed (e.g., α=β=1), eliminating validation needs.

Experiments validate SPADE on tabular (Covertype, Thyroid, Drug) and image (MVTec, Magnetic Tile) datasets under three mismatch scenarios. In “new anomaly types” tests, labeled data includes only subset of anomaly classes; SPADE improves AUC by up to 10.6% over baselines by generalizing to unseen types. In “easy-labeling” simulations, logistic regression identifies high-confidence samples as labeled; SPADE matches or exceeds alternatives, enabling cost-efficient annotation. In PU settings, SPADE outperforms Weighted Elkanoto and BaggingPU by >5% AUC on missed anomalies. On real fraud datasets (Kaggle Credit, Xente), SPADE consistently surpasses FixMatch, VIME, and DANN across labeling ratios (5–20%), demonstrating robustness to temporal drift.

Implementation occurs within a computing environment comprising server devices (executing SPADE training), user devices (providing input/output), and distributed storage. Instructions and data flow through networks (LAN/WAN/cloud), with models deployed via computer-readable media. The framework’s modularity supports integration into fraud detection pipelines, manufacturing QA systems, or cybersecurity platforms, offering a scalable solution for label-scarce, distribution-shifted anomaly detection.
