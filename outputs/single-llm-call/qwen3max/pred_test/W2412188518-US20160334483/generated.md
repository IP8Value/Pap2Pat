# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) techniques that employ ultrashort echo times (UTE), such as zero echo time (ZTE) and sweep imaging with Fourier transformation (SWIFT), are uniquely capable of capturing signals from tissues exhibiting extremely short transverse relaxation times (T₂*), which are otherwise invisible to conventional MRI sequences. These tissues include cortical bone, tendons, ligaments, menisci, and certain components of the inner ear—structures critical for clinical diagnosis but historically difficult to visualize due to rapid signal decay. However, a persistent challenge in UTE imaging is image blurring caused by off-resonance effects and accelerated T₂* decay, particularly at higher static magnetic field strengths (e.g., 7 Tesla). Off-resonance arises from chemical shift differences between fat and water or from magnetic susceptibility variations at tissue–air interfaces, leading to spatial misregistration and duplicated edges in reconstructed images. This blurring worsens with increasing field strength because chemical shifts scale linearly with B₀, while T₂* decay accelerates due to enhanced spin dephasing. A straightforward remedy is to increase the readout bandwidth during data acquisition, which compresses the time window over which off-resonance phase accrual occurs, thereby mitigating blurring. Yet, in conventional ZTE sequences, the excitation and readout bandwidths are inherently coupled: gradients are applied before and during a hard radiofrequency (RF) pulse, forcing the RF pulse duration—and thus its bandwidth—to match the readout bandwidth. Consequently, increasing the readout bandwidth necessitates a shorter, higher-power RF pulse, which dramatically elevates both the specific absorption rate (SAR) and peak RF power. These parameters are strictly regulated in human MRI due to safety concerns regarding tissue heating and hardware limitations, rendering high-bandwidth ZTE impractical in clinical settings.

Pointwise-encoding time reduction with radial acquisition (PETRA) was developed to circumvent another critical limitation of ZTE: the requirement for ultrafast transmit-to-receive (T/R) switching. In standard clinical MRI systems, T/R switching delays exceed 20 microseconds, causing a gap in k-space sampling around the origin—the region most critical for image contrast and resolution. PETRA addresses this by supplementing radial ZTE acquisitions with single-point imaging (SPI) scans that explicitly sample the missing central k-space points. While effective, PETRA inherits ZTE’s bandwidth coupling problem. When higher readout bandwidths are used to combat blurring, the excitation bandwidth must also increase, exacerbating SAR and RF power demands. Moreover, a higher excitation bandwidth lengthens the time interval during which k-space center points remain unsampled (proportional to pulse width plus T/R delay), thereby expanding the missing central region and requiring more SPI acquisitions. This significantly prolongs scan time, undermining PETRA’s efficiency. Thus, despite its advantages, PETRA remains constrained by the inseparability of excitation and readout bandwidths, limiting its utility in high-field imaging of short-T₂* tissues where blurring, SAR, and acquisition time must all be simultaneously optimized. There exists a clear and unmet need for an MRI method that decouples these bandwidths—enabling high readout bandwidths to suppress blurring while maintaining low excitation bandwidths to satisfy SAR and hardware constraints—without demanding specialized scanner modifications or sacrificing image quality.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention introduces a novel magnetic resonance imaging technique referred to herein as gradient-modulated PETRA (GM-PETRA), which fundamentally decouples the excitation bandwidth from the readout bandwidth in radial ultrashort echo time imaging. In GM-PETRA, a constant-amplitude gradient is applied during RF excitation to define a low excitation bandwidth, ensuring minimal SAR and RF peak power. Immediately following the T/R switching delay, the gradient amplitude is dynamically ramped upward to a higher value, thereby increasing the readout bandwidth during data acquisition. This gradient modulation enables independent control over excitation and readout spectral widths, a capability absent in both conventional ZTE and standard PETRA. The primary advantage of this approach is the ability to achieve high readout bandwidths—essential for mitigating off-resonance blurring and preserving signal from rapidly decaying short-T₂* species—while retaining a long, low-power RF pulse that complies with clinical SAR limits and avoids excessive demands on RF amplifier hardware. Furthermore, because the excitation bandwidth remains low, the temporal gap during which central k-space points are unsampled (determined by RF pulse width plus T/R delay) is minimized, reducing the number of required SPI acquisitions and thus shortening total scan time compared to high-bandwidth PETRA. The invention thereby resolves the core trade-offs that have historically plagued ultrashort TE imaging: it simultaneously improves image sharpness, reduces energy deposition, maintains compatibility with standard clinical scanners, and enhances acquisition efficiency. GM-PETRA is particularly advantageous for high-field MRI (e.g., 7 T) of anatomical regions prone to strong susceptibility artifacts and short T₂* decay, such as the inner ear, skull base, lungs, and musculoskeletal tissues, enabling unprecedented visualization of fine structural details previously obscured by blurring or signal loss.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

Conventional PETRA combines radial zero echo time (ZTE) sampling with single-point imaging (SPI) to overcome the transmit-to-receive (T/R) switching limitations of clinical MRI scanners. In standard PETRA, a hard RF pulse is applied while constant-amplitude gradients are active, defining both the excitation bandwidth and the initial k-space trajectory. Data acquisition begins after a fixed T/R delay, during which no signal is captured, resulting in a missing conical region around the center of k-space. This missing region is subsequently filled by SPI, wherein individual k-space points near the origin are sampled by stepping down the gradient amplitude in discrete increments. The hybrid k-space sampling pattern—radial spokes for peripheral regions and pointwise sampling for the center—enables full k-space coverage without requiring sub-microsecond T/R switches. However, in this scheme, the excitation bandwidth (BW_ex) is rigidly tied to the readout bandwidth (BW_read) via the constant gradient amplitude (G_ex), since BW = γ·G·FOV, where γ is the gyromagnetic ratio and FOV is the field of view. Increasing BW_read to reduce off-resonance blurring thus forces a proportional increase in BW_ex, which shortens the RF pulse duration (pw ∝ 1/BW_ex), elevating SAR (∝ FA²·BW_ex) and RF peak power (∝ FA·BW_ex^{1/2}), where FA is the flip angle. Additionally, the number of missing central k-space points scales with pw + t_s (t_s being the T/R delay), so higher BW_ex enlarges the SPI acquisition burden.

The present invention, gradient-modulated PETRA (GM-PETRA), modifies this paradigm by introducing a time-varying gradient waveform. During RF excitation, gradients are held at a baseline amplitude G_ex, establishing a low BW_ex that ensures safe SAR levels and manageable RF power. Immediately after the T/R gate opens, the gradient amplitude is ramped linearly or nonlinearly from G_ex to a higher maximum amplitude G_max over a controlled ramp time. This amplitude-modulated gradient increases the instantaneous readout bandwidth during signal acquisition, effectively accelerating k-space traversal and reducing the time over which off-resonance phase accumulates. Critically, because excitation occurs only under G_ex, BW_ex remains low, while BW_read is governed by G_max. The data acquisition process thus benefits from high readout bandwidth without the penalties of high excitation bandwidth. The relationship between RF peak power and SAR is preserved in its dependence on BW_ex, not BW_read, allowing GM-PETRA to operate within regulatory limits even when BW_read exceeds 100 kHz.

The k-space sampling pattern in GM-PETRA comprises three segments: (1) a central unsampled cone defined by |k| < γ·G_ex·(pw/2 + t_s), identical to conventional PETRA; (2) a transition region during gradient ramp-up, where k-space is traversed nonlinearly; and (3) a high-bandwidth radial segment under G_max. The missing central points are still acquired via SPI, but because BW_ex is low, the radius of the missing cone (k_SPI = γ·G_ex·(pw/2 + t_s)) is smaller than in high-BW_ex PETRA, reducing SPI acquisition time. SPI in GM-PETRA proceeds identically to conventional PETRA: gradients are stepped down from G_ex to lower amplitudes to sample k = 0 outward. The key advantage of GM-PETRA lies in this decoupling: high BW_read minimizes blurring from off-resonance and T₂* decay, while low BW_ex controls SAR, RF power, and SPI overhead.

In an exemplary implementation, a 7 Tesla MRI system equipped with a multi-channel head coil was used to image phantoms and human subjects. Sequence parameters included a flip angle of 4°–5°, repetition time (TR) of 5 ms, and total radial spokes of 65,536. For GM-PETRA, G_ex corresponded to 60 kHz readout bandwidth, while G_max was set to 125 or 200 kHz. Gradient slew rate was limited to 100 mT/m/ms to suppress eddy currents. Image reconstruction employed iterative density compensation to account for non-uniform k-space sampling during ramp-up, followed by non-uniform fast Fourier transform (NUFFT). Resultant images demonstrated markedly reduced blurring in off-resonance regions (e.g., fat-water interfaces) and enhanced visibility of short-T₂* structures (e.g., apple mesocarp, inner ear bones) compared to conventional PETRA at matched or even higher excitation bandwidths. The benefits of GM-PETRA are thus threefold: superior image fidelity through high effective readout bandwidth, compliance with SAR and hardware constraints via low excitation bandwidth, and improved scan efficiency by minimizing SPI requirements.

### Example

An ex vivo equine knee and in vivo human inner ear were imaged to demonstrate the practical advantages of GM-PETRA. In the equine knee study, a breast phantom containing fat (off-resonance at 1050 Hz) and water compartments was scanned using both conventional PETRA and GM-PETRA. The GM-PETRA sequence employed an excitation bandwidth of 60 kHz (pulse width 20 μs) and gradient modulation to 125 or 200 kHz readout bandwidth, with a ramp slew rate of 100 mT/m/ms. Conventional PETRA was tested at both 60 kHz and 120 kHz excitation bandwidths; the latter required 925 SPI acquisitions versus 123 for 60 kHz, illustrating the SPI burden of high BW_ex. For GM-PETRA, only 123 SPI points were needed, as BW_ex remained at 60 kHz. Image reconstruction utilized a custom C++/CUDA pipeline implementing iterative density correction for the non-Cartesian, ramp-sampled k-space data, followed by NUFFT-based 3D reconstruction. Resultant images showed that GM-PETRA at 60–125 kHz achieved off-resonance artifact suppression comparable to PETRA at 120 kHz, but with substantially lower SAR and shorter scan time. In apple imaging, GM-PETRA revealed fine mesocarp fibers invisible to 3D gradient echo (GRE) due to ultra-short T₂* and less blurred than PETRA due to reduced off-resonance effects.

Human inner ear imaging was performed under IRB approval at 7 T using an 8-transmit/24-receive channel head coil. B₁⁺ shimming optimized flip angle homogeneity over the temporal bone. GM-PETRA used 4° flip angle (due to SAR constraints), 60 kHz excitation bandwidth, and 125 kHz readout bandwidth. Conventional PETRA was limited to 60 kHz excitation, as 120 kHz exceeded SAR limits. Maximum intensity projection (MIP) images from GM-PETRA clearly delineated cochlear turns and semicircular canals, whereas PETRA exhibited blurring at air-bone interfaces. The MRI system comprised an operator workstation for protocol setup, a pulse sequence server executing GM-PETRA waveforms, a data acquisition server digitizing received signals, a data processing server performing reconstruction, and a data store server archiving results. A physiological acquisition controller gated scans to respiration if needed. The scan room interface circuit managed communication between control electronics and hardware. Patient positioning used a motorized table with laser alignment. The RF system included multi-channel transmit amplifiers and receive preamplifiers. The gradient system delivered the modulated waveforms with controlled slew rates. The magnet assembly provided a stable 7 T B₀ field. This integrated system enabled robust implementation of GM-PETRA, demonstrating its clinical feasibility and diagnostic superiority.
