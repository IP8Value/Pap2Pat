## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates generally to the field of data analysis and visualization, particularly in the context of high-dimensional biological data such as flow cytometry and mass cytometry. More specifically, the invention provides methods, systems, and graphical user interfaces for automated cluster matching, alignment, and comparative analysis of cell populations across multiple samples, enabling robust identification of biologically relevant differences while accommodating technical variations inherent in experimental data collection.

## BACKGROUND

Comparative analysis of biological samples is fundamental to biomedical research and clinical diagnostics, particularly in immunology, oncology, and infectious disease studies. Flow cytometry and mass cytometry generate high-dimensional datasets that capture the phenotypic and functional characteristics of individual cells within complex mixtures. These technologies enable researchers to identify, quantify, and compare distinct cell populations across different experimental conditions, patient cohorts, or time points. However, the effective comparison of samples requires accurate alignment of corresponding cell subsets between samples, a task that becomes increasingly challenging as the dimensionality of the data increases.

Manual gating, the traditional approach to identifying cell populations in cytometry data, involves sequential selection of regions of interest in two-dimensional plots based on expert knowledge. While this method has proven useful for low-dimensional analyses, it suffers from several critical limitations. Manual gating is inherently subjective, as different analysts may draw gates differently, leading to inconsistent results. It is also extremely labor-intensive and time-consuming, making it impractical for large-scale studies involving hundreds or thousands of samples. Furthermore, manual gating does not scale well to high-dimensional data, where the number of possible parameter combinations becomes prohibitively large for human analysis.

To address these limitations, automated clustering methods have been developed to objectively identify cell populations without manual intervention. These methods can process high-dimensional data efficiently and consistently, but they introduce new challenges in the subsequent step of matching corresponding clusters across different samples. This cluster matching problem is complicated by several factors, including biological variability between samples, technical variations due to instrument drift or calibration differences, and the potential presence or absence of specific cell populations in different samples.

The "curse of dimensionality" presents a fundamental challenge in high-dimensional data analysis. As the number of dimensions increases, the volume of the space increases exponentially, causing data to become sparse and statistical methods to lose validity. Distance metrics that work well in low dimensions may become meaningless in high dimensions, as all points tend to be equidistant from each other. This phenomenon affects both clustering and cluster matching algorithms, making it difficult to distinguish biologically meaningful differences from technical noise.

Existing approaches to cluster matching can be broadly categorized into two types. The first approach involves clustering each sample independently and then aligning the resulting clusters post-hoc. Methods such as FLAME and flowMatch implement this strategy by computing dissimilarity measures between clusters from different samples and finding optimal matches based on these measures. While computationally efficient for low-dimensional data, this approach can fail when cluster locations vary significantly between samples or when populations appear or disappear entirely. In high-dimensional settings, the curse of dimensionality further compromises the reliability of this approach.

The second approach, exemplified by methods such as Joint Clustering and Matching (JCM) and ASPIRE, addresses these limitations by pooling all samples together and performing simultaneous clustering and matching. This joint approach treats multiple samples as different realizations of an underlying biological model, creating a template of meta-clusters that represent distinct biological cell types. While this method can better handle variations in cluster location and presence/absence, it is computationally expensive and still susceptible to the curse of dimensionality. Most joint clustering methods rely on fitting mathematical models to the data, and the number of parameter combinations increases dramatically with dimensionality, making model fitting infeasible beyond three or four dimensions.

These limitations of existing approaches highlight the need for improved cluster matching methods that can effectively handle high-dimensional cytometry data while remaining computationally efficient and robust to technical variations. Additionally, there is a need for user guidance in sequential gating workflows, where analysts must make decisions about which parameters to examine next and how to interpret complex multidimensional relationships. The present invention addresses these needs by providing a novel cluster matching algorithm based on a multivariate extension of the quadratic form distance measure, combined with intuitive visualization tools that guide users through the analysis process.

## BRIEF SUMMARY

The present invention provides a comprehensive solution for multidimensional cluster matching and visualization of high-dimensional biological data. The core innovation is a method for matching clusters in data that overcomes the limitations of existing approaches while maintaining computational efficiency and biological relevance. The method begins by obtaining first sample data and second sample data, which may represent flow cytometry or mass cytometry measurements from different biological samples, experimental conditions, or time points.

The method proceeds by identifying clusters in the first sample data and second sample data using any appropriate clustering algorithm, such as density-based merging or other automated clustering techniques. Once clusters are identified in each sample, the method performs multivariate adaptive binning on the combined dataset formed by merging the two samples. This adaptive binning process recursively partitions the k-dimensional measurement space into hyper-rectangular bins such that each bin contains approximately the same number of data points, thereby adapting to the structure of the data and avoiding the curse of dimensionality.

The combined binning pattern derived from the merged dataset is then applied separately to each of the original samples. This ensures that both samples are represented using the same binning structure, enabling direct comparison. For each identified cluster in both samples, histograms are generated using the common binning pattern, where each histogram represents the relative frequency distribution of the cluster across the bins.

The method then determines dissimilarity scores for all possible combinations of clusters, where one cluster is from the first sample and the other is from the second sample. These dissimilarity scores are calculated using a quadratic form distance measure specifically designed for multidimensional data. The quadratic form distance takes into account both the magnitude of differences in relative frequencies and the spatial relationships between bins, ensuring that the dissimilarity measure reflects biologically meaningful differences while being insensitive to minor technical variations.

Based on the dissimilarity scores, the method identifies matched clusters as those pairs with the lowest dissimilarity scores. Unmatched clusters are treated as merging candidates, and each merging candidate is combined with its nearest cluster in the same sample. The dissimilarity score is then recalculated for the combined cluster to determine whether the merging candidate corresponds to a split cluster (where the original cluster was artificially divided) or a missing cluster (where the cluster is genuinely absent from one sample).

The method specifically performs multivariate adaptive binning in k-dimensions by dividing the data into k-dimensional bins through recursive partitioning along the dimension with maximum variance at each step. The dissimilarity score for each combination is determined using a specific equation that incorporates a spatial dissimilarity matrix A, where the elements aij are calculated as 1 minus the normalized Euclidean distance between the centers of mass of bins i and j. The distance between geometric means of matched clusters is also determined to provide additional information about the nature of the match.

The invention also provides a system for matching clusters in data, comprising memory and storage for data, a processor, and specialized modules including an adaptive binning module, a dissimilarity module, a matching and merging candidate identification module, and a determination module. These modules work together to implement the cluster matching method described above.

Additionally, the invention provides a method for rendering a graphical user interface that facilitates user-guided analysis. The interface includes a first interactive display showing a two-dimensional plot of the data and a second interactive display showing single parameter charts or graphs. Users can select guidance features, portions of the two-dimensional plot, or thresholds, and the interface dynamically updates the displays to reflect these selections, providing intuitive visualization of multidimensional relationships and supporting informed decision-making during the analysis process.

### Definitions

For the purposes of this invention, an "item" refers to any measurable entity within a biological sample, such as a cell, particle, or microorganism. "Gating" refers to the process of selecting a subset of items from a larger population based on their characteristics in one or more dimensions. A "gate" is the specific region or criterion used to define such a subset. A "marker" is any measurable characteristic of an item, such as the expression level of a surface protein, intracellular molecule, or functional property. A "reagent" is any substance used to detect or modify markers, including antibodies, dyes, or other binding molecules. A "stain" or "staining reagent" specifically refers to reagents that produce a detectable signal when bound to their target markers.

The singular forms "a," "an," and "the" include plural referents unless the context clearly indicates otherwise. The phrase "in one embodiment" and similar expressions do not necessarily refer to the same embodiment, and the use of such phrases throughout this document should not be interpreted as limiting the scope of the invention to any particular embodiment.

Cluster matching in the context of this invention refers to the process of identifying corresponding cell subsets or populations across different samples. Clustering involves grouping similar items together based on their multidimensional characteristics, while aligning cell subsets specifically refers to establishing correspondence between clusters from different samples that represent the same biological population. Population matching is the broader goal of comparing the composition and characteristics of cell populations across samples.

Conventional methods for cluster matching typically employ either separate clustering and matching, where each sample is clustered independently followed by post-hoc alignment, or joint clustering and matching, where all samples are pooled together for simultaneous clustering and matching. Both approaches have significant limitations, as discussed previously. The present invention introduces a novel dissimilarity measure based on the quadratic form distance that addresses these limitations.

The criteria for an effective dissimilarity measure in cytometry data analysis include: (1) possession of metric properties (non-negativity, symmetry, triangle inequality, and identity of indiscernibles); (2) continuity with respect to small changes in subset location or frequency; (3) non-parametric nature to accommodate complex population structures; and (4) computational efficiency for high-throughput analysis. Common distance metrics such as Euclidean distance, Mahalanobis distance, and chi-square distance fail to meet these criteria adequately. The Earth Mover's Distance (EMD) satisfies the continuity requirement but is computationally intensive.

The quadratic form distance measure introduced herein provides a computationally efficient alternative to EMD while maintaining the desirable continuity property. The QF dissimilarity score is defined as D²(h,f) = (h - f)ᵀA(h - f), where h and f are relative frequency vectors for two histograms, and A is a spatial dissimilarity matrix. The QF score possesses several advantageous properties: it is non-negative for appropriate choices of A, it increases monotonically with both the magnitude of frequency differences and the spatial separation between populations, and it is computationally efficient to calculate.

The multi-dimensional cluster matching method of the present invention extends these advantages to high-dimensional data through adaptive binning and careful construction of the spatial dissimilarity matrix. The method handles high-dimensional cluster matching by recursively partitioning the data space along dimensions of maximum variance, ensuring that the binning adapts to the data structure while maintaining computational tractability. Two-dimensional density-based merging is used for initial cluster identification, providing a robust foundation for subsequent matching.

Various cluster identification methods can be employed, including template formation where reference patterns are established for known cell types, meta-cluster formation where related clusters are grouped into higher-level categories, and hierarchical template formation where multiple levels of organization are captured. The resulting template provides a comprehensive representation of the cell populations present in the samples.

Conventional clustering methods suffer from several problems in high-dimensional settings, including sensitivity to parameter choices, inability to handle varying cluster densities, and poor performance with overlapping populations. The QF distance metric addresses these issues by providing a robust measure of dissimilarity that accounts for both frequency and spatial relationships. The multivariate QF distance metric extends this capability to multiple dimensions while maintaining computational efficiency.

The cluster matching method provides several advantages over existing approaches, including robustness to technical variations, ability to handle missing or split populations, computational efficiency, and biological relevance. Example method 100 begins by obtaining sample data sets from two or more biological samples. Clusters are identified in each sample data set using appropriate clustering algorithms. Multivariate adaptive binning is performed on the combined data set, and the resulting binning pattern is applied separately to each original sample. Histograms are generated for each identified cluster, and dissimilarity scores are calculated using the quadratic form distance.

The quadratic form distance equation is specifically designed to incorporate spatial relationships between bins through the matrix A, where aij = 1 - dMij/dmax, with dMij being the Euclidean distance between centers of mass of bins i and j, and dmax being the maximum such distance. This formulation ensures that the dissimilarity score reflects both the magnitude of differences and their spatial context.

The method for forming templates involves iteratively applying the cluster matching process to build comprehensive representations of cell populations across multiple samples. Meta-clusters are matched using the same principles, with the resulting template capturing the hierarchical organization of cell populations. Relative frequency is defined as the proportion of items in a cluster relative to the total number of items in the sample.

The spatial dissimilarity matrix A is derived from the geometric relationships between bins in the measurement space. The QF dissimilarity score is applied to identify matched clusters by finding pairs with minimum dissimilarity scores. Merging candidates are determined as unmatched clusters that may represent split or missing populations. The QF dissimilarity score is recalculated after merging to confirm the nature of the relationship.

Split or missing clusters are identified based on whether the dissimilarity score decreases (indicating a split) or increases (indicating a missing population) after merging. The multidimensional application calculates additional information such as distances between geometric means in each dimension, expressed in standard deviation units of the reference cluster. This enables tracking of population changes across samples and verification of the QF dissimilarity score's sensitivity to biologically relevant differences.

The QF dissimilarity score demonstrates sensitivity to binning parameters while maintaining robustness across a range of reasonable choices. It shows invariance to sample size, ensuring that comparisons are not biased by differences in the number of events collected. ### Example 1—Workflow for Automated Clustering and Alignment of Cell Populations in Flow Cytometry Data

Example 1 demonstrates the complete workflow for automated clustering and alignment of cell populations in flow cytometry data using the methods described herein. The process begins with data preprocessing, which includes compensation for spectral overlap in fluorescence flow cytometry data, transformation using Logicle transformation to handle the wide dynamic range of cytometry data, and initial clustering using two-dimensional density-based merging algorithms. The preprocessed data is then subjected to the QFMatch algorithm for cluster matching.

The method 100 is applied to align clusters between two samples, following the six-step process described previously. Adaptive binning is performed on the combined dataset, the binning pattern is applied to each sample separately, histograms are generated for each cluster, and dissimilarity scores are calculated using the quadratic form distance. Matched clusters are identified, and merging candidates are evaluated to determine whether they represent split or missing populations.

The results of cluster matching demonstrate the algorithm's ability to accurately align corresponding cell populations even when their locations vary significantly between samples. The dissimilarity scores provide quantitative measures of the similarity between matched clusters, while the additional information about relative frequencies and distances between geometric means offers insights into the biological significance of the matches. The computational efficiency of the algorithm enables rapid processing of large datasets, making it suitable for high-throughput applications.

### Example 2—Matching of Basophil Populations Between Patient Samples, Even when Marker Expression Levels Vary Between Patients

Example 2 illustrates the application of the QFMatch algorithm to real-world clinical data involving basophil populations from patient samples. The data collection involved fluorescence flow cytometry using a standardized panel of markers, including CD123 as a key identifier for basophils. Preprocessing followed the standard workflow of compensation, Logicle transformation, and density-based clustering.

The method 100 was applied to match basophil populations across three different patient samples, where the expression levels of CD123 varied significantly (MFI ranging from 1033 to 6672) and the relative frequencies of basophils showed substantial differences. Despite these variations, the QFMatch algorithm successfully identified corresponding basophil populations across all samples.

The results of cluster matching demonstrated the algorithm's robustness to biological variability between patients. The dissimilarity scores remained low for matched basophil populations, indicating strong correspondence despite the differences in marker expression levels. The distances between geometric means, expressed in standard deviation units, provided quantitative measures of the shifts in population location, ranging from [0.6; 0.7]σ to [5.1; 1.6]σ between different patient pairs. This example highlights the algorithm's ability to handle clinically relevant variations in patient samples while maintaining accurate population matching.

### Example 3—Detection of Missing Lymphocyte Populations in the Peritoneal Cavity of RAG Knockout (RAG−/−) Mice

Example 3 demonstrates the algorithm's ability to detect missing cell populations by comparing wild-type (BALB/c) and knockout (RAG−/−) mouse peritoneal cavity cells. The data collection involved flow cytometry analysis using CD5 and CD19 as markers to identify T and B lymphocytes, respectively. Preprocessing followed the standard workflow, and clusters were identified representing the major cell populations in each sample.

The method 100 was applied to align the samples, with the wild-type sample serving as the reference. The algorithm correctly identified the lymphocyte populations in the wild-type sample and attempted to find corresponding matches in the RAG−/− sample. As expected, no corresponding lymphocyte populations were found in the knockout sample, as RAG−/− mice lack mature T and B lymphocytes due to the genetic defect in V(D)J recombination.

The results of cluster matching confirmed the absence of lymphocyte populations in the RAG−/− sample. The unmatched clusters in the wild-type sample were automatically treated as merging candidates, but the merging process did not decrease the initial dissimilarity scores, confirming that these populations were genuinely missing rather than split. This example demonstrates the algorithm's ability to distinguish between split populations and genuinely missing populations, providing valuable biological insights.

### Example 4—Cluster Matching of the Murine Lymphoid, Myeloid and Granuloid Lineages Between PerC and Spleen

Example 4 applies the cluster matching algorithm to compare immune cell populations between peritoneal cavity (PerC) and spleen samples from wild-type mice. The data collection involved a 12-parameter high-dimensional flow cytometry panel, with side scatter (SSC-A) and CD11b used as key markers for initial analysis. Preprocessing followed the standard workflow, and clusters were identified representing lymphoid, myeloid, and granuloid lineages.

The method 100 was applied to match cell populations between the two tissue types. The algorithm successfully aligned the immune cell subsets that are shared between PerC and spleen, including dendritic cells, natural killer cells, and eosinophils. At the same time, it detected the missing cell subsets that are tissue-specific, such as mast cells and peritoneal macrophages in PerC, and monocytes and neutrophils in spleen.

The results of cluster matching highlighted the tissue-specific differences in immune cell composition while accurately identifying the common populations. The unmatched cell subsets were clearly distinguished in the visualization, with different colors indicating tissue-specific populations. Additional analysis of eosinophil populations revealed differences in Gr-1 expression levels between the two tissues, consistent with known biological differences in inflammatory conditions. This example demonstrates the algorithm's ability to handle complex biological comparisons involving multiple cell types and tissue-specific variations.
