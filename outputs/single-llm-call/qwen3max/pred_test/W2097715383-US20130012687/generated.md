# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

The field of recombinant protein production has long relied on affinity-based purification strategies to isolate proteins of interest from complex biological mixtures. Among the most widely adopted techniques is the use of polyhistidine (His6) tags, which enable rapid and efficient purification via immobilized metal affinity chromatography (IMAC). Despite the convenience offered by such tags, a significant limitation remains: many recombinant proteins exhibit poor solubility, low expression yields, or instability when expressed in bacterial systems such as *Escherichia coli*. To address these challenges, researchers have employed fusion partners—such as NusA, glutathione S-transferase (GST), or small ubiquitin-like modifier (SUMO)—to enhance the solubility, folding, and stability of target proteins. However, these fusion tags often interfere with downstream applications, including structural studies, enzymatic assays, or therapeutic development, necessitating their removal after purification.

Current methods for tag removal typically involve the addition of exogenous site-specific proteases, such as thrombin, factor Xa, or tobacco etch virus (TEV) protease. While effective in principle, these approaches suffer from several drawbacks. First, exogenous proteases are often expensive and may exhibit suboptimal activity under standard purification conditions. Second, prolonged incubation with high concentrations of protease increases the risk of non-specific cleavage within the target protein, particularly if internal recognition sites exist. Third, the protease itself must be removed from the final preparation through additional chromatographic steps, which reduces overall yield and complicates the workflow. Moreover, some proteases display limited solubility or require specific cofactors that are incompatible with certain buffer systems. These limitations underscore the need for a more streamlined, cost-effective, and high-fidelity method for producing untagged recombinant proteins without compromising expression, solubility, or integrity.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a novel protein purification system based on a self-cleaving protease tag derived from the cysteine protease domain (CPD) of the *Vibrio cholerae* MARTX toxin. This system enables the one-step purification of untagged recombinant proteins directly from bacterial lysates, combining the advantages of affinity tagging with the precision of site-specific autocleavage. The CPD is fused in-frame to the C-terminus of a target protein and carries a C-terminal His6 affinity tag. Because the CPD is inactive in bacterial cells—due to the absence of its eukaryotic-specific activator, inositol hexakisphosphate (InsP6)—the full-length fusion protein can be stably expressed and purified using standard IMAC protocols. Upon binding to Ni²⁺-NTA resin, the addition of InsP6 triggers rapid and specific autocleavage at the junction between the target protein and the CPD, releasing the untagged target protein into solution while the His6-tagged CPD remains immobilized on the resin. This approach eliminates the need for exogenous proteases, reduces processing time, minimizes non-specific cleavage, and simplifies downstream purification. Furthermore, the CPD fusion enhances the expression, solubility, and stability of a wide range of target proteins, including those that are traditionally difficult to express in bacterial systems.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE PREFERRED EMBODIMENTS

The invention centers on an on-bead cleavage purification system that integrates affinity capture, site-specific cleavage, and tag separation into a single operational step. This system leverages the unique biochemical properties of the *Vibrio cholerae* MARTX toxin cysteine protease domain (CPD), which functions as an inducible, autocatalytic protease. Unlike conventional protease-based tag removal strategies, the CPD is genetically fused directly to the target protein, ensuring a 1:1 stoichiometry that maximizes cleavage efficiency while minimizing off-target effects.

A key advantage of this system is its ability to significantly improve the expression and solubility of recombinant proteins. Empirical data demonstrate that fusion to the CPD-His6 tag consistently enhances both total and soluble expression levels across diverse protein classes, including structured enzymes, intrinsically disordered domains, and eukaryotic proteins heterologously expressed in *E. coli*. For instance, the intracellular domain (ICD) of the cytokine receptor gp130, which is natively unstructured and prone to aggregation, exhibited a three-fold increase in soluble expression when fused to CPD-His6 compared to a His6-only control. Similarly, biotin ligase (BirA) from *E. coli* showed markedly improved yield and purity as a CPD fusion.

The choice of CPD as the proteolytic module is motivated by its stringent substrate specificity and conditional activation. The CPD cleaves exclusively after leucine (Leu) residues, and its catalytic activity is strictly dependent on the presence of InsP6—a molecule absent in prokaryotic cells. This regulatory mechanism ensures that the fusion protein remains intact during expression and initial purification, preventing premature cleavage. Structural studies confirm that the P1 Leu residue fits precisely into the S1 substrate-binding pocket of the CPD, explaining its high fidelity. Moreover, the CPD exhibits minimal transcleavage activity, meaning it does not efficiently cleave other proteins in *trans*, thereby preserving the integrity of the target protein even if internal Leu residues are present. This was validated using the gp130 ICD, which contains multiple Leu residues yet underwent cleavage only at the engineered CPD junction.

The purification method employs a CPD-His6 tag appended to the C-terminus of the target protein via standard molecular cloning. Expression vectors based on the pET backbone were engineered to accommodate cloning at restriction sites such as SalI, SacI, or BamHI, allowing control over the number of residual amino acids left on the target protein after cleavage. For example, cloning into the BamHI site with an added Leu codon results in a single C-terminal Leu residue post-cleavage, whereas SalI cloning leaves a four-residue extension (Val-Asp-Ala-Leu). An HA epitope tag can also be incorporated N-terminal to the CPD for detection or alternative purification strategies.

Feasibility was demonstrated using green fluorescent protein (GFP) as a model substrate. GFP-CPD-His6 was expressed in *E. coli*, captured on Ni²⁺-NTA resin, and released upon InsP6 addition in a dose-dependent manner, with complete cleavage achieved within two hours. The released GFP retained full fluorescence, confirming functional integrity.

The system was further validated with challenging targets. *Plasmodium falciparum* SENP1 (PfSENP1), a malarial protease, was purified to near homogeneity using the CPD system, yielding material suitable for crystallography—unattainable with traditional His6-thrombin methods due to contaminating bands. Similarly, the CRAC-activation domain (CAD) of STIM1, previously deemed unstable, was successfully stabilized and purified as a CPD fusion. Mouse macrophage metalloelastase (MMP12), which normally forms inclusion bodies, was recovered in soluble, active form using the CPD system, bypassing days of refolding and multi-step chromatography.

Comparative analyses reveal that CPD fusions consistently outperform conventional tags in expression, solubility, and purity. Unlike intein- or sortase-based systems, the CPD does not require extreme pH, temperature shifts, or high salt, making it compatible with sensitive proteins. Additionally, CPD-mediated cleavage proceeds efficiently across a broad pH range (6.5–8.5), at temperatures from 4°C to 37°C, and in the presence of standard protease inhibitors.

Mutants of the CPD have been generated to modulate activity or alter cleavage specificity, though the wild-type enzyme remains optimal for most applications. Potential uses span structural biology, drug screening, diagnostics, and industrial enzyme production, particularly in high-throughput settings where simplicity and reliability are paramount.

### TERM DEFINITIONS

As used herein, “CPD” refers to the cysteine protease domain of the *Vibrio cholerae* MARTX toxin, encompassing amino acids 3440–3650 of the MARTX polypeptide, or functionally equivalent variants thereof that retain InsP6-inducible autocleavage activity after Leu residues. “MARTX” denotes the multifunctional autoprocessing repeats-in-toxin protein produced by *V. cholerae*, a large secreted toxin that delivers effector domains into host cells. An “inducer” is a molecule that activates the CPD protease; specifically, inositol hexakisphosphate (InsP6) is the physiological inducer that binds the CPD and triggers conformational changes enabling autocatalysis.

### Methods

Bacterial cultures were grown in Luria-Bertani (LB) or 2YT media at 37°C with appropriate antibiotics (e.g., 100 µg/mL carbenicillin for pET22b vectors). Expression was induced with 250 µM IPTG at OD₆₀₀ ≈ 0.6, followed by incubation at 16–30°C for 3–8 hours depending on protein stability.

Multiple pET-CPD expression vectors were constructed. pET-CPDSalI was made by cloning CPD (aa 3440–3650) into SalI/XhoI sites of pET22b/pET28a. pET-HA-CPDSalI included an N-terminal HA tag. pET-CPDBamHI-Leu encoded CPD starting at aa 3444 with a C-terminal Leu codon for single-residue cleavage. Other variants used SacI or BamHI sites to modulate C-terminal extensions.

Target genes (GFP, gp130 ICD, BirA, STIM1 CAD, MMP12) were PCR-amplified and cloned into NdeI/SalI sites of pET-CPDSalI derivatives to generate in-frame CPD fusions. All constructs were verified by sequencing.

His6-tagged CPD fusion proteins were expressed in *E. coli* BL21(DE3), lysed by sonication in buffer (50 mM Tris pH 7.5, 500 mM NaCl, 15 mM imidazole, 10% glycerol), and clarified by centrifugation. Lysates were incubated with Ni²⁺-NTA agarose at 4°C for 2–4 hours. Beads were washed, then treated with 50–100 µM InsP6 in lysis buffer for 1–2 hours to induce on-bead cleavage. Supernatants containing untagged target proteins were collected; residual His6-CPD was eluted with 175 mM imidazole.

For PfSENP1 and BirA, purifications were performed at room temperature to prevent aggregation. MMP12 purifications included 1 mM TCEP and 16°C induction to maintain solubility. Control His6-tagged proteins lacking CPD were purified in parallel using identical IMAC protocols.

Gel filtration chromatography (Superdex 200) was used for final polishing of PfSENP1 and MMP12 in appropriate buffers. Activity assays confirmed functionality: GFP fluorescence (ex 488 nm, em 511 nm) and MMP12 proteolysis of Mca-PLGLDL(Dpa)AR (ex 325 nm, em 395 nm).

Variants of MARTX CPDs from other bacterial species may be used if they share InsP6 dependence and Leu-specific cleavage. Preferred bacterial hosts include *E. coli* strains such as BL21(DE3), Rosetta, or Tuner. The system operates effectively across pH 6.5–8.5 and salt concentrations from 0–500 mM NaCl, offering flexibility in buffer design. Critically, fusion to CPD consistently increases expression levels of target proteins, often by 2–5 fold, as quantified by densitometry and Bradford assays.
