# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNICAL FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates generally to the field of microfluidics and, more specifically, to on-chip flow rate sensing devices for real-time monitoring of fluid dynamics in microscale environments. The invention provides a novel nanomembrane-based flow sensor that integrates a microwave resonator with a thin, mechanically robust membrane to detect fluid flow rates in both liquid and gaseous media. This technology is particularly suited for applications requiring high sensitivity, rapid response times, and compatibility with complex or variable fluid compositions, such as organ-on-a-chip platforms, digital microfluidics, precision drug delivery systems, and biomedical microreactors. The invention enables localized, non-invasive, and continuous measurement of flow rates without reliance on external calibration or assumptions about fluid thermal properties, thereby addressing critical limitations of existing flow sensing methodologies.

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION (PRIOR ART)

Accurate and localized measurement of fluid flow rates is essential in numerous microfluidic applications, including controlled nutrient delivery in cell culture chambers, modulation of cellular adhesion dynamics, particle sorting by size, and precise dosing of therapeutic agents in tumor biopsy assays. In organ-on-a-chip systems, maintaining cellular viability demands optimal flux control, while digital microfluidics requires exquisite regulation of droplet formation through coordinated flow manipulation. Despite these needs, conventional flow actuation methods—such as syringe or peristaltic pumps—suffer from inherent mechanical delays, pulsations, and poor responsiveness, which compromise system controllability and experimental reproducibility. Although commercial flow sensors exist, they are typically macroscopic, off-chip components that cannot provide spatially resolved measurements within individual microchannels, thus limiting their utility in integrated lab-on-a-chip architectures.

Thermal flow sensors represent one widely adopted approach; they operate by measuring heat dissipation in a fluid stream and do not require moving parts. However, their accuracy is critically dependent on prior knowledge of the fluid’s thermal properties, such as specific heat capacity and thermal conductivity. In applications involving complex or dynamically changing fluid compositions—such as biological media containing proteins, cells, or varying solvent mixtures—unaccounted heat transfer pathways can introduce significant errors, rendering thermal sensors unreliable. Coriolis flowmeters offer broader dynamic range and composition independence by detecting inertial forces in oscillating channels, but their implementation at microscale is hindered by fabrication complexity, high cost, small channel diameters, and undesirable fluid heating.

Passive mechanical approaches have also been explored. Capacitive sensors have been used to infer flow-induced drag forces, while cantilever-based designs with perforated structures translate flow into measurable deflections. Although achieving sensitivities down to 1 nL/min, such systems require fluid to pass directly through the sensing element, potentially causing clogging, shear stress on biological samples, or flow perturbation. Radio-frequency and microwave-based non-contact sensors using elastomeric membranes (e.g., polydimethylsiloxane, PDMS) have demonstrated sub-microliter-per-minute sensitivity by detecting membrane deflection via proximity-coupled resonators. However, the viscoelastic nature of PDMS results in slow mechanical relaxation—often several minutes—severely limiting temporal resolution and suitability for dynamic flow control.

These limitations underscore a pressing need for an on-chip flow sensor that combines high sensitivity, fast response, mechanical robustness, compatibility with diverse fluids (including both liquids and gases), and seamless integrability with standard microfluidic platforms. The present invention addresses this unmet need through a novel architecture based on a crystalline nanomembrane coupled to a microwave resonator, enabling multiple complementary mechanisms for flow rate quantification with unprecedented performance characteristics.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a nanomembrane-based flow rate sensor comprising a thin, mechanically compliant membrane formed from a crystalline material—preferably silicon nitride—and integrated with a microwave resonator configured as a coplanar waveguide (CPW). The membrane forms part of a microfluidic channel wall, and fluid flow induces mechanical deformation or dynamic oscillation of the membrane. These mechanical responses modulate the electrical characteristics of the microwave resonator, specifically its capacitance and resonance frequency, which are detected via phase-locked loop circuitry to infer flow rate. The invention exploits three distinct sensing mechanisms: (1) quasi-static deformation leading to a stepwise shift in resonance frequency upon flow initiation; (2) periodic pulsations of the membrane under steady flow, where the pulsation period or frequency correlates inversely or directly with flow rate; and (3) continuous tracking of resonance frequency shifts during dynamic flow conditions. 

A key advantage of the microwave resonator lies in its high sensitivity to minute changes in electrode geometry, enabling detection of nanoscale membrane displacements. Unlike thermal sensors, the invention is insensitive to fluid composition, as it relies solely on mechanical interaction rather than thermal properties. The use of a ceramic silicon nitride membrane—220 nm thick—confers exceptional durability, chemical inertness, and rapid mechanical recovery (on the order of seconds), overcoming the slow relaxation of elastomeric alternatives. Furthermore, the same device architecture functions effectively for both liquid and gas flows, demonstrating versatility across application domains. The invention thus fulfills multiple aims: providing real-time, on-chip flow monitoring; enabling high-resolution sensing from sub-microliter to over 100 µL/min flow rates; supporting integration into complex microfluidic circuits; and offering multiple operational modes to suit different temporal and sensitivity requirements.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

The flow rate sensor of the present invention comprises a microfluidic channel bonded atop a semiconductor chip containing a thin nanomembrane and an integrated microwave resonator. The nanomembrane, fabricated from a 220 nm thick layer of stoichiometric silicon nitride, serves as a deformable wall segment of the microfluidic channel. Silicon nitride is selected for its high Young’s modulus, low internal damping, chemical stability, and compatibility with standard microfabrication processes. Typical membrane dimensions range from 600 to 1500 µm in length and 300 to 500 µm in width, though larger variants (e.g., 3 mm × 1 mm) are employed for higher flow regimes. The membrane’s mechanical compliance allows it to deflect under fluid pressure or exhibit self-sustained oscillations under steady flow, phenomena arising from fluid-structure interactions in confined geometries.

The microwave resonator is implemented as a coplanar waveguide (CPW) with signal and ground electrodes patterned directly onto the membrane surface using photolithography and gold deposition (100 nm thickness). The CPW is designed for 50 Ω impedance matching and features a narrowed gap (20 µm) over the membrane region compared to the wider feed lines (400 µm), defining an active sensing zone where capacitance is maximally sensitive to membrane displacement. As fluid flows through the channel, hydrodynamic forces deform the membrane, altering the inter-electrode distance and thereby modulating the resonator’s capacitance. This capacitance change shifts the resonance frequency, which is tracked in real time using a phase-locked loop (PLL) system that maintains a constant phase (e.g., 0°) by adjusting the input frequency; the required frequency correction serves as the output signal proportional to flow rate.

Three primary mechanisms enable flow rate quantification. First, upon initiating flow from a reset (zero-flow) condition, a sharp upward shift in resonance frequency occurs, with magnitude correlating to flow rate. This quasi-static response provides immediate flow estimation with short-term sensitivity of approximately 0.5 µL/min. Second, under constant pressure-driven flow, the membrane exhibits periodic pulsations—bistable transitions between deformed states—observed optically and electronically. The pulsation period decreases monotonically with increasing flow rate (e.g., from 14 s at 5 µL/min to 7 s at 10 µL/min), enabling flow inference from timing intervals. Third, during continuous operation, the absolute resonance frequency exhibits a monotonic relationship with flow rate, allowing direct readout without waiting for discrete events.

Fabrication begins with a silicon wafer having a buried oxide layer (2 µm SiO₂) and top silicon nitride layer (220 nm). Backside photolithography defines an etch window, followed by deep reactive ion etching and KOH wet etching to release the membrane. Front-side processing defines the CPW electrodes via alignment lithography and gold evaporation. Microfluidic channels (e.g., 300 µm wide, 150 µm deep) are molded in SU-8 photoresist, replicated in PDMS, and plasma-bonded to the chip, aligning the channel over the membrane. The device operates with either liquid (e.g., DI water) or gas (e.g., pressurized air), with experiments confirming linear frequency-shift response up to 4 bar pressure.

Verification experiments employed a commercial thermal flow sensor (0–120 µL/min range) upstream for reference. Data acquisition synchronized microwave phase/frequency readings (via LabVIEW and lock-in amplifier) with reference flow values at 50 ms intervals. Optical microscopy confirmed cyclic membrane deformation, with pulsation characteristics dependent on membrane size: smaller membranes activate at lower flows but saturate earlier, while larger ones extend dynamic range. Air flow tests showed similar frequency-shift trends, albeit with reduced sensitivity at low pressures due to lower fluid density.

The sensor responds rapidly to abrupt flow changes, with frequency shifts occurring within seconds. Recovery after flow cessation involves a ~50 s downward drift, yet secondary measurements remain possible via phase tracking or successive pulsation analysis. Crystalline silicon nitride ensures minimal hysteresis and long-term stability, unlike polymers. In summary, the invention provides a versatile, high-performance, on-chip flow sensing platform leveraging microwave-nanomechanical transduction for broad applicability in advanced microfluidic systems.
