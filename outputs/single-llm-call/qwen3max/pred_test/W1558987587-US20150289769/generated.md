# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention pertains to the field of cardiac health, specifically to methods and systems for assessing cardiac function and predicting the risk of adverse cardiac events such as heart failure and atrial fibrillation. More particularly, the invention relates to a novel quantitative metric derived from cardiac imaging data—referred to herein as the Strain Rate Index (SRI)—that provides an integrated assessment of myocardial relaxation and tissue compliance during the diastolic phase of the cardiac cycle. This metric is computed using data obtained from cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) imaging, though it may also be adapted for use with other imaging modalities capable of capturing myocardial deformation over time. The invention further encompasses computer-implemented systems, non-transitory computer-readable media, and user interfaces configured to calculate, display, and interpret the SRI in clinical or research settings. By offering a more sensitive and physiologically grounded indicator of early diastolic dysfunction than conventional measures, the invention addresses a critical unmet need in cardiovascular diagnostics and risk stratification.

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

Cardiovascular disease remains a leading cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide, with heart failure and atrial fibrillation representing major clinical endpoints that often arise from underlying diastolic dysfunction. Diastolic dysfunction refers to the inability of the left ventricle to relax and fill properly during diastole, even when systolic function appears normal. Early detection of diastolic impairment is crucial for timely intervention, yet current diagnostic approaches suffer from significant limitations. Echocardiography, the most widely used modality for assessing diastolic function, relies on surrogate parameters such as mitral inflow velocities (E and A waves), tissue Doppler-derived e' velocities, and the E/e' ratio. However, these metrics are load-dependent, operator-sensitive, and often inconsistent across patient populations, particularly in individuals with preserved ejection fraction. Cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) offers superior spatial resolution and reproducibility but has historically lacked standardized, quantitative indices of diastolic performance that integrate both active relaxation and passive compliance. Existing CMR-based strain analyses focus predominantly on systolic deformation, with limited attention to the nuanced dynamics of early diastole. Consequently, there exists a pressing need for a robust, imaging-derived biomarker that captures the integrated physiology of ventricular relaxation and stiffness, enabling more accurate risk prediction and personalized management of patients at risk for heart failure or atrial fibrillation.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention introduces a novel method for evaluating cardiac function and determining the risk of future cardiac failure by computing a Strain Rate Index (SRI) from cardiac imaging data. The SRI is derived from time-resolved myocardial deformation measurements, specifically circumferential strain and strain rate curves obtained during the cardiac cycle. In one embodiment, the method involves acquiring cardiac images using an imaging modality such as tagged cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR), processing the images to generate mid-ventricular mid-wall circumferential strain and strain rate waveforms, identifying key temporal and amplitude features—including systolic strain peak, post-systolic strain peak, and early diastolic strain rate peak—and computing the SRI as a normalized ratio that reflects both the timing of myocardial relaxation and the compliance of ventricular tissue. The resulting SRI value is then used to determine a level of risk for developing heart failure and/or atrial fibrillation, with higher SRI values indicating greater diastolic impairment and elevated risk. In another embodiment, the invention is implemented as a system comprising an imaging device, a processor configured to execute SRI computation algorithms, a non-transitory computer-readable medium storing executable instructions, and a user interface for displaying deformation curves, SRI values, and associated risk assessments. This system enables clinicians to integrate advanced biomechanical insights into routine cardiac evaluation, thereby improving diagnostic accuracy and prognostic stratification.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present patent application discloses a comprehensive method and system for determining the likelihood of adverse cardiac events through the computation and interpretation of a novel Strain Rate Index (SRI). The primary purpose of this invention is to provide a quantitative, imaging-based biomarker that overcomes the limitations of existing diastolic function assessments by integrating two fundamental physiological components: the kinetics of myocardial relaxation and the mechanical compliance of ventricular tissue. The determination of cardiac events—particularly incident heart failure (HF) and atrial fibrillation (AF)—is motivated by the urgent clinical need for early, reliable predictors that can guide preventive therapy before irreversible remodeling occurs. The disclosed system and method operate by first acquiring high-fidelity cardiac images, preferably using tagged cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR), which enables pixel-level tracking of myocardial motion throughout the cardiac cycle. These images are then subjected to harmonic phase (HARP) analysis or equivalent speckle-tracking techniques to generate time-resolved circumferential strain and strain rate curves at the mid-ventricular mid-wall level. From these curves, three critical features are identified: the time to peak systolic strain (indicating end of active contraction), the time to post-systolic strain peak (reflecting delayed relaxation), and the magnitude of the early diastolic strain rate peak (E peak), which serves as a measure of tissue compliance during rapid filling. The SRI is calculated as the difference between post-systolic and systolic strain peak times, divided by the early diastolic strain rate peak, and further normalized by the total diastolic interval—defined as the RR interval minus the systolic interval—to account for heart rate variability. This normalization ensures that the SRI remains physiologically meaningful across different heart rates. Current CMR methods for diastolic assessment are largely qualitative or rely on volumetric filling rates, which do not directly probe myocardial material properties. In contrast, the SRI leverages the intrinsic relationship between strain dynamics and tissue biomechanics. Circumferential strain, in particular, is highly sensitive to subendocardial fiber behavior, which is often the first to be affected in diastolic dysfunction. The systolic, post-systolic, and early diastolic strain peaks correspond to distinct phases: active shortening, incomplete relaxation, and elastic recoil, respectively. The isovolumic relaxation time (IVRT)—the period between aortic valve closure and mitral valve opening—is implicitly captured in the temporal offset between systolic and post-systolic peaks. The early diastolic strain rate (E peak) reflects how rapidly the myocardium lengthens during initial filling, a process governed by both restoring forces and chamber stiffness. The development of the SRI was motivated by the recognition that neither relaxation timing nor compliance alone suffices to characterize diastolic health; their integration yields a more holistic metric. The calculation proceeds algorithmically: after curve extraction, peak detection algorithms identify local maxima/minima, temporal differences are computed, and normalization is applied. A method flow diagram would illustrate image acquisition → strain curve generation → feature extraction → SRI computation → risk classification. System components include an MRI scanner, a computational server running the SRI algorithm, storage for image and metadata, and a graphical user interface that overlays strain curves, annotates peaks, and displays the final SRI value alongside interpretive guidance (e.g., low, moderate, high risk). The system may be embodied in a non-transitory computer-readable medium containing instructions for performing these steps. Deformation curves are illustrated with time on the x-axis and strain (%) or strain rate (1/s) on the y-axis, clearly marking systolic trough, post-systolic inflection, and early diastolic peak. The SRI algorithm is deterministic and reproducible, minimizing inter-observer variability. An example study—such as the Multi-Ethnic Study of Atherosclerosis (MESA)—validates the SRI’s predictive power. In conclusion, the SRI emerges as a robust, physiology-informed predictor of HF and/or AF, offering significant advantages over conventional metrics.

### EXAMPLE

An example study was conducted using data from the Multi-Ethnic Study of Atherosclerosis (MESA), a large prospective cohort designed to investigate subclinical cardiovascular disease. Participants who underwent both echocardiography and tagged cardiac MRI on the same day at Johns Hopkins Hospital between 2006 and 2008 were included in the analysis. Cardiac MRI studies were performed using standard protocols, with harmonic phase (HARP) analysis applied to compute mid-ventricular mid-wall circumferential strains and strain rates. From these waveforms, the Strain Rate Index (SRI) was calculated as previously described: the difference between post-systolic and systolic strain peak times, divided by the early diastolic strain rate peak, and normalized by total diastolic duration. Established cardiovascular risk factors—including age, sex, hypertension, diabetes, and body mass index—were recorded at baseline. Additionally, N-terminal pro–B-type natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP) levels were measured as a biochemical marker of ventricular wall stress, and coronary artery calcium scores were quantified via computed tomography to assess atherosclerotic burden. Incident heart failure and atrial fibrillation were classified as primary outcomes based on rigorous adjudication criteria: heart failure required hospitalization with physician diagnosis and supporting evidence (e.g., symptoms, imaging, elevated BNP), while atrial fibrillation was confirmed by electrocardiogram or hospital records. A combined endpoint of either HF or AF was also analyzed. Statistical analysis employed Cox proportional hazards regression to evaluate the association between SRI and event incidence, adjusting for covariates. Hazard ratios and 95% confidence intervals were reported per standard deviation increase in SRI. Model performance was assessed using Harrell’s C-statistic for discrimination, the integrated discrimination improvement (IDI) index to quantify incremental predictive value, and the net reclassification improvement (NRI) to evaluate correct reclassification of risk categories. Secondary analyses examined the SRI’s performance relative to established risk factors and conventional diastolic parameters. Model calibration was verified using Hosmer-Lemeshow tests. Kaplan-Meier survival curves were generated to visualize event-free survival across SRI tertiles. Baseline characteristics of the 125 participants revealed a mean age of 61±8 years, with 41% male, 56% White, 44% African-American, 50% hypertensive, and 14% diabetic. Over follow-up, incident HF and AF occurred at rates consistent with population expectations. Notably, SRI demonstrated significant correlations with CMR-derived mass-to-volume ratio (r=0.42, p<0.001) and echo-based E/e' (r=0.31, p<0.001), while inversely correlating with septal and lateral e' velocities. SRI values increased progressively across diastolic dysfunction grades (p=0.03). Kaplan-Meier curves showed clear separation by SRI tertile, with higher SRI associated with greater event incidence. Univariate and multivariate Cox models confirmed SRI as an independent predictor after adjustment. IDI and NRI analyses demonstrated statistically significant improvements in risk prediction when SRI was added to baseline models. In conclusion, the SRI proved to be a strong, independent predictor of incident heart failure and/or atrial fibrillation, validating its utility as a novel imaging biomarker for diastolic dysfunction and adverse cardiac outcomes.
