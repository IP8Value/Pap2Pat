## BACKGROUND

Advanced manufacturing technologies have emerged as transformative tools for fabricating complex functional materials and devices across aerospace, biomedical, energy, and electronics sectors. Among these, additive manufacturing—commonly referred to as 3D printing—enables the direct digital fabrication of components with intricate external geometries and internal architectures that are unattainable via conventional subtractive or formative methods. This capability is particularly valuable for ceramic-based systems, where geometric complexity often correlates with enhanced performance in applications such as catalysis, filtration, sensing, and electrochemical energy conversion. However, despite significant progress in polymer and metal additive manufacturing, the extension of these techniques to ceramics remains hindered by fundamental material challenges inherent to inorganic, non-metallic solids.

Additive manufacturing of ceramics faces several critical limitations. First, ceramic precursors typically require high-temperature sintering to achieve densification and mechanical integrity, a process that induces substantial volumetric shrinkage—often exceeding 20–30%—leading to dimensional inaccuracies, warping, and cracking in printed structures. Second, the incorporation of organic additives such as binders, plasticizers, and solvents into printable ceramic pastes or inks, while necessary for extrudability and shape retention, complicates post-processing and can result in residual porosity or carbon contamination after thermal decomposition. Third, the intrinsic brittleness of ceramics renders them highly susceptible to thermal shock during rapid heating or cooling cycles, making conventional furnace-based sintering incompatible with fast, on-demand manufacturing. Fourth, achieving high-resolution features or smooth surface finishes in green (unsintered) ceramic parts is difficult due to the coarse particle size of many ceramic powders and the limited control over filament deposition dynamics in extrusion-based systems. Finally, the integration of multi-material or multi-functional components—such as dense electrolytes bonded to porous electrodes in electrochemical cells—requires precise co-sintering protocols that are rarely compatible with standard additive manufacturing workflows. These limitations collectively impede the scalable, cost-effective production of high-performance ceramic devices with tailored microstructures and complex geometries, especially for emerging protonic ceramic energy technologies operating at intermediate temperatures (300–600 °C).

## SUMMARY

The present invention introduces a novel laser-assisted 3D printing (L3DP) method for the fabrication of protonic ceramic components and devices with controlled geometry, microstructure, and crystal phase. Central to this method is the integration of microextrusion-based additive manufacturing with in-situ laser processing, including rapid drying, precise cutting, surface polishing, and—critically—rapid laser reactive sintering (RLRS). In one embodiment, a silica film is formed by depositing a sol-gel-derived precursor layer onto a substrate and subjecting it to localized CO₂ laser irradiation, which induces rapid densification into fused silica without bulk thermal exposure. The CO₂ laser sintering process is performed using a wavelength of 10.6 μm, with scanning parameters optimized to achieve full densification while minimizing thermal stress and cracking.

In a specific implementation, an erbium-doped (Er-doped) silica film is fabricated for scintillation applications by incorporating Er³⁺ ions into the sol-gel matrix prior to laser sintering. The resulting film exhibits high optical transparency, controlled thickness, and enhanced luminescence properties compared to furnace-sintered counterparts. Furthermore, the invention encompasses a ceramic component fabrication method wherein a printable ceramic paste—comprising proton-conducting oxides such as BaZr₀.₈Y₀.₂O₃₋δ (BZY20) or BaCe₀.₇Zr₀.₁Y₀.₁Yb₀.₁O₃₋δ (BCZYYb), optionally mixed with NiO—is extruded through a micro-nozzle to form green bodies of arbitrary 3D geometry. These green bodies are then subjected to CO₂ laser scanning under controlled atmospheric conditions to induce reactive sintering directly into phase-pure, dense or porous protonic ceramic structures.

The CO₂ laser scanning is conducted with a power ranging from 10 W to 90 W and a scanning speed between 0.1 mm/s and 15 mm/s, depending on the desired outcome—drying, partial densification, or full sintering. Multiple scanning paths, including hatching patterns with variable line spacing, are employed to ensure uniform energy distribution and homogenous microstructural development. The ceramic paste materials are formulated with optimized rheology through controlled addition of water, dispersants (e.g., Darvan 821A), and binders (e.g., hydroxypropyl methylcellulose), enabling stable extrusion and inter-layer adhesion.

The invention further enables 3D printing of fused silica glass components by direct laser melting of silica-based pastes or preforms, wherein a CO₂ laser melts deposited layers sequentially, with each layer fused to the previous one through precise control of laser power and scan rate. Layer deposition and melting occur in a continuous, layer-by-layer fashion, allowing the construction of transparent, crack-free glass structures with complex curvilinear forms.

Additionally, the invention provides an optical fiber sensor fabrication method involving laser machining of a microchannel into a ceramic substrate using a picosecond (ps) laser, followed by filling the channel with a ceramic slurry and sealing it via CO₂ laser sintering. This embeds an all-glass optical fiber sensor within a monolithic ceramic body, enabling high-temperature structural health monitoring. A related fiber-optic sensor fabrication method utilizes 3D printing of a fused silica housing via paste extrusion and CO₂ laser melting, into which an optical fiber is inserted and fixed by localized laser irradiation.

Finally, the invention discloses a protonic ceramic energy device fabrication method based on rapid laser reactive sintering (RLRS), wherein multi-layer protonic ceramic fuel cells (PCFCs) or electrolysis cells (PCECs) are assembled by sequentially printing and laser-sintering anode, electrolyte, and cathode layers in a single integrated process. This approach eliminates prolonged furnace firing, reduces thermal mismatch stresses, and enables on-demand manufacturing of customized energy devices with high proton conductivity and mechanical robustness.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention is implemented using an integrated laser processing and additive manufacturing system designed for the fabrication of advanced ceramic, glass, and carbide components. This system combines digital design, precision motion control, and multi-modal laser interaction to enable the production of parts with unprecedented geometric and microstructural fidelity. The system is particularly suited for protonic ceramics, fused silica glass, silicon carbide composites, and other high-performance inorganic materials requiring controlled densification and phase formation.

The core of the system comprises four primary subsystems: a CO₂ laser heating system, a picosecond (ps) laser microfabrication system, a free-forming extrusion system, and an ultrasonic spray coating system. Together, these enable comprehensive additive manufacturing capability with in-situ material processing at every stage—from green body formation to final densification. The CO₂ laser heating system operates at a wavelength of 10.6 μm and delivers power up to 100 W, enabling rapid drying, sintering, and melting of oxide-based materials that exhibit strong absorption in the mid-infrared range. The ps laser microfabrication system employs a 1064 nm YAG laser with pulse durations in the picosecond regime, facilitating cold ablation with minimal heat-affected zones for high-resolution cutting, drilling, and surface polishing of both green and sintered ceramics. The free-forming extrusion system utilizes pneumatic or screw-driven microextruders with nozzle diameters as small as 0.2 mm to deposit ceramic pastes with controlled flow rates and layer thicknesses. The ultrasonic spray coating system allows uniform deposition of thin precursor films for electrolyte or functional coatings.

Key processing parameters are tightly controlled to ensure reproducibility and performance. For CO₂ laser processing, power levels between 10–90 W, scanning speeds of 0.1–15 mm/s, and defocus distances of 10–20 mm are used to tailor energy density and thermal penetration depth. The Galvo scanner, with angular resolution and response times enabling scan rates up to 5 m/s, directs the laser beam along programmable toolpaths generated from CAD models. Ps laser parameters include repetition rates of 1–10 kHz, pulse energies of 100–150 μJ, and objective lenses (e.g., 5×, NA=0.13) to achieve spot sizes of 10–20 μm for micron-scale feature definition. Optimal printing parameters for extrusion involve paste viscosities of 100–1700 P, extrusion flow rates of 0.1–0.5 mL/min, and stage speeds of 5–20 mm/s, calibrated to produce filaments with widths of 300–800 μm and layer heights of 300–500 μm.

The invention is applicable to a wide range of materials. Silicon carbide (SiC) components are fabricated using SiC-based pastes with boron nitride (BN) or zirconium diboride (ZrB₂) additives to engineer compliant interfaces or enhance thermal conductivity. Protonic ceramic fuel cell (PCFC) components—including anodes (e.g., NiO-BZY20), electrolytes (e.g., BCZYYb), and cathodes (e.g., BCFZY₀.₁)—are processed via RLRS to achieve phase-pure perovskite structures with high proton conductivity (>10⁻³ S/cm at 600 °C). Glass objects, particularly fused silica, are fabricated by direct laser melting of silica pastes or sol-gel films, with optimal laser parameters (e.g., 20 W, 1 mm/s, defocused beam) yielding transparent, low-porosity structures with >90% transmission in the visible spectrum.

For glass processing, the CO₂ laser’s wavelength matches the strong phonon absorption band of silica, enabling efficient volumetric heating. By controlling scan speed and power, the melt pool depth and solidification rate are tuned to suppress bubble formation and microcracking. The resulting glass components exhibit smooth surfaces, sharp edges, and complex 3D forms unattainable by molding or machining.

## EXAMPLES

### Example 1—Integrated Multi-Laser 3D Manufacturing System

An integrated multi-laser 3D manufacturing system was constructed to enable the L3DP process. The system includes a CO₂ laser heating system comprising a Synrad firestar v20 laser (10.64 μm wavelength) mounted on a Z-axis translation stage with ±1 μm positioning accuracy. The laser beam is focused or defocused using interchangeable lenses, and scanning is performed either by moving the substrate on a high-precision X-Y stage (resolution: 100 nm, speed: up to 5 m/s) or by a Galvano scanner (intelliSCAN® III 14) for high-speed rastering. A picosecond laser microfabrication system employs an Attodyne Olive-1064-10 ps-laser (1064 nm, 10 kHz rep rate) coupled to a 5× objective lens (NA=0.13), producing a 18 μm focal spot for precision ablation. The free-forming extrusion system uses a ViscoTec preeflow eco-PEN 300 microextruder with 0.5 mm nozzles, driven by regulated compressed air to deliver consistent paste flow. All subsystems are synchronized via a central control center running custom software that generates toolpaths from CAD models and coordinates laser-extrusion timing. The system arrangement integrates all components on a vibration-isolated optical table, with real-time monitoring via CCD cameras and pyrometers. This configuration enables seamless transitions between printing, drying, cutting, polishing, and sintering within a single workflow.
