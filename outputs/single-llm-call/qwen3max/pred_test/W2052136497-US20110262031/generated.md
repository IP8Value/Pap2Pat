# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

Image-Based Visual Hull (IBVH) is a well-established technique in the field of 3D reconstruction and image-based modeling that leverages multiple silhouette images of an object captured from different viewpoints to generate a visual hull—an approximation of the object’s geometry. The core principle underlying IBVH is derived from the “Shape-from-Silhouette” method, wherein each silhouette defines a back-projected volume in 3D space, and the intersection of these volumes across all input views yields a convex hull that encloses the object. This approach offers significant advantages over traditional geometry-based reconstruction methods, particularly in terms of reduced data acquisition costs and the ability to produce visually realistic renderings without requiring dense point clouds or complex surface scanning equipment. Despite its efficiency and practicality, conventional IBVH suffers from two fundamental limitations that hinder its applicability in scenarios where accurate representation of object bottoms and concave surfaces is critical.

The first limitation concerns the inability of conventional IBVH to accurately reconstruct the bottom surfaces of objects. In typical IBVH setups, reference images are captured from viewpoints distributed around the equatorial region of the object, with cameras positioned at similar heights relative to the object’s base. As a result, the bottom of the object remains occluded and unobserved in all reference silhouettes. Without direct visual information of the bottom, the reconstructed visual hull tends to taper into a conical or pointed shape, failing to preserve the true flat or structured geometry of the base. While one might consider capturing additional images by physically flipping the object to photograph its underside, such an approach introduces significant complications. Specifically, the flipped images would exist in a different spatial reference frame, necessitating complex alignment and registration procedures to integrate them with the original set of reference images. These alignment processes not only increase computational overhead but also introduce potential errors due to inaccuracies in pose estimation and coordinate transformation.

The second limitation pertains to the inherent inability of silhouette-based methods to capture concave geometries. By definition, a visual hull is a convex approximation; any indentation, cavity, or inward curvature on the object’s surface cannot be distinguished from the silhouette alone, as silhouettes only encode the outermost boundary of the object from a given viewpoint. Consequently, concave regions—such as the interior of a cup, the hollow of a bowl, or intricate carvings on an artifact—are erroneously reconstructed as convex protrusions. This geometric distortion is often accompanied by severe texture misplacement, as the rendering algorithm projects colors from reference images onto an incorrect surface topology. Although prior knowledge or auxiliary imaging techniques could theoretically aid in approximating concave features, existing IBVH implementations lack a systematic framework to incorporate such information without compromising the method’s efficiency or simplicity.

These limitations have constrained the use of IBVH in applications where fidelity to the complete object geometry—including undersides and internal cavities—is essential, such as in cultural heritage preservation, forensic analysis, medical imaging, and high-fidelity virtual exhibitions. While alternative approaches involving mirrors or multi-camera rigs have been explored to expand viewpoint coverage, they often fail to address both the bottom and concave problems simultaneously or require elaborate calibration procedures. Thus, there remains a pressing need for an improved IBVH methodology that seamlessly integrates bottom and concave surface reconstruction while maintaining the computational efficiency and ease of use that characterize the original technique.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides a novel method for reconstructing three-dimensional models of concave objects using an enhanced Image-Based Visual Hull (IBVH) framework that overcomes the limitations of conventional approaches in rendering both bottom surfaces and concave regions. The method employs a specialized image acquisition platform comprising a planar glass support and a planar mirror positioned beneath it, enabling simultaneous capture of top and bottom views of an object in a single photographic exposure. This configuration ensures that all reference images—whether depicting the object’s exterior, base, or interior concavities—are inherently aligned within a common coordinate system, thereby eliminating the need for post-acquisition alignment or complex multi-frame registration.

In one embodiment, the invention includes an apparatus comprising a storage medium and a plurality of processing modules configured to execute the improved reconstruction method. The storage medium retains reference image data, including both direct and reflected views of the object, as well as calibration parameters and intermediate computational results. The processing modules include a pre-processing module for segmenting silhouettes, a virtual-image synthesis module for generating transformed representations of reflected views, and a point relocation module for refining the 3D coordinates of surface points in concave and bottom regions. Together, these components enable efficient and accurate reconstruction of complex object geometries.

In an alternative embodiment, the apparatus further comprises a computing device equipped with a processor, system memory, and a bus interface controller, wherein the concave object modeling processing application is stored in non-removable storage and executed in conjunction with an operating system and supporting libraries. The application integrates modules for silhouette processing, virtual camera calibration, and visual hull refinement, all operating within a unified computational pipeline.

The method further includes a step of generating a virtual inside-out image for each concave region by synthesizing a negative silhouette cone from a virtual camera positioned within the cavity. This virtual image is used during rendering to subtract erroneous convex projections and relocate surface points to their correct concave positions. Similarly, for bottom surfaces, the method determines the 3D coordinates of the bottom plane boundary and adjusts ray-surface intersections accordingly when rendering from below-horizon viewpoints.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention addresses the challenge of modeling concave objects by extending the Image-Based Visual Hull (IBVH) technique to accurately reconstruct both bottom surfaces and concave regions through a combination of novel image acquisition and computational processing. Traditional visual hull methods rely on the intersection of silhouette cones—3D volumes back-projected from 2D object silhouettes—to form a convex approximation of the object. However, this convexity constraint inherently prevents the representation of indentations, cavities, or undersides, leading to geometric and textural distortions in the final model.

To reconstruct an image of an object with high fidelity, the disclosed method begins by capturing a set of reference images using a specialized platform that includes a transparent planar glass surface placed above a planar mirror. The object is positioned on the glass, allowing a single camera to capture both the direct view of the object’s top and the mirror-reflected view of its bottom in the same image frame. This dual-view acquisition ensures spatial consistency without requiring separate calibration or alignment steps. For concave objects, such as cups or vases, an additional image is captured from directly above the concave opening to provide silhouette information of the interior cavity.

Conventional processing methods, when applied to such objects, produce rendering results wherein the bottom tapers into a cone and the interior of a cup appears as a solid protrusion, as illustrated in comparative figures. In contrast, the disclosed approach introduces virtual cameras and virtual images to reinterpret reflected and overhead views within the IBVH framework. Specifically, each bottom-view reflection is transformed into a virtual image corresponding to a virtual camera positioned symmetrically below the mirror plane. The calibration parameters of this virtual camera are derived analytically from those of the real camera, leveraging the known geometry of the mirror plane.

Texture information is preserved by projecting 3D surface points onto the original reference images and computing a weighted average of color values, with weights assigned based on the angular proximity of the reference camera to the rendering viewpoint. This ensures photorealistic appearance even in previously unreconstructable regions.

The computing device implementing the invention includes a processor connected via a memory bus to system memory, which stores program data, operating system instructions, and the concave object modeling processing application. The processor features multiple cores, each with dedicated registers and access to hierarchical cache levels, enabling parallel processing of silhouette data. A memory controller manages data flow between the processor and storage devices, which may include non-removable solid-state drives and removable media such as SD cards. Computer storage media retain the application code, reference image data, and intermediate buffers.

The concave object modeling processing application comprises several key modules. The pre-processing module performs image segmentation to isolate object silhouettes from background. The silhouette processing module organizes silhouette edges into directional bins based on angle rather than slope, improving robustness when epipoles lie within the object area. The virtual-image synthesis module generates transformed images for bottom and concave views, while the point relocation module adjusts 3D point positions during rendering to conform to inferred bottom planes or concave boundaries.

The IBVH technique is enhanced by incorporating negative silhouette cones for concave regions. A virtual camera is placed inside the cavity, and its associated silhouette—derived from the overhead reference image—is used to define a subtraction volume. During rendering, the intersection of the viewing ray with the standard visual hull is computed, then modified by excluding segments that fall within the negative cone, thereby carving out the concave shape.

Shape-from-silhouette 3D reconstruction is thus extended beyond convex hulls. The silhouette cone for each view is defined by rays emanating from the camera center through silhouette pixels. The visual hull is the intersection of all such cones. To eliminate protuberant-shaped distortion in concave areas, the method determines the 3D coordinates of the concave region boundary by projecting segmented 2D silhouette contours back into 3D space using calibrated camera parameters.

Image segmentation isolates the object foreground, and positions on the 2D reference picture are projected back to 3D using inverse perspective mapping. The position for the virtual camera is chosen to lie along the central axis of the concave region, at a depth sufficient to capture the entire cavity. Camera parameters—including rotation and translation—are determined by mirroring the real camera’s pose relative to the mirror plane or cavity opening.

Finally, a virtual inside-out image is generated by rendering the concave silhouette from the virtual camera’s perspective. This image is used exclusively for geometric refinement, not texture synthesis, ensuring that the reconstructed model accurately reflects the object’s true concave topology while maintaining photorealistic surface appearance through projection from original reference images.
