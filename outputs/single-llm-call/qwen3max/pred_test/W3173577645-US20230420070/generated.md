# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

Protein structure prediction represents one of the most formidable and persistently active challenges in computational biology, with profound implications for drug discovery, enzyme design, and understanding molecular mechanisms of disease. The three-dimensional conformation of a protein governs its biological function, yet experimental determination of protein structures—through techniques such as X-ray crystallography, cryo-electron microscopy, or nuclear magnetic resonance spectroscopy—remains time-consuming, expensive, and often technically infeasible. Consequently, computational methods that can accurately predict protein structures from amino acid sequences alone have become indispensable tools in modern structural biology. Over the past several decades, numerous approaches have been developed, ranging from physics-based simulations to knowledge-driven statistical models and, more recently, deep learning architectures. Among these, fragment assembly has emerged as a particularly successful ab initio strategy, wherein short segments of known protein structures—termed fragments—are assembled into full-length models based on sequence similarity and energetic compatibility. Despite the recent breakthroughs achieved by end-to-end deep learning systems such as AlphaFold2, fragment-based methods continue to play a critical role in modeling proteins lacking homologous templates, especially in free modeling scenarios. However, the rich structural information embedded within high-quality fragment libraries—comprising not only local backbone conformations but also inter-residue distances, torsion angles, and secondary structure propensities—has remained largely underutilized beyond the narrow scope of fragment assembly protocols. Current state-of-the-art protein property prediction pipelines rely predominantly on sequential features derived from multiple sequence alignments (MSAs) and evolutionary profiles, neglecting the direct structural insights that fragment libraries can provide. This limitation becomes particularly acute for proteins with sparse or shallow MSAs, where evolutionary signals are weak and predictive accuracy suffers. Moreover, while gradient descent-based folding frameworks leverage predicted structural properties—such as Cβ–Cβ distances and backbone torsion angles—as energy potentials to guide conformational search, these potentials are typically derived solely from sequence-based predictors, thereby missing complementary constraints inherent in fragment-derived structural ensembles. The absence of systematic methods to extract, evaluate, and integrate fragment library information into both protein property prediction and structure optimization pipelines constitutes a significant gap in the field. Addressing this gap requires not only novel computational frameworks capable of transforming fragment-level data into actionable features or potentials but also rigorous evaluation metrics that quantify the fidelity of structural information at the fragment level, rather than merely at the residue or whole-protein level. Thus, there exists a compelling need for an integrated approach that leverages the full spectrum of structural detail encoded in fragment libraries to enhance both the prediction of protein structural properties and the accuracy of de novo protein structure modeling.

## SUMMARY

The present invention introduces a comprehensive and integrated solution for protein structure prediction that systematically exploits the rich structural information embedded within high-quality fragment libraries. This solution comprises two synergistic components: first, a method for transforming fragment libraries into protein-specific potential functions that guide gradient descent-based protein folding; and second, a deep neural network architecture that uses fragment libraries as input features to predict key structural properties with enhanced accuracy. The invention begins by constructing a fragment library for a target protein using a state-of-the-art fragment recruitment algorithm, such as DeepFragLib, which selects short structural templates from a database of known protein structures based on sequence homology and co-evolutionary signals. These fragments are then subjected to a “smoothing” operation that normalizes variable-length fragments into fixed-length sub-fragments, enabling consistent extraction of structural properties. Six key structural properties—including backbone torsion angles (ϕ and ψ), backbone angles (θ and τ), and inter-residue Cα–Cα and Cβ–Cβ distances—are extracted from each fragment and modeled using weighted Gaussian Mixture Models (wGMMs), where weights are assigned based on predicted fragment quality (e.g., predicted RMSD). These wGMMs are converted into differentiable potential functions via negative log-likelihood, which are then incorporated into a gradient descent-based folding framework such as SAMF to refine protein conformations. Concurrently, the invention provides a Fragment-Assisted Deep Neural Network (FA-DNN) that encodes the fragment library into a high-dimensional feature representation using a multi-layer convolutional encoder. This fragment-derived embedding is combined with traditional sequence-based features (e.g., PSSM, DCA statistics) and fed into a two-dimensional residual neural network to predict both one-dimensional properties (torsion and backbone angles) and two-dimensional properties (Cβ–Cβ distances). The entire system is trained on non-redundant, high-resolution protein structures and evaluated on independent benchmark datasets including CASP13 FM, CASP13 TBM, CAMEO, and CASP14 FM. The benefits of this solution are manifold: it significantly improves the accuracy of predicted structural properties compared to state-of-the-art methods that rely solely on sequence information; it enhances the quality of de novo protein models by providing complementary local structural constraints; and it enables more reliable model selection by narrowing the gap between top-ranked and best-performing decoys. Furthermore, the invention introduces novel fragment-level evaluation metrics—such as fragment secondary structure accuracy and angle/distance errors defined over fragment ensembles—that enable rigorous comparison of fragment library construction algorithms and facilitate the selection of optimal methods for downstream applications. By bridging the gap between fragment-based knowledge and deep learning-driven prediction, the present invention establishes a new paradigm for integrative protein structure modeling that leverages both explicit and implicit representations of structural information from fragment libraries.

### Structural Properties of Proteins and Fragments

Proteins are polymers of amino acids folded into specific three-dimensional conformations stabilized by a complex interplay of covalent and non-covalent interactions. The structural properties of proteins can be described at multiple levels, from local backbone geometry to long-range inter-residue contacts. Key structural descriptors include inter-residue distances, which define the spatial proximity of atoms in the folded state. Among these, the Cα–Cα distance—the Euclidean distance between alpha carbon atoms of two residues—provides a coarse-grained measure of backbone topology, while the Cβ–Cβ distance—between beta carbon atoms (or gamma carbon in glycine)—offers a more detailed representation of side-chain packing and tertiary interactions. Inter-residue orientations are captured by torsion angles, which describe the rotation around bonds in the polypeptide chain. The backbone torsion angles φ (phi) and ψ (psi) define the conformation of the main chain, with φ corresponding to the N–Cα bond and ψ to the Cα–C bond. Additional backbone angles θ and τ further refine the description: θ is the planar angle formed by three consecutive Cα atoms (Cα^{i−1}–Cα^i–Cα^{i+1}), and τ is the dihedral angle defined by four successive Cα atoms (Cα^{i−1}–Cα^i–Cα^{i+1}–Cα^{i+2}). These angles collectively encode the local curvature and twist of the protein backbone. Other orientations between atoms, such as side-chain chi angles, are also relevant but are less emphasized in the current invention. Bond lengths and bond angles, though relatively invariant due to covalent constraints, form the foundational geometry upon which higher-order structure is built. The secondary structure of a fragment—classified as helix (H), strand (E), coil (C), or other (O)—reflects the repetitive hydrogen-bonding patterns and local conformational preferences of contiguous residues. These structural properties, whether one-dimensional (e.g., torsion angles) or two-dimensional (e.g., distances), constitute the essential physical constraints that govern protein folding and are directly extractable from fragment libraries, forming the basis for both potential functions and neural network features in the present invention.

### Evaluation of Fragment Library

The evaluation of fragment libraries is critical to ensuring the quality of downstream protein structure prediction tasks. Traditional metrics such as precision—the fraction of fragments with RMSD below a threshold—and coverage—the fraction of residue positions spanned by at least one good fragment—provide a global assessment but fail to capture the fidelity of specific structural properties. To address this limitation, the present invention introduces a suite of fragment-level evaluation metrics that quantify the accuracy of seven key structural properties: fragment secondary structure accuracy, and the mean absolute error (MAE) of torsion angles φ and ψ, backbone angles θ and τ, and Cα–Cα and Cβ–Cβ distances. These metrics are defined as expectations over all fragments at each position and then averaged across all positions of the target protein, thereby reflecting the ensemble quality of the fragment library. Precision and coverage remain useful for overall library assessment, but the structural property metrics offer granular insight into the reliability of specific geometric constraints. For example, the accuracy of fragment secondary structure is computed by comparing the dominant secondary structure class of each fragment (H, E, C, or O) to that of the corresponding native segment, yielding a binary correctness score that is averaged across the library. Similarly, angle errors are computed as the circular MAE between predicted and native values, accounting for the periodic nature of angular data. Distance errors are computed as standard MAE over all intra-fragment residue pairs. Using these metrics, fragment libraries generated by different algorithms—such as NNMake, Flib-Coevo, and DeepFragLib—can be rigorously compared. The invention describes a process for selecting the optimal fragment library construction algorithm: first, generate fragment libraries for a representative set of proteins using candidate algorithms; second, compute all evaluation metrics for each library; third, compare the metrics across algorithms; and fourth, select the algorithm that demonstrates superior performance, particularly in terms of low error and high accuracy across multiple structural properties. DeepFragLib, for instance, consistently outperforms alternatives in empirical evaluations, justifying its use as the default fragment generator in the invention. The advantages of using these evaluation metrics include objective algorithm selection, quality control of fragment libraries, and correlation analysis between library quality and downstream prediction performance. In summary, the evaluation metrics provide a comprehensive, multi-dimensional assessment of fragment library quality, enabling informed decisions in protein structure prediction pipelines. The conclusion of this evaluation phase is the identification of a high-fidelity fragment library that serves as the foundation for subsequent prediction steps.

### Prediction of Protein Structure

Protein structure prediction in the present invention is achieved through a gradient descent-based optimization framework that incorporates potentials derived from fragment libraries. The process begins with the extraction of structural properties—specifically φ, ψ, θ, τ, Cα–Cα, and Cβ–Cβ—from a smoothed fragment library, where fragments are normalized to a fixed length (e.g., 7 residues) via a sliding window. These properties are then used to build weighted Gaussian Mixture Models (wGMMs) for each residue position and each structural property. The wGMMs account for the multimodal nature of conformational space by fitting multiple Gaussian components, with the number of components (e.g., four) determined via Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) minimization. Weights in the wGMMs are assigned based on predicted fragment RMSD, using a softmax function with a temperature parameter (e.g., T=0.1), so that higher-quality fragments contribute more strongly to the model. The resulting wGMMs are converted into potential functions through a negative log-likelihood transformation, yielding differentiable energy terms that penalize deviations from the fragment-derived distributions. For example, the potential for φ angles is defined as the negative log-sum of weighted Gaussian probabilities across all components, and similarly for other properties. These individual potentials are then combined into a unified target function via a weighted sum, where the weights are tuned on a reference dataset (e.g., CASP12 FM) to maximize structural accuracy (e.g., TM-Score). The target function, which may also include baseline potentials such as trRosetta-predicted Cβ–Cβ distances, is minimized using gradient descent to iteratively refine the protein conformation. The predicted structure is the final conformation that minimizes the total energy. The advantages of using potential functions derived from fragment libraries include the incorporation of local, position-specific structural constraints that complement global distance predictions, leading to improved model accuracy and better decoy ranking. Empirical comparisons show that models incorporating fragment-derived potentials achieve higher average TM-Scores and a greater number of correctly folded topologies (TM-Score > 0.5) than baseline methods. Statistical tests confirm the significance of these improvements. In summary, the protein structure prediction method leverages the explicit structural information in fragment libraries to construct customized, differentiable potentials that guide conformational search toward native-like states. The process concludes with the generation of a high-quality predicted structure that integrates both sequence-derived and fragment-derived constraints.

### Prediction of Protein Structural Properties

The prediction of protein structural properties is performed by a deep neural network termed FA-DNN (Fragment-Assisted Deep Neural Network), which integrates fragment library information with traditional sequence features. The process begins with the extraction of a fragment library property set for the target protein. For each residue position, up to 50 high-quality fragments are selected, and their structural properties—including secondary structure labels and sine/cosine-transformed torsion angles φ, ψ, θ, and τ—are encoded into a numerical format. These fragments are padded to a uniform length (e.g., R=15 residues) to form a four-dimensional tensor of size L×F×R×D, where L is the protein length, F is the number of fragments (e.g., 50), R is the padded fragment length, and D is the feature dimension. This tensor is input to a feature encoder, which consists of a hierarchical convolutional architecture. The encoder applies one-dimensional convolutions along the fragment length dimension, using multiple residual blocks with ELU activations and batch normalization, to learn local residue interactions within fragments. The hidden representation of the first residue of each fragment—which corresponds to the target position—is then extracted, and the representations across all F fragments are averaged to produce a per-residue embedding of size L×D’. This fragment library feature set is combined with a sequence feature set derived from the target amino acid sequence, including one-hot encoding, position-specific scoring matrices (PSSMs), and direct coupling analysis (DCA) statistics. Both feature sets are input to a property predictor, which employs a two-dimensional residual neural network with 30 residual blocks. The predictor performs pre-processing by tiling 1D features into 2D maps and concatenating them with pairwise DCA features. A symmetrization operation ensures that predicted distance matrices are symmetric. The network branches into two heads: one predicts 1D properties (φ, ψ, θ, τ) via global pooling and fully connected layers, and the other predicts 2D Cβ–Cβ distances using a distance mapping function (e.g., tanh-based) to emphasize short-range interactions. The loss function is a weighted sum of MAEs across all properties. Trained on a high-resolution dataset (HR5916), FA-DNN achieves state-of-the-art accuracy in predicting both 1D and 2D properties, outperforming methods like SPOT-1D and trRosetta, especially on targets with limited MSA depth. Thus, the invention provides a powerful framework for structural property prediction that leverages the implicit structural knowledge embedded in fragment libraries through deep learning.

### Example Method and Example Implementations

The invention provides a method for protein structure prediction that operates as follows: first, determine a plurality of fragments for each residue position of a target protein using a fragment library construction algorithm such as DeepFragLib. These fragments are generated by querying structural databases with the target sequence and selecting templates based on sequence similarity and co-evolutionary signals. Next, generate a first feature representation of protein structures by extracting structural properties from the fragment library and encoding them either explicitly (for potential functions) or implicitly (for neural network features). The method then determines a prediction of either the full 3D structure or specific structural properties. In the case of structural property prediction, the method determines a property value (e.g., φ angle) by processing the fragment library through FA-DNN, which outputs a probability distribution or point estimate for each property. For full structure prediction, the method generates a potential function from the wGMMs fitted to fragment-derived properties, combines it with other potentials (e.g., distance constraints), and defines a target function for the structure prediction model. The prediction of structure is obtained by minimizing this target function via gradient descent. The determination of the plurality of fragments involves initial fragment recruitment followed by length normalization to a predetermined number of residues (e.g., 7 or 15) to ensure consistency. Structural properties encompass both 1D (angles, secondary structure) and 2D (distances) descriptors. The first feature representation is generated by either statistical modeling (wGMMs) or deep encoding (convolutional networks). The method may also determine a plurality of structural properties simultaneously, encoding them via the feature encoder and predicting them via the property predictor. Additionally, the method includes a step for selecting the target algorithm for fragment library construction: reference property values are computed from native structures, true property values are extracted from candidate fragment libraries, differences (errors) are calculated, and the algorithm with the smallest differences is selected. The invention may be embodied in an electronic device comprising a processing unit and memory storing instructions that, when executed, perform the above steps. It may also be implemented as a computer program product comprising a computer-readable medium with program code, or as a machine-readable medium storing instructions for hardware logic components such as FPGAs or ASICs. These implementations enable automated, high-throughput protein structure prediction that leverages the full informational content of fragment libraries.
