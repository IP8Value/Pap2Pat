# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNICAL FIELD

The present invention relates generally to the field of semiconductor materials and device fabrication, and more specifically to methods for the direct growth of single-crystal III-V semiconductor structures on amorphous substrates. The invention encompasses a templated liquid-phase (TLP) crystal growth process that enables the formation of high-quality, single-crystalline III-V semiconductor features—such as indium phosphide (InP), gallium phosphide (GaP), and indium antimonide (InSb)—in user-defined geometries on non-epitaxial, amorphous surfaces including glass, silicon dioxide, and flexible polymers. This technique overcomes fundamental limitations of conventional epitaxial growth methods, which require lattice-matched crystalline substrates, and provides a scalable, low-temperature route to integrate optoelectronic-grade III-V semiconductors into heterogeneous systems, including silicon-based electronics, flexible displays, and photonic integrated circuits. The invention further includes doped semiconductor structures, complex three-dimensional architectures, and functional devices such as metal-oxide-semiconductor field-effect transistors (MOSFETs) and photo-MOSFETs fabricated from these TLP-grown materials.

## BACKGROUND

III-V semiconductors, composed of elements from group III (e.g., In, Ga, Al) and group V (e.g., P, As, Sb) of the periodic table, are critical materials for high-performance electronic and optoelectronic applications due to their superior electron mobility, direct bandgaps, and tunable optical properties. Conventional fabrication of high-quality III-V semiconductors relies on epitaxial growth techniques such as molecular beam epitaxy (MBE) or metal-organic chemical vapor deposition (MOCVD), which demand single-crystal substrates with closely matched lattice constants to minimize defect formation. This requirement severely restricts the choice of substrate materials, excludes the use of low-cost amorphous platforms like glass or plastic, and complicates monolithic integration with silicon complementary metal-oxide-semiconductor (CMOS) technology. Moreover, epitaxial processes are often conducted at high temperatures, involve complex gas handling systems, and exhibit limited scalability for large-area or patterned growth. These constraints have impeded the widespread adoption of III-V semiconductors in next-generation flexible electronics, large-area sensors, and cost-sensitive photonic systems. There exists a long-felt but unmet need for a method capable of producing single-crystal III-V semiconductors directly on arbitrary substrates without the stringent requirements of lattice matching, high temperature, or expensive single-crystal wafers.

## SUMMARY

The invention provides a method for fabricating single-crystal III-V semiconductor structures on amorphous substrates through a templated liquid-phase (TLP) crystal growth process. In a primary embodiment, a patterned layer of a group III element—such as indium—is deposited onto an amorphous substrate coated with a thin nucleation-promoting layer, and then encapsulated within a ceramic template. The structure is subsequently exposed to a group V element in gaseous form—such as phosphine—at elevated temperatures, enabling diffusion of the group V species through the ceramic template and reaction with the molten group III element to form a single-crystal III-V semiconductor that replicates the original template geometry. In an alternative embodiment, the method is extended to ternary and quaternary III-V compounds by co-depositing multiple group III elements or introducing additional dopant gases during growth, thereby enabling precise control over composition, doping, and optoelectronic properties. The resulting semiconductor structures are single-crystalline, exhibit excellent electronic quality, and can be integrated into functional devices such as MOSFETs and photo-detectors on diverse substrates including glass, silicon dioxide, and flexible polymers.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The following detailed description sets forth specific embodiments of the invention to enable those skilled in the art to make and use the disclosed technology. The scope of the invention is not limited to the described embodiments but encompasses all equivalents falling within the spirit and claims of the appended patent claims.

The invention addresses critical limitations of prior art methods for III-V semiconductor fabrication, which are inherently constrained by the necessity of epitaxial alignment with single-crystal substrates. Traditional approaches fail to produce high-quality single crystals on amorphous surfaces due to uncontrolled nucleation and polycrystalline grain formation. The present invention overcomes these challenges by introducing mechanical confinement and controlled supersaturation via a templated liquid-phase growth mechanism.

The motivation for enabling single-crystal growth on amorphous substrates stems from the desire to integrate high-performance III-V semiconductors with low-cost, large-area, and flexible platforms. However, this goal has been hindered by the thermodynamic and kinetic barriers associated with nucleation and grain boundary formation in non-epitaxial environments. Prior attempts to grow III-V materials on glass or oxide layers have resulted in polycrystalline films with poor electronic properties, unsuitable for advanced devices.

The TLP crystal growth process begins with the preparation of an amorphous substrate, such as a borosilicate glass slide or a silicon wafer with a thermal oxide layer. A resist material—typically defined by photolithography—is patterned to define the desired shape of the final semiconductor structure. The resist features may have lateral dimensions ranging from sub-micron to tens of microns, with heights of 100–500 nm, depending on the target application.

At block 102 of the process flow, the structure consists of the patterned resist on the amorphous substrate. A nucleation layer of molybdenum oxide (MoOx), typically 1–10 nm thick, is then deposited via thermal evaporation. This layer enhances the wetting and nucleation of the subsequent group III metal and promotes the formation of a single-crystal phase during reaction.

At block 104, a layer of a group III element—such as indium—is deposited onto the nucleation layer, filling the resist-defined cavities. The indium thickness is controlled to match the desired final semiconductor thickness, typically between 100 and 200 nm. Following this, a ceramic material—specifically silicon oxide (SiOx)—is evaporated to encapsulate the indium, forming a conformal cap layer 10–100 nm thick. Angled evaporation may be used to ensure complete side-wall coverage, creating a fully enclosed template.

At block 108, the resist is removed via lift-off, leaving behind isolated MoOx/In/SiOx pillars or patterns anchored to the substrate. The ceramic SiOx layer serves as a diffusion barrier that confines the liquid indium during heating and allows selective permeation of the group V element.

The structure at block 110 comprises discrete, mechanically confined indium features capped by SiOx. A second deposition of ceramic material may be performed to fully encapsulate any exposed indium surfaces, ensuring uniform confinement. This deposition is typically carried out by physical vapor deposition under cryogenic substrate conditions (<150 K) to maintain smooth film morphology.

At block 112, the entire structure is placed in a furnace and heated to a temperature between 500°C and 535°C in a nitrogen-group element gas atmosphere—such as 10% phosphine (PH3) diluted in hydrogen. Under these conditions, indium melts (melting point ~157°C) but remains confined within the SiOx template. Phosphorus atoms diffuse through the SiOx cap, dissolve into the liquid indium, and reach supersaturation, leading to nucleation of InP.

The diffusion of phosphorus through the ceramic material is thermally activated and rate-limited by the partial pressure of PH3 and the thickness of the SiOx layer. Once nucleation occurs, a phosphorus depletion zone forms around the growing crystal, suppressing secondary nucleation events within the same template. This self-limiting mechanism is key to achieving single-crystal growth.

Growth proceeds for 10–20 minutes, during which time the entire indium volume converts to InP. The final single-crystal semiconductor structure replicates the original template geometry—whether circles, squares, letters, or complex logos—with lateral dimensions from 150 nm to 80 μm. The height corresponds to the initial indium thickness, typically 100–200 nm.

The resulting InP structures are confirmed to be stoichiometric (1:1 In:P) by energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) and exhibit zincblende crystal structure by X-ray diffraction. Electron backscatter diffraction (EBSD) mapping confirms single-crystalline nature per feature, with preferential (10n) orientation where n = 1–2. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) reveals a sharp interface between crystalline InP and the amorphous substrate, mediated by a thin MoOx/MoPx interlayer, confirming non-epitaxial growth.

The ceramic material may be optionally removed post-growth using a wet etch, such as buffered oxide etch (BOE), to expose the semiconductor surface for device processing. This etching process is selective and does not degrade the InP crystal.

The TLP process offers significant advantages: it operates at lower temperatures than MOCVD, uses only one vapor-phase precursor (group V), enables deterministic shape control, and is inherently self-limiting. It is compatible with standard lithography and device fabrication tools, allowing seamless integration into existing semiconductor manufacturing lines.

Scalability is demonstrated by growth across a full 4-inch wafer in a cold-wall furnace, with uniform photoluminescence indicating consistent crystal quality. The method is also substrate-agnostic: InP has been grown directly on borosilicate glass and transferred onto polyethylene terephthalate (PET) using a polyamic acid-based transfer process, enabling flexible electronics.

Moreover, the technique supports complex 3D architectures, such as vertically stacked InP/SiOx layers, by starting with multilayer templates. In situ doping is achieved by introducing germane (GeH4) during growth, yielding n-type InP with carrier concentrations up to 1.3 × 10¹⁹ cm⁻³. Photoluminescence spectra show Burstein–Moss shift and Urbach tail parameters comparable to epitaxial wafers, confirming high optoelectronic quality.

Functional devices include long-channel Schottky n-type MOSFETs with top gates, exhibiting ON-currents of 120 μA/μm, ON/OFF ratios >10⁵, and electron mobility of 675 cm²/V·s. Top-gated photo-MOSFETs with indium tin oxide (ITO) gates show responsivity of ~700 A/W and specific detectivity of 8.4 × 10¹¹ Jones, rivaling state-of-the-art detectors.

The method is extensible to other III-V systems, including GaP and InSb, as demonstrated in supplementary experiments. The invention thus provides a universal platform for single-crystal III-V semiconductor integration on arbitrary substrates, enabling new paradigms in flexible optoelectronics, large-area sensing, and heterogeneous integration.
