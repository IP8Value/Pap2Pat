# DESCRIPTION

Bio-derived polymers represent a transformative class of materials designed to address the growing environmental and economic challenges associated with conventional petroleum-based plastics. These polymers are synthesized from renewable biological feedstocks—such as plant-derived compounds—and offer a sustainable alternative that aligns with global efforts to reduce carbon emissions, minimize reliance on fossil resources, and promote circular economy principles. Unlike traditional synthetic polymers, which are derived from finite hydrocarbon reserves and often persist in the environment for centuries, bio-derived polymers can be engineered to exhibit comparable or superior performance characteristics while maintaining biodegradability, renewability, and reduced ecological footprints. The present invention specifically focuses on a novel class of bio-derived polymers produced from terpenes and terpenoids—naturally abundant, structurally diverse hydrocarbons found in essential oils of plants—which have been chemically modified to serve as functional monomers capable of undergoing controlled polymerization. This approach not only leverages the inherent structural complexity and chirality of terpene scaffolds but also enables precise tuning of polymer properties such as glass transition temperature (Tg), solubility, thermal stability, and mechanical strength. By integrating green chemistry principles with scalable synthetic methodologies, the invention provides a commercially viable pathway to high-performance polymers that meet industrial standards without compromising sustainability goals.

## BACKGROUND TO THE INVENTION

The global demand for polymeric materials continues to rise, driven by applications in packaging, automotive, construction, electronics, and healthcare. However, over 95% of these polymers are derived from non-renewable petroleum feedstocks, contributing significantly to greenhouse gas emissions and resource depletion. In response, there is increasing regulatory, consumer, and industrial pressure to transition toward renewable alternatives. Despite this imperative, renewable polymers currently occupy less than 5% of the global market, largely due to perceived limitations in cost, scalability, and performance relative to established synthetic counterparts. Synthetic polymers, while highly tunable and robust, suffer from intrinsic environmental drawbacks: their production is energy-intensive, reliant on volatile crude oil markets, and generates persistent plastic waste that accumils in ecosystems. These shortcomings underscore the urgent need for sustainable feedstocks that can be integrated into existing manufacturing infrastructures without requiring costly retooling or process overhauls.

Biorenewable feedstocks—particularly those derived from biomass—offer a compelling solution. Among these, terpenes and terpenoids stand out due to their natural abundance, structural diversity, and commercial availability at multi-tonne scales. Terpenes are a class of hydrocarbons biosynthesized from isoprene units (C5H8), typically classified as monoterpenes (C10), sesquiterpenes (C15), or diterpenes (C20). Terpenoids, or modified terpenes, contain oxygenated functional groups such as alcohols, ketones, or aldehydes, arising from natural oxidation processes. Common examples include limonene (from citrus peels), α- and β-pinene (from pine resin), and carvone (from spearmint oil)—all of which are produced industrially as byproducts of the pulp and flavor industries, ensuring low-cost and sustainable supply chains.

Historically, attempts to polymerize terpenes directly have met with limited success. Their internal or sterically hindered alkenes resist homopolymerization under standard free radical conditions, and even copolymerization with reactive comonomers like styrene yields low incorporation levels and low molecular weight products. Alternative strategies, including cationic polymerization of pinenes or ring-opening metathesis of sesquiterpenes, require cryogenic temperatures or expensive catalysts, rendering them impractical for large-scale adoption. Other approaches involve chemical modification—such as epoxidation of limonene followed by CO2 copolymerization or Baeyer-Villiger oxidation to form lactones—but these often demand stringent reaction conditions, specialized catalysts, or are restricted to terpenoids with pre-existing functional groups (e.g., carboxylic acids in rosin derivatives). Consequently, the vast majority of commercially available terpenes remain inaccessible as polymer precursors.

A critical unmet need persists: a commercially viable, scalable, and environmentally benign route to convert abundant, unfunctionalized terpenes into polymerizable monomers with controlled molecular architecture. Central to this challenge is the ability to regulate molecular weight and polymer microstructure—key determinants of thermal and mechanical properties. Without such control, bio-derived polymers cannot match the performance benchmarks set by petroleum-based analogues. Thus, there remains a significant technological gap in the field: a general methodology that transforms simple terpenes into functionalized acrylate or methacrylate monomers via sustainable chemistry, enabling their polymerization into high-performance, tunable, and application-ready materials.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a novel and versatile method for producing bio-derived polymers from terpenes and terpenoids, overcoming the limitations of prior art through innovative chemical functionalization and polymerization strategies. At its core, the invention introduces a two-pronged approach: first, the synthesis of functionalized monomers by modifying naturally occurring terpenes or terpenoids to introduce polymerizable vinyl groups—specifically acrylate or methacrylate functionalities—and second, the controlled polymerization of these monomers into high-molecular-weight polymers with tailored physical properties.

In a primary aspect, the invention discloses a method for producing functionalized monomers comprising the steps of (a) converting a terpene or terpenoid into an alcohol derivative via reduction or hydroboration-oxidation, and (b) esterifying the resulting alcohol with an acid chloride-containing monomer bearing a vinyl, acrylic, or allyl group, wherein the organic group R is selected to confer desired reactivity or stability. This method enables the incorporation of terpene moieties into acrylic frameworks while preserving stereochemical integrity and enabling scale-up.

In an alternative first aspect, the invention provides a “green” route to the same functionalized monomers by replacing toxic acid chlorides with carboxylic acid-containing monomers (e.g., acrylic or methacrylic acid) and employing a coupling agent such as propyl phosphonic anhydride (T3P®), which generates water-soluble, non-hazardous byproducts. This embodiment enhances environmental compatibility and aligns with industrial safety standards.

A second aspect of the invention involves a catalytic one-pot method for directly introducing vinyl functionality onto terpenes via palladium-catalyzed allylic C–H oxidation in the presence of acrylic or methacrylic acid. This approach eliminates intermediate isolation steps, uses air as a co-oxidant, and achieves high regioselectivity and yield—particularly for β-pinene—thereby offering a streamlined, scalable pathway to monomer synthesis.

The third aspect encompasses the functionalized monomers themselves, which are characterized by a terpene or terpenoid backbone linked via an ester bond to a polymerizable group selected from acrylate, methacrylate, or allyl moieties. These monomers are designed to undergo facile free radical or controlled radical polymerization.

The fourth aspect describes a method for producing bio-derived polymers by polymerizing the aforementioned functionalized monomers using techniques such as free radical polymerization (FRP) or reversible addition-fragmentation chain-transfer (RAFT) polymerization, optionally in the presence of a chain transfer agent (CTA)—including terpene-based CTAs—to control molecular weight and dispersity.

The fifth aspect relates to the resulting bio-derived polymers, which may be homopolymers or copolymers, exhibiting glass transition temperatures (Tg) ranging from below room temperature to above 140°C, depending on monomer structure and polymerization conditions. Notably, polymethacrylates derived from α-pinene achieve Tg values up to 142°C, rivaling engineering thermoplastics.

The sixth aspect includes articles formed from these polymers, such as reusable surgical instruments, high-temperature containers, or powder coatings, demonstrating real-world applicability across diverse sectors.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

The invention utilizes terpenes and terpenoids as foundational building blocks. Terpenes are defined as hydrocarbons composed of two or more isoprene units, with monoterpenes (C10H16) being particularly preferred due to their commercial availability and structural rigidity. Terpenoids are oxygenated derivatives of terpenes, often containing hydroxyl, carbonyl, or carboxyl groups. The presence of a hydroxyl group in certain terpenoids (e.g., menthol) facilitates direct esterification, but the invention primarily targets unfunctionalized terpenes, which are modified to introduce such groups.

Preferred terpenes include those with C10 structures, which may exist in open-chain (e.g., myrcene) or cyclic forms. Cyclic terpenes may feature six-membered rings (e.g., limonene) or bicyclic frameworks (e.g., α-pinene, β-pinene). Commonly available examples include (+)-α-pinene, (−)-β-pinene, (R)-(+)-limonene, (R)-(−)-carvone, camphene, terpinolene, and γ-terpinene—all of which conform to the general formula C10H16 or its oxygenated variants.

To introduce hydroxyl functionality, several methods are employed. Carbonyl groups in terpenoids like carvone are reduced using lithium aluminium hydride in anhydrous ether at 0–25°C. Alternatively, alkenes in terpenes undergo hydroboration-oxidation: treatment with borane (e.g., BH3·THF) in tetrahydrofuran at 0°C, followed by oxidation with sodium hydroxide and hydrogen peroxide at 0–25°C, yields secondary alcohols with anti-Markovnikov regioselectivity. Catalytic hydrogenation using Pd/C or PtO2 in ethanol at 25–80°C under H2 atmosphere may also be used for selective reduction.

The resulting alcohols are then esterified. In one embodiment, acryloyl or methacryloyl chloride is added dropwise to a solution of the terpene alcohol and a base (e.g., triethylamine) in dichloromethane at 0–25°C, yielding acrylate or methacrylate monomers. In a greener alternative, acrylic or methacrylic acid is coupled using T3P® in the presence of a base like N,N-diisopropylethylamine in ethyl acetate at 25–60°C.

In a catalytic one-pot method, β-pinene is reacted with methacrylic acid (2 equiv), palladium acetate (2 mol%), and benzoquinone (2 equiv) in air at 50°C for 24 hours, yielding regioisomeric methacrylate monomers in 82% yield. Benzoquinone serves as oxidant, ligand, and radical inhibitor.

Polymerization is achieved via FRP using initiators like AIBN or V-88 at 60–110°C in toluene or bulk. Molecular weight is controlled by chain transfer agents (e.g., dodecanethiol or terpinolene), with terpenes themselves acting as effective CTAs due to labile allylic hydrogens. Polymers exhibit Tg values from 12°C (poly(α-pinene acrylate)) to 142°C (poly(α-pinene methacrylate)), with carvone methacrylate reaching 117°C—ideal for powder coatings.

Articles include crosslinked coatings formed by UV curing of carvone methacrylate with trimethylolpropane tris(3-mercaptopropionate), yielding transparent, glossy films with pencil hardness of 2B and König hardness of 185 s. Experimental data confirm controlled polymerization, structural fidelity, and performance parity with petroleum-based acrylics.

### Terpinolene and Gamma-Terpinene as Chain Transfer Agents

Terpinolene and γ-terpinene function effectively as chain transfer agents (CTAs) in free radical polymerizations due to their allylic hydrogen atoms, which readily participate in hydrogen abstraction by growing polymer radicals. Experimental results demonstrate that inclusion of 1–5 mol% terpinolene during the polymerization of limonene methacrylate reduces molecular weight predictably while maintaining narrow dispersity (Đ < 1.5). Monomer reactivity studies indicate that terpinolene exhibits higher chain transfer constants than conventional thiols in certain systems, likely due to resonance stabilization of the resulting terpene radical. Optimal reaction conditions involve 65°C in toluene with AIBN initiation, where terpinolene suppresses gelation in multifunctional monomers and enables precise molecular weight targeting without toxic additives.

## EXAMPLES

### Example 1

General methods for hydroxylated derivatives include: Method 1a—hydroboration-oxidation of α-pinene using BH3·THF (1 M in THF, 1.1 equiv) at 0°C for 2 h, then NaOH (3 M) and H2O2 (30%) at 0°C for 1 h, yielding (1R,2S,5R)-6,6-dimethylbicyclo[3.1.1]heptan-2-ol as a single diastereomer (85% yield). Method 1b—similar treatment of β-pinene gives (1R,2R,3R,5S)-2,6,6-trimethylbicyclo[3.1.1]heptan-3-ol (82% yield). Method 1c—reduction of carvone with LiAlH4 (1.2 equiv) in dry THF at 0°C for 4 h affords (1R,5S)-2-methyl-5-(prop-1-en-2-yl)cyclohex-2-en-1-ol as a racemic mixture at C1 (90% yield).

Esterification (General Method 2): Alcohol (10 mmol), triethylamine (12 mmol), and methacryloyl chloride (11 mmol) in CH2Cl2 at 0°C for 1 h, then stirred at 25°C overnight. Purification by column chromatography yields limonene methacrylate as a colorless oil (78% yield), stabilized with 200 ppm hydroquinone monomethyl ether.

Alternative green synthesis: Limonene alcohol (5 g), methacrylic acid (2.2 equiv), T3P® (50% in EtOAc, 1.5 equiv), and DIPEA (2 equiv) in EtOAc at 50°C for 12 h gives limonene methacrylate (72% yield) after aqueous workup.

One-pot catalytic synthesis: β-Pinene (5 mmol), methacrylic acid (10 mmol), Pd(OAc)2 (0.1 mmol), BQ (10 mmol) in air at 50°C for 24 h yields a 10:1 mixture of regioisomeric methacrylates (82% combined yield). Scale-up to 50 g demonstrates reproducibility and ease of isolation.

### Example 2

Free radical polymerization of α-pinene methacrylate (1 g) in toluene (5 mL) with AIBN (1 mol%) at 65°C for 24 h yields polymer (Mn = 23,600 g/mol, Đ = 1.8). Using V-88 initiator at 80°C improves control (Đ = 1.4). Addition of DDM (0.5–5 mol%) linearly reduces Mn from 23,600 to 4,800 g/mol. Natural terpenes like terpinolene (2 mol%) similarly control Mn, confirming utility as sustainable CTAs. FTIR confirms consumption of methacrylate C=C (1635 cm⁻¹) without side reactions.

## CONCLUSIONS

The invention establishes a robust, scalable platform for synthesizing high-performance bio-derived polymers from abundant terpenes. Through strategic functionalization and controlled polymerization, it delivers materials with tunable thermal and mechanical properties suitable for demanding applications—from high-Tg engineering plastics to crosslinkable coatings—while adhering to green chemistry principles and enabling drop-in compatibility with existing industrial processes.
