# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND OF INVENTION

Electromagnetic rotary drives represent a foundational class of electromechanical systems used across industrial, aerospace, and energy applications to convert electrical energy into controlled rotational motion. These drives typically consist of a stator with windings and a rotor, which may include permanent magnets, salient poles, or conductive elements depending on the motor type. In conventional implementations, such as permanent magnet synchronous motors (PMSMs), induction motors, or switched reluctance machines, the primary objective is torque production with high efficiency and minimal mechanical losses. To achieve stable operation, these systems rely on mechanical bearings that constrain the rotor’s degrees of freedom, allowing only rotation about a fixed axis while supporting radial and axial loads.

The conventional control system for such electromagnetic rotary drives employs field-oriented control (FOC) or similar vector control strategies to independently regulate torque-producing (q-axis) and flux-producing (d-axis) currents in a rotor-synchronous reference frame. This approach enables precise speed and torque control by decoupling the electromagnetic dynamics. However, this architecture assumes perfect mechanical alignment and static support provided by bearings, which physically prevent lateral displacement or axial drift of the rotor. Consequently, the control system is designed solely for motoring performance and does not account for dynamic stabilization of the rotor’s position in space.

Despite their widespread use, conventional control systems coupled with mechanical bearings suffer from several critical limitations. Mechanical bearings introduce friction, wear, and the need for lubrication, all of which reduce system lifetime and reliability—especially in inaccessible or extreme environments such as space-based flywheel energy storage systems or embedded generators within jet engines operating at temperatures exceeding 1000°F. Moreover, mechanical bearings impose physical constraints on rotor design, often necessitating longer axial lengths to accommodate separate bearing housings. This elongation lowers the natural bending mode frequencies of the rotor shaft, creating a risk that operational speeds may exceed these critical frequencies, leading to resonance, instability, and catastrophic failure. Even when magnetic bearings are employed instead of mechanical ones, traditional approaches require dedicated levitation windings and additional iron structures that do not contribute to torque production, thereby increasing system mass, volume, and complexity without enhancing power density.

In response to these challenges, the bearingless motor-generator has emerged as an integrated solution that combines torque generation and magnetic levitation within a single electromagnetic structure. Unlike hybrid systems that pair a conventional motor with discrete magnetic bearings, a true bearingless motor uses the same stator windings and magnetic circuit for both functions. Prior art in this domain typically employs dual-winding configurations: one set of windings with *p* poles for torque production and another with *p±2* poles for levitation force generation. While this decouples control to some extent, it inherently partitions the available copper and iron resources, preventing dynamic reallocation of electrical capacity between motoring and levitation based on instantaneous system demands. This rigidity limits fault tolerance and operational flexibility, particularly in scenarios where transient levitation forces dominate or where maximum torque must be sustained under positional disturbances.

These shortcomings motivate the development of a new control system for bearingless motor-generators—one that eliminates the need for separate windings, fully utilizes all conductive and magnetic materials for both torque and levitation, and enables five-axis active suspension (two radial planes plus axial translation, plus two tilt axes implicitly stabilized through radial control). Such a system must overcome the fundamental symmetry of conventional motor windings, which inherently cancel lateral forces, and instead exploit controlled asymmetries to generate net levitation forces without compromising motoring performance. The present invention addresses this need through a novel winding topology, advanced current regulation in multiple pole-pair subsystems, and a unified control framework capable of simultaneous torque and multi-axis force command execution.

## SUMMARY OF INVENTION

The present invention introduces a control system for a bearingless motor-generator that achieves full five-axis magnetic levitation using a single set of stator windings without dedicated levitation coils. This is accomplished by electrically isolating individual pole pairs within the stator winding configuration, thereby enabling independent control of d-axis and q-axis currents in each pole pair’s rotor-synchronous reference frame. By modulating the d-axis current supplied to each pole pair, the invention creates intentional imbalances in the magnetic flux distribution around the rotor periphery, resulting in controllable net radial and axial forces that levitate the rotor without mechanical contact.

In a preferred embodiment, the bearingless motor-generator comprises a cylindrical or conical rotor equipped with surface-mounted permanent magnets and a stator wound with three or more pole pairs, each connected to its own independent power electronic inverter leg. The control system executes field-oriented control for torque production via q-axis currents while simultaneously regulating levitation forces through precisely coordinated d-axis current commands to each pole pair. The cylindrical embodiment provides two-axis radial levitation in a single plane, whereas the conical embodiment—featuring two oppositely oriented conical air gaps sharing a common rotor—extends this capability to include axial force generation, thereby enabling full five-degree-of-freedom suspension.

The invention further includes a real-time force synthesis algorithm that maps a desired levitation force vector in Cartesian coordinates to optimal combinations of d-axis currents in the available pole pairs. This mapping leverages a pre-characterized set of six fundamental force vectors, each corresponding to positive or negative d-axis excitation of one of the three pole pairs. By selecting the two bounding vectors that enclose the desired force direction and solving a linear combination in that local basis, the controller computes the minimal current commands necessary to produce the target force with reduced ripple and improved efficiency. This approach is seamlessly integrated with a position feedback loop that stabilizes the rotor against disturbances using proportional-derivative (PD) control augmented with negative stiffness compensation to counteract inherent magnetic instabilities.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The cylindrical motor control system of the present invention operates by treating each pole pair as an independently addressable electromagnetic actuator. In a standard three-phase motor with three pole pairs (i.e., six poles total), conventional practice connects all pole-pair windings in series or parallel, enforcing identical current waveforms and thus symmetric magnetic forces that sum to zero net lateral force. The invention departs from this norm by leaving the pole pairs electrically disconnected, allowing a multi-phase inverter with eighteen semiconductor switches—six per pole pair—to inject distinct d-axis and q-axis currents into each subsystem. This architecture enables the control system to break rotational symmetry deliberately, generating a net force on the rotor by unbalancing the magnetic pressure around its circumference.

A key advantage of this new control system is its ability to dynamically allocate electrical resources between torque and levitation. During high-torque operation, q-axis currents dominate, while d-axis currents are minimized except as needed for flux weakening. Conversely, during levitation-critical phases—such as startup, shutdown, or disturbance rejection—d-axis currents are modulated to produce stabilizing forces without sacrificing the availability of copper for motoring. This flexibility enhances fault tolerance; for instance, if one inverter leg fails, the remaining pole pairs can compensate by redistributing force commands, albeit with reduced bandwidth.

The winding configuration features individually stimulated pole pairs, each comprising a full three-phase set wound to produce a specific spatial harmonic. For a machine with three pole pairs, the phase currents *i_a1*, *i_b1*, *i_c1* drive the first pole pair, *i_a2*, *i_b2*, *i_c2* the second, and *i_a3*, *i_b3*, *i_c3* the third. These are transformed into rotor reference frames rotating at the respective electrical speeds (which are identical in a synchronous machine) to yield d-q components *i_d1*, *i_q1*, etc. Crucially, only the d-axis components *i_d1*, *i_d2*, *i_d3* are used for force generation, as they directly modulate the permanent magnet flux linkage.

To analyze force production, the system models fictional coils aligned with the d-axis of each pole pair in the rotor reference frame. When a d-axis current flows, it either augments or opposes the permanent magnet flux in that sector, altering the local air-gap flux density. Due to the rotor’s circular symmetry, this imbalance creates a net Lorentz-like force perpendicular to the gradient of flux density. Simulations confirm that exciting *i_d1* alone produces a force vector whose magnitude remains constant over electrical angle but whose direction rotates synchronously with the rotor. Similar behavior is observed for *i_d2* and *i_d3*, yielding six distinct force vectors (positive and negative for each pole pair) spaced 60 electrical degrees apart.

The force magnitude exhibits minimal ripple due to the sinusoidal nature of the underlying magnetic circuit, and the phase of each force vector is a deterministic function of rotor position. By plotting these phases, the controller identifies which two adjacent vectors bound the direction of the desired force command. A coordinate transformation then expresses the target force as a linear combination of these two basis vectors, from which the required d-axis currents are derived via inversion of a 2×2 matrix relating current to force.

This force synthesis method defines a hexagonal region of achievable forces in the x-y plane, with boundaries formed by the six fundamental vectors. Currents along these boundaries correspond to maximum utilization of two pole pairs, while interior points use sub-maximal currents. The control system continuously updates this decomposition at each control cycle (e.g., every 0.3 ms in the prototype), ensuring rapid response to position errors.

The mechanical model treats the rotor as a rigid body with lateral position *x + jy* and velocity *ẋ + jẏ* expressed as complex quantities. Newton’s second law gives *m(ẍ + jÿ) = F_x + jF_y − k(x + jy)*, where *k* represents destabilizing negative magnetic stiffness. Linearizing yields a state-space system with matrices capturing mass, damping, and stiffness. Controllability analysis confirms full rank, verifying that arbitrary forces can stabilize the system.

Angular dynamics are similarly modeled using rotor angle *θ* and speed *ω*, governed by *J dω/dt = τ − Bω*, where *τ* is electromagnetic torque from q-axis currents. The angular controllability matrix is also full rank, ensuring independent speed regulation.

The rudimentary magnetic bearing controller implements PD feedback on position error, augmented by feedforward cancellation of negative stiffness. During levitation tests, the rotor is spun up using torque commands while the levitation controller maintains centering. Experimental plots show stable x-y trajectories, low-position ripple, and smooth phase currents. When motoring current is added, the levitation performance remains robust, demonstrating decoupling.

To avoid saturation and maintain stability, the motor is de-rated from its theoretical maximum power, reserving current capacity for levitation transients. Finally, the conical motor embodiment uses two stators with opposing conical air gaps. Axial force arises from the differential d-axis current sum between top and bottom motors. An axial control block computes a compensating bias current to null unintended axial forces from radial commands, enabling independent z-axis control.
