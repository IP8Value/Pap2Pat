# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD

Spectral imaging refers to the acquisition of a three-dimensional spectral data cube comprising two spatial dimensions and one spectral dimension, wherein the intensity of light from a source object is measured across a discrete set of wavelengths within a defined spectral range. This technique enables the characterization of materials, biological tissues, and other subjects based on their unique spectral signatures, thereby facilitating applications in diverse fields such as medical diagnostics, remote sensing, food safety inspection, art conservation, astronomy, and industrial quality control. The present invention pertains specifically to snapshot spectral imaging (SSI), a modality that captures the entire spectral cube in a single integration period of an image sensor, thereby enabling real-time or near real-time spectral analysis of dynamic scenes without the need for mechanical scanning or sequential filtering. The invention further relates to optical systems that integrate compressed sensing principles with specially engineered optical diffusers to achieve high-fidelity spectral reconstruction from a single dispersed and diffused monochromatic image captured by a standard digital camera architecture.

## BACKGROUND

Snapshot spectral imagers have emerged as a critical advancement over traditional spectral imaging techniques that rely on time-sequential acquisition methods, such as pushbroom scanning or filter-wheel-based systems, which are inherently unsuitable for capturing rapidly changing phenomena. Among existing SSI architectures, Coded Aperture Snapshot Spectral Imagers (CASSI) represent a notable approach that employs a coded aperture mask placed at an intermediate image plane to encode spectral information onto a two-dimensional detector array. These systems typically utilize binary or grayscale masks, or even programmable spatial light modulators, to modulate the incoming light before it is spectrally dispersed by a grating or prism. While effective, such designs necessitate additional optical components—including relay lenses to form an intermediate image—thereby increasing the system’s physical footprint, weight, complexity, and cost. Compressed sensing (CS)-based spectral imaging (CS-SI) has been proposed as a computational alternative that leverages the inherent sparsity of natural images in certain transform domains to reconstruct high-dimensional spectral data from underdetermined measurements. CS theory posits that if a signal is sparse or compressible in some basis, it can be accurately recovered from far fewer linear measurements than dictated by the Nyquist–Shannon sampling theorem, provided the measurement matrix satisfies certain incoherence or restricted isometry properties (RIP). Despite these theoretical advances, practical implementations of CS-SI have often retained complex optical layouts that hinder miniaturization and integration into consumer-grade imaging platforms. A persistent limitation across known solutions is the trade-off between optical simplicity, spectral fidelity, spatial resolution, and system size. There remains a compelling need for a truly compact, low-cost, and mechanically robust snapshot spectral imager that can be retrofitted onto or derived from conventional digital cameras without requiring intermediate imaging optics, moving parts, or specialized sensors, while still enabling accurate reconstruction of full spectral cubes through efficient computational algorithms.

## SUMMARY

The present invention introduces a novel snapshot spectral imaging (SSI) apparatus and method based on compressed sensing (CS) principles, wherein spectral and spatial information is encoded in a single monochromatic image via a specially designed optical element referred to herein as a Restricted Isometry Property (RIP) diffuser. This RIP diffuser is a static, phase-only optical component positioned at the entrance pupil of a standard digital camera, configured to simultaneously diffuse and disperse incident polychromatic light in a wavelength-dependent manner. The resulting dispersed and diffused (DD) image, captured by a monochromatic image sensor in a single snapshot, contains a linear mixture of the spectral cube’s spatial and spectral data. The invention further comprises a digital processing unit that reconstructs the original three-dimensional spectral cube from this single DD image using CS-based iterative algorithms, particularly Bregman iteration schemes. In one embodiment, the apparatus includes a digital camera equipped with a monochromatic sensor, a band-pass filter to define the operational spectral range, and the RIP diffuser mounted at the aperture stop. The RIP diffuser is engineered such that its impulse response, when combined with the imaging optics, yields a sensing matrix that satisfies the RIP condition, thereby ensuring stable and accurate reconstruction of sparse representations of the spectral cube. The reconstruction process exploits the fact that natural spectral cubes are highly compressible in wavelet or framelet transform domains, allowing recovery from significantly fewer measurements than unknowns. Specific embodiments include variations in optical layout—such as double-aperture configurations, reflective-refractive beam splitters, turning mirrors, diffractive dispersers, and integrated posterior optical blocks—all designed to maintain compatibility with compact form factors while optimizing light throughput and dispersion characteristics. The invention thus provides a pathway to miniaturized, low-cost, high-performance SSI systems suitable for integration into smartphones, drones, medical endoscopes, and other portable devices.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The invention provides a snapshot spectral imager that integrates a digital camera with a monochromatic image sensor and a RIP diffuser positioned at the entrance pupil. This configuration eliminates the need for intermediate image planes, coded apertures, or moving parts, thereby enabling a compact and robust design. The RIP diffuser possesses unique optical properties: it is a phase-only element that imparts a spatially varying, wavelength-dependent phase shift to incident light, effectively acting as both a diffuser and a dispersive element. This dual functionality ensures that each wavelength component of the incoming light is spatially scrambled in a distinct, deterministic yet pseudo-random manner, producing a DD image that encodes the full spectral cube into a single 2D intensity pattern. Compressed sensing theory underpins the reconstruction methodology, relying on the observation that multispectral images are compressible—i.e., they admit sparse representations—in appropriate transform domains such as 2D framelets or 3D wavelets. The sensing process is modeled as a linear transformation **Y = HX**, where **Y** is the vectorized DD image, **X** is the vectorized spectral cube, and **H** is the sensing matrix derived from the system’s point spread function (PSF) across all wavelengths. The matrix **H** is constructed such that it satisfies the Restricted Isometry Property (RIP) of order *K*, ensuring that any *K*-sparse signal can be stably recovered. Reconstruction is performed via Bregman iteration methods, which solve a constrained optimization problem balancing data fidelity (**||HX − Y||₂²**) and sparsity (**||ΨᵀX||₁**), where **Ψ** is the sparsifying transform. FIG. 1A illustrates the overall method: step 104 involves iterative reconstruction using Bregman techniques. Specifically, linearized Bregman iteration (FIG. 1C) updates estimates via gradient descent on the data term coupled with soft-thresholding to enforce sparsity, while split Bregman iteration (FIG. 1D) decouples the L1 and L2 terms using auxiliary variables for improved convergence. The spectral cube is represented as a 3D matrix of size *N × N × L*, where *N* is the spatial resolution and *L* is the number of spectral bands. At each wavelength λᵢ, the imaging process is modeled as a 1D convolution along rows due to the 1D nature of the diffuser, yielding a linear transformation **yⱼ = Hⱼxⱼ**, where **xⱼ** is the *j*-th row of the concatenated spectral data and **yⱼ** is the corresponding sensor measurement. The full sensing matrix **H** is block-structured, with each block **H_λ** corresponding to a specific wavelength’s PSF. Calibration involves measuring the PSF experimentally using narrowband filters and a point source, enabling accurate construction of **H**. The RIP condition guarantees that **(1−δ_K)||x||₂² ≤ ||Hx||₂² ≤ (1+δ_K)||x||₂²** for all *K*-sparse **x**, with δ_K < √2−1 ensuring uniqueness of the sparse solution. Both deterministic (e.g., based on designed phase profiles) and random (e.g., Gaussian or Bernoulli) sensing matrices may be employed, though the RIP diffuser enables a physically realizable deterministic matrix with favorable coherence properties.

### Diffuser Design Formulation

The RIP diffuser is mathematically formulated to generate a sensing matrix **H** that facilitates stable CS reconstruction. Let the spectral cube be reshaped into a matrix **X** of size *N × ML*, where *M* accounts for vertical spatial sampling and *L* is the number of spectral bands. Vectorization yields **x ∈ ℝ^{NML}**. For a 1D diffuser, each row of the DD image corresponds independently to a row of **X**, simplifying modeling. At wavelength λᵢ, the imaging system’s incoherent PSF **h_λ(x)** is calculated as the squared magnitude of the inverse Fourier transform of the pupil function modified by the diffuser’s phase profile. This PSF defines a 1D linear convolution operator **H_λ**, implemented as a Toeplitz matrix. The dispersed image intensity at pixel position is the sum over all wavelengths of the convolved monochromatic intensities, weighted by the system’s spectral sensitivity **k_λ**. Digital processing involves capturing both a regular image (without diffuser) and the DD image (with diffuser), though in practice only the DD image is used for reconstruction. The total sensing operation is expressed as **Y = HX**, where **H** is a block matrix aggregating all **H_λ** scaled by **k_λ**. This formulation directly maps to the CS problem: recover **X** from underdetermined **Y** given **H**. The diffuser’s phase function is designed—e.g., as a randomly permuted nonlinear sawtooth profile—to ensure that **H** exhibits low mutual coherence and satisfies RIP, thereby enabling accurate recovery of sparse **X** in a transform domain **Ψ**.

### Finally

The dispersed image intensity recorded by the sensor is a superposition of wavelength-dependent convolutions of the spectral cube’s rows. The sensing matrix **H**, of size *N × NL* (assuming square spatial sampling), acts on the vectorized spectral cube **x** to produce the measurement vector **y**. Each column of **H** corresponds to the system’s response to a single voxel of the spectral cube. The CS problem is well-posed only if **H** allows unique recovery of sparse signals, which is guaranteed if **HΨ** satisfies the RIP, where **Ψ** is the sparsifying matrix (e.g., inverse wavelet transform). The incoherence condition—requiring that the sensing basis (**H**) and sparsifying basis (**Ψ**) are maximally uncorrelated—is inherently promoted by the RIP diffuser’s randomized phase structure. Deterministic sensing matrices, such as those derived from structured optical elements, are preferred for hardware implementation over purely random matrices (e.g., Gaussian), as they are physically realizable and reproducible. The RIP diffuser is thus engineered not merely as a scatterer but as a computational optical element whose physical transfer function is co-designed with the reconstruction algorithm to satisfy CS theoretical requirements, enabling high-quality spectral cube recovery from a single snapshot.

### Exemplary RIP Diffuser Implementation

An exemplary RIP diffuser is fabricated as a transparent phase plate with a 1D random phase mask. Its phase profile φ(x) is derived from a nonlinear sawtooth function subjected to random permutation, ensuring spatial randomness while maintaining manufacturability. The optical transfer function of the diffuser is *exp[iφ(x/λ)]*, making it inherently wavelength-dependent—thus providing dispersion. In discrete form, for pixel index *n* and wavelength λₗ, the phase is φₙˡ = φ(nΔx / λₗ), where Δx is the spatial sampling interval. This wavelength scaling ensures that different spectral bands are dispersed by different amounts, creating the necessary spectral multiplexing. The diffuser’s performance is limited by fabrication tolerances, finite aperture size, and the trade-off between diffusion strength and light throughput. Nevertheless, when integrated into the imaging system, it produces a PSF that varies sufficiently across wavelengths to yield a well-conditioned sensing matrix. Experimental validation confirms that such a diffuser enables accurate spectral reconstruction when paired with CS algorithms, fulfilling its role as a key enabler of snapshot spectral imaging in a miniaturized platform.

### Apparatus Embodiments

Embodiment 200 comprises a standard digital camera with a monochromatic sensor, a band-pass filter defining the operational band (e.g., 400–720 nm), and a RIP diffuser mounted at the entrance pupil. Light from the scene passes through the diffuser, which disperses and diffuses it, then through the imaging lens onto the sensor, producing a DD image. Embodiment 200' enhances light efficiency by employing a double-aperture camera, where two sub-apertures each contain a RIP diffuser, doubling the encoded information. Embodiment 300 uses a reflective-refractive beam splitter to fold the optical path, reducing overall length while maintaining dispersion. Embodiment 400 incorporates a turning mirror to redirect light orthogonally, enabling compact packaging in constrained spaces. Embodiment 500 augments the RIP diffuser with a weak diffractive disperser (e.g., low-line-density grating) to enhance spectral separation. Embodiment 600 integrates all optical elements—including lens, diffuser, and sensor—into a single posterior block for ruggedized, alignment-free operation. In all embodiments, a band-pass filter ensures that only the desired spectral range reaches the sensor, preventing out-of-band contamination. Image processing involves calibrating the system PSF, constructing the sensing matrix **H**, and applying Bregman-based CS reconstruction to yield the spectral cube, which may then be converted to RGB using standard colorimetric transforms. These optical schemes are implementable using off-the-shelf digital cameras with minor modifications, demonstrating the invention’s practicality and scalability.

### Computer Simulations

Computer simulations validate the CS reconstruction framework. In one simulation, a synthetic spectral cube of a natural scene is processed using a framelet-based Split Bregman iteration scheme. The sensing matrix **H** is constructed from a theoretical PSF model of the RIP diffuser. The dispersed image **Y** is generated via **Y = HX**, and reconstruction yields a spectral cube with high peak signal-to-noise ratio (PSNR > 30 dB), confirming algorithmic accuracy. Another simulation employs a random Toeplitz sensing matrix, mimicking the RIP diffuser’s structure. Using linearized Bregman iterations followed by split Bregman refinement, the original cube is recovered with minimal error. A third simulation uses a real spectral cube of human skin tissue acquired by a VTT Fabry-Perot imager. After preprocessing (cropping, zero-padding, and 3D wavelet decomposition), the low-frequency sub-cube is used as ground truth. The simulated DD image is formed using a theoretically computed **H**, and reconstruction via 2D framelet-based SBI achieves high fidelity, with spectral features of melanoma regions preserved. PSNR metrics across wavelengths and spatial locations confirm robustness. These simulations collectively demonstrate that the proposed SSI method, combining RIP diffuser optics with advanced CS algorithms, enables accurate, single-shot spectral imaging across diverse scenarios.
