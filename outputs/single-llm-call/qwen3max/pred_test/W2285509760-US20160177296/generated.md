# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

Avian influenza virus (AIV) is a highly contagious pathogen that poses significant threats to both the global poultry industry and public health. AIV belongs to the Orthomyxoviridae family and is classified into subtypes based on the antigenic properties of its surface glycoproteins, hemagglutinin (HA) and neuraminidase (NA). These viruses are further categorized as low pathogenic avian influenza (LPAI) or high pathogenic avian influenza (HPAI), depending on their virulence in poultry. HPAI strains, particularly those of the H5 and H7 subtypes, can cause near 100% mortality in infected flocks within days, leading to massive economic losses and necessitating widespread culling of birds to contain outbreaks. The global impact of AIV has been profound, with millions of birds culled during recent outbreaks and billions of dollars lost in trade, production, and control measures. Beyond economic consequences, AIV represents a zoonotic threat, as certain strains have demonstrated the ability to cross the species barrier and infect humans, raising concerns about potential pandemic emergence.

The effects of AIV on the poultry industry are multifaceted and severe. Outbreaks disrupt supply chains, trigger international trade embargoes, and erode consumer confidence. Even LPAI infections, which may cause mild or no clinical signs in poultry, can lead to significant reductions in egg production and weight gain, thereby diminishing profitability. Furthermore, LPAI viruses can mutate into HPAI forms during circulation in poultry populations, escalating the risk of catastrophic outbreaks. Current control strategies rely heavily on biosecurity, surveillance, and vaccination; however, each of these approaches has notable limitations that compromise their effectiveness in preventing or containing AIV spread.

Current vaccines for AIV, while useful in some contexts, suffer from several critical shortcomings. Most commercially available AIV vaccines are inactivated whole-virus formulations that require individual parenteral administration—typically via subcutaneous or intramuscular injection—which is labor-intensive, stressful for birds, and impractical for rapid deployment in large commercial operations. Moreover, vaccinated birds often require two to three weeks to develop protective immunity, leaving them vulnerable during the critical early phase of an outbreak. Improper storage, handling, or administration of vaccines can further reduce efficacy, leading to vaccine failure. Perhaps most significantly, conventional AIV vaccines are HA-subtype specific, meaning that a vaccine developed against one HA subtype (e.g., H5) offers little or no protection against another (e.g., H7 or H9). This narrow specificity necessitates constant surveillance and frequent reformulation of vaccine stocks, rendering stockpiles obsolete as new viral strains emerge.

The antigenic evolution of AIV exacerbates these challenges. Influenza viruses undergo continuous genetic drift and occasional shift, leading to the emergence of novel variants that can evade pre-existing immunity. This antigenic variability means that even within a single HA subtype, minor changes in the HA protein can render a vaccine ineffective. Consequently, vaccine development must constantly chase the evolving virus, creating a reactive rather than proactive defense posture. Additionally, the use of conventional vaccines complicates disease surveillance because vaccinated birds may become infected and shed virus without showing clinical signs, acting as silent carriers. Furthermore, serological tests cannot distinguish between antibodies generated by vaccination and those resulting from natural infection, hindering the implementation of “Differentiating Infected from Vaccinated Animals” (DIVA) strategies that are essential for effective outbreak management and trade resumption.

Problems with vaccine administration extend beyond logistical hurdles. Mass vaccination campaigns in poultry are often delayed due to the time required to identify the causative subtype and produce a matched vaccine. During this window, the virus can spread rapidly through a flock and to neighboring farms. Alternative delivery methods, such as in ovo or spray vaccination, have shown limited success for AIV due to inconsistent immune responses and poor mucosal immunity at the primary site of viral entry—the respiratory tract.

Growing concerns about antiviral drug resistance further underscore the need for alternative prophylactic strategies. While antiviral drugs like amantadine and neuraminidase inhibitors have been used in some contexts, their utility in poultry is limited by cost, regulatory restrictions, and the rapid emergence of resistant viral strains. Drug resistance not only compromises treatment efficacy but also raises public health concerns if resistant strains are transmitted to humans.

Given these limitations, there is an urgent and unmet need for novel prophylactic strategies that offer broad-spectrum, rapid-acting, and easily deployable protection against diverse AIV subtypes. Such a strategy should ideally provide immediate antiviral activity upon administration, target conserved regions of the virus to minimize the risk of escape mutants, and be deliverable via mass application methods such as aerosolization or oral dosing. It should also avoid interference with serological surveillance and not pose safety risks to birds, handlers, or consumers.

The economic impact of AIV outbreaks is staggering. Beyond direct losses from bird mortality and culling, indirect costs include trade restrictions, decreased market demand, increased insurance premiums, and expenses related to disinfection, quarantine, and restocking. Countries experiencing HPAI outbreaks often face long-term exclusion from international markets, even after the virus is eradicated, due to perceived ongoing risk. These economic burdens disproportionately affect smallholder farmers and developing economies, where poultry represents a critical source of protein and income.

Transmission risk and the duration of viral shedding are central to AIV epidemiology. Infected birds shed large quantities of virus through respiratory secretions and feces, facilitating rapid horizontal transmission within and between flocks. The shedding period can last several days, during which time the virus contaminates the environment and spreads to susceptible birds. Reducing the magnitude and duration of viral shedding is therefore a key objective in outbreak control, as it directly limits transmission potential.

In summary, current vaccination strategies for AIV are hampered by subtype specificity, slow onset of immunity, logistical challenges in administration, interference with surveillance, and vulnerability to viral evolution. These limitations collectively highlight the critical need for innovative, broadly effective, and rapidly deployable prophylactic interventions that can complement or replace existing approaches to mitigate the devastating impact of AIV on animal health, food security, and global economies.

## BRIEF SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides novel small interfering RNA (siRNA) compositions and methods for treating or reducing the risk of avian influenza virus (AIV) infection in poultry. Specifically, the invention discloses engineered nonpathogenic Escherichia coli bacteria that serve as delivery vehicles for short hairpin RNA (shRNA) molecules designed to interfere with the replication of AIV by targeting essential viral genes. These recombinant bacteria are modified to express two key proteins—invasin (encoded by the inv gene) and listeriolysin O (encoded by the hlyA gene)—that enable efficient uptake by avian epithelial cells and subsequent release of shRNA into the host cytoplasm, where it is processed into active siRNA that mediates sequence-specific degradation of AIV mRNA.

The siRNA compositions of the invention are designed to target conserved regions of the nucleoprotein (NP), polymerase acidic (PA), polymerase basic 1 (PB1), or polymerase basic 2 (PB2) mRNA of AIV. These viral genes are highly conserved across multiple AIV subtypes, including both LPAI and HPAI strains, enabling the invention to provide broad-spectrum protection against diverse influenza A viruses. By focusing on conserved sequences, the invention minimizes the likelihood of viral escape through mutation and offers a universal approach that does not require subtype matching.

In a preferred embodiment, the invention employs a “cocktail” approach, wherein multiple siRNAs targeting different regions of one or more AIV genes are co-delivered to enhance antiviral potency and reduce the probability of resistance development. Specific siRNA sequences are disclosed, including those targeting the NP and PA genes with 100% sequence identity across numerous AIV strains isolated from avian, swine, equine, canine, and human hosts. These sequences have been validated for lack of off-target effects in chicken genomes through rigorous BLAST analysis, particularly ensuring no homology in the critical seed region (nucleotides 2–8 of the antisense strand) to any known chicken mRNA.

The shRNA constructs are expressed from plasmid vectors under the control of strong RNA polymerase promoters, such as T7, T3, or SP6, ensuring high-level transcription within the bacterial vector. The plasmid backbone includes selection markers (e.g., kanamycin resistance) and features that prevent conjugation or integration into host genomes, enhancing biosafety.

The invention further provides a method for treating or reducing the risk of AIV in a subject, comprising administering to the subject an effective amount of a pharmaceutical composition comprising the recombinant E. coli bacterium. Administration may be performed via intranasal, oral, ocular, or aerosol routes, all of which are compatible with mass application in commercial poultry settings. The nonpathogenic E. coli strain used (e.g., CEQ221) is auxotrophic for diaminopimelic acid (Dap), rendering it unable to survive or replicate in standard environments, thereby minimizing environmental persistence and biosafety risks.

In another aspect, the invention encompasses a pharmaceutical composition comprising at least two distinct recombinant E. coli bacteria: a first bacterium expressing shRNA targeting the NP gene of AIV, and a second bacterium expressing shRNA targeting the PA gene of AIV. This dual-vector formulation enables synergistic antiviral activity and is particularly effective in reducing viral shedding and transmission.

The method of treatment involves prophylactic or early therapeutic administration of the composition to poultry prior to or shortly after exposure to AIV. The tkRNAi (transkingdom RNA interference) platform ensures targeted delivery to mucosal epithelial cells—the primary site of AIV replication—resulting in rapid intracellular release of shRNA, processing into siRNA, and silencing of viral gene expression. This leads to a significant reduction in infectious viral particle production, as demonstrated by up to 4-log10 reductions in viral titer in vitro.

Overall, the invention represents a paradigm shift in AIV control, offering a scalable, cost-effective, rapidly deployable, and broadly protective antiviral strategy that overcomes the major limitations of conventional vaccines and addresses the urgent need for next-generation prophylactics in the face of evolving influenza threats.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE PREFERRED EMBODIMENT

The development of effective anti-influenza technology for poultry is driven by the persistent threat of AIV outbreaks and the inadequacy of current control measures. Conventional vaccination strategies, while partially effective, are fundamentally limited by their requirement for subtype-specific matching, slow induction of immunity, and logistical barriers to mass administration. These constraints render them poorly suited for rapid response during emerging outbreaks. Similarly, while RNA interference (RNAi) has long held promise as a potent antiviral mechanism, its clinical translation has been hindered by the challenge of safe and efficient intracellular delivery of siRNA molecules. Synthetic carriers suffer from low efficiency, toxicity at high doses, and poor stability, while viral vectors raise concerns about immunogenicity, insertional mutagenesis, and long-term safety.

To address these limitations, the present invention introduces a transkingdom RNAi (tkRNAi) delivery platform specifically adapted for avian applications. This system leverages a genetically engineered, nonpathogenic, and non-colonizing strain of Escherichia coli (e.g., CEQ221) as a biological vector to deliver shRNA directly to mucosal epithelial cells. The tkRNAi system comprises three essential components: (1) a plasmid vector encoding shRNA under the control of a strong promoter (e.g., T7); (2) the inv gene, which directs the expression of invasin protein on the bacterial surface; and (3) the hlyA gene, which encodes listeriolysin O (LLO), a pore-forming toxin. Upon administration, the invasin protein binds to β(1) integrin receptors abundantly expressed on the surface of avian epithelial cells, triggering receptor-mediated endocytosis and internalization of the entire bacterium. Once inside the endosome, LLO disrupts the endosomal membrane, allowing the bacterial contents—including the shRNA-expressing plasmid—to be released into the host cytoplasm. The shRNA is then processed by the host RNAi machinery into active siRNA, which guides the RNA-induced silencing complex (RISC) to cleave complementary AIV mRNA, thereby inhibiting viral replication.

This tkRNAi platform was successfully applied to develop anti-AIV vectors targeting the NP and PA genes of influenza A virus. An avian tissue model using chicken hepatocellular carcinoma epithelial (LMH) cells was established to evaluate vector uptake, safety, and antiviral efficacy. LMH cells were confirmed to express β(1) integrin both under normal conditions and post-AIV infection, validating their suitability as a model for tkRNAi delivery. Vector invasion was demonstrated using red fluorescent protein (RFP)-tagged bacteria, with flow cytometry confirming intracellular uptake efficiencies exceeding 74%. Cytotoxicity assays established a safe working concentration of 7.8 × 10⁵ CFU/mL, below which no cytopathic effects were observed.

The antiviral efficacy of the anti-AIV vectors was evaluated by infecting LMH cells with two LPAI strains—H6N2 and H8N4—following vector pretreatment. Infectious virus titers in cell supernatants were quantified by TCID₅₀ assay on MDCK cells. Results demonstrated significant reductions in viral shedding, with the cocktail vector (targeting both NP and PA) achieving up to 4.0 log₁₀ reduction against H8N4 and 1.9 log₁₀ against H6N2. Even single-gene targeting vectors showed substantial inhibition, confirming the potency of the selected siRNA sequences.

The NP and PA proteins play critical roles in the influenza virus life cycle. NP encapsidates the viral RNA genome, forming ribonucleoprotein complexes essential for replication and transcription, while PA is a component of the viral RNA-dependent RNA polymerase complex. Both are highly conserved across influenza A subtypes, making them ideal targets for broad-spectrum antivirals. The siRNA sequences used in this invention were designed against regions showing 100% identity across over 10,000 influenza A isolates, including H5, H7, and H9 HPAI strains, ensuring relevance to current and emerging threats.

While mammalian cell models have been extensively used in influenza research, they do not fully recapitulate avian host-pathogen interactions. The use of an avian-specific cell line (LMH) in this study provides more physiologically relevant data for poultry applications. AIV is recognized as a Category C priority pathogen by the U.S. National Institute of Allergy and Infectious Diseases due to its economic and public health implications. Outbreaks of HPAI and LPAI continue to occur globally, with recent H5N1 and H7N9 strains causing significant poultry losses and sporadic human infections. Preventing AIV in poultry is therefore a critical One Health objective.

The tkRNAi system offers several advantages over conventional vaccination. It provides immediate antiviral activity without requiring adaptive immune activation, enables mass administration via aerosol or spray, avoids interference with serological surveillance (since it does not induce anti-AIV antibodies), and can be stockpiled indefinitely due to bacterial stability. Intranasal administration targets the Harderian gland and respiratory mucosa, key sites of AIV replication and mucosal immune response, enhancing local protection.

Efficacy evaluation in the avian model demonstrated that the anti-AIV vectors significantly reduce virus shedding—a key determinant of transmission risk. Industry standards for vaccine efficacy often focus on prevention of clinical disease or mortality, but reducing shedding is equally important for limiting spread. The tkRNAi approach achieves this by directly inhibiting viral replication at the cellular level.

Cost-effectiveness is another major advantage. The E. coli vectors can be produced at scale using standard fermentation techniques, purified, and formulated for long-term storage. Manufacturing does not require high-containment facilities or complex purification steps, unlike inactivated vaccines. Targeted delivery to respiratory tissues via inhalation or spray allows for efficient use of material and minimizes waste.

Large-scale application is feasible through existing poultry house ventilation systems, enabling rapid treatment of entire flocks within minutes. This capability is invaluable during outbreak response, where speed is critical. By reducing shedding titers and duration, the invention lowers the basic reproduction number (R₀) of AIV, potentially interrupting transmission chains.

Trade barriers associated with vaccination—such as restrictions on movement of vaccinated birds or products—are avoided because the tkRNAi-treated birds do not seroconvert to AIV antigens. This facilitates DIVA-compliant surveillance and faster market recovery post-outbreak.

While the current data are promising, further studies are needed to validate efficacy in live birds, optimize dosing regimens, and assess long-term safety. Challenges in AIV vaccination, including antigenic mismatch and cold-chain requirements, underscore the need for alternatives like tkRNAi. Preemptive culling policies, though sometimes necessary, are economically and ethically costly; the invention offers a humane and sustainable alternative by enabling proactive protection.

In envisioning the future of AIV control, tkRNAi technology represents a transformative solution—combining the precision of RNAi with the practicality of bacterial delivery to deliver rapid, broad, and scalable protection for global poultry populations.

### Design of siRNAs and Use of Viral Specific siRNAs to Inhibit Avian Influenza in an Avian Tissue Model

Avian influenza virus (AIV) constitutes a significant economic threat to the global poultry industry, with outbreaks leading to massive culling, trade disruptions, and financial losses amounting to billions of dollars annually. The limitations of current prophylactic strategies, particularly conventional vaccines, have created an urgent need for novel approaches that can provide rapid, broad-spectrum, and easily deployable protection. In response to this need, the present invention describes the development and validation of an avian tissue transfection and AIV infection model to demonstrate the antiviral potential of small interfering RNA (siRNA) molecules specifically designed to target essential AIV genes.

The study utilized chicken hepatocellular carcinoma epithelial cells (LMH cells, ATCC CRL-2117), a well-established avian cell line that supports robust AIV replication and is amenable to transfection. Two low pathogenic avian influenza (LPAI) virus strains—H8N4 (A/turkey/Colorado/235497/2003) and H6N2 (A/chicken/Texas/473-2/2010)—were selected for evaluation based on their ability to replicate to high titers in LMH cells and induce clear cytopathic effects (CPE) within 48 hours post-infection. These strains represent distinct HA and NA subtypes commonly found in poultry, providing a relevant testbed for antiviral efficacy.

Virus titers were quantified using the 50% tissue culture infectious dose (TCID₅₀) assay on Madin-Darby canine kidney (MDCK) cells, a gold standard method for measuring infectious virus. The optimal multiplicity of infection (MOI) was empirically determined to be 0.01, ensuring consistent CPE without overwhelming the cellular system.

Two siRNA oligonucleotides were designed to target highly conserved regions of the AIV nucleoprotein (NP) and polymerase acidic (PA) genes. The NP-siRNA sense strand sequence (5′-GGATCTTATTTCTTCGGA G-3′) targets nucleotides 1486–1504 of the H8N4 NP gene and 1453–1471 of the H6N2 NP gene. The PA-siRNA sense strand sequence (5′-GCAATTGAGGAGTGCCTGA-3′) targets nucleotides 2077–2095 of H8N4 PA and 2065–2083 of H6N2 PA. Both sequences exhibited 100% identity to the corresponding regions in the respective viral strains, as confirmed by BLAST alignment against GenBank accession numbers KM244053 (H6N2 NP), GU051910 (H8N4 NP), KM244051 (H6N2 PA), and GU051912 (H8N4 PA).

To ensure specificity and minimize off-target effects, the siRNA sequences were rigorously screened against the chicken genome. Particular attention was paid to the seed region (nucleotides 2–8 of the antisense strand), which is critical for miRNA-like off-target silencing. BLAST analysis confirmed 0% homology between the siRNA seed regions and any known chicken mRNA, indicating a low risk of unintended gene silencing.

Transfection efficiency was optimized by testing multiple reagents for cytotoxicity and delivery performance. The BLOCK-iT Alexa Fluoro Red Fluorescent dsRNA Control was used to visually assess uptake, and the final transfection reagent was selected based on high efficiency and absence of CPE. The optimal siRNA incubation period was determined to be 24 hours prior to viral challenge.

LMH cells were transfected with NP-siRNA, PA-siRNA, or a cocktail of both, then infected with either H8N4 or H6N2 at MOI 0.01. Supernatants were harvested at 48 hours post-infection and titrated on MDCK cells to quantify infectious virus. Statistical analysis was performed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test for pairwise comparisons and multivariable linear regression to adjust for inter-experimental variability.

Results demonstrated significant reductions in viral shedding titers across all siRNA-treated groups compared to untreated controls. Multivariable linear regression analysis, controlling for experimental day, revealed adjusted mean log₁₀ TCID₅₀/mL reductions of 3.8 for NP-siRNA and 2.5 for PA-siRNA against H8N4, and 1.6 and 1.4, respectively, against H6N2. The cocktail approach yielded the greatest inhibition, with 4.0 log₁₀ reduction against H8N4 and 1.9 against H6N2. These findings provide compelling in vitro evidence that siRNA-mediated silencing of NP and PA can effectively suppress AIV replication in avian cells, laying the foundation for the development of RNAi-based antivirals for poultry.

### Inhibiting Avian Influenza Replication in a Chicken Cell Model Using a Unique RNAi Delivery Technology

The replication of avian influenza virus (AIV) in poultry presents a persistent challenge that demands innovative solutions beyond traditional vaccines and antivirals. RNA interference (RNAi) offers a powerful mechanism for sequence-specific gene silencing, but its therapeutic application has been historically limited by inefficient and unsafe delivery methods. To overcome this barrier, the present invention employs a unique transkingdom RNAi (tkRNAi) delivery technology that utilizes engineered nonpathogenic Escherichia coli as a biological vector to transport short hairpin RNA (shRNA) into avian epithelial cells.

The experimental goals were threefold: (1) to validate the expression of β(1) integrin receptors on chicken LMH cells, which are necessary for bacterial uptake; (2) to construct and characterize shRNA-expressing E. coli vectors targeting AIV NP and PA genes; and (3) to evaluate the antiviral efficacy of these vectors in an in vitro AIV infection model.

Cell cultures included chicken LMH cells and MDCK cells for virus propagation and titration, as previously described. The two LPAI strains—H6N2 and H8N4—were used for infection assays. RT-PCR confirmed the expression of β(1) integrin mRNA in LMH cells under both normal and AIV-infected conditions, establishing the molecular basis for tkRNAi vector uptake.

shRNA vectors were constructed by cloning DNA templates encoding the NP and PA siRNA sequences into the pmbv43 plasmid backbone, which contains T7 promoter-driven shRNA expression cassettes. The DNA template for each shRNA included a BamHI site, the sense sequence, a hairpin loop (5′-TTC AAG AGA-3′), the antisense sequence, a poly-T termination signal, and a SalI site. The NP/pmbv43 plasmid was synthesized commercially, while the PA/pmbv43 plasmid was assembled in-house by annealing and ligating synthetic oligonucleotides. Successful ligation was confirmed by PCR screening and Sanger sequencing.

The plasmids were transformed into CEQ221 E. coli, a Dap-auxotrophic, nonpathogenic strain. Transformants were selected on Brain Heart Infusion (BHI) agar containing kanamycin and Dap, screened by PCR, and validated by sequencing. Glycerol stocks of anti-AIV/NP, anti-AIV/PA, and anti-AIV/scramble (control) vectors were prepared and enumerated by colony counting, yielding concentrations of approximately 10⁹ CFU/mL.

To confirm intracellular uptake, an RFP-expressing tkRNAi vector was constructed by co-transforming CEQ221 with pmbv43/scramble and the pE2-Crimson RFP plasmid. Fluorescent microscopy and flow cytometry demonstrated efficient invasion of LMH cells, with 74–76% of cells showing intracellular RFP signal at 20–36 hours post-invasion. Cytotoxicity assays established a safe working concentration of 7.8 × 10⁵ CFU/mL.

For antiviral testing, LMH cells were invaded with anti-AIV vectors at the optimized concentration, then infected with H6N2 or H8N4 at MOI 0.01. Supernatants were harvested at 48 hours post-infection and titrated on MDCK cells. Results showed that both NP and PA shRNA vectors significantly reduced infectious virus titers compared to scramble vector controls. The cocktail vector (NP + PA) achieved the greatest inhibition, with up to 4.0 log₁₀ reduction in H8N4 titer.

Statistical analysis using multivariable linear regression confirmed that the observed reductions were statistically significant (p < 0.001 for H8N4; p < 0.05 for H6N2), even after adjusting for inter-experimental variability. Interestingly, the scramble vector also exhibited modest antiviral activity, likely due to innate immune stimulation by bacterial components such as lipopolysaccharide (LPS), which can act as an adjuvant by activating Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4) and inducing type I interferon production.

This in vitro proof-of-concept demonstrates that tkRNAi technology can effectively deliver functional shRNA to avian cells and inhibit AIV replication. The system’s safety, efficiency, and scalability position it as a promising platform for developing next-generation antivirals against influenza and other mucosal pathogens in poultry.

### Prophylactic Treatment of Chicken Populations with Anti-AIV Vectors

Effective control of avian influenza virus (AIV) outbreaks hinges on the availability of rapid, scalable, and broadly protective prophylactic interventions. Building on the in vitro success of anti-AIV tkRNAi vectors, the present invention extends these findings to in vivo applications, demonstrating the feasibility of prophylactic treatment in live chickens. Previous work established the safety and antiviral efficacy of NP- and PA-targeting shRNA vectors in LMH cells; this section outlines the translation of these vectors into a poultry-compatible delivery system and evaluates their performance in experimentally challenged birds.

The experimental design encompassed multiple pilot studies and a proof-of-concept (POC) trial. Pilot Study 1 assessed the uptake and localization of RFP-tagged tkRNAi vectors following intranasal administration in chickens. Fluorescence imaging confirmed vector delivery to the Harderian gland and respiratory mucosa—key sites of AIV replication—validating the route of administration. Pilot Study 2 determined the optimal vector dose and evaluated tolerance, with histopathological analysis revealing no adverse effects at doses up to 10⁸ CFU/bird. Pilot Study 3 confirmed the susceptibility of the experimental birds to H6N2 challenge, establishing a reproducible infection model.

The POC study involved three groups: (1) anti-AIV cocktail vector (NP + PA shRNA), (2) scramble vector (control), and (3) untreated positive control (PC). Birds were treated intranasally with 10⁷ CFU of vector 24 hours before H6N2 challenge. Clinical illness was scored daily, and oropharyngeal (OP) swabs were collected for viral load quantification by RT-qPCR. Serology was performed to confirm infection status.

Results demonstrated that cocktail vector-treated birds exhibited significantly lower viral shedding titers and reduced proportions of positive OP swabs compared to PC birds. Multivariable linear regression (MLR) analysis, controlling for day post-infection, showed a 1.5 log₁₀ reduction in median virus titer and a 60% decrease in the odds of shedding. Mortality was prevented in the cocktail group, whereas 20% of PC birds succumbed to infection. A sentinel chicken study further revealed that contact transmission was significantly reduced in pens housing vector-treated birds, indicating lowered transmission potential.

Notably, the scramble vector also provided partial protection, consistent with in vitro observations. This effect is attributed to innate immune activation by bacterial LPS, which stimulates proinflammatory cytokines and type I interferons, creating an antiviral state. However, the cocktail vector conferred significantly greater protection, confirming the added benefit of specific RNAi-mediated silencing.

The tkRNAi platform offers several advantages for poultry prophylaxis: (1) rapid onset of action (within 24 hours); (2) compatibility with mass administration via spray or aerosol; (3) no interference with serological surveillance (DIVA-compliant); (4) broad-spectrum activity against diverse AIV subtypes; and (5) cost-effective production and long-term stability. Concerns about immune activation are mitigated by the use of nonpathogenic E. coli and the absence of pathology in histological assessments.

While the current study used commercial broilers, future work will evaluate efficacy in layer and breeder flocks. The long-term goal is to develop a stockpiled, ready-to-deploy antiviral that can be rapidly distributed during outbreaks, reducing reliance on culling and enabling more sustainable poultry production. The protective efficacy demonstrated here—measured by reduced shedding, mortality, and transmission—provides strong justification for continued development and field trials of this innovative technology.
