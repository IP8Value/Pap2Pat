# DESCRIPTION

Microfluidic devices and systems represent a transformative approach to handling minute volumes of liquids with high precision, enabling applications ranging from chemical synthesis to biological diagnostics. These devices operate by manipulating fluids within channels whose dimensions are typically on the order of micrometers, thereby exploiting unique physical phenomena that dominate at small scales, such as surface tension, capillary forces, and laminar flow. In particular, droplet-based microfluidics has emerged as a powerful paradigm wherein discrete aqueous droplets are suspended in an immiscible continuous phase—often an oil—and transported, merged, split, or otherwise manipulated through microfabricated networks. The present invention relates to a class of microfluidic devices specifically engineered to perform elementary operations on microdroplets without requiring precise external control over flow rates, timing, or synchronization, thereby enhancing robustness and usability in both laboratory and point-of-care settings.

The operation of microdroplets within microfluidic channels is governed by a delicate interplay between interfacial tension, viscous resistance, and geometric constraints. When a droplet flows through a channel, its shape and velocity are determined not only by the driving pressure but also by the curvature of its interface, which is dictated by the Laplace pressure jump across the droplet. This pressure difference is highly sensitive to the local geometry of the channel, particularly constrictions, expansions, or side features that alter the cross-sectional area available to the continuous phase. By strategically designing such geometrical features, it becomes possible to exert deterministic control over droplet behavior—such as trapping, merging, metering, or delaying—without relying on active valves, pumps, or real-time feedback mechanisms. The present invention leverages this principle to create passive, hard-wired microfluidic modules that execute predefined liquid-handling protocols encoded directly into the chip’s architecture.

Microdroplet-based microsystems offer several compelling advantages over conventional fluidic approaches. First, they enable extreme miniaturization of reaction volumes, reducing reagent consumption and waste generation while increasing throughput through parallelization. Second, each droplet acts as an isolated microreactor, preventing cross-contamination between samples and allowing for independent environmental control. Third, the high surface-to-volume ratio facilitates rapid heat and mass transfer, accelerating reaction kinetics and improving mixing efficiency. Finally, because droplet dynamics are dominated by interfacial forces rather than inertial effects, operations can be rendered largely insensitive to variations in flow rate or timing, provided the system operates in the low capillary number regime where viscous stresses are negligible compared to surface tension. These attributes make droplet microfluidics especially well-suited for applications requiring simplicity, reliability, and portability, such as field-deployable diagnostic tools or automated screening platforms.

The motivation for employing microfluidic systems in chemistry stems from the need to conduct reactions under highly controlled conditions with minimal material usage. Traditional batch reactors often suffer from poor mixing, slow thermal equilibration, and difficulty in scaling down. In contrast, microfluidic reactors provide exceptional control over residence time, stoichiometry, and reaction environment, enabling reproducible synthesis of complex molecules, nanoparticles, and biomaterials. Moreover, the compartmentalization afforded by droplets allows for the execution of thousands of parallel reactions under identical conditions, facilitating high-throughput experimentation and optimization. However, the full potential of these systems has been limited by the complexity of controlling droplet generation, transport, and manipulation, which traditionally requires sophisticated instrumentation and precise parameter tuning. The present invention addresses this limitation by introducing passive geometric modules that automate key operations without external intervention.

Existing robotic stations for chemical reactions, while capable of high precision, are typically bulky, expensive, and require significant user expertise. They rely on motorized syringe pumps, solenoid valves, and computer-controlled actuators to dispense, mix, and transfer liquids—a level of complexity incompatible with resource-limited or decentralized settings. Although some progress has been made in integrating automation into microfluidic chips, most solutions still depend on active components or external control systems. There remains a critical need for truly passive microfluidic architectures that can perform complex sequences of operations using only the intrinsic physics of droplet–channel interactions, thereby enabling robust, low-cost, and user-friendly platforms for chemical and biological analysis.

A central challenge in passive droplet microfluidics is the reliable generation of microdroplets on demand—i.e., producing droplets of predetermined size and at specified times without active actuation. Conventional methods such as T-junctions or flow-focusing geometries require fine-tuned flow rate ratios between the dispersed and continuous phases to achieve consistent droplet sizes. Even minor fluctuations in these parameters can lead to significant variability in droplet volume, undermining the reproducibility of downstream assays. To overcome this, researchers have explored geometrically defined traps that passively meter fixed volumes of liquid based on channel dimensions alone. Among these, valve-based microdroplet generation strategies attempt to mimic macroscopic pipetting by using physical barriers to isolate specific volumes. However, such designs often suffer from incomplete filling, leakage, or dependence on flow directionality, limiting their utility in integrated systems.

The design of effective passive traps hinges critically on the relationship between channel geometry and Laplace pressure. When a droplet encounters a constriction or obstacle, the resulting deformation increases the interfacial curvature, thereby elevating the Laplace pressure required to push the droplet forward. If the pressure drop along the droplet is insufficient to overcome this barrier, the droplet becomes immobilized. Conversely, if the droplet is long enough to block bypass pathways that allow the continuous phase to flow around it, the pressure builds up until the droplet is forced through the obstacle. This binary response—trapping short droplets while allowing long ones to pass—forms the basis of many passive control strategies. However, achieving reliable and reversible operation demands careful engineering of the channel cross-section, barrier height, and bypass configuration to balance Laplace pressure against hydrodynamic resistance.

Several prior art solutions have attempted to implement this principle using bypasses and side intrusions. For instance, Niu et al. developed a device featuring a central chamber flanked by two side bypasses, with a pillar acting as a barrier at the outlet. While this design enabled serial dilution by trapping a sample droplet and sequentially flushing it with buffer droplets, it suffered from incomplete release of the diluted sample and sensitivity to droplet spacing. Similarly, Zagnoni et al. introduced a shift register-like structure where droplets were advanced one position at a time via flow reversal, but this required precise synchronization and could not prevent contact between consecutive droplets, risking cross-contamination. Another variant by Niu et al. employed a single outlet chamber without symmetric bypasses, leading to asymmetric flow fields and inconsistent trapping efficiency.

Bai et al. proposed an array of microwells or traps arranged in a grid to capture large numbers of droplets simultaneously. Although useful for high-throughput screening, this design lacked the ability to selectively merge or release individual droplets on command, limiting its versatility. Du et al. explored geometrical control via tapered channels and asymmetric obstacles to direct droplet motion, but their system was highly sensitive to flow rate and exhibited poor repeatability across different operating conditions. Tan et al. utilized a widened channel section to create a temporary storage zone, yet droplets tended to drift or coalesce due to insufficient confinement. Ahn et al. designed parallel channels with staggered traps, but the lack of integrated bypasses resulted in high backpressure and inefficient continuous-phase flow.

Takinoue et al. introduced a blind channel section—a dead-end branch—to trap droplets, but this configuration trapped the continuous phase as well, causing bubble formation and flow instability. Dangla et al. developed a mechanism of droplet anchoring based on capillary pinning at sharp corners, which worked well for static trapping but offered no means for controlled release or merging. Sun et al. implemented an array of traps with integrated electrodes for electrocoalescence, adding complexity and requiring external power. Takahashi et al. used a capillary trap formed by a sudden expansion followed by a contraction, but this design was prone to clogging and exhibited hysteresis during droplet release.

Collectively, these existing approaches highlight a persistent gap in the art: the absence of a unified, passive, and robust platform capable of performing all elementary droplet operations—metering, merging, trapping, delaying, and releasing—with high fidelity and minimal external control. Each prior solution excels in one or two functions but fails to integrate seamlessly into larger workflows or maintain performance under variable flow conditions. This limitation has hindered the development of truly autonomous microfluidic systems suitable for point-of-care diagnostics or field-deployable assays.

The present invention addresses this unmet need by introducing a novel class of microfluidic devices whose functionality is entirely encoded in their geometric architecture. The primary objective is to provide a set of modular, passive components that can be combined into functional networks to execute complex liquid-handling protocols—such as serial dilution, sample aliquoting, or reagent mixing—without requiring precise control over flow rate, timing, or synchronization. By leveraging the dominance of Laplace pressure over viscous forces in low-capillary-number regimes, these devices ensure reproducible operation across a wide range of flow conditions, including manual or gravity-driven actuation.

The key advantages of the present invention include: (1) complete passivity—no valves, pumps, or external actuators are needed; (2) robustness to flow variations—protocols succeed even with imprecise or fluctuating flow rates; (3) modularity—individual trap types can be freely arranged into custom networks; (4) compatibility with standard fabrication techniques—devices can be manufactured via milling, embossing, or lithography using a single structured layer; and (5) scalability—systems can be expanded to handle dozens or hundreds of droplets in parallel. These features collectively enable the construction of microfluidic platforms that are simple to use, reliable in operation, and adaptable to diverse applications.

At the core of the invention are microfluidic devices comprising a main channel with integrated geometric features that govern droplet behavior through passive hydrodynamic and capillary effects. Specifically, the devices include a chamber or trap region bounded by obstructions such as barriers or side intrusions, accompanied by one or more bypass channels that run parallel to the main channel and allow the continuous phase to flow around trapped droplets. This configuration ensures that short droplets are immobilized by Laplace pressure while long droplets advance by blocking the bypasses and building up sufficient pressure to overcome the barrier.

Operations on microdroplets in the present invention are executed through a sequence of flow reversals or directional switches that exploit the binary response of droplets to geometric cues. For example, a metering trap captures a fixed volume of liquid by allowing excess to flow past via bypasses, while a merging trap fuses two droplets by permitting them to coalesce before releasing the combined volume. Critically, these operations do not depend on the absolute flow rate but only on the relative timing of flow direction changes, which can be implemented manually or with simple switching valves.

A defining feature of the invention is the inclusion of a bypass running in parallel to the chamber or trap region. This bypass is dimensioned to allow the continuous phase to flow around a trapped droplet with minimal resistance, thereby reducing the pressure drop along the droplet and stabilizing its position. The advantage of this design is twofold: it prevents unintended displacement of trapped droplets due to background flow, and it enables efficient mixing of the droplet contents through recirculation in the thin films and gutters surrounding the droplet.

Furthermore, the invention employs a single, appropriately shaped inlet and outlet for each trap module, eliminating the need for multiple access ports and simplifying device integration. These inlets and outlets are contoured to minimize dead volumes and ensure smooth transition of droplets into and out of the trap, reducing the risk of fragmentation or residual liquid retention. The narrowing of the lumen of the main channel adjacent to the trap enhances the Laplace pressure gradient, reinforcing the trapping mechanism for short droplets while facilitating the release of longer ones.

Another critical element is the barrier, which is constructed to a height equal to the height of the channel walls or a defined fraction thereof. This barrier creates a localized constriction that increases the interfacial curvature of an approaching droplet, thereby raising the Laplace pressure required for passage. The advantage of matching the barrier height to the wall height is that it ensures uniform confinement across the entire cross-section, preventing leakage or partial bypassing of the droplet.

The invention also incorporates droplet identification and tracking capabilities through geometric encoding—e.g., by varying trap dimensions or positions to create unique signatures for each droplet. This allows for the monitoring of droplet history and content without optical or electronic sensors, enhancing the autonomy of the system. Additionally, the content of each single droplet can be precisely controlled through sequential merging with reagent droplets of known volume, enabling programmable reaction protocols.

Formally, a microfluidic device according to the present invention comprises a substrate defining a microfluidic channel with a transverse dimension (e.g., width or depth) on the order of 10–500 micrometers. Within this channel, a barrier is formed as a protrusion extending from the floor or ceiling, with a shape selected from rectangular, trapezoidal, or curved profiles to optimize pressure response. The barrier width is typically 10–200 micrometers, and a second obstruction may be present at the opposite end of the trap to enable bidirectional operation.

Side intrusions—recesses or indentations in the channel walls—serve as alternative or complementary trapping elements. These intrusions have dimensions comparable to the droplet diameter and are positioned to maximize Laplace pressure without inducing instability. The side channel lumen, which connects the bypass to the main channel, is designed with a reduced cross-section to regulate flow distribution. A baffle—a partial obstruction within the side channel—further refines the flow field, enhancing droplet immobilization. In some embodiments, a second side channel and corresponding baffle are included to provide symmetry and bidirectional functionality.

The positioning of side channels relative to the main trap is optimized to ensure that the continuous phase can fully bypass the droplet while maintaining structural integrity. The channel cross-section is typically rectangular, with aspect ratios tailored to promote stable droplet confinement. In a second aspect of the invention, the microfluidic device includes a looped channel configuration that enables cyclic operations such as repeated dilution or incubation. Transverse dimensions of the channels in the loop are gradually varied to control droplet speed and mixing, with ratios of inlet to outlet widths maintained within 1:1 to 1:5 to ensure predictable behavior.

A third aspect involves a microfluidic system comprising multiple interconnected devices configured to perform multi-step protocols. This system includes means for mixing droplets—such as serpentine channels or chaotic advection structures—integrated downstream of merging traps. A fourth aspect provides a method for generating serial dilutions by sequentially merging a sample droplet with buffer droplets in a cascade of metering and merging traps. A fifth aspect enables digital dilution droplet PCR by combining trap-based dilution with thermal cycling zones. A sixth aspect supports point-of-care diagnostics by preloading reagents into traps and delivering patient samples via simple flow protocols.

The use of the microfluidic device according to the invention offers significant advantages in terms of simplicity, cost, and reliability. It eliminates the need for expensive instrumentation, reduces operator training requirements, and minimizes the risk of human error. A further aspect specifies precise dimensional tolerances—for example, microfluidic channels with widths of 50–200 µm, obstructions with heights of 20–100 µm, and side channels with depths of 10–50 µm—to ensure consistent performance across fabrication batches. The baffle in the side channel may be offset or angled to promote vortex formation, and symmetrical side channels enable reversible operation. The cross-section of the microfluidic channel is maintained uniform except at designated trap regions, ensuring predictable flow behavior throughout the system.

## EXAMPLES

### Example 1

The metering trap is a foundational component of the present invention, designed to isolate a precise volume of liquid from a larger droplet or stream. It consists of a microfluidic channel with an obstruction—typically a barrier—positioned at one end of a defined trap region. Two side channels, or bypasses, run parallel to the main channel along the length of the trap and connect to the main channel only at the entrance and exit of the trap region. The lumen of these side channels is deliberately reduced in cross-sectional area—often to one-quarter the depth of the main channel—to restrict the flow of the continuous phase while still permitting it to bypass a trapped droplet. This reduction in lumen is crucial because it balances the hydrodynamic resistance of the bypass against the Laplace pressure generated by the droplet at the barrier, ensuring stable trapping without leakage.

In operation, when a droplet shorter than the trap length enters the module, it advances until its leading edge contacts the barrier. At this point, the increased curvature of the droplet interface raises the Laplace pressure, halting further motion. The continuous phase flows around the droplet through the side channels, maintaining a low pressure drop along the droplet and preventing accidental displacement. If a longer droplet enters, its trailing edge blocks the entrance to the side channels, forcing the continuous phase to push the droplet forward. The droplet advances until its rear aligns with the side channel inlets, at which point the Rayleigh-Plateau instability severs the excess liquid, leaving a precisely metered volume trapped in the chamber.

Flow reversal can be used to release the trapped droplet by reversing the pressure gradient, though in many applications the droplet is released by the arrival of a subsequent droplet that pushes it out. Modifications to the trap include varying the cross-section of the main channel (e.g., tapering), adjusting the depth of the side channels, altering the shape or length of the barrier, or using different materials to tune wettability. Changing the width of the main or side channels affects the critical droplet length required for release, enabling customization for different applications. Bidirectional traps, which feature barriers at both ends, allow trapping and release from either direction, unlike unidirectional variants. Droplet merging, pushing, or replacement can be achieved by sequencing multiple droplets through the trap, and the trap volume—determined by the distance between barrier and side channel inlets—dictates the metered volume with high accuracy.

### Example 2

The merging trap is engineered to fuse two droplets and release the combined volume for downstream processing. It features side intrusions—recesses in the channel walls—at both the inlet and outlet of the trap region, which act as Laplace pressure barriers. Unlike the sharp barrier in the metering trap, these intrusions produce a more gradual deformation of the droplet interface, stabilizing the neck and preventing breakup during release. When a short droplet enters, it is trapped at the outlet intrusion as the continuous phase flows through the bypasses. A second droplet then flows in, expelling the continuous phase between them via the bypasses, allowing coalescence. If the merged droplet exceeds the trap length, it blocks the bypasses and is pushed out intact.

Droplet size critically affects operation: only droplets shorter than the trap are initially trapped, while merged droplets must be long enough to trigger release. Flow reversal can reset the trap or enable bidirectional merging. Possible modifications include asymmetric intrusions for directional control, varied intrusion depths, or integration with delay elements to enhance mixing.

### Example 3

The hybrid metering-merging trap combines features of both trap types, with a cutting barrier on one end and a merging intrusion on the other. This allows it to first meter a precise volume and then merge it with an incoming droplet, releasing the mixture only when the combined volume exceeds the trap length. This design is particularly useful for controlled dilution or reagent addition where exact stoichiometry is required.

### Example 4

Modification of traps with baffles—partial obstructions within the side channels—enhances droplet immobilization by disrupting the continuous-phase flow and increasing local pressure gradients. In operation, the baffle creates vortices that stabilize the droplet position and improve mixing. In circular traps, baffles can be arranged radially to promote rotation of the droplet, accelerating homogenization. Release is achieved by flow reversal or droplet pushing, and the key advantage is improved retention under variable flow conditions, making the trap suitable for long-term incubation or observation.

### Example 5

Droplets of predetermined size are generated by flowing a continuous stream of dispersed phase through a series of metering traps. Each trap captures a fixed volume, and excess liquid is either discarded or recycled. The volume is determined solely by the trap geometry, yielding high uniformity (<5% CV) across a wide range of flow rates.

### Example 6

The dilutor device uses a metering trap preloaded with sample and sequentially flushed with buffer droplets. Each buffer droplet merges with the sample, homogenizes via flow through the bypasses, and ejects an equal volume of diluted sample. The concentration after *n* steps is *Cₙ = C₀ / 2ⁿ*, assuming equal volumes. Experimental results confirm exponential decay with high reproducibility.

### Example 7

The DOMINO device integrates metering and merging traps in a cascade. Solvent droplets are first loaded into merging traps. A sample droplet twice the volume is then passed through: the first half is metered, the second half merges with solvent and is released at half concentration. This process repeats down the line, generating a geometric dilution series. Operation requires only three flow direction switches, with no timing or rate control. Images show successful generation of 1:2, 1:4, 1:8, etc., dilutions.

### Example 8

The Trapostat device uses lock&shift traps to array droplets for long-term culture. Nutrients are periodically flushed through bypasses, sustaining microbial growth without disturbing the trapped droplets. Applications include antibiotic susceptibility testing and evolution experiments.

### Example 9

A trap with baffle and one bypass enables droplet locking for lipid bilayer studies, where stable droplet positioning is essential. The “loop problem”—where droplets in branched channels choose paths unpredictably—is addressed by the derailer module. Using the Hagen-Poiseuille equation, ΔP = (8μLQ)/(πr⁴), and Laplace pressure ΔP = γ(1/R₁ + 1/R₂), the design ensures that resistance dominates in one branch while Laplace pressure dominates in the other. The derailer loop features anti-symmetric geometry so that droplets always take the same path regardless of size. Fabricated in polycarbonate, it enables reliable routing for digital assays. Applications include simultaneous droplet generation, antibiotic merging for MIC testing, and digital dilution droplet PCR—where serial dilutions are made on-chip before thermal cycling, enabling absolute quantification without standards.
