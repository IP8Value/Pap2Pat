# DESCRIPTION

Malaria is a life-threatening infectious disease caused by protozoan parasites of the genus *Plasmodium*, transmitted to humans through the bite of infected female *Anopheles* mosquitoes. Among the five species known to infect humans, *Plasmodium falciparum* is responsible for the most severe form of the disease and accounts for the majority of malaria-related morbidity and mortality worldwide, particularly in sub-Saharan Africa. The disease manifests with symptoms such as fever, chills, headache, and in severe cases, cerebral malaria, organ failure, and death. Malaria remains a major public health challenge, with hundreds of millions of clinical cases and hundreds of thousands of deaths reported annually, disproportionately affecting young children and pregnant women in endemic regions. Despite decades of research and control efforts, including vector control, chemoprophylaxis, and treatment with antimalarial drugs, the global burden of malaria persists, underscoring the urgent need for effective and durable preventive interventions.

Malaria is widely recognized as a serious disease due to its high transmission rates, complex life cycle, and capacity to evade host immune responses. The parasite’s ability to undergo antigenic variation and establish chronic infections further complicates vaccine development. Moreover, the emergence and spread of drug-resistant *P. falciparum* strains have diminished the efficacy of many first-line antimalarial therapies, heightening the reliance on preventive strategies. In this context, vaccination represents a cornerstone of long-term malaria control and potential eradication, offering a cost-effective and scalable solution to reduce transmission and disease incidence.

Preventative medicines, including chemoprophylactic agents such as atovaquone-proguanil, doxycycline, and mefloquine, are commonly used by travelers and military personnel visiting endemic areas. However, these drugs are not suitable for widespread or long-term use in endemic populations due to issues of cost, compliance, toxicity, and the risk of promoting drug resistance. Furthermore, chemoprophylaxis does not confer immunological memory and offers no protection once administration ceases, limiting its utility as a sustainable public health tool.

Current preventative medicines are thus insufficient for achieving broad and lasting malaria control, highlighting the critical importance of vaccine development. While several antimalarial drugs can treat established infections, they do not prevent initial infection or block transmission effectively in community settings. This gap has driven extensive research into prophylactic vaccines that can induce robust and durable immune responses capable of preventing parasite establishment or progression.

Vaccination programs against malaria have historically faced significant scientific and logistical hurdles. Unlike bacterial or viral pathogens, *Plasmodium* parasites exhibit complex stage-specific biology, with distinct antigenic profiles during the sporozoite, liver, blood, and sexual stages. This complexity necessitates precise targeting of antigens expressed during vulnerable phases of the parasite’s life cycle, particularly the pre-erythrocytic (liver) stage, where intervention can completely prevent blood-stage infection and clinical disease. Several candidate vaccines have been evaluated in clinical trials, but only a few have advanced to late-stage testing.

Among the most advanced candidates are the RTS,S/AS01 and ChAd63-MVA ME-TRAP vaccines. RTS,S is a recombinant protein vaccine based on a portion of the *P. falciparum* circumsporozoite protein (PfCSP) fused to the hepatitis B surface antigen, formulated with the AS01 adjuvant system. It has demonstrated partial efficacy in large-scale Phase III trials, reducing clinical malaria by approximately 30–50% over four years in young children, but protection wanes rapidly and is incomplete. The ChAd63-MVA ME-TRAP regimen employs a heterologous prime-boost strategy using chimpanzee adenovirus 63 (ChAd63) and modified vaccinia Ankara (MVA) viral vectors encoding the *P. falciparum* thrombospondin-related anonymous protein (PfTRAP). This approach induces strong T cell responses but has shown only modest protective efficacy in human challenge trials, typically delaying but not preventing blood-stage infection.

The limitations of current vaccines stem from their reliance on a narrow set of antigens—primarily PfCSP and PfTRAP—that may not optimally engage protective immune mechanisms. Both antigens are expressed during the sporozoite and early liver stages, but their immunogenicity and protective capacity appear suboptimal compared to the "gold-standard" of whole-parasite immunization. Irradiated sporozoites, administered via repeated bites of infected mosquitoes, have been shown to confer sterile protection in human volunteers by inducing robust CD8+ T cell responses against multiple liver-stage antigens. This model demonstrates that complete protection is achievable, but it is impractical for mass vaccination due to manufacturing, delivery, and safety constraints.

The central challenge in malaria vaccine development lies in identifying suitable antigens that can recapitulate the protective immunity observed with irradiated sporozoites. Historically, antigen selection has been guided by expression profiling, conservation across strains, and evidence of natural immunogenicity. However, the lack of a reliable small animal model that supports *P. falciparum* infection has severely hampered preclinical evaluation. Rodent malaria models using *P. berghei* or *P. yoelii* have been employed, but cross-species differences in antigen sequence and immune recognition limit their predictive value for human vaccines.

Liver-stage antigen 1 (LSA-1) emerged as a promising candidate due to its exclusive expression in the liver stage of *P. falciparum* and its association with protection in naturally exposed individuals and irradiated sporozoite-immunized volunteers. LSA-1 is a large, repetitive protein localized to the parasitophorous vacuole membrane (PVM), where it may play a role in merozoite development and egress. Early clinical trials of recombinant LSA-1 protein vaccines failed to demonstrate efficacy, likely due to an inability to induce CD8+ T cell responses, which are thought to be critical for eliminating infected hepatocytes.

These failures underscore the need for alternative antigens and more effective delivery platforms. Viral vector-based vaccines, such as ChAd63-MVA, offer a potent means to induce cellular immunity, particularly CD8+ T cells, which are essential for targeting intracellular liver-stage parasites. The present invention addresses these unmet needs by identifying novel *Plasmodium falciparum* antigens—PfLSA1, PfLSAP2, PfUIS3, PfI0580c, and PfSPECT-1—that elicit robust protective immune responses when delivered via viral vectors.

The invention provides an antigenic composition or vaccine comprising one or more nucleic acids encoding *Plasmodium falciparum* proteins, specifically PfLSA1, PfLSAP2, PfUIS3, PfI0580c, or PfSPECT-1, operably linked to regulatory elements for expression in mammalian cells. PfLSA1 is a liver-stage antigen 1 protein derived from *P. falcipararum* strain 3D7, modified to remove repetitive regions and enhance immunogenicity. PfLSAP2 is a newly identified liver-stage associated protein 2, also codon-optimized and engineered for improved expression. PfUIS3 (upregulated in sporozoites 3) is a well-conserved liver-stage protein involved in parasitophorous vacuole formation. PfI0580c and PfSPECT-1 are additional *P. falciparum* proteins selected based on high liver-stage expression and evidence of T cell recognition.

The method of identifying key candidate antigens involved screening for proteins expressed during the liver stage, targeted by T cells in natural infection or irradiated sporozoite immunization, and capable of inducing protection in preclinical models. Viral vectors, particularly replication-deficient adenoviruses (e.g., ChAd63) and MVA, were used to deliver these antigens, leveraging their ability to induce strong CD8+ T cell responses. Results demonstrated that PfLSA1 and PfLSAP2 conferred high levels of sterile protection in both inbred and outbred mice, significantly exceeding the efficacy of PfCSP and PfTRAP. PfUIS3 and PfI0580c showed moderate protective efficacy, primarily as delays in patency.

A "protective immune response" as used herein refers to an adaptive immune reaction that prevents or significantly reduces the establishment of blood-stage malaria following sporozoite challenge. This response is characterized by the presence of antigen-specific CD8+ T cells producing interferon-gamma (IFNγ), tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNFα), and/or expressing the degranulation marker CD107a, as well as the generation of liver-resident effector memory T cells. Humoral responses may also contribute, though cellular immunity is dominant in the models tested.

The viral vectors used in the invention are modified to enhance safety and immunogenicity. The nucleic acid sequences encoding the *Plasmodium* proteins are codon-optimized for human expression and may include redundant codons to maintain amino acid sequence while improving mRNA stability and translation efficiency. The vectors are engineered to lack replication competence and contain promoters (e.g., CMV) driving high-level antigen expression.

The composition may comprise two or more different viral vectors in a prime-boost regimen, such as ChAd63 followed by MVA, to amplify and sustain T cell responses. Pharmaceutical compositions include the viral vectors in a pharmaceutically acceptable carrier, optionally with adjuvants such as AS01, CpG, or aluminum salts, though viral vectors often function without traditional adjuvants. Nucleic acids encoding both viral proteins (for vector production) and the *Plasmodium* antigens are provided for manufacturing.

Specific embodiments include nucleic acids encoding PfLSA1, PfLSAP2, PfUIS3, PfI0580c, or PfSPECT-1, each fused to a tissue plasminogen activator (tPA) leader sequence (except for transmembrane proteins like PfUIS3) to enhance secretion and immunogenicity. The invention encompasses viruses comprising these nucleic acids, virus particles displaying the *Plasmodium* proteins, and host cells (e.g., HEK293) used for vector production.

Methods of eliciting an immune response involve administering the composition to a subject, preferably via intramuscular injection, in a single dose or prime-boost schedule. The vaccine is intended for prevention or treatment of malaria, particularly *P. falciparum* infection. Kits are provided containing the viral vectors, instructions for administration, and optionally diluents or syringes. The method of manufacturing involves cloning the antigen sequence into viral shuttle plasmids, transfecting packaging cells, and purifying the recombinant virus.
