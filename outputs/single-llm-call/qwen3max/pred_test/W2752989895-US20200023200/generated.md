## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates generally to the field of radiation therapy and, more specifically, to electron beam therapy used in the treatment of cancerous and non-cancerous lesions in human patients. Electron beam therapy is a form of external beam radiotherapy that utilizes high-energy electrons generated by a linear accelerator to deliver a prescribed radiation dose to a target volume located at or near the surface of the body. The energy of the electron beam typically ranges from approximately 4 MeV to 20 MeV, corresponding to therapeutic penetration depths in tissue from about 1 cm to 5 cm. In addition to electron beams, other radiation sources employed in clinical radiotherapy include photon (x-ray) beams, proton beams, heavy ion beams, and brachytherapy sources. The radiation dose, defined as the energy deposited per unit mass of tissue (measured in Gray, where 1 Gy = 1 J/kg), is the fundamental quantity used to prescribe and evaluate treatment efficacy and safety. The physical mechanisms by which radiation deposits dose in tissue involve interactions between charged particles (such as electrons) and atomic constituents of the medium, primarily through ionization and excitation events. A critical phenomenon affecting electron beam delivery is multiple Coulomb scattering (MCS), wherein electrons undergo numerous small-angle deflections as they traverse matter, leading to lateral spreading of the beam and degradation of spatial dose conformity. This scattering effect motivates the need for precise control over the spatial distribution of beam intensity across the treatment field to achieve optimal dose conformity to the planning target volume (PTV). Deep-seated PTVs are typically treated with higher-energy photon or proton beams due to their superior depth-dose characteristics, whereas superficial PTVs—those located within a few centimeters of the skin surface—are ideally suited for electron beam therapy because of the rapid dose fall-off beyond the therapeutic range, thereby sparing underlying healthy tissues. To enhance the conformality of electron dose distributions to irregularly shaped superficial targets, electron conformal therapy (ECT) techniques have been developed. However, current ECT methods suffer from significant limitations, particularly the inability to modulate the intensity of the electron beam across the field, which restricts the ability to produce homogeneous or deliberately heterogeneous dose distributions tailored to complex anatomical geometries. The present invention addresses these shortcomings by introducing a novel, passive method for achieving intensity modulation in electron beam therapy.

## BACKGROUND AND DESCRIPTION OF PRIOR ART

Electron conformal therapy (ECT) encompasses several approaches designed to shape the electron dose distribution to match the three-dimensional geometry of a superficial target volume. These include segmented-field ECT, bolus ECT, and modulated electron radiation therapy (MERT). All of these techniques either require or would significantly benefit from the capability to modulate the intensity of the electron beam across the treatment field. Historically, it was anticipated that electron intensity modulation would be achieved using electron multileaf collimators (eMLCs), analogous to the multileaf collimators (MLCs) widely used in intensity-modulated x-ray therapy (IMXT). However, conventional x-ray MLCs are unsuitable for electron beams due to their large air gaps between leaves, which exacerbate electron scattering and degrade field edge sharpness. Dedicated eMLC designs have been proposed and even commercialized by third-party vendors, such as Euromechanics of Germany, featuring thinner leaves and reduced inter-leaf distances to mitigate scattering. Despite these engineering advances, eMLCs have not gained widespread clinical adoption. Reasons include high acquisition and integration costs, mechanical complexity, inability to retract during non-electron treatments, limited compatibility with existing treatment planning systems (TPS), and the relatively low patient volume requiring electron therapy in most clinics. Alternative approaches involving scanned electron pencil beams have also been explored, but these require helium-filled beamlines to minimize MCS in air—a costly and logistically challenging modification to standard linear accelerators. Proton beam therapy, by contrast, has successfully implemented intensity modulation via spot-scanning techniques, but protons are fundamentally different from electrons in terms of interaction physics and clinical applicability, particularly for superficial targets. Consequently, despite decades of research, practical, cost-effective, and clinically integrable methods for electron beam intensity modulation remain unavailable. Existing passive methods, such as single shielding blocks for lens protection or saw-toothed collimator edges to broaden penumbrae, exploit principles of electron scattering but do not provide full-field, two-dimensional intensity modulation. Thus, there exists a long-felt but unmet need for a simple, low-cost, and TPS-compatible solution that enables true intensity-modulated electron therapy without requiring major hardware modifications to existing radiotherapy equipment.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention introduces a novel method for passive radiotherapy intensity modulation specifically designed for therapeutic electron beams, referred to herein as PRIME (Passive Radiotherapy Intensity Modulator for Electrons). The core innovation lies in the strategic use of “Island Blocks” and/or “Island Apertures” arranged in a planar configuration within or adjacent to the standard electron collimating insert of a linear accelerator. Island Blocks are small, discrete, electron-opaque structures positioned within the open aperture of the collimator, while Island Apertures are small, discrete openings formed within an otherwise solid collimating plate. By varying the size, shape, spacing, and spatial arrangement of these elements, the transmitted electron fluence—and thus the delivered dose intensity—can be precisely modulated across the treatment field. This modulation exploits the natural phenomenon of multiple Coulomb scattering: electrons blocked by Island Blocks create localized deficits in fluence, which are partially filled in downstream by scattered electrons from surrounding unblocked regions; conversely, electrons passing through Island Apertures scatter laterally, creating overlapping beamlets that sum to produce a controlled intensity level in regions where direct transmission is minimal. The PRIME method requires no moving parts, no gas-filled beamlines, and no specialized add-on hardware beyond a custom-fabricated collimator insert. It is inherently low-cost, leveraging established fabrication techniques for electron shielding blocks, and can be readily incorporated into existing clinical workflows using standard treatment planning systems with appropriate dose calculation algorithms. The invention thus provides a practical, scalable, and economically viable pathway to intensity-modulated electron therapy for a wide range of superficial malignancies.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

The method of the present invention involves selecting either Island Blocks, Island Apertures, or a combination thereof, based on the desired intensity reduction factor (IRF) across the treatment field. For intensity levels between 50% and 100% of the unmodulated beam, Island Blocks are preferred; for intensities between 0% and 50%, Island Apertures are employed. The shape of these elements is typically circular in cross-section to minimize the ratio of side surface area to projected area, thereby reducing unwanted electron scatter from block or aperture walls. They may be arranged on a regular grid—such as a hexagonal lattice—or in an irregular pattern optimized via inverse planning algorithms to achieve a specific dose distribution. The location of Island Blocks or Island Apertures is within the plane of the electron collimating insert, preferably just upstream of the final trimmer aperture, and their central axes are aligned with the divergent rays emanating from the virtual source of the electron beam to maintain geometric fidelity. The entire assembly is mounted on an electron-transparent substrate, such as low-density, machineable foam (e.g., Styrofoam), which provides structural support while contributing negligible attenuation or scatter. Island Blocks function as electron-blocking posts, fabricated from high-density, medically non-hazardous materials such as tungsten alloy, copper, or lead-based alloys like Cerrrobend, with sufficient thickness (typically 2 cm or equivalent in g/cm²) to fully attenuate primary electrons across the clinical energy range (4–20 MeV). Conversely, Island Apertures are electron-transparent pathways formed by drilling or machining holes through a solid collimating plate made of the same high-density material, with thickness matched to that of standard electron inserts. Variations in the height, shape, and spacing of both Island Blocks and Island Apertures enable fine control over the local intensity. When the electron beam passes through this modulator, primary electrons are either absorbed (by blocks) or transmitted (through apertures), while secondary electrons generated via MCS recombine downstream in the patient or phantom, producing a spatially modulated fluence distribution. FIG. 1 illustrates exemplary configurations: (a) an array of circular Island Blocks on a hexagonal grid within a collimator aperture, (b) an array of Island Apertures in a solid collimator, and (c) a hybrid design. FIG. 2 depicts the relative electron fluence at 1 cm depth in water for a single Island Block with varying air gaps, demonstrating how increased distance enhances scatter-mediated dose fill-in. FIG. 3 shows a half-field of Island Blocks and the resulting intensity profile, confirming that average intensity reduction correlates with the fractional area blocked. The physical basis of modulation relies on electron multiple Coulomb scattering, which causes individual beamlets to spread laterally after passing through apertures or around blocks. These beamlets recombine at depth, and the degree of recombination—and hence the smoothness and accuracy of the modulated intensity—is controlled by the distance between the modulator plane and the depth of interest. Proper selection of block/aperture diameter (d) and center-to-center separation (r) allows prediction of local intensity using analytical formulas derived from hexagonal packing geometry, though full optimization requires inverse planning due to the non-local effects of scattering.

### EXAMPLE 1

A prototype electron beam intensity modulator was constructed to demonstrate proof of principle. The device comprised lead wires of 0.2 cm diameter and 2.0 cm length inserted vertically into a 2.0 cm thick slab of Styrofoam, arranged on a hexagonal grid with a center-to-center spacing (r) of 0.5 cm. This configuration corresponded to a theoretical intensity reduction factor (IRF) of 0.85. The block matrix, consisting of five rows containing six to seven pins each, was positioned flush against the upstream face of the final trimmer collimator of an Elekta Infinity linear accelerator. Irradiation was performed using a 16 MeV electron beam with a 10 × 10 cm² field at 100 cm source-to-surface distance (SSD). Relative dose measurements were acquired at 2.0 cm depth in a water phantom using a p-type electron diode detector (IBA Dosimetry EFD3G) with 0.2 cm active diameter, scanned along the in-plane (x = 0) direction. The measured off-axis dose profile exhibited excellent agreement with predictions from the Hogstrom pencil beam algorithm (PBA), with deviations within 1% across the modulated region. The minimum relative dose recorded was 0.82, closely matching the expected value of 0.85, thereby validating the fundamental principle that spatially distributed blocking elements can produce predictable, uniform intensity reduction via scatter-mediated dose fill-in.

### EXAMPLE 2

The relative intensity profile generated by the prototype modulator described in Example 1 was analyzed to quantify modulation fidelity. At 2.0 cm depth in water and 100 cm SSD, the off-axis ratio (OAR) profile displayed a flat, reduced-intensity plateau across the central region of the field, with an average value of 0.82 ± 0.01, consistent with the theoretical IRF of 0.85 adjusted for minor scattering contributions from the Styrofoam substrate and finite detector size. The transition zone at the edge of the modulated region showed a smooth gradient due to lateral electron transport, with no significant oscillations or artifacts. Comparison with PBA calculations confirmed that the dose distribution could be accurately modeled when the exact geometry and material composition of the modulator were included in the fluence computation. This example demonstrates that even a simple, manually assembled array of uniform-diameter Island Blocks can produce a clinically acceptable level of intensity modulation, provided that the block spacing and size are chosen within empirically validated parameter ranges that ensure dose uniformity within ±2% of the target IRF.

### EXAMPLE 3

An intensity modulator was designed for a real patient previously treated with bolus electron conformal therapy for right buccal mucosa cancer. The desired intensity pattern, derived from an inverse-planned IM bolus ECT treatment aiming to improve PTV dose homogeneity, required spatially varying IRFs ranging from 0.70 to 1.00 across the field. Using an optimization algorithm based on the hexagonal packing model and accounting for electron scattering at 16 MeV and 103 cm SSD, a set of circular Island Blocks was generated on a hexagonal grid with fixed spacing (r = 0.5 cm) and variable diameters (d = 0.12–0.29 cm). The modulator was fabricated as a custom collimator insert with lead alloy blocks embedded in low-density foam. Dose calculations performed with the PBA at 2.0 cm depth in water showed that the delivered intensity pattern closely matched the desired distribution, with iso-intensity contours aligning within 1–2 mm. Similar accuracy was achieved at 0.5 cm depth, confirming the robustness of the design across clinically relevant depths. This example illustrates the clinical applicability of PRIME for complex, patient-specific intensity modulation in bolus ECT, enabling restoration of PTV dose homogeneity from 30% spread to under 12%, as demonstrated in prior planning studies.
