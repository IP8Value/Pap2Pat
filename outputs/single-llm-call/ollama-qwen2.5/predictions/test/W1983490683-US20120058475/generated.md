- Chromatid painting, a novel cytogenomics-based method utilizing strand-specific hybridization strategies and bioinformatics for probe design, offers high-resolution detection of chromosomal inversions. This technique represents an order-of-magnitude improvement over traditional banding methods, enabling cell-by-cell analysis with potential resolution down to very small inversions.
- The current average resolution for inversion detection is around 1 MB based on probe set spacing. However, more densely targeted probes can detect much smaller inversions, though the probability of detection decreases as inversion size diminishes due to random breakpoint locations.
- Chromatid painting revealed low spontaneous inversion frequencies in normal human cells (<0.3%), which is crucial for evaluating genotoxic agent-induced inversions. This method supports genome database assembly and initial evidence suggests individuals share similar sequence directionality, though fluorescent signals can vary based on chromatin condensation states.
- The technique's versatility allows it to complement comparative and evolutionary studies by developing probe sets for any sequenced species with suitable chromosomes. Unlike conventional chromosome painting, chromatid painting simultaneously detects both intrachromosomal (inversions) and inter-chromosomal rearrangements (dicentrics, translocations), expanding its diagnostic and prognostic applications.
- Directional genomic hybridization could significantly advance our understanding of inversions' prevalence, size distribution, and association with human disorders. Improved detection of small inversions will aid in characterizing their contribution to structural variation in the human genome, potentially benefiting disease diagnosis and treatment, including cancer, autism, developmental delay, and idiopathic infertility.
