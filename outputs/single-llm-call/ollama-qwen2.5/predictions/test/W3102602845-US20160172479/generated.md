# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates to electron gas electronic circuit elements, particularly those utilizing two-dimensional electron gases (2DEGs) and two-dimensional hole gases (2DHGs). These devices are designed to enhance the performance of semiconductor electronics by leveraging the unique properties of 2D gases, such as high mobility and quantum effects.

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

Two-dimensional electronic gases (2DEGs) have emerged as a promising material for advanced electronic devices due to their exceptional electrical properties. The motivation behind using 2DEGs lies in their ability to support ballistic transport, where electrons can travel without scattering over macroscopic distances, leading to high electron mobilities. Research on 2DEGs has shown that these gases can be created at the interface of certain semiconductor materials, such as GaAs/AlGaAs heterostructures.

Two-dimensional hole gases (2DHGs) are another form of 2D gas that exhibit similar properties but with holes instead of electrons. Both 2DEGs and 2DHGs have been studied extensively for their potential in high-speed and low-power electronic devices. These gases can support quantum phenomena such as the integer and fractional quantum Hall effects, which are critical for applications in quantum computing and other advanced technologies.

The creation of 2DEGs and 2DHGs typically involves complex fabrication processes that can damage the ultra-high mobility materials used. Traditional methods involve direct deposition of gates on the semiconductor material using techniques like e-beam lithography, which can introduce defects and reduce the performance of the devices. These issues have led to a need for alternative fabrication methods that can preserve the high mobility of 2D gases.

To address these challenges, the present invention introduces a mechanical flip-chip technique for fabricating split-gate devices on ultra-high electron mobility materials. This method avoids performing any potentially damaging steps directly on the high-mobility wafer, thereby maintaining the integrity and performance of the 2DEG or 2DHG.

The flip-chip technique involves creating the gates on a separate substrate, typically a sapphire plate, using conventional e-beam lithography. The gates are then aligned and placed onto the 2DEG material with extreme precision. This approach ensures that the critical interfacing surfaces remain clean and free from contamination, which is crucial for maintaining high electron mobilities.

The flip-chip method has been successfully demonstrated on GaAs/AlGaAs heterostructures grown at Sandia National Laboratories and Princeton University. The results show that devices fabricated using this technique exhibit excellent performance, including clear quantization of conductance and robust operation in high magnetic fields.

In comparison to conventional QPCs (CQPCs), the flip-chip QPCs (FCQPCs) have shown a lower gate efficiency due to the presence of an air gap between the flip-chip gates and the 2DEG. However, this issue can be mitigated by reducing the contact area between the two mechanical parts of the device. Despite this limitation, FCQPCs demonstrate superior performance in terms of pinch-off voltage and the absence of hysteresis, which are common issues in traditional devices.

The flip-chip technique has also been characterized in the quantum Hall regime at very low temperatures and high magnetic fields. The results confirm that the devices can operate effectively under these conditions, making them suitable for advanced applications such as interferometric measurements with the 5/2 and 12/5 fractional quantum Hall effects.

In summary, the mechanical flip-chip technique offers a promising solution for fabricating high-performance electronic devices based on 2DEGs and 2DHGs. By avoiding direct fabrication steps on ultra-high mobility materials, this method preserves the unique properties of these gases, paving the way for future advancements in semiconductor electronics.
