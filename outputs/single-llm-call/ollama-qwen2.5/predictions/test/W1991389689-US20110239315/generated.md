The study demonstrated that TAL proteins can function as potent and specific gene switches in human cells, opening up new possibilities for biotechnology applications. The Golden TAL Technology developed by the researchers allows flexible assembly of designer TAL proteins with different repeats, termini and tags. This user-friendly method does not rely on PCR and minimizes errors compared to previous approaches. The tailored HuTALs strongly induced reporter genes over 30-fold and also activated endogenous human gene expression, albeit to a lesser extent. Further optimization will be needed to achieve robust regulation of endogenous targets.

The researchers constructed artificial TAL proteins with VP16 activation domains that matched promoters of various human target genes. These HuTALs were able to strongly activate luciferase reporter constructs up to 30-fold in transiently transfected cells. When tested on endogenous gene loci, the HuTALs induced expression of PUMA, IFNα1 and IFNβ1 mRNAs 1.5-3.5 fold. While less potent than on reporters, this represents a promising first step towards using TAL proteins to specifically regulate genes in human cells.

The Golden TAL Technology relies on a library of plasmid subclones containing individual repeats rather than PCR products. This avoids errors introduced by PCR and allows construction of TAL proteins with 1-24 repeats. The method is modular, enabling flexible insertion of different termini, tags and activation domains. A key advantage is the use of Golden Gate cloning for efficient one-step assembly reactions. Overall, this provides a powerful platform to rapidly generate designer TAL proteins.

The study highlights both the potential and challenges in using TAL proteins as gene switches in human cells. While potent activators of reporters, the HuTALs showed more modest effects on endogenous genes likely due to differences in chromatin context and copy number. Further optimization will be needed to improve efficacy at native loci. Nevertheless, this work represents an important advance that paves the way for using TAL proteins for precise gene regulation in human cells and tissues.
