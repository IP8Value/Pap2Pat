The study found that the maize gene ZAR1, a putative orthologue of Arabidopsis ARGOS, increases plant and organ size in maize by boosting cell number rather than cell size. Ectopic expression of ZAR1 enhanced growth rate without extending the growth period. Similar to ARGOS in Arabidopsis, ZAR1's effect on maize is primarily through increased cell proliferation.

The transgenic overexpression of ZAR1 not only enlarged plant and organ sizes but also improved agronomic traits linked to drought stress, such as reduced anthesis-silking interval (ASI), tip kernel abortion, and barrenness. These positive effects were more pronounced under higher planting densities and in hotter, drier environments, suggesting that ZAR1 may enhance drought tolerance.

The research demonstrated that the efficacy of the ZAR1 transgene interacts with environmental conditions, impacting complex crop performance traits. Unlike simpler traits like insect or weed resistance, which tend to be effective across broad regions, the ZAR1 gene's effects on yield and stress tolerance were more pronounced under specific environmental stresses, such as drought.

The study observed a parallel between the transgenic efficacy of ZAR1 alleles and their distribution in elite maize germplasm. Two founder ZAR1 alleles (SS1 and NS1) are predominant in Pioneer's SS and NS heterotic groups, indicating selection for these alleles during breeding. The alleles' performance in hybrids, where they are heterozygous, aligns with the principles of heterosis in hybrid maize development.

The native ZAR1 alleles (SS1 and NS1) showed functional diversity through expression differences and protein structure variations, suggesting their potential significance in drought tolerance. When these alleles were transgenically stacked in a heterozygous state, they outperformed individual alleles, mimicking heterotic effects. This finding supports the idea that genetic material already selected under specific agronomic conditions can be valuable for crop improvement through both conventional and transgenic approaches.

The ZAR1 alleles' transgenic effects and their native variation in elite breeding germplasm highlight a shared genetic basis governing their contributions to favorable traits, including environmental interaction dependencies. Genetic variations from the same species, particularly those already selected by breeding under targeted environments like drought stress, can be useful for crop improvement using both transgenic and cisgenic methods.
