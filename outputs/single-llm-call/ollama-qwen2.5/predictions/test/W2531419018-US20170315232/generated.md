### Introduction
The use of HF coastal radars to detect and warn of approaching tsunamis has been a topic of interest for nearly four decades. Initially proposed in the early 1980s, this technology gained significant attention after the devastating Banda Aceh tsunami in 2004, which claimed over a quarter of a million lives. The tragic event highlighted the critical need for an effective local warning system. Following the 2011 Tohoku earthquake and tsunami in Japan, a substantial number of HF radars were installed globally, providing valuable data for developing detection algorithms and methodologies.

### Detection Algorithm Development
Several studies have demonstrated the effectiveness of detection algorithms using HF coastal radars. These algorithms focus on measuring the orbital velocity of the tsunami wave, which is distinct from other sensors that measure wave height. The 2011 Tohoku event provided a significant dataset that was instrumental in refining these algorithms. Subsequent developments have aimed to optimize the methodology for near-field monitoring and enhance the reliability of tsunami detection.

### Recent Work on Tsunami Simulation
Recent advancements include the simulation of tsunami height and velocity, which allows for more accurate assessment of potential warnings at specific site locations. These simulations take into account offshore bathymetry, background currents, and noise levels. By using exact methods to solve linear partial differential equations, researchers can better predict the arrival time and intensity of tsunamis, improving the overall performance of detection systems.

### Site Evaluation
The evaluation of potential radar sites is crucial for optimizing tsunami warning capabilities. Factors such as offshore bathymetry, background currents, and noise levels are analyzed to determine the effectiveness of a site. Simulations help in assessing the warning time possible at a given location, allowing for better planning and resource allocation. This approach ensures that radars are placed where they can provide the most significant benefit in terms of early detection and reduced false alarms.

### False-Alarm Reduction
False alarms remain a critical challenge in tsunami warning systems. Various methods have been developed to reduce these occurrences while maintaining high detection rates. One approach involves correlating q-factor alerts from adjacent HF radars. If multiple radars detect similar signals within a short time frame, the likelihood of a real tsunami is significantly increased. Another method is to correlate q-factor alerts with earthquake notifications to filter out non-tsunamigenic events.

### Conclusions
The integration of HF coastal radars into tsunami warning systems has made significant strides in recent years. The ability to detect and warn of approaching tsunamis using these radars is now a viable technology, as demonstrated by the 21 detections recorded over the past five years. The key factors influencing detection times are offshore bathymetry and the presence of shallow shelf edges. Ongoing collaborations with organizations like NOAA and IOOS are crucial for transitioning this capability into operational use, enhancing public safety and response efforts.
