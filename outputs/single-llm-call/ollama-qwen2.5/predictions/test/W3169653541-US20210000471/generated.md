- **Introduction**: This study examines how the graft-to-vein anastomosis angle affects pathological flow fields in brachio-axillary arteriovenous grafts. Pathological shear rates are linked to myointimal hyperplasia, stenosis, and thrombosis, which can lead to graft failure. The goal is to identify an optimal anastomosis angle that minimizes these issues.

- **Methodology**: Using computational fluid dynamics (CFD) simulations, we modeled blood flow through arteriovenous grafts with varying anastomosis angles. We analyzed shear strain rates over the vein wall during a single cardiac cycle to determine the impact of different angles on pathological flow fields.

- **Results - High Shear Rates**: For high graft-to-vein anastomosis angles (e.g., 90°), significant areas of pathologically high shear rates were observed early in the heart cycle, particularly at the distal end of the anastomosis and opposite vein wall. These high shear rates decreased with decreasing anastomosis angle.

- **Results - Low Shear Rates**: Conversely, low graft-to-vein anastomosis angles (e.g., 13°) showed minimal high shear rates but increased pathologically low shear rates midway through the heart cycle, localized to the vein wall just distal to the anastomosis area. This pattern suggests a trade-off between high and low shear rates.

- **Optimal Angle**: A graft-to-vein anastomosis angle of 30° provided the most balanced flow fields with minimal pathological shear rates. Angles above 30° led to increased high shear rates, while angles below 30° led to increased low shear rates. This optimal angle could potentially improve graft patency and reduce complications.

- **Discussion**: The findings suggest that optimizing the anastomosis angle can significantly impact graft success. Future research will explore the range of optimal angles relative to different axillary vein diameters. Manufacturing grafts with pre-set, optimized angles could be a novel approach to improving primary and secondary patency rates in hemodialysis patients.

- **Limitations**: The study has limitations, including static blood vessel models and fixed blood flow parameters. Hypertension and variations in vein diameter were not accounted for, though these factors are typically managed pharmacologically. Dynamic models of peripheral arteries and veins could further refine the results.

- **Conclusion**: Optimizing the graft-to-vein anastomosis angle can enhance flow fields, reduce pathological shear strain rates, and potentially improve graft patency rates in hemodialysis patients. A 30° angle appears to be the most optimal based on our findings.
