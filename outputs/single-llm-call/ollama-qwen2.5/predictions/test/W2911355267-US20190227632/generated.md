- The study investigates how tactile and non-tactile inputs contribute to estimating fingertip distance during dexterous manipulation. It used custom haptic devices to apply controlled forces to thumb and index finger while subjects performed a perceptual matching task.

- Results showed that when both tactile and non-tactile signals were available, the perceptual bias in estimating fingertip distance was greater than when each input was provided alone. Tactile inputs contributed about 60% to the overall estimation.

- Analysis suggested that fingertip distance is estimated through a dynamic linear combination of tactile and non-tactile cues, with relative weighting changing based on context like same vs opposite force directions. 

- The findings are consistent with neurophysiological evidence of somatosensory cortex activity during finger manipulation tasks in primates. They suggest higher-order brain areas like the inferior parietal lobule and cerebellum integrate tactile and proprioceptive signals to form a neural representation of fingertip relative position.

- This work provides insights into the sensorimotor mechanisms underlying dexterous manipulation, highlighting the critical role of tactile inputs and their integration with other sensory and motor signals. The results have implications for understanding perception in natural tasks as well as designing haptic interfaces for virtual reality and robotic systems.
