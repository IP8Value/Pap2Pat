- **Introduction**: This study investigates the role of mitochondrial superoxide production in diet-induced intestinal barrier dysfunction and its impact on lifespan. Using specific small-molecule suppressors (S3QELs) of electron leak at site IIIQo of complex III, the researchers aimed to identify the source of superoxide causing this dysfunction in both Drosophila and mice.

- **Methods**: The study utilized a combination of genetic manipulations and pharmacological interventions. In Drosophila, Sod knockdown in enterocytes was used to raise superoxide levels, while S3QELs were fed to suppress site IIIQo electron leak. Mice were fed a high-fat diet with or without S3QELs to assess intestinal permeability and related metabolic parameters.

- **Results**: In Drosophila, increased superoxide levels due to Sod knockdown in enterocytes led to intestinal barrier dysfunction and reduced lifespan. Feeding S3QELs protected against these effects, extending the diet-compromised lifespan. Similarly, in mice, high-fat feeding induced intestinal permeability, which was significantly mitigated by S3QEL treatment.

- **Mechanistic Insights**: The study revealed that hyperglycemia and ER stress, both associated with a high-fat diet, increased superoxide production at site IIIQo of the mitochondrial electron transport chain in gut epithelial cells. This superoxide production stabilized Hif-1α, contributing to intestinal barrier dysfunction.

- **Conservation of Effects**: The protective effects of S3QELs against intestinal permeability were observed in both Drosophila and mice, suggesting a conserved mechanism across species. The low systemic concentration of S3QELs in mouse plasma indicated that the protection was likely due to direct exposure of enterocytes to the compounds from the gut lumen.

- **Discussion**: This study highlights the critical role of mitochondrial superoxide production at site IIIQo in diet-induced intestinal barrier dysfunction and its impact on lifespan. General antioxidants, which can interfere with important oxidative signaling, are less effective compared to S3QELs, which specifically target the source of superoxide. The findings have implications for understanding and treating metabolic diseases and aging.

- **Conclusion**: Superoxide production from site IIIQo of mitochondrial complex III is a key driver of diet-induced intestinal barrier dysfunction in both flies and mice. Suppressing this superoxide production using S3QELs can extend lifespan in Drosophila and protect against intestinal permeability in mice, suggesting potential therapeutic applications for metabolic disorders and premature aging.
