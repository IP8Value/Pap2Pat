- The WIS (Wavefront Imaging Sensor) is a novel sensor that can transform standard microscopes into wavefront measuring devices without requiring complex optical setups. It provides quantitative normalized phase gradient images, which are improvements over DIC (Differential Interference Contrast) microscopy in terms of being immune to birefringence artifacts and clearly separating intensity and phase information.
- The WIS works by using an aperture sieve above the sensor pixels to capture both intensity and phase information simultaneously. This design allows it to provide high-density wavefront sensing while maintaining a compact form factor, making it easy to integrate into existing microscope systems without major modifications.
- One of the key advantages of the WIS is its ability to perform quantitative phase imaging, which can be used for various applications such as measuring optical thickness in cells or determining chemical concentrations in microfluidic systems. This capability simplifies setup and usage compared to traditional interferometric methods.
- The fabrication process for the WIS is compatible with semiconductor foundry procedures, making it possible to produce these sensors at low cost and in large quantities. This accessibility can significantly impact fields like adaptive optics, machine recognition, texture assessment, and object ranging, as well as medical applications such as LASIK surgery and high-resolution retinal imaging.
- The WIS can also be adapted for color imaging by integrating with sensor chips that have stratified color sensing sites or by developing more sophisticated projection spot localization algorithms. This dual functionality would allow microscopists to collect both phase-gradient and color images using a single sensor, eliminating the need to switch between different cameras.
- In practical applications, the WIS provides an easy and cost-effective solution for researchers and clinicians to incorporate phase imaging into their current microscope systems. It can also facilitate deep tissue imaging by correcting aberrations generated by tissue heterogeneity, enhancing the capabilities of multiphoton microscopy and other advanced imaging techniques.
