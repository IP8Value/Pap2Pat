The study reveals that manganese ions intercalated within graphene sheets demonstrate significantly enhanced relaxivity. This enhancement is attributed to the unique confinement effect rather than particle size or shape. The approach modifies key parameters like q (number of inner-sphere water molecules) and τM (water-residence lifetime), leading to r1 values as high as 55 mM−1 at 20 MHz for macromolecular complexes, compared to 4–10 mM−1s−1 for small molecule chelates. This novel strategy opens avenues for developing highly efficient Mn2+-based MRI contrast agents.

The NMRD profiles of the manganese-intercalated graphene samples were fitted using SBM equations, which describe inner-sphere interactions. The fits provided reasonable values for parameters like D (zero-field splitting energy) and τS (correlation time for zero-field splitting). However, the fits were more accurate for oxidized micro-graphite and reduced graphene nanoplatelets than for oxidized graphene nanoplatelets and nanoribbons. This suggests that while the SBM model is useful, it may not fully capture all interactions in some samples.

The water-residence lifetime (τM) was estimated using 17O measurements at 14 T, corroborating NMRD fit results for oxidized micro-graphite and oxidized graphene nanoplatelets. However, τM values for reduced graphene nanoplatelets and nanoribbons were 100 times greater than those from NMRD fits. Fixing τM to these higher values improved the fits but only marginally for graphene nanoribbons. This discrepancy highlights the complexity of water exchange dynamics in different graphene structures.

The separation distance (rMnH) between manganese ions and water protons was allowed to vary slightly during SBM fitting, leading to improved accuracy. Despite this sensitivity, the fitted values were close to the commonly reported 2.9 Å. This adjustment underscores the importance of precise parameter tuning in accurately modeling relaxivity. The results suggest that nanoconfinement within graphene can significantly influence rMnH and, consequently, enhance MRI contrast.

The physiochemical properties and high relaxivity of manganese-intercalated graphene structures make them promising candidates for further development as MRI contrast agents. In vitro and in vivo studies are needed to assess their safety and efficacy. Manganese's natural presence in the body and its role as a regulatory cofactor offer advantages over gadolinium-based agents, which have been linked to nephrogenic systemic fibrosis. This work could lead to a new class of high-efficacy Mn2+-carbon nanostructure complexes for MRI applications.

The MRI contrast agent market is projected to grow significantly, driven by the increasing use of MRI procedures and concerns over gadolinium toxicity. Manganese-based agents offer a safer alternative due to their natural presence in the body and lower toxicity risk. The development of manganese-intercalated graphene structures could address these needs, providing high relaxivity and potentially reducing side effects associated with current contrast agents. Further research will focus on optimizing synthesis methods and evaluating clinical potential.
