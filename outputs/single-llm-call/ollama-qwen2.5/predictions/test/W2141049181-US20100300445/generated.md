- **Survey Context**: This study surveyed critical care physicians and respiratory therapists at a large academic institution to understand their ventilator settings choices. Despite a low response rate possibly due to the holiday season, it captured a snapshot of mechanical ventilation practices during that period.

- **Variability in Settings**: The survey revealed significant variability in clinicians' initial ventilator settings, sometimes deviating from established guidelines and physiological principles. This inconsistency highlights the challenge of ensuring protocol adherence in clinical practice.

- **Computer Algorithms**: Adaptive Support Ventilation (ASV) and Mid-Frequency Ventilation (MFV) are computerized targeting schemes that aim to optimize ventilation parameters. ASV minimizes work rate, while MFV maximizes alveolar ventilation and minimizes tidal volume, leading to different outcomes based on the underlying lung condition.

- **Normal Lung Physiology**: In normal lungs, ASV settings closely matched those chosen by clinicians, with a protective tidal volume of 6.9 mL/kg. MFV, however, used much lower tidal volumes (3.5 mL/kg), resulting in reduced end-inspiratory volumes but minimal differences in airway pressures.

- **ARDS and Obesity**: In ARDS, ASV settings were slightly higher than clinicians' choices but remained within lung-protective ranges. For obesity, ASV used a lower tidal volume with longer inspiratory times, while MFV significantly reduced tidal volumes and increased end-expiratory volumes, aligning with its goal of minimizing alveolar stretching.

- **Obstructive Disorders**: Clinicians varied their settings between COPD (low tidal volume/low respiratory rate) and status asthmaticus (low tidal volume/high respiratory rate). Both ASV and MFV used similar patterns for both conditions, reflecting a focus on prolonged expiratory phases to reduce air trapping.

- **Limitations of the Study**: The study's reliance on a simple lung model and lack of respiratory effort may limit its applicability. Additionally, the closed-loop nature of computer algorithms could bias results towards more optimized settings compared to initial clinician choices.

- **Conclusion**: Computer-controlled targeting schemes can result in similar or very different ventilator settings compared to clinicians, depending on the physiological condition and the algorithm's optimization goal. This highlights the potential for these systems to improve adherence to lung-protective strategies.
