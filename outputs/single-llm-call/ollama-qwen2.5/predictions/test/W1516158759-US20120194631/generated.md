The study explored using a kinetic proxy with swiveling displays to improve remote collaboration in hub-and-satellite teams. Motion helped project attention and engage participants, but had tradeoffs like excluding some when the display turned away. Explicit control was more intentional but incurred delay, while implicit control reduced this but made incidental movements seem meaningful. Overall, motion improved interaction despite not achieving true eye contact. The researchers plan to explore designs that leverage physical motion's benefits without exclusion, perhaps using pointers or convex displays.
The satellite confederate found explicit control most comfortable as it amplified her "voice" and communicated intent clearly. However, the swiveling display could exclude some hub participants when turned away from them. Hub members sometimes stopped talking during awkward interruptions caused by the kinetic motion. The delay in aiming the proxy may have contributed to longer speech segments measured for the explicit condition.
The study found mixed results on whether implicit or explicit control felt more natural. Some hub participants reported not even noticing the stationary display in the baseline condition, despite behavioral measures showing reactions to the motion conditions. This suggests attention awareness operates at a subconscious level. The researchers are interested in teasing apart gaze and attention awareness as separate mechanisms that can be designed for independently.
The kinetic proxy showed promise in addressing the skip-over effect by giving hub participants better awareness of the satellite's focus. However, it did not solve the newscaster problem of poor eye contact, as turning the display also turned the camera away from some participants. The researchers plan to explore alternative designs like pointers or convex displays that can project attention without excluding anyone visually.
The study provides insights applicable beyond this specific turntable proxy design. Motion can help convey a sense of presence and engagement even without true eye contact.
