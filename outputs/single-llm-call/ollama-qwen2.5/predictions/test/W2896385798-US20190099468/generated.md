- We discovered that Dynasore and Dyngo-4a protect ocular surface cells from oxidative stress damage. This was unexpected as these compounds were originally developed to inhibit endocytosis. The protection likely involves preventing membrane permeabilization and glycocalyx shedding, suggesting new therapeutic potential for treating ocular surface diseases by protecting new cells as they rise to the surface.
- Our findings using stratified and differentiated cell cultures highlight their importance in modeling the ocular surface. Unlike monolayer cells, these more closely resemble the corneal epithelium and showed no endocytosis stimulation or cytoplasmic dye concentration after oxidative stress, indicating limited endocytic activity as part of barrier function.
- We propose that vital dye uptake occurs through small breaches in plasma membrane and glycocalyx barriers caused by oxidative stress. This allows dyes to enter cells more freely without necessarily causing cell death. Concentration mechanisms may then keep the dye inside living cells, explaining the requirement for live cells in hyperfluorescence.
- While our study focused on oxidative stress, the findings likely apply to other forms of damaging stress such as surfactants in multipurpose solutions. These can disrupt plasma membrane barrier function and increase vital dye staining similar to oxidative stress. Future studies should test multiple stressors to confirm these mechanisms are broadly applicable to sublethal cell damage.
- The protective effects of Dynasore and Dyngo-4a against oxidative stress may involve dynamin-dependent or independent mechanisms affecting mitochondria, lipid rafts, actin dynamics, or other cellular processes. Determining the specific mechanism will require further investigation but could open new therapeutic avenues for preventing ocular surface disease by protecting cells from damage.
- Our results suggest Dynasore and Dyngo-4a have potential as topical treatments to gradually resolve chronic ocular surface diseases. By protecting new cells rising to the surface over time, these compounds may allow damaged tissue to heal. Further research is needed but this novel application could offer an important advance in treating difficult-to-manage conditions affecting the corneal epithelium.
