- **Introduction**: The study investigates accelerated sintering in phase-separating nanostructured alloys, focusing on the W–Cr and Cr–Ni systems. It demonstrates that nanophase separation can significantly enhance sintering kinetics, leading to full density at lower temperatures and shorter times compared to conventional methods.

- **W–Cr System**: In the W–Cr system, nanocrystalline W with 15 at% Cr was mechanically alloyed and compacted into disks. The resulting material exhibited accelerated sintering, achieving full density at 1,350°C, much lower than the typical sintering temperature for pure W.

- **Control Experiments**: Control experiments were conducted to isolate the effects of nanocrystallinity and supersaturation. Only the sample that combined both properties showed accelerated sintering, confirming the importance of these conditions in enhancing sintering kinetics.

- **Microstructure Characterization**: Microstructure analysis using SEM and TEM revealed the formation of a nanoscale Cr-rich phase at grain boundaries during sintering. This phase acted as a rapid diffusion path, facilitating densification. EDS maps confirmed the distribution of Cr within the W matrix.

- **Cr–Ni System**: Similar accelerated sintering was observed in the Cr–Ni system. Nanocrystalline Cr with 15 at% Ni showed significant densification at lower temperatures compared to pure Cr. The activation energy for sintering aligned with the diffusion of Cr in Ni, indicating that nanophase separation played a crucial role.

- **Sintering of Larger Specimens**: A bulk specimen of W-15 at% Cr, 15 mm in diameter and 11 mm in height, was sintered to full density under the same conditions, demonstrating the scalability of the accelerated sintering process. This confirms that the phenomenon is not limited to small lab-scale samples.

- **Conclusion**: The study highlights the potential of nanophase separation for enhancing sintering kinetics in nanostructured alloys. By combining nanocrystallinity and supersaturation, it is possible to achieve full density at lower temperatures and shorter times, which could have significant implications for the manufacturing of advanced materials.
