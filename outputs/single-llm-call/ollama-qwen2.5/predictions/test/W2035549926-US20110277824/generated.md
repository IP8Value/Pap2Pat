# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

Solar cells are critical components in the transition to sustainable energy systems. As the demand for alternative energy sources grows, solar cells have become a focal point due to their ability to convert sunlight directly into electricity. The basic structure of a solar cell typically consists of a p-n junction formed within a semiconductor material, such as silicon. When light strikes the cell, it excites electrons, creating electron-hole pairs that are separated by the electric field at the p-n junction, generating an electrical current.

The photovoltaic effect is the fundamental principle underlying the operation of solar cells. It involves the absorption of photons from sunlight, which generates free charge carriers (electrons and holes) in the semiconductor material. These charge carriers are then collected at the electrodes to produce an electric current. To enhance efficiency, modern solar cells often incorporate a selective emitter structure, where the front surface is selectively doped to reduce recombination losses.

However, traditional methods of forming selective emitters using photolithographic masking processes have several limitations. The photolithographic process involves multiple steps, including photoresist application, exposure, development, and etching, which are time-consuming and costly. Additionally, the stripping process required to remove the photoresist can damage the underlying semiconductor material, leading to increased surface recombination.

The etching process used in conventional methods also poses challenges. Wet chemical etching, for instance, can lead to non-uniform doping profiles and excessive material loss, reducing the overall efficiency of the solar cell. Dry etching techniques, such as reactive ion etching (RIE), offer better control but can introduce surface damage due to the high energy of the ions used in the process.

Conventional methods of forming selective emitters typically involve multiple steps that are complex and prone to errors. For example, the formation of a mask layer using photolithography followed by doping and etching processes can be difficult to optimize, leading to variations in performance across different cells. These limitations highlight the need for a new method that simplifies the manufacturing process while maintaining or improving the efficiency of solar cells.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates to a novel method for manufacturing a solar cell with a selective emitter structure and the solar cell itself. The method significantly reduces etchant consumption by employing a simplified masking technique, thereby minimizing material loss during the formation of heavily doped regions. This approach also allows for easy patterning of the front surface, reducing surface recombination and serial resistance.

By using a novel mask layer and optimized etching conditions, the invention achieves a more uniform and controlled doping profile, leading to improved photoelectric conversion efficiency. The method simplifies the manufacturing process, reduces costs, and enhances the overall performance of the solar cell. Key advantages of the invention include reduced surface recombination, lower serial resistance, and higher short-circuit current density (Isc), all contributing to increased cell efficiency.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF EXEMPLARY EMBODIMENTS

The formation of the emitter layer is a critical step in the manufacturing process of the solar cell. In this embodiment, the emitter layer is formed by doping a p-type silicon wafer with phosphorus using a conventional tube furnace and POCl3 liquid source. The doping process creates a shallow diffused region on the front surface of the wafer, which will later be selectively doped to form heavily doped regions.

To achieve selective doping, a mask layer is formed on the front surface of the emitter layer. This mask layer serves as a diffusion barrier and can be deposited using various techniques, such as chemical vapor deposition (CVD) or sputtering. In this embodiment, a SiO2 layer is deposited as the mask layer to prevent phosphorus from diffusing into the silicon wafer in undesired areas.

The mask layer is then patterned to expose specific regions where heavy doping will occur. This patterning can be achieved using an etch paste or a similar material that selectively removes the mask layer in the desired areas. The etch paste is applied and cured, and the exposed regions of the emitter layer are heavily doped during a second diffusion process.

After the formation of the heavily doped regions, the remaining mask layer is removed to prepare the surface for further processing. This removal can be accomplished using a 10% HF solution, which effectively etches away the SiO2 without damaging the underlying silicon wafer. The resulting structure has a well-defined selective emitter with low sheet resistance in the heavily doped regions and higher sheet resistance in the lightly doped regions.

To enhance light absorption and reduce reflection, an anti-reflective coating is applied to the front surface of the solar cell. This coating can be a single layer or a multi-layer stack, depending on the desired optical properties. In this embodiment, a silicon nitride (SiN) layer is deposited using plasma-enhanced chemical vapor deposition (PECVD), providing excellent anti-reflective and passivation properties.

The formation of the front contact involves depositing a conductive material, such as silver or aluminum, to create the grid lines that collect the generated current. The grid lines are typically formed using screen printing or electroplating techniques, ensuring good electrical contact with the heavily doped regions while minimizing shading losses.

On the back surface of the solar cell, a back contact is also formed to complete the electrical circuit. This back contact can be a full-area aluminum layer or a patterned structure, depending on the design requirements. The back contact ensures effective collection of holes and provides mechanical support for the cell.

To further improve the performance of the solar cell, an optimized deep reactive ion etching (DRE) process is employed to remove plasma-induced damage from the RIE texturing step. This DRE treatment smooths the textured surface, reducing reflectance and enhancing the short-circuit current density (Isc). The optimal DRE time is determined experimentally to balance the benefits of reduced reflectance with the need to maintain a high Isc.

By combining these innovative techniques, the present invention provides a method for manufacturing solar cells with improved efficiency, lower costs, and simplified production processes. The resulting solar cells exhibit enhanced photoelectric conversion efficiency, making them suitable for various applications in renewable energy systems.
