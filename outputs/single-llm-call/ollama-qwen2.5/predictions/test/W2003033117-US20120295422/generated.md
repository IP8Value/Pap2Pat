# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

InGaN semiconductor materials are a critical component in modern optoelectronic devices due to their unique properties. These materials belong to the group III-V nitride family and have been extensively used in various applications such as blue/green LEDs, laser diodes, and solar cells. The versatility of InGaN arises from its ability to emit light across a wide spectrum, making it highly valuable for both visible and ultraviolet applications.

Multi-quantum well (MQW) structures are widely employed in these devices to enhance their performance. MQWs consist of alternating layers of different semiconductor materials, typically a thin layer of lower bandgap material (the quantum well) sandwiched between thicker layers of higher bandgap material (the barriers). This structure allows for the confinement of charge carriers and improves the efficiency of light emission.

However, conventional methods for fabricating InGaN MQWs have several limitations. One major challenge is the difficulty in achieving high-quality growth of InGaN layers with a high indium content, which is essential for producing light at longer wavelengths such as green. The high thermal mismatch between InGaN and the substrate materials often leads to strain-induced defects, cracking, and poor crystal quality. Additionally, the interfaces between the quantum wells and barriers are often not abrupt, leading to reduced efficiency and reliability of the devices.

These limitations have motivated the need for new methods that can overcome these challenges. Specifically, there is a pressing need for techniques that can produce high-quality InGaN MQWs with well-defined interfaces and improved indium distribution, particularly for applications in green LEDs and other long-wavelength devices.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides a method for fabricating an active region of a GaN-based LED, which addresses the limitations of conventional methods. The method involves several key steps to ensure high-quality growth of InGaN/GaN multi-quantum well (MQW) structures. 

First, the process includes the fabrication of a potential well layer. This is achieved by growing an InGaN layer on a suitable substrate, such as silicon or sapphire. The growth conditions are carefully controlled to ensure uniform indium incorporation and minimize strain-induced defects.

Next, the potential well is annealed and stabilized. This step involves subjecting the grown InGaN layer to a high-temperature treatment to improve its crystal quality and stability. The annealing process helps to reduce defects and enhance the uniformity of the indium distribution within the quantum well.

Finally, the method includes the fabrication of a potential barrier layer. A GaN layer is grown on top of the annealed InGaN potential well. The growth conditions for the GaN layer are optimized to ensure an abrupt interface with the InGaN layer, which is crucial for efficient charge carrier confinement and light emission.

By following this method, the invention achieves high-quality InGaN/GaN MQW structures with improved indium distribution and well-defined interfaces, leading to enhanced performance in GaN-based LEDs, particularly for green light emission.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

### Overview

The invention provides a novel process for growing single InGaN/GaN quantum well (QW) structures, which can be extended to multi-quantum well (MQW) structures. The process is designed to overcome the limitations of conventional methods and produce high-quality MQWs with improved indium distribution and abrupt interfaces.

A temperature vs. time diagram illustrates the critical steps in the growth process. Initially, a thin AlN/AlxGa(1−x)N stress-releasing layer is grown on the substrate at a lower temperature to reduce thermal mismatch and strain. This is followed by the growth of a thick GaN:Si buffer layer, which serves as a high-quality template for subsequent layers.

The InGaN/GaN MQW structure is then fabricated by alternating the growth of InGaN well layers and GaN barrier layers. A key step in this process is the introduction of a same-temperature GaN cap layer after each InGaN well. The thickness of this cap layer can be adjusted to optimize the indium distribution and prevent decomposition during subsequent high-temperature steps.

A cross-sectional view of an exemplary LED demonstrates the structure resulting from this method. The active region consists of multiple periods of InGaN wells and GaN barriers, with a thick p-GaN:Mg contact layer on top to facilitate hole injection.

Conventional methods for fabricating active regions in GaN-based LEDs typically involve growing all layers at different temperatures, which can lead to thermal stress and poor interface quality. A flow chart of the conventional process highlights these steps, including the growth of the buffer layer, QW layers, and contact layers.

However, these conventional methods have several limitations. The high-temperature ramping between well and barrier growth can cause the InGaN wells to decompose, leading to heterogeneous indium distribution and poor interface quality. This results in reduced efficiency and reliability of the devices.

The need for a new method is thus evident, particularly for producing green LEDs and other long-wavelength devices. The present invention addresses these limitations by introducing a same-temperature GaN cap layer after each InGaN well. This cap layer protects the well from decomposition during subsequent high-temperature steps, leading to improved indium distribution and abrupt interfaces.

The process for fabricating the active region of a GaN-based LED according to the invention is illustrated in a flow chart. The method begins with the growth of a stress-releasing layer and buffer layer. This is followed by the alternating growth of InGaN wells and GaN barriers, with the critical step of introducing a same-temperature GaN cap layer after each well.

By following this method, the invention achieves high-quality InGaN/GaN MQW structures with improved indium distribution and well-defined interfaces. The resulting devices exhibit enhanced performance, particularly in terms of efficiency and reliability for green light emission.
