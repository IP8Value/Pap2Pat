# Flow Rate Sensing by Frequency Shift in Membrane Deformation

In the first experiments, we measured the shift in the resonance frequency of the microwave sensor as a function of flow rate. We investigated the response of the device from the reset condition (where there was no fluid flow) to the target flow rate while monitoring the resonance frequency of the microwave sensor. When the flow rate reached a target value, sharp upward shifts in the resonance were observed. Each of these shifts depended on the magnitude of the flow rate, indicating that the amount of frequency shift can be used as a sensing parameter for the target flow rate.

The baseline frequency fluctuations were about ~1 kHz, from which one can extrapolate a short-term sensitivity level of 0.5 µL/min. However, as shown with the error bars, repeating the experiment at the same flow rate resulted in a dispersion in the frequency shifts with a standard deviation of 5.5 kHz, larger than the baseline frequency noise. The sensor demonstrated here exhibited a time resolution of only several seconds when the flow rate was increased stepwise. When the flow was stopped, we observed a downward frequency drift that lasted for approximately 50 seconds.

After liquid flow rate experiments, we switched to experiments with pressurized air. For the first air flow experiment, we used a membrane with dimensions of 1.7 mm length and 1 mm width within a channel width of 0.25 mm. The resonance frequency was approximately 2.55 GHz. We spanned the pressure from 20 mbar until 1000 mbar. An almost linear relationship for the higher-pressure values was observed, but at low pressures, the trend deviated slightly from the linear response.

To work at higher air flow rates, another device was fabricated with an increased channel width of 1.3 mm (instead of 0.25 mm in the previous device). The size of the membrane was 3 x 1 mm and the resonance frequency of the microwave sensor was approximately 2.08 GHz. A monotonically rising frequency response was observed as the applied pressure increased. The experiment demonstrated that the device with a 220 nm Silicon nitride membrane could endure an applied pressure up to 4 bar.

# Flow Rate Sensing by Membrane Pulsation Period

The interaction of thin membranes with microfluidic channels can result in dynamical bistability. In our experiments at constant liquid flows, we observed periodic deformations of the membrane under optical microscopy. To electronically read out these periodic deformations, the microwave sensor on the thin membrane can be used as an integrated transducer. The period of the modulations depends on the fluid flow rate.

The relationship between the phase response of the resonator and the effective flow rate is illustrated in Figure 5. Two different flow rates, 10 and 5 µL/min, were tested. For a given constant flow rate, the time between two consecutive pulses (pulse interval) remained constant. As the flow rate was set to a different constant value, the pulse interval changed. At 10 µL/min, the pulse interval was approximately 7 seconds, whereas at 5 µL/min, it increased to about 14 seconds.

We conducted control experiments where the commercial flow rate sensor was removed from the system and the fluid flow was provided through a vial kept at constant pressure. In this case, membrane pulsations still occurred, verifying that the pulsations are not dependent on cyclical mechanisms like syringe pumps but rather on the constant pressure source driving the flow.

After initial observations, we used three different device geometries to span different flow rates. To enhance the sensitivity of the resonator, a large membrane (1.5 mm x 0.3 mm) was fabricated and used to resolve slight increments in the flow rate. The resonance frequency of the microwave sensor coupled to this membrane was 3.66 GHz. Until 0.3 µL/min, the device did not register any pulsation in the resonance frequency. After reaching a flow rate of 0.4 µL/min, discernible pulses began.

A second device with membrane dimensions of 0.8 mm by 0.35 mm and a microwave resonance frequency of 2.36 GHz was used to span a higher flow rate range. Starting from 5 µL/min, we recorded pulses for 300 seconds at each flow rate value. The flow rate was increased in 5 µL/min steps, reaching 50 µL/min. A third device with membrane dimensions of 3 x 1 mm and a microwave resonance frequency of 2.08 GHz was used to operate at higher flow rates. Starting from 24 µL/min, we increased the flow rate until 120 µL/min, observing a similar trend where the pulsation frequency correlates with the fluid flow rate.

# Conclusion

In conclusion, we demonstrated that thin membranes integrated with microwave resonators can detect fluid flow rates. Fluid flow induces deformations on the membrane, which modulate the capacitance of a microwave resonator. The capacitance change is measured by tracking the resonance frequency of the microwave resonator. A sturdy Silicon Nitride membrane enables measurements of both liquid and gas flow through the same microfluidic architecture. In addition to quasi-static deformation, steady flow induces periodic pulsations on the thin membrane, which can be sensitively measured by changes in the phase response of the microwave sensor.

For high flow rates, the observed pulsations occur frequently, resulting in rapid update times for flow monitoring. At low flow rates, the duration between pulses gets longer, slowing the update rate. This secondary flow sensing mechanism has a shortcoming at low flow rates but provides an additional method for precise flow rate detection. The high sensitivity of microwave resonators serves as a potent transducer for probing the interaction dynamics of fluid flow and thin membranes.
