- **Introduction to BMAA and Neurodegeneration**: BMAA, a non-protein amino acid, has been linked to neurodegenerative diseases like ALS/PDC. This study investigates how BMAA can be misincorporated into human proteins, leading to protein misfolding and aggregation.

- **Protein Misfolding and Aggregation**: Protein misfolding is a key feature of neurodegenerative diseases. Few environmental agents, such as prions, are known to induce this process. This study suggests that BMAA can also cause protein misfolding through misincorporation for serine.

- **BMAA Incorporation Mechanism**: The study found that BMAA incorporation into human proteins is a protein synthesis-dependent process. It can be inhibited by l-serine, indicating that tRNA synthetases may mistakenly incorporate BMAA in place of serine.

- **Experimental Evidence**: Four lines of evidence support BMAA's misincorporation: 1) Protein synthesis inhibition reduces BMAA incorporation, 2) d-serine does not affect incorporation, 3) BMAA can be released by acid hydrolysis and proteolysis, and 4) BMAA induces protein aggregation and apoptosis.

- **Implications for Neurodegeneration**: Motor neurons are particularly vulnerable to protein aggregates due to their post-mitotic nature. BMAA-induced misfolding could trigger a neurodegenerative cascade similar to prion diseases. This suggests that BMAA exposure might contribute to neurodegenerative illnesses like ALS.

- **Exposure and Epidemiology**: Human exposure to BMAA has been documented in various regions, including Guam, where it is found in cycad flour and brain tissues of ALS/PDC patients. The study's findings could help explain putative associations between cyanobacterial blooms and higher ALS incidence rates.

- **Potential Therapeutic Implications**: L-serine can block BMAA misincorporation into proteins, suggesting a potential therapeutic approach for ALS. This finding highlights the importance of further research to develop novel treatments targeting protein misfolding in neurodegenerative diseases.
